1. Traveller's diarrhea
	**Traveller's Diarrhoea (TD)**
	
	- **Definition**: The **most common travel-related infectious illness**.
	- **Epidemiology**:
	    - Affects **20-50% of travellers** from temperate industrialized countries to tropical regions of Asia, Africa, and Central and South America.
	- **Clinical Presentation**:
	    - **Sudden onset** of abdominal cramps, anorexia, and watery diarrhoea.
	    - Typically begins within the first **3-5 days** of travel.
	    - Generally **self-limited**, lasting **1-5 days**.
	- **Etiological Agents**:
	    - **Bacterial (50-75% of cases)**:
	        - **Most Common Overall**: **Enterotoxigenic _Escherichia coli_ (ETEC)** (10-45%).
	        - Followed by **Enteroaggregative _E. coli_ (EAEC)** (5-35%).
	        - _**Campylobacter jejuni**_: **More common in Asia** (5-25%).
	        - Others: _Shigella_, Enteroinvasive _E. coli_, Non-typhoidal salmonellae, _Aeromonas_, _Plesiomonas_, and _Vibrio cholerae_.
	    - **Viral (<20% of cases)**:
	        - **Norovirus**: Associated with **cruise ship travel** (<10%).
	- **Management (Treatment)**:
	    - **Mainstay**: **Fluid therapy (rehydration)** is crucial.
	    - **Anti-motility agents** (e.g., loperamide) and **adsorbents** (e.g., bismuth subsalicylate) may be considered for moderate-to-severe cases.
	    - **Antibiotics**: Generally **avoided** unless for **severe diarrhoea**.
	        - For severe TD (ETEC or EAEC suspected): **Azithromycin** is indicated.
	        - Rifaximin (oral non-absorbable antibiotic) can also be given for severe TD.
	        - If _Campylobacter_ is suspected (especially from Asia, given its prevalence): **Azithromycin** for 3 days. (Note: Ciprofloxacin/levofloxacin are general empirical options for severe diarrhoea, but azithromycin is specified for _Campylobacter_).

		**Viral Gastroenteritis**
		
		- **Definition**: Acute inflammation of the stomach and intestine primarily caused by viruses, resulting in diarrhoea, vomiting, and abdominal pain, with or without fever or dehydration. Viral aetiology accounts for the majority of acute infectious gastroenteritis cases worldwide.
		    
		- **Etiological Agents & Key Features**:
		    
		    - **Rotavirus**:
		        - **Most common cause of severe endemic diarrhoeal illness in children worldwide**.
		        - Genome: **Segmented dsRNA**.
		        - Morphology: Wheel-shaped capsid.
		        - Pathogenesis: Multiplies in enterocytes of the small intestine, damaging transport mechanisms and causing secretory diarrhoea; non-structural protein-NSP4 acts as an enterotoxin.
		        - Clinical features: Abrupt onset, characterized by vomiting followed by **watery diarrhoea**, fever, and abdominal pain. Recovery usually occurs, but severe fluid and electrolyte loss can lead to dehydration.
		        - Diagnosis: Direct detection of virus by immunoelectron microscopy (IEM), or viral antigen by ELISA or nucleic acid (RNA) by PCR in stool.
		        - Vaccine: Two brands of **live attenuated vaccines** are available (e.g., Rotavac) and introduced under national immunization schedules in some countries.
		    - **Norovirus**:
		        - Genome: ssRNA.
		        - Causes outbreaks of vomiting and diarrhoeal illness in **all ages**, especially in older children and adults. Also associated with **cruise ship travel**.
		    - **Adenovirus** (types 40 and 41):
		        - Genome: dsDNA.
		        - Second most common viral agent of endemic diarrhoeal illness in infants and young children worldwide.
		    - **Caliciviruses**:
		        - Includes Norovirus and Sapovirus.
		        - Genome: ssRNA.
		        - Sapovirus: Causes sporadic cases and occasional outbreaks in infants, young children, and the elderly.
		    - **Astrovirus**:
		        - Genome: ssRNA.
		        - Morphology: Exhibits a distinctive **star-like morphology** under electron microscope.
		        - Causes sporadic cases and occasional outbreaks in infants, young children, and the elderly.
		- **General Clinical Management**:
		    
		    - **Fluid therapy (rehydration)** is the mainstay of treatment to correct water and electrolyte loss.
		    - Antivirals are generally not available for most viral diarrhoeal diseases.
		- **Diagnostic Methods (General for Viral Gastroenteritis)**:
		    
		    - **Electron microscopy (EM)** of stool for virus particles (e.g., rotavirus, astrovirus, coronavirus).
		    - **Antigen detection** (e.g., ELISA for rotavirus).
		    - **Molecular methods** (e.g., PCR for specific genes of enteric pathogens, BioFire FilmArray GI panel).
		    - Viral culture is generally poor for diagnosis of viral meningitis and is implicitly also poor for gastroenteritis (though not explicitly stated for all GI viruses, it's a general viral diagnostic principle).
