1. Describe the dimorphic fungi causing infections and lab diagnosis of each
	Dimorphic fungi are a group of fungi that can exist in **two distinct morphological forms** depending on environmental conditions, particularly temperature. They typically grow as **molds (hyphal form) at ambient environmental temperatures (25°C)** and transform into **yeasts in human tissues at body temperature (37°C)**. This ability to switch forms is crucial for their pathogenicity, allowing them to adapt and cause systemic infections.
	
	Here is a high-yield overview of medically important dimorphic fungi and their laboratory diagnosis:
	
	---
	
	### Dimorphic Fungi Causing Infections & Laboratory Diagnosis
	
	**I. General Characteristics of Dimorphism**
	
	- **Definition**: Fungi existing as **molds (hyphae)** in the environment (25°C) and **yeasts** in human tissues (37°C).
	- **Exception**: _Coccidioides_ is a unique dimorphic fungus that grows as mold at both 25°C and 37°C in usual culture media, forming spherules in special media at 37°C.
	- **General Lab Diagnosis Principles**:
	    - **Specimen Collection**: Depends on site of infection (e.g., sputum, tissue biopsies, blood, CSF).
	    - **Microscopy**:
	        - **KOH mount**: Digests keratinized tissue to visualise fungal hyphae.
	        - **Histopathological stains**: **Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS)** (stains fungi magenta/pink), **Gomori Methenamine Silver (GMS)** (stains fungi black).
	        - **Gram stain**: Yeasts may appear gram-positive.
	    - **Culture**:
	        - **Gold Standard**: Isolation is often the gold standard.
	        - **Media**: **Sabouraud's Dextrose Agar (SDA)** and **Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) agar** or **blood agar** (for fastidious fungi).
	        - **Temperature**: Incubate duplicate cultures at **25°C (mold form)** and **37°C (yeast form)**. Incubation typically takes 2-3 weeks.
	    - **Molecular Methods**: PCR, real-time PCR, DNA sequencing for accurate identification and speciation.
	    - **Serology/Antigen Detection**: Useful, especially for systemic infections.
	
	**II. Specific Dimorphic Fungi & Diagnostic Features**
	
	A. _**Histoplasma capsulatum**_
	
	- **Disease**: Histoplasmosis ("Darling's disease").
	- **Clinical Relevance**: Causes systemic granulomatous disease, often beginning as pulmonary infection.
	- **Lab Diagnosis**:
	    - **Microscopy**: Tiny oval **yeast cells (2–4 µm) with narrow-based budding** _within macrophages_ on histopathological stains (PAS, Giemsa, GMS).
	    - **Culture**:
	        - 25°C (mold): White to buff-brown colonies producing **tuberculate macroconidia** (thick walls, finger-like projections).
	        - 37°C (yeast): Creamy white colonies.
	    - **Serology**: Antibodies by CFT, immunodiffusion (useful in chronic stage, may be negative in early or disseminated disease).
	    - **Molecular**: PCR targeting specific genes (e.g., ITS D1/D2 gene).
	    - **Skin Test**: Histoplasmin antigen (indicates prior exposure).
	
	B. _**Blastomyces dermatitidis**_
	
	- **Disease**: Blastomycosis. Endemic in North America.
	- **Clinical Relevance**: Systemic fungal disease, can cause pulmonary or extrapulmonary manifestations.
	- **Lab Diagnosis**:
	    - **Microscopy**: **Thick-walled round yeast cells (8–15 µm) with single broad-based budding** (described as "Figure of 8 appearance") in tissue biopsy specimens.
	    - **Culture**:
	        - 25°C (mold): Mycelial form with small pear-shaped conidia.
	        - 37°C (yeast): Yeast form conversion occurs.
	    - **Antigen Detection**: Blastomyces antigen in **urine (more sensitive)** and serum.
	    - **Antibody Detection**: Immunodiffusion test against yeast phase antigens (Antigen-A, BAD-1, ASWS antigen).
	    - **Molecular**: DNA probe hybridization and real-time PCR.
	    - **Skin Test**: Blastomycin antigen (demonstrates delayed type hypersensitivity).
	
	C. _**Coccidioides immitis**_ (and _C. posadasii_)
	
	- **Disease**: Coccidioidomycosis ("desert rheumatism," "Valley fever," "California fever").
	- **Clinical Relevance**: Systemic fungal disease, soil-dwelling, often causes pulmonary infection that can disseminate.
	- **Lab Diagnosis**:
	    - **Microscopy**: Presence of **spherules** (large, sac-like, 20–80 µm, thick, double refractile wall, filled with endospores) in sputum or tissue biopsy.
	    - **Culture**: Grows as mold at both 25°C and 37°C in usual culture media. Produces fragmented hyphae with **barrel-shaped arthrospores** (with alternate cells distorted). **Requires biosafety level-3 precautions** due to high infectivity of spores.
	    - **Serology**: Antibodies detected by immunodiffusion and Complement Fixation Test (CFT).
	    - **Skin Test**: Fungal extracts (coccidioidin or spherulin) for delayed hypersensitivity.
	
	D. _**Paracoccidioides brasiliensis**_
	
	- **Disease**: Paracoccidioidomycosis ("South American blastomycosis," "Lutz-Splendore-de Almeida disease"). Endemic in South American countries.
	- **Clinical Relevance**: Systemic disease, often affecting lungs, skin, oral mucosa, and lymph nodes.
	- **Lab Diagnosis**:
	    - **Microscopy**: Demonstration of **round thick-walled yeasts with multiple narrow-necked buds attached circumferentially** (giving a "Mickey mouse" or "pilot wheel" appearance) in pus, tissue biopsies, or sputum by histopathological staining.
	    - **Culture**: Dimorphic, will grow as mold at 25°C and yeast at 37°C.
	
	E. _**Penicillium marneffei**_ (now _Talaromyces marneffei_)
	
	- **Disease**: Penicilliosis.
	- **Clinical Relevance**: Opportunistic infection, primarily in **HIV-infected patients**. Can cause systemic infection resembling disseminated histoplasmosis and warty skin lesions.
	- **Lab Diagnosis**:
	    - **Microscopy**: Oval or elliptical **yeast cells with central septation** (divide by transverse fission rather than budding) in tissue sections, skin scrapings, or blood smear.
	    - **Culture**:
	        - 25°C (mold): Mold form, microscopic appearance similar to other _Penicillium_ species.
	        - 37°C (yeast): Yeast-like colonies with a characteristic **brick red pigment**.
	
	Understanding the specific morphology of the yeast and mold forms, as well as the preferred diagnostic techniques for each, is key for medical school examinations.

2. Deep mycotic conditions and their causative agents
	Deep mycotic conditions, also known as **systemic mycoses** or **deep fungal infections**, are severe fungal infections that typically involve multiple internal organs. They are often acquired by inhalation of spores, leading to primary pulmonary infection, which can then disseminate to various other body systems. These conditions are particularly relevant in immunocompromised patients.
	
	Here's a high-yield overview of deep mycotic conditions and their causative agents from the sources:
	
	---
	
	### Deep Mycotic Conditions & Causative Agents
	
	**I. Classification of Deep Mycotic Infections** Deep mycoses are broadly categorized into:
	
	- **Systemic Mycoses (Endemic Mycoses)**: Caused by **thermally dimorphic fungi** that exist as molds in the environment (25°C) and convert to yeast forms in human tissues (37°C).
	- **Opportunistic Mycoses**: Primarily affect immunocompromised individuals, though some can cause infection in immunocompetent hosts under specific conditions.
	
	**II. Systemic Mycoses (Endemic Dimorphic Fungi)** These are **thermally dimorphic fungi**, typically acquired via **inhalation of spores**:
	
	A. _**Histoplasma capsulatum**_
	
	- **Disease**: Histoplasmosis ("Darling's disease").
	- **Clinical Relevance**: Causes **systemic granulomatous disease**, often starting as a pulmonary infection, which can disseminate, especially in HIV-infected individuals. Oral mucosal lesions are particularly seen in Indian patients.
	- **Key Features**: In human tissues (37°C), it presents as **tiny oval yeast cells (2–4 µm) with narrow-based budding, typically found _within macrophages_**. In culture (25°C), it produces **tuberculate macroconidia**.
	
	B. _**Blastomyces dermatitidis**_
	
	- **Disease**: Blastomycosis. Endemic in North America.
	- **Clinical Relevance**: Can cause pulmonary or extrapulmonary disease.
	- **Key Features**: In human tissues (37°C), it appears as **thick-walled round yeast cells (8–15 µm) with single broad-based budding** (classic "Figure of 8 appearance").
	
	C. _**Coccidioides immitis**_ (and _C. posadasii_)
	
	- **Disease**: Coccidioidomycosis ("desert rheumatism," "Valley fever," "California fever").
	- **Clinical Relevance**: Soil-dwelling fungus, highly infectious. Can cause pulmonary infection that may disseminate.
	- **Key Features**: Unique among dimorphic fungi, it grows as **mold at both 25°C and 37°C in usual culture media**, forming **spherules (20–80 µm, thick-walled, filled with endospores)** in specialized media at 37°C or directly in tissues. In culture (mold form), it produces **barrel-shaped arthrospores**. **Requires biosafety level-3 precautions**.
	
	D. _**Paracoccidioides brasiliensis**_
	
	- **Disease**: Paracoccidioidomycosis ("South American blastomycosis," "Lutz-Splendore-de Almeida disease"). Endemic in South American countries.
	- **Clinical Relevance**: Systemic disease affecting lungs, skin, oral mucosa, and lymph nodes.
	- **Key Features**: In human tissues (37°C), it appears as **round thick-walled yeasts with multiple narrow-necked buds attached circumferentially** (classic "Mickey mouse" or "pilot wheel" appearance).
	
	E. _**Talaromyces marneffei**_ (formerly _Penicillium marneffei_)
	
	- **Disease**: Penicilliosis.
	- **Clinical Relevance**: **Opportunistic infection**, primarily in **HIV-infected patients**. Can cause systemic infection resembling disseminated histoplasmosis and warty skin lesions.
	- **Key Features**: In human tissues (37°C), it presents as **oval or elliptical yeast cells with central septation** (dividing by transverse fission, not budding). In culture (mold form), colonies produce a characteristic **brick red pigment**.
	
	**III. Opportunistic Deep Fungal Infections** These fungi usually cause disease in individuals with weakened immune systems.
	
	A. _**Candida species**_
	
	- **Disease**: Systemic Candidiasis.
	- **Clinical Relevance**: **Most common etiological agent of fungemia**, especially in immunocompromised patients. Can cause infections in various organs including blood (septicaemia), joints, bones, and meninges. _C. auris_ is an emerging, multidrug-resistant species causing healthcare outbreaks.
	- **Key Features**: Gram-positive **oval budding yeast cells with pseudohyphae**. _C. albicans_ has a unique ability to switch between yeast, pseudohyphae, and true hyphae forms. **Germ tube test is positive for _C. albicans_**.
	
	B. _**Cryptococcus neoformans**_
	
	- **Disease**: Cryptococcosis, particularly **cryptococcal meningitis**.
	- **Clinical Relevance**: A major cause of fatal meningitis in **HIV-infected individuals** with low CD4 T cell counts. _C. gattii_ can infect immunocompetent individuals.
	- **Key Features**: **Capsulated yeast**. **Negative staining (India ink stain)** demonstrates the capsule as a clear halo around the budding yeast cells against a dark background. Mucicarmine stain colours the cell wall. Culture on **Niger seed agar or bird seed agar** demonstrates melanin production (brown colonies).
	
	C. **Zygomycosis (e.g., _Mucor, Rhizopus, Lichtheimia_)**
	
	- **Disease**: Mucormycosis.
	- **Clinical Relevance**: Life-threatening infections, especially in patients with **diabetic ketoacidosis**. Can cause rhinocerebral, pulmonary, cutaneous, and disseminated forms. Angioinvasive nature leads to ischemic necrosis.
	- **Key Features**: **Broad, aseptate hyaline hyphae with wide-angle branching** in tissue. Cultures produce **white cottony woolly colonies**. _Rhizopus_ has unique **nodal rhizoids**.
	
	D. _**Aspergillus species**_ (e.g., _A. fumigatus, A. flavus, A. niger_)
	
	- **Disease**: Aspergillosis (invasive and allergic diseases).
	- **Clinical Relevance**: Invasive aspergillosis common in **immunocompromised patients**, especially those on glucocorticoids or with profound neutropenia. Pulmonary aspergillosis is the most common form.
	- **Key Features**: **Narrow, septate hyaline hyphae with acute-angle branching** in tissue. Cultures produce rapidly growing colonies with characteristic **conidiophores, vesicles, and chains of conidia**. **Galactomannan antigen detection** is useful for early diagnosis.
	
	**IV. Other Relevant Deep Fungal Infections**
	
	- _**Sporothrix schenckii**_
	    - **Disease**: Sporotrichosis ("Rose Gardner's disease").
	    - **Clinical Relevance**: Causes subcutaneous noduloulcerative lesions, but is a **dimorphic fungus** that can be considered a deep mycosis due to potential for dissemination.
	    - **Key Features**: Yeast form (37°C) may show **cigar-shaped asteroid bodies** in tissue. Mold form (25°C) has **flower-like conidia** arrangement.
	
	This summary provides a concise overview for medical exam preparation, emphasizing key diagnostic features and clinical contexts for each dimorphic and major opportunistic deep fungal pathogen.
	
3. Fungi affecting the nail  
	Here's a concise, high-yield, note-style overview of fungi that affect nails and those that do not, based on the provided sources:
	
	---
	
	### Fungi that Affect Nails
	
	- **Candida species**:
	    - _Candida_ is a yeast-like fungus that produces pseudohyphae and is the most common fungal disease in humans, affecting the skin, mucosa, and various internal organs.
	    - It causes cutaneous candidiasis, including **nail infections such as paronychia and onychomycosis**.
	    - _Candida albicans_ is specifically identified as causing _onychomycosis_.
	- **Dermatophytes**:
	    - These are a group of related fungi capable of infecting keratinised tissues like skin, hair, and **nail**.
	    - They cause **dermatophytoses (tinea or ringworm)**, including _tinea unguium_ (nail plate infection).
	    - Specific genera include:
	        - _**Trichophyton species**_: Infect skin, hair, and **nail**. _T. mentagrophytes_ is an agent for _tinea unguium_.
	        - _**Epidermophyton species**_: Infect skin and **nail**. _E. floccosum_ is an agent for _tinea unguium_.
	- **Aspergillus species**:
	    - These are hyaline molds that cause invasive and allergic diseases.
	    - _Aspergillus_ can cause **nail bed infection (onychomycosis)**.
	- **Penicillium species**:
	    - These can cause various opportunistic infections.
	    - Other _Penicillium_ species (beyond _P. marneffei_) can cause superficial diseases, including **onychomycosis**.
	- **Fusarium species**:
	    - These are soil and plant saprophytes that rarely cause human infections.
	    - In immunocompetent individuals, _F. solani_ (most common), _F. oxysporum_, and _F. verticillioides_ can cause **keratitis and onychomycosis**.
	
	### Fungi that Do Not Affect Nails
	
	- **Microsporum species**:
	    - These dermatophytes infect skin and hair but **do not infect nails**.
	- **Cryptococcus neoformans**:
	    - A capsulated yeast, it primarily causes **cryptococcal meningitis**, especially in HIV-infected individuals. While fungemia is observed, its principal sites of infection are the Central Nervous System (CNS) and respiratory system. There is no indication in the sources that it infects nails.
	- **Zygomycetes (e.g., _Mucor, Rhizopus, Lichtheimia_)**:
	    - These cause life-threatening infections, particularly **rhinocerebral mucormycosis** in patients with diabetic ketoacidosis, and can also manifest as pulmonary, cutaneous, and disseminated forms. _Cutaneous mucormycosis_ is noted. The sources do not mention nail involvement.
	- **Histoplasma capsulatum**:
	    - A dimorphic fungus causing **histoplasmosis**, a systemic granulomatous disease, often starting as a pulmonary infection and potentially disseminating. Oral mucosal lesions are also noted. The sources do not mention nail involvement.
	- **Blastomyces dermatitidis**:
	    - A dimorphic fungus causing **blastomycosis**, which can present as pulmonary or extrapulmonary disease. The sources do not mention nail involvement.
	- **Coccidioides immitis**:
	    - A dimorphic fungus causing **coccidioidomycosis**, often presenting as a pulmonary infection that may disseminate. The sources do not mention nail involvement.
	- **Paracoccidioides brasiliensis**:
	    - A dimorphic fungus causing **paracoccidioidomycosis**, a systemic disease affecting lungs, skin, oral mucosa, and lymph nodes. The sources do not mention nail involvement.
	- **Talaromyces marneffei** (formerly _Penicillium marneffei_):
	    - This is a dimorphic fungus causing opportunistic systemic infection resembling disseminated histoplasmosis and **wart-like skin lesions**, primarily in HIV-infected patients. While it's a _Penicillium_ species, the source does not list nail infection for _T. marneffei_ specifically, unlike other _Penicillium_ species.
	- **Pneumocystis jirovecii**:
	    - Causes **Pneumocystis pneumonia (PCP)**, typically in HIV-infected patients. It is classified as a fungus. The sources do not mention nail involvement.
	- **Malassezia furfur**:
	    - A lipophilic fungus causing **tinea versicolor** (a superficial infection of the stratum corneum with scaly hypo- to hyperpigmented patches). It also causes seborrheic dermatitis, atopic dermatitis, and folliculitis. The sources do not mention nail involvement.
