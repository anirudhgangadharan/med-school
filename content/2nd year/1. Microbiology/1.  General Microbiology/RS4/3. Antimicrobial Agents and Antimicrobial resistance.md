1. Drug resistance in bacteria-mechanism with examples
	Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is the development of resistance to an antimicrobial agent by a microorganism, rendering standard treatments ineffective and leading to prolonged illness, higher healthcare expenditures, and increased mortality.
	
	### Types of Drug Resistance
	
	1. **Intrinsic Resistance:**
	    
	    - An **innate ability** of a bacterium to resist a class of antimicrobial agents due to its inherent structural or functional characteristics.
	    - **Non-transferable** and poses negligible threat, but clinicians must be aware to avoid these drugs.
	    - **Examples:**
	        - **Gram-negative bacteria** are intrinsically resistant to antimicrobials specific for Gram-positive organisms (e.g., clindamycin, daptomycin, vancomycin, linezolid, macrolides). Exceptions include _Salmonella_ and _Shigella_ species, which are susceptible to azithromycin.
	        - **Gram-positive bacteria** are intrinsically resistant to aztreonam, polymyxin B/colistin, and nalidixic acid.
	        - _**Staphylococcus aureus**_ is intrinsically resistant to the same drugs as other Gram-positive bacteria.
	        - **Enterococci** are intrinsically resistant to cephalosporins, clindamycin, and co-trimoxazole. _E. gallinarum_ and _E. casseliflavus_ are intrinsically resistant to vancomycin. Aminoglycosides are effective against Enterococci only when given with cell-wall acting drugs (e.g., penicillin, ampicillin, vancomycin) due to synergy.
	        - _**B. pseudomallei**_ is intrinsically resistant to penicillin, ertapenem, polymyxins, and fosfomycin.
	        - _**Klebsiella pneumoniae**_ is intrinsically resistant to ampicillin and ticarcillin.
	        - _**Acinetobacter**_ is intrinsically resistant to ampicillin, amoxicillin, ertapenem, aztreonam, chloramphenicol, and fosfomycin.
	        - **_Serratia_ species** are intrinsically resistant to ampicillin, first and second-generation cephalosporins, amoxicillin-clavulanate, ampicillin-sulbactam, nitrofurantoin, and polymyxins.
	        - **Anaerobic bacteria** are intrinsically resistant to aminoglycosides.
	        - **Mycoplasma** are intrinsically resistant to cell wall active antibiotics (e.g., penicillin) as they lack a cell wall.
	2. **Acquired Resistance:**
	    
	    - Emergence of resistance in bacteria ordinarily susceptible to antimicrobials, by acquiring resistance genes.
	    - **Overuse and misuse of antimicrobial agents** is the single most important cause. Antibiotic use creates **selective pressure**, allowing resistant bacteria to thrive.
	    - Spread is favoured by poor infection control, inadequate sanitation, inappropriate food handling, and uncontrolled sale of antibiotics.
	3. **Mutational vs. Transferable Drug Resistance:**
	    
	    - **Mutational Resistance:**
	        - Develops due to **mutation of resident genes**.
	        - Typically seen in _**Mycobacterium tuberculosis**_ against anti-tubercular drugs.
	        - Usually **low-level resistance** to one drug at a time.
	        - Can be **overcome by using combination of different classes of drugs** (e.g., multidrug therapy for TB).
	        - **Not transferable** to other organisms; spreads only vertically to offspring.
	        - **Examples:** Rifampicin resistance in _M. tuberculosis_ due to mutations in RNA polymerase. Fluoroquinolone resistance in _Salmonella_ due to mutations in _gyrA_ and _parC_ genes.
	    - **Transferable Drug Resistance:**
	        - **Plasmid-coded** and usually transferred by **conjugation** (most common), or rarely by **transduction** or **transformation**.
	        - The **R plasmid** (resistance factor) carries multiple genes, each coding for resistance to one class of antibiotic.
	        - Results in **high degree of resistance to multiple drugs**.
	        - **Cannot be overcome by using combination of drugs** that share the same resistance mechanism.
	        - **Examples:** Beta-lactamase production in _S. aureus_ is plasmid-coded and transferred by transduction. Multi-drug resistant (MDR) _E. coli_ and _Klebsiella pneumoniae_ are common examples of transferable resistance.
	
	### Mechanisms of Antimicrobial Resistance
	
	Bacteria develop AMR through several key mechanisms:
	
	4. **Decreased Permeability across the Cell Wall:**
	    
	    - Bacteria modify their **cell membrane porin channels** (frequency, size, or selectivity), preventing antimicrobial entry.
	    - **Examples:** _Pseudomonas_, _Enterobacter_, and _Klebsiella_ species against imipenem, aminoglycosides, and quinolones. Biofilm formation by _Pseudomonas_ also prevents antibiotic entry.
	5. **Efflux Pumps:**
	    
	    - Bacteria possess **efflux pumps** that expel drugs from the cell shortly after entry, preventing intracellular accumulation.
	    - **Examples:** Seen with tetracyclines, macrolides, chloramphenicol, and streptogramins. Also contributes to carbapenem resistance and fluoroquinolone resistance in _Salmonella_.
	6. **Drug Inactivation (Enzymatic Degradation):**
	    
	    - Bacteria produce enzymes that **destroy or modify the antibiotic molecule**, rendering it inactive.
	    - **Beta-lactamases:** Enzymes that hydrolyse the β-lactam ring (active site) of β-lactam antibiotics, deactivating them.
	        - Produced by both Gram-positive and Gram-negative organisms.
	        - Often plasmid-coded and transferred by conjugation (e.g., in _Enterobacteriaceae_).
	        - **Types of Beta-lactamases:**
	            - **Extended Spectrum β-lactamases (ESBL):** Confer resistance to all penicillins, 1st, 2nd, and 3rd generation cephalosporins, and monobactams. Susceptibility to carbapenems and cephamycins usually maintained. Resistance can be overcome by β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations (e.g., piperacillin-tazobactam). _K. pneumoniae_ strains in India are frequently ESBL producers.
	            - **AmpC β-lactamases:** Confer resistance to ESBL-affected drugs plus cephamycins (e.g., cefoxitin). Sensitive to carbapenems. Resistance **cannot** be overcome by β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations.
	            - **Carbapenemases:** Confer resistance to all antibiotics affected by AmpC, _plus_ carbapenems. Resistance **cannot** be overcome by β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations. Important examples include **Klebsiella pneumoniae Carbapenemase (KPC)** and **New Delhi Metallo-beta-lactamase (NDM)**. Carbapenem resistance in _K. pneumoniae_ is high in India.
	    - **Chloramphenicol acetyltransferase:** Produced by _Enterobacteriaceae_; destroys chloramphenicol structure.
	7. **Modification of Target Sites:**
	    
	    - Alterations in the bacterial target sites where antimicrobial agents bind, reducing drug affinity.
	    - **Penicillin-binding protein (PBP) alteration:**
	        - **Methicillin-resistant _Staphylococcus aureus_ (MRSA):** _mecA_ gene alters PBP to **PBP-2a**, which has less affinity for β-lactam antibiotics, leading to resistance to all β-lactams.
	        - **Penicillin resistance in Pneumococci:** Due to alteration of PBP to **PBP2b**.
	    - **Ribosomal protein/rRNA modification:**
	        - **Streptomycin resistance in _M. tuberculosis_:** Due to modification of ribosomal proteins or 16S rRNA.
	        - Resistance to macrolides, oxazolidinones, and lincosamides also involves ribosomal target alteration.
	    - **RNA polymerase mutation:**
	        - **Rifampicin resistance in _M. tuberculosis_:** Due to mutations in RNA polymerase.
	    - **DNA gyrase enzyme mutation:**
	        - **Quinolone resistance:** Seen in _S. aureus_ and _S. pneumoniae_.
	    - **Peptidoglycan side chain alteration:**
	        - **Vancomycin-resistant Enterococci (VRE):** _van_ gene alters the D-Ala-D-Ala side chain of peptidoglycan to D-Ala-D-Ser or D-Ala-D-Lac, reducing vancomycin affinity.
	        - **Vancomycin-resistant _S. aureus_ (VRSA):** Mediated by the _vanA_ gene, acquired from VRE.
	        - **Vancomycin-intermediate _S. aureus_ (VISA):** Due to increased cell wall thickness.
	    - **Enzyme modification/overproduction:**
	        - Resistance to antifolates (e.g., pyrimethamine) due to point mutation in **DHFR (dihydrofolate reductase) gene**.
