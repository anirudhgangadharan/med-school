1. Lab diagnosis of Hepatitis B	
	**LABORATORY DIAGNOSIS OF HEPATITIS B INFECTIONS**
	
	**I. Core Diagnostic Principle:**
	
	- **Definitive diagnosis relies on serological demonstration of viral markers**.
	
	**II. Detection Methods Utilised:**
	
	- **ELISA, chemiluminescence, ELFA** (e.g. Vidas) and **rapid methods** (e.g. ICT) are used for detecting HBV antigens and antibodies.
	- **PCR** (Polymerase Chain Reaction) is used for viral DNA detection, and **real-time PCR** for quantification.
	- **HBV does not grow in any conventional culture system**.
	
	**III. Key Viral Markers and their Significance:**
	
	**A. Antigen Markers:**
	
	1. **Hepatitis B Surface Antigen (HBsAg)**
	    
	    - **First marker to be elevated** following infection.
	    - **Appearance**: Usually appears 1-12 weeks (typically 8-12 weeks) post-infection, **2-6 weeks before biochemical/clinical evidence of hepatitis** (i.e., during the incubation period).
	    - **Significance**:
	        - Indicates **onset of infectivity**.
	        - Remains elevated throughout acute hepatitis; persistence beyond 6 months indicates **chronic hepatitis or carrier state**.
	        - Used as an **epidemiological marker of hepatitis B infection**.
	2. **Hepatitis B Precore Antigen (HBeAg) and HBV DNA**
	    
	    - **Appearance**: Appear concurrently with or shortly after HBsAg.
	    - **Significance**:
	        - **Markers of active viral replication** and **high viral infectivity** (high transmission rate).
	        - Their presence indicates **actively multiplying virus**, seen in acute active hepatitis, chronic active hepatitis, or highly infectious carriers ("super carriers").
	        - **HBV DNA load helps in monitoring treatment response**.
	        - _Clinical Relevance_: Patients with precore mutants may not produce HBeAg, leading to late diagnosis of severe chronic hepatitis. Escape mutants, altered HBsAg, can also lead to underdiagnosis.
	3. **Hepatitis B Core Antigen (HBcAg)**
	    
	    - Considered a **"hidden" antigen**; it is **non-secretory** and **cannot be detected in blood**.
	    - **Detection**: Can only be detected in **hepatocytes by immunofluorescence test**.
	
	**B. Antibody Markers:**
	
	4. **Anti-HBc IgM (Hepatitis B Core Antibody)**
	    
	    - **First antibody to be elevated** following infection.
	    - **Appearance**: Appears 1-2 weeks after HBsAg, lasts 3-6 months.
	    - **Significance**:
	        - Indicates **acute hepatitis B infection**.
	        - **Important marker during the "window period"** (when HBsAg has disappeared but anti-HBs has not yet appeared).
	5. **Anti-HBc IgG (Hepatitis B Core Antibody)**
	    
	    - **Appearance**: Appears in late acute stage and **remains positive indefinitely**.
	    - **Significance**:
	        - Indicates **past infection, chronic stage, carrier state, or recovery**.
	        - Can also be used as an **epidemiological marker of HBV infection**.
	6. **Anti-HBe (Hepatitis B Precore Antibody)**
	    
	    - **Appearance**: Appears after HBeAg clearance.
	    - **Significance**: Signifies **diminished viral replication** and **decreased infectivity**.
	7. **Anti-HBs (Hepatitis B Surface Antibody)**
	    
	    - **Appearance**: Appears after HBsAg clearance and **remains elevated indefinitely**.
	    - **Significance**:
	        - Indicates **recovery, immunity, and non-infectivity** (stoppage of transmission).
	        - **The ONLY marker of hepatitis B vaccination**.
	
	**C. Molecular Markers:**
	
	8. **HBV DNA Detection (by PCR and Real-time PCR)**
	    - **Indicates active viral replication and high infectivity**.
	    - **Quantification by real-time PCR** is critical for **monitoring treatment response**.
	
	**IV. Non-Specific Findings:**
	
	- Elevated **liver enzymes** and **serum bilirubin** indicate clinically active infection.
	
	**V. Summary of Marker Interpretations (High-Yield for Exams):**
	
	- **Early Acute/Incubating Hepatitis**: HBsAg positive.
	- **Acute Hepatitis (High Infectivity)**: HBsAg, HBeAg, Anti-HBc IgM all positive.
	- **Chronic Hepatitis (High Infectivity)**: HBsAg, HBeAg, Anti-HBc IgG all positive.
	- **Chronic Hepatitis (Low Infectivity)**: HBsAg, Anti-HBc IgG, Anti-HBe all positive.
	- **Immunotolerant Chronic HBV Infection ("Super Carrier")**: HBsAg, HBeAg, Anti-HBc IgG all positive (asymptomatic, normal liver enzymes).
	- **Chronic Inactive HBV Infection ("Simple Carrier")**: HBsAg, Anti-HBc IgG, Anti-HBe all positive (asymptomatic, normal liver enzymes).
	- **Precore-Mutant Hepatitis B Infection**: HBsAg, Anti-HBc IgG, HBV DNA positive; HBeAg negative, Anti-HBe may or may not be present.
	- **Escape Mutant Hepatitis B Infection**: HBsAg negative (often underdiagnosed by routine serology), Anti-HBc IgG positive.
	- **Recovery**: Anti-HBs, Anti-HBc IgG positive.
	- **Post-Vaccination**: Only Anti-HBs positive.
	- **Occult Hepatitis B Infection**: HBsAg negative, Anti-HBc IgG positive (± HBV DNA).
2. Markers for Acute Hepatitis Infection
	**MARKERS FOR ACUTE HEPATITIS INFECTION**
	
	**I. General Principles of Acute Hepatitis Diagnosis:**
	
	- **Elevated liver enzymes and serum bilirubin** are non-specific findings that indicate clinically active infection.
	- **Molecular methods (PCR)** are increasingly becoming gold standard for early detection of viral RNA/DNA and confirming active replication.
	
	**II. Specific Viral Markers for Acute Infection:**
	
	**A. Hepatitis A Virus (HAV)**
	
	- **HAV is a common cause of acute viral hepatitis in children**.
	- **Anti-HAV IgM (IgM antibodies to HAV):**
	    - **Appearance**: Appears during the acute phase, peaking about 2 weeks after liver enzyme elevation. Disappears within 3-6 months.
	    - **Significance**: **Indicates acute infection with HAV**.
	- _Note_: IgG antibodies (Anti-HAV IgG) indicate past infection or recovery, _not_ acute infection if IgM is absent. HAV particles or antigens may be detected in stool around the time of jaundice, but antibody detection is the method of choice. No chronic or carrier state exists for HAV.
	
	**B. Hepatitis B Virus (HBV)**
	
	- **HBsAg (Hepatitis B Surface Antigen):**
	    - **Appearance**: **First marker to be elevated** following infection, appearing 1-12 weeks (usually 8-12 weeks) post-infection. It appears during the incubation period, 2-6 weeks _before_ biochemical/clinical evidence of hepatitis.
	    - **Significance**: Indicates **onset of infectivity**. Persistence beyond 6 months indicates chronic hepatitis or a carrier state.
	- **HBeAg (Hepatitis B precore Antigen) & HBV DNA:**
	    - **Appearance**: Appear concurrently with or shortly after HBsAg.
	    - **Significance**: Markers of **active viral replication** and **high viral infectivity**. Their presence indicates actively multiplying virus, seen in acute active hepatitis, chronic active hepatitis, or highly infectious carriers ("super carriers"). HBV DNA load helps monitor treatment response.
	- **Anti-HBc IgM (IgM antibody to Hepatitis B Core Antigen):**
	    - **Appearance**: **First antibody to be elevated** following infection, appearing 1-2 weeks after HBsAg. Lasts for 3-6 months.
	    - **Significance**: **Crucial marker of acute hepatitis B infection**. It is often the _only_ marker present during the **"window period"** (when HBsAg has disappeared but anti-HBs has not yet appeared).
	**C. Hepatitis C Virus (HCV)**
	- **HCV RNA Detection (by Real-time RT-PCR):**
	    - **Appearance**: Can be detected in blood as early as **2-3 weeks after infection**.
	    - **Significance**: **Gold standard for confirmation of active HCV infection**. Also used for quantification (viral load) and genotyping.
	- **HCV Antigen Detection:**
	    - **Significance**: Can be used as an alternative to HCV RNA testing for diagnosis of active/current infection.
	- _Note_: HCV Antibody Detection Assays (e.g., ELISA) are standard for screening but are not sufficient for diagnosing acute infection alone, as antibodies appear later (around 5 weeks post-infection for third-generation ELISA). A positive antibody test should be followed by HCV RNA testing to confirm active infection.
	
	**D. Hepatitis D Virus (HDV)**
	
	- **HDV is a defective virus, dependent on HBV for replication**. Acute infection occurs either as **co-infection** (simultaneous HBV and HDV) or **super-infection** (HDV infecting an existing HBV carrier).
	- **In Co-infection:**
	    - **IgM against both HDAg and HBcAg are elevated**. IgM anti-HDV may appear late and be short-lived.
	    - **HDV RNA is detectable** in blood and liver just before and in early days of acute phase.
	- **In Super-infection:**
	    - IgM anti-HDV is present initially (as the patient progresses to chronic state, a mixture of IgM and IgG anti-HDV persists).
	    - IgG anti-HBc will be detected (as HBV infection is already established as a carrier).
	    - HDV RNA is detectable.
	
	**E. Hepatitis E Virus (HEV)**
	
	- **HEV causes an enterically transmitted hepatitis, primarily occurring as epidemics**.
	- **IgM anti-HEV (IgM antibody to HEV):**
	    - **Appearance**: Appears in serum simultaneously with increased liver enzyme levels.
	    - **Significance**: **Indicates acute infection**.
	- _Note_: IgG anti-HEV replaces IgM in 2-4 weeks (after symptoms resolve) and persists for years, indicating recovery or past infection. No chronic infection or carrier state.
3. Mode of transmission of Hepatitis B infection
	**MODE OF TRANSMISSION OF HEPATITIS B INFECTION**
	
	Hepatitis B Virus (HBV) transmission occurs via multiple routes, making it one of the most widespread and important hepatitis viruses. Humans are the **only reservoir** of infection, including both cases and carriers. Patients are considered infectious as long as **HBsAg is present in blood** (from about a month before jaundice to several months or years for chronic carriers). **Maximum infectivity** is observed when **HBeAg is elevated** in serum.
	
	**I. Key Modes of Transmission:**
	
	1. **Parenteral Route (Most Common in Developing Countries):**
	    
	    - **Blood and Blood Products Transfusion**: A major mode of transmission.
	    - **Needle Prick Injuries**: **Highly infectious**. The chance of HBV transmission after a contaminated needle prick is approximately **30%**, compared to 3% for HCV and 0.3% for HIV.
	    - **Percutaneous Inoculation**: Occurs during surgical or dental procedures, or via shared razors and toothbrushes.
	    - **High Infectivity**: As little as **0.00001 mL of blood can be infectious**. HBV can remain infectious on environmental surfaces for at least 7 days.
	2. **Sexual Transmission (Most Common in Developed Countries):**
	    
	    - Often seen in developed countries, particularly among **homosexual males** who are at higher risk.
	3. **Vertical (Perinatal) Transmission:**
	    
	    - Significant mode of transmission, especially in regions like China and Southeast Asia.
	    - Occurs at any stage:
	        - **In utero**
	        - **During delivery** (maximum risk)
	        - **During breastfeeding**
	    - Risk is **maximum if the mother is HBeAg positive**.
	4. **Direct Skin Contact:**
	    
	    - Transmission can occur through contact with infected open skin lesions, such as impetigo (especially in children).
	
	_Note_: Although HBV can survive in mosquitoes, **no transmission has been observed** via this route.
	
	**II. High-Risk Groups:**
	
	- **Healthcare Professionals**: Surgeons, technicians, phlebotomists, paramedical workers.
	- **Sex Workers**: Especially homosexual males.
	- **Recipients**: Individuals receiving blood transfusions and organ transplantation.
	- **Drug Addicts**.
