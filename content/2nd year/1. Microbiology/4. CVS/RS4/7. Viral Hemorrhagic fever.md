	**1. Clinical Presentation Analysis** The patient's symptoms – high-grade fever, severe joint pain (arthralgia), back pain, myalgia, petechial rashes, jaundice, hepatosplenomegaly, low platelet count (30,000/cmm), and a positive tourniquet test – are highly suggestive of a severe febrile illness.
	
	**2. Probable Clinical Diagnosis and Transmission**
	
	- **Probable Clinical Diagnosis:** The most probable diagnosis, given the constellation of symptoms including high-grade fever, petechial rashes, low platelet count, and a positive tourniquet test, is **Dengue Hemorrhagic Fever (DHF)**. This is classified as a severe form of Dengue.
	- **Transmission:** Dengue is an **arboviral infection**. It is transmitted by **arthropod vectors**, primarily the **_Aedes aegypti_** mosquito, and secondarily by _Aedes albopictus_.
	
	**3. Pathogenesis of the Condition**
	
	- **Etiological Agent:** Dengue virus, an enveloped RNA virus belonging to the Flaviviridae family.
	- **Vascular Damage & Leakage:** Dengue, as a viral hemorrhagic fever, causes **vascular damage** leading to **symptomatic bleeding (hemorrhage)**.
	- **Thrombocytopenia:** A defining feature of DHF is **thrombocytopenia** (platelet count < 1 Lakh/mm$^3$).
	- **Increased Vascular Permeability:** This leads to plasma leakage, indicated by a **raised hematocrit** and potentially resulting in significant bleeding under the skin, in internal organs, or from external body orifices.
	- **Organ Involvement:** Severe infections can lead to various organ manifestations, including liver involvement (jaundice and hepatomegaly) [423, though this source primarily discusses Yellow Fever, the clinical overlap is relevant here for severe hepatitis in Dengue].
	
	**4. Confirmation of Diagnosis**
	
	Diagnosis of Dengue is typically confirmed through a combination of tests, with specific guidelines often followed (e.g., NACO for HIV, though no specific NACO guideline for Dengue mentioned here beyond diagnostic methods):
	
	- **Antigen Detection:**
	    - **NS1 Antigen Detection:** This antigen can be detected in the blood from **–1 to +5 days of symptom onset**.
	- **Antibody Detection (Serology):**
	    - **IgM Antibody Capture ELISA (MAC-ELISA):** This is the **recommended serological testing in India**. It detects **dengue virus-specific IgM antibodies**, which typically appear early in the infection. The principle involves capturing human IgM antibodies and then adding specific viral antigens.
	- **Molecular Methods:**
	    - **Reverse-Transcriptase PCR (RT-PCR):** This method detects specific **viral RNA** (e.g., 3’-UTR region). It is highly sensitive (80-90%) and specific (95%) and can be used for **detection of serotypes and quantification of viral load** in blood. Viral RNA can be detected in blood from **–1 to +5 days of symptom onset**. A negative PCR result after 5 days of illness should be confirmed by serology.
	- **Virus Isolation:**
	    - Dengue virus can be isolated from blood (from –1 to +5 days of symptom onset) by inoculation into mosquito cell lines (e.g., C6/36, AP61) or in mice. This method is primarily used for **research purposes and in reference centers**.
	
	**5. Complications of the Infection**
	
	Dengue infection can progress to severe forms with significant complications:
	
	- **Dengue Shock Syndrome (DSS):** This is the most severe complication, occurring when all criteria for DHF are met, along with signs of **shock**. These signs include:
	    - **Rapid and weak pulse**.
	    - **Narrow pulse pressure (<20 mm Hg) or hypotension**.
	    - **Cold and clammy skin**.
	    - **Restlessness**.
	- **Severe Hemorrhages:** Bleeding can manifest under the skin, in internal organs, or from external body orifices like the mouth, eyes, or ears.
	- **Organ Failure:** Severely ill patients can develop **kidney failure**.
	- **Neurological Manifestations:** In severe cases, patients may present with **delirium, seizures, and coma**.
	- **High Mortality:** Severely ill patients, if not treated promptly, can have a fatal outcome.
	
2. **Mosquito-Borne Viral Infections (Arboviruses)**
	Arboviruses (arthropod-borne viruses) are a diverse group of **RNA viruses** transmitted by **blood-sucking arthropods** (insect vectors) from one vertebrate host to another. Viruses must multiply inside the insects and establish a lifelong harmless infection in them. Humans are often accidental hosts and typically do not play a role in the maintenance or transmission cycle, with the exceptions of urban yellow fever and dengue.
	**1. Dengue Fever / Dengue Virus (DENV)**
	- **Etiological Agent**: Dengue virus (DENV), a **Flavivirus** (family Flaviviridae).
	    - **ssRNA**, **enveloped virus**.
	    - **Four serotypes (DEN-1 to DEN-4)** exist, all widespread in India.
	- **Vectors**: **`Aedes aegypti`** is the principal vector, followed by `Aedes albopictus`.
	    - `A. aegypti` is a **nervous feeder** (bites repeatedly to multiple persons) and resides in domestic places, making it a very efficient vector. They bite during the daytime.
	- **Pathogenesis**:
	    - **Primary infection**: First exposure to any DENV serotype.
	    - **Secondary infection**: Occurs due to infection with a different serotype months to years later, often leading to more severe illness.
	    - **Antibody-Dependent Enhancement (ADE)**: Non-neutralizing antibodies from a primary infection can bind to a new serotype, enhancing viral entry into monocytes/macrophages via Fc receptors, leading to increased viral replication and higher viral load. **Serotype 1 followed by serotype 2 is remarkably observed to be the most severe**.
	- **Clinical Manifestations**:
	    - **Incubation Period**: 10-14 days.
	    - **Dengue Fever (DF)**: Abrupt high fever (**"break bone fever"**, biphasic/saddleback fever), **maculopapular rashes**, severe frontal headache, muscle/joint pains, lymphadenopathy, retro-orbital pain, nausea/vomiting.
	    - **Dengue Hemorrhagic Fever (DHF)**: High fever, hepatomegaly, **thrombocytopenia (<1 Lakh/mm³)**, **raised hematocrit (>20%)**, and **hemorrhages** (e.g., positive tourniquet test >20 petechial spots/sq inch).
	    - **Dengue Shock Syndrome (DSS)**: DHF criteria plus **shock** (rapid/weak pulse, narrow pulse pressure <20 mmHg or hypotension, cold/clammy skin, restlessness).
	    - **2009 WHO Classification**: Dengue with or without warning signs, and Severe Dengue.
	    - **Factors Determining Outcome**: Infecting serotype (Type 2 more dangerous), sequence of infection (DEN-1 then DEN-2), age (<12 years prone to DHF/DSS), malnutrition.
	- **Laboratory Diagnosis**:
	    - **NS1 Antigen Detection**: Detectable from **Day 1 of fever up to 18 days** by ELISA/ICT. Good for **early detection** and highly specific.
	    - **Antibody Detection**:
	        - **MAC-ELISA (IgM Antibody Capture ELISA)**: Recommended in India. IgM appears early and lasts 2-3 months. Cross-reactivity with other flaviviruses is a limitation.
	        - IgG antibodies persist for life; **four-fold rise in titer** indicates acute infection.
	    - **Rapid Diagnostic Tests (RDTs)**: ICTs for NS1 or IgM available, but have **poor sensitivity and specificity**. Positive RDTs should be **confirmed by ELISA**.
	    - **Virus Isolation**: From blood (**Day -1 to +5 of symptom onset**). Primarily for research/reference.
	    - **Molecular Methods (RT-PCR / Real-time RT-PCR)**:
	        - **Most sensitive (80-90%) and specific (95%)**.
	        - Detects viral RNA from blood (**Day -1 to +5 of symptom onset**).
	        - Used for **serotype detection and viral load quantification**.
	        - Negative PCR result is "indeterminate" and needs serological confirmation after Day 5.
	    - **Non-specific Findings**: Leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, increased hematocrit.
	- **Treatment**: **Symptomatic and supportive** (fluid replacement, electrolyte correction). No specific antiviral therapy.
	- **Prevention**:
	    - **Vaccine (Dengvaxia / CYD-TDV)**: Live-attenuated, tetravalent. Indicated for **9-45 years**. **WHO recommends use only in people previously infected with dengue** to avoid increased severe dengue risk in naive individuals upon subsequent infection. Contraindicated in immunodeficient, pregnant/breastfeeding women.
	    - **Mosquito control measures**.
	**2. Chikungunya Virus (CHIKV)**
	- **Etiological Agent**: Chikungunya virus (CHIKV), an **Alphavirus** (family Togaviridae).
	- **Vectors**: **`Aedes aegypti`** and `Aedes albopictus`.
	- **Transmission Cycle**: Urban cycle (human-`Aedes aegypti`) and sylvan/jungle cycle (monkeys-forest `Aedes`). Rarely, vertical transmission or blood transfusion.
	- **Clinical Manifestations**:
	    - **Incubation Period**: About 5 days (3-7 days).
	    - Acute stage: Most common symptoms are **fever and severe joint pain (arthralgia/arthritis)**. Other symptoms include maculopapular rashes, myalgia, headache, and fatigue.
	    - **Complications**: Persistent arthralgia for months to years is common. Rarely, hemorrhagic fever.
	- **Laboratory Diagnosis**:
	    - **Virus Isolation**: In mosquito cell lines (early diagnosis, 0-7 days).
	    - **Antibody Detection**: IgM appears after 4 days, lasts 3 months; IgG appears after 2 weeks, lasts years. **MAC-ELISA** for IgM is best (95% sensitivity, 98% specificity), with little cross-reactivity with dengue.
	    - **Molecular Method (RT-PCR)**: Detects specific gene (e.g., nsP1, nsP4) in blood.
	    - **Hematological Findings**: Leukopenia with lymphocyte predominance, thrombocytopenia (rare), elevated ESR and C-reactive protein.
	- **Treatment**: Symptomatic.
	**3. Japanese Encephalitis Virus (JEV)**
	- **Etiological Agent**: Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), a **Flavivirus** (family Flaviviridae).
	- **Vectors**: Mainly **`Culex tritaeniorhynchus`** (major vector worldwide and India), also `Culex vishnui` in India.
	- **Reservoir**: Birds are the natural host, **pigs are amplifier hosts**. Humans are dead-end hosts.
	- **Geographical Distribution**: Endemic in Asia, including India.
	- **Clinical Manifestations**: Leading cause of **viral encephalitis** in Asia.
	- **Laboratory Diagnosis**:
	    - **Molecular Methods**: **Real-time PCR** from throat/nasal swabs, CSF, urine, and blood, especially in early stages.
	    - **Antibody Detection**: ELISA (IgM and IgG) in later stages.
	- **Treatment**: Symptomatic.
	- **Prevention**:
	    - **Vaccines**: Both **live-attenuated and inactivated vaccines** are available for human use.
	**4. Yellow Fever Virus (YFV)**
	- **Etiological Agent**: Yellow fever virus (YFV), an **Arbovirus** belonging to the family **Flaviviridae**.
	- **Vectors**: **`Aedes aegypti`** or the tiger mosquito.
	- **Geographical Distribution**: **Endemic in West Africa and Central/South America**. **Not found in India**, but India has potential risk due to `A. aegypti` presence and tropical climate.
	- **Transmission Cycle**: **Jungle cycle** (monkeys-forest mosquitoes) and **Urban cycle** (humans-`Aedes aegypti`).
	- **Clinical Manifestations**: Infects the liver, causing **hepatitis and jaundice** ("yellow fever"), and also **hemorrhagic fever**.
	- **Treatment**: Only **symptomatic care**.
	- **Prevention**:
	    - **Vaccine (Yellow Fever 17D Vaccine)**: A **live attenuated vaccine**, prepared in chick embryo. Recommended for travelers.
	    - Mosquito control.
	**5. Zika Virus (ZIKV)**
	- **Etiological Agent**: Zika virus (ZIKV), a **Flavivirus** (family Flaviviridae).
	- **Vectors**: Primarily **`Aedes aegypti`**.
	- **Geographical Distribution**: Explosive epidemic in Brazil (2015-16), spread to Americas, Caribbean, Europe, and Pacific.
	- **Transmission**: Primary is mosquito-borne. Also, **mother-to-child transmission** (especially in first trimester) and rarely sexual transmission.
	- **Clinical Manifestations**: Mild febrile illness, arthralgia, rashes. Most notably, **congenital infection causing microcephaly** in newborns.
	- **Laboratory Diagnosis**:
	    - **Molecular Methods**: **Multiplex real-time RT-PCR** (e.g., targeting NS5 region).
	    - **Antibody Detection**: IgM antibodies appear after 1 week. **Cross-reactivity with dengue antibodies** is common.
	- **Treatment**: No specific antiviral drug available.
	**6. West Nile Virus (WNV)**
	- **Etiological Agent**: West Nile virus, a **Flavivirus** (family Flaviviridae).
	- **Vectors**: Mainly **`Culex`** mosquitoes.
	- **Geographical Distribution**: Endemic in Americas, Africa, West Asia.
	- **Clinical Manifestations**: Can cause **meningitis and encephalitis**.
	- **Treatment**: Symptomatic.
3. Dengue fever/dengue virus/dengue hemorrhagic fever - pathogenesis, infections caused, clinical manifestations, complications, lab diagnosis
	 Here's a concise, high-yield, note-style overview of Dengue Fever/Dengue Virus/Dengue Hemorrhagic Fever, optimized for medical school exams:
	
	---
	
	**Dengue Fever / Dengue Virus / Dengue Hemorrhagic Fever**
	
	**1. Etiological Agent and General Characteristics**
	
	- **Dengue virus (DENV)** is the most common arbovirus found in India.
	- It belongs to the family **Flaviviridae**.
	- It is an **enveloped virus**, containing **single-stranded RNA (ssRNA)**.
	- It has **four serotypes (DEN-1 to DEN-4)**, with a fifth serotype (DEN-5) discovered in 2013. All four serotypes have been isolated from India, with DEN-1 and DEN-2 being widespread.
	- **Vector**: `Aedes aegypti` is the principal vector, followed by `Aedes albopictus`. `A. aegypti` is a nervous feeder, biting repeatedly and residing in domestic places, making it a very efficient vector. They bite during the daytime.
	
	**2. Pathogenesis**
	
	- **Primary dengue infection** occurs when a person is infected with dengue virus for the first time with any one serotype.
	- **Secondary dengue infection** is when a more severe form of dengue illness appears, occurring months to years later due to infection with a second serotype different from the first.
	- **Antibody-Dependent Enhancement (ADE)**: This phenomenon contributes to the severity of secondary infections.
	    - Neutralizing antibodies are protective against the infective serotype (lifelong) and other serotypes (temporary cross-protection).
	    - Non-neutralizing antibodies (produced during primary infection) can bind to the new serotype in a subsequent infection, forming antigen-antibody complexes.
	    - These complexes enhance viral entry into monocytes/macrophages via Fc receptors, leading to increased viral replication and higher viral load.
	    - ADE is remarkably observed when serotype 1 infection is followed by serotype 2, claiming to be the most severe form of dengue infection.
	
	**3. Clinical Manifestations & Complications** Dengue virus can be associated with more than one clinical syndrome.
	
	- **Incubation period**: About 10-14 days.
	    
	- **Traditional (1997) WHO Classification**: Divides dengue into three clinical stages.
	    
	    - **a. Dengue Fever (DF)**:
	        
	        - **Abrupt onset of high fever** (biphasic, break bone fever, or saddleback fever).
	        - **Maculopapular rashes** over the chest and upper limbs.
	        - Severe frontal headache.
	        - Muscle and joint pains.
	        - Lymphadenopathy.
	        - Retro-orbital pain.
	        - Loss of appetite, nausea, and vomiting.
	    - **b. Dengue Hemorrhagic Fever (DHF)**:
	        
	        - High-grade continuous fever.
	        - Hepatomegaly.
	        - **Thrombocytopenia** (platelet count <1 Lakh/mm³).
	        - **Raised hematocrit** (packed cell volume) by 20%.
	        - Evidence of **hemorrhages**, detected by:
	            - **Positive tourniquet test** (>20 petechial spots per square inch area in cubital fossa).
	            - Spontaneous bleeding from skin, nose, mouth, and gums.
	    - **c. Dengue Shock Syndrome (DSS)**:
	        
	        - All criteria of DHF are present, _plus_ manifestations of **shock**.
	        - Rapid and weak pulse.
	        - Narrow pulse pressure (<20 mm Hg) or hypotension.
	        - Presence of cold and clammy skin.
	        - Restlessness.
	- **2009 WHO Classification**: Grades dengue into two stages based on severity.
	    
	    1. Dengue with or without warning signs.
	    2. Severe dengue.
	- **Factors Determining Outcome**:
	    
	    - **Infecting serotype**: Type 2 is apparently more dangerous.
	    - **Sequence of infection**: Serotype 1 followed by serotype 2 seems more dangerous and can lead to DHF and DSS more often.
	    - **Age**: Children less than 12 years are more prone to develop DHF and DSS.
	    - Malnutrition can have a paradoxical effect on host immune response in dengue.
	
	**4. Laboratory Diagnosis** The outline of laboratory diagnosis of dengue is similar to other arboviruses.
	
	- **a. NS1 Antigen Detection**
	    
	    - **ELISA and ICT formats** are available for detecting NS1 antigen in serum.
	    - Gained popularity due to **early detection** of infection.
	    - NS1 antigen becomes detectable from **Day 1 of fever and remains positive up to 18 days**.
	    - **Highly specific**: Differentiates between flaviviruses and can be specific to different dengue serotypes.
	- **b. Antibody Detection**
	    
	    - **MAC-ELISA (IgM Antibody Capture ELISA)**:
	        - **Recommended serological testing in India**.
	        - Principle: Double sandwich ELISA capturing human IgM antibodies on a microtiter plate, followed by addition of dengue virus serotype-specific envelope protein antigens.
	        - **Limitation**: Cross-reactivity with other flaviviruses.
	        - IgM appears early and remains elevated for 2-3 months.
	    - **Neutralization tests** (e.g., plaque reduction test, microneutralization tests) are cumbersome but are the **most specific serologic tests**.
	    - IgG antibodies persist for life. Seroconversion or a four-fold rise in IgG titer is more meaningful.
	- **c. Rapid Diagnostic Tests (RDTs)**
	    
	    - ICTs for dengue IgM antibodies or NS1 antigen are available.
	    - Have **poor sensitivity and specificity**.
	    - In India, a positive RDT for dengue NS1 or IgM should be considered a probable diagnosis and **must be confirmed by ELISA** (Government of India order, 2016).
	- **d. Virus Isolation**
	    
	    - Dengue virus can be detected in blood from **-1 to +5 days of symptom onset**.
	    - Can be done by inoculation into mosquito cell lines (e.g., C6/36, AP61) or in mice.
	    - Primarily available for **research purposes and in reference centers**.
	- **e. Molecular Methods (RT-PCR / Real-time RT-PCR)**
	    
	    - Detection of specific genes of viral RNA (e.g., 3’-UTR region).
	    - **Most sensitive (80-90%) and specific (95%) assay**.
	    - Can be used for **detection of serotypes and quantification of viral load** in blood.
	    - Viral RNA can be detected in blood from **-1 to +5 days of symptom onset**.
	    - A **negative PCR result is interpreted as "indeterminate"** and requires serological confirmation after the 5th day of illness.
	    - Real-time RT-PCR is used for quantification of viral RNA.
	    - Multiplex real-time RT-PCR is commercially available in India, targeting ZIKV, CHIKV, and DENV (1-4) specific regions.
	- **f. Non-specific Findings**
	    
	    - Leukopenia.
	    - Thrombocytopenia (common in DHF).
	    - Increased hematocrit (in DHF).
	
	**Treatment**
	
	- **No specific antiviral therapy** is available for dengue.
	- Treatment is **symptomatic and supportive**, including:
	    - Replacement of plasma losses.
	    - Correction of electrolyte and metabolic disturbances.
	    - Platelet transfusion if needed.
	
	**Prevention**
	
	- **Vaccine**: Dengvaxia (CYD-TDV), a Chimeric Yellow Fever-Dengue, Live-Attenuated, Tetravalent Dengue Vaccine, has been licensed since 2015.
	    - Indicated for **9-45 years of age**.
	    - WHO recommends its use **only in people previously infected with dengue**. Giving it to naive individuals may increase the risk of severe dengue upon subsequent infection.
	    - Contraindicated in immunodeficient individuals (e.g., HIV), pregnant and breastfeeding women.
	- **Mosquito control measures**.
