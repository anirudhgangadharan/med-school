1. Enteric fever- lab diagnosis\
		### **Enteric Fever: Laboratory Diagnosis (High-Yield Notes)**
	
	**Enteric fever** is a potentially fatal multisystem illness caused by _Salmonella Typhi_ (typhoid fever) and _S. Paratyphi A, B, and C_ (paratyphoid fever). These are Gram-negative bacteria belonging to the family Enterobacteriaceae.
	
	---
	
	**I. General Principles of Diagnosis**
	
	- **Timely detection** of the causative agent is crucial.
	- Laboratory diagnosis depends on the **isolation of the causative agent from blood by performing blood culture**.
	- **Specimen choice depends on the duration of illness**.
	
	---
	
	**II. Specimen Collection & Culture (Stage-Dependent)**
	
	1. **First Week of Illness (Acute/Febrile Phase)**:
	    
	    - **Specimen**: **Blood culture** is the ideal method.
	        - **Yield**: Positive in ~90% of cases in the first week, declining thereafter.
	        - **Collection**:
	            - **Aseptic technique** is critical to avoid skin flora contamination.
	            - **Two blood culture sets** should be collected with >12 hr interval, or **three sets over one hour** (e.g., 30 min gap).
	            - **Volume**: At least **8–10 mL of blood per bottle for adults** recommended.
	            - **Timing**: Collect **before antibiotic therapy**; if already on antibiotics, collect just before the next dose.
	            - **Culture Media**: Conventional (Brain Heart Infusion broth/agar) or automated blood culture systems (e.g., BACTEC or BacT/ALERT).
	    - **Alternative Specimens (First Week)**:
	        - **Bone marrow culture**: Useful if blood culture is negative, especially if the patient is on antibiotics. Sensitivity 55–90%.
	        - **Duodenal aspirate culture**: Recommended if both blood and bone marrow cultures are negative.
	        - **Combination of blood, bone marrow, and intestinal secretions culture** is best in the first week (>90% sensitivity).
	2. **Second/Third Week of Illness**:
	    
	    - **Specimen**: **Serum for serology** (e.g., Widal test).
	3. **Third/Fourth Week of Illness (Convalescent/Carrier Phase)**:
	    
	    - **Specimen**: **Urine and stool culture**.
	        - **Stool culture**: Recommended 3–4 weeks into illness.
	            - **Media**: Enrichment broths (Selenite F, tetrathionate, gram-negative broth), low selective (MacConkey agar), or highly selective (DCA, XLD agar, Wilson Blair’s Bismuth sulphite medium).
	            - _Appropriate media should be used to inhibit commensals in stool_.
	        - **Urine culture**: 3–4 weeks into illness, on MacConkey agar; seldom positive.
	
	---
	
	**III. Identification of Causative Agent**
	
	- **Culture Smear & Motility**:
	    - Motile, Gram-negative bacilli.
	    - Non-sporing and non-capsulated bacilli.
	    - Peritrichous flagella.
	- **Biochemical Identification**:
	    - Catalase **positive** and Oxidase **negative**.
	    - ICUT tests: Indole(-), Citrate(+/-), Urease(-).
	    - TSI: K/A (alkaline slant/acid butt), gas(+) (except _S. Typhi_), H2S (S. Typhi- small speck, S. Paratyphi A-absent, S. Paratyphi B-abundant).
	- **Automated Systems**: MALDI-TOF or VITEK for accurate species identification.
	- **Slide Agglutination Test**: To confirm the serotype.
	
	---
	
	**IV. Serological Tests (Antibody/Antigen Detection)**
	
	1. **Widal Test**:
	    
	    - Detects **O (somatic) and H (flagellar) antibodies** against _S. Typhi_ and _S. Paratyphi_.
	    - **Principle**: Agglutination of bacterial suspensions by patient's antibodies.
	    - **Interpretation**:
	        - Significant titre in India: **H agglutinin > 200, O agglutinin > 100**.
	        - Low titres should be ignored in endemic areas.
	        - **False-positive** results can occur due to anamnestic response (e.g., malaria, dengue) or non-fimbriae-free antigen suspensions.
	    - _Note: Serogrouping is based on O antigen, serotypes on H antigen_.
	2. **Antigen Detection (Serum & Urine)**:
	    
	    - By **ELISA**.
	
	---
	
	**V. Molecular Methods**
	
	- **PCR-based methods** (e.g., nested PCR) are available to detect and differentiate typhoidal salmonellae.
	- **Targets**: Flagellin gene, _iro B_ and _fliC_ gene.
	- **Advantages**: Rapid, sensitive, and specific.
	
	---
	
	**VI. Non-Specific Findings**
	
	- **Neutropenia** (15-25% of cases), but leukocytosis more common in children or with complications.
	- Moderately deranged liver function tests.
	- Moderately elevated muscle enzyme levels.
	
	---
	
	**VII. Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (AST)**
	
	- Performed by disk diffusion method (on Mueller Hinton agar) or MIC-based method (e.g., VITEK).
	- Essential for guiding appropriate therapy, especially given the emergence of drug resistance (e.g., to ceftriaxone and ciprofloxacin).
	
	---
	
	**Clinical Relevance & Key Takeaways:**
	
	- **Blood culture is the gold standard for early diagnosis** (first week).
	- **Widal test is for later stages** (second/third week) and prone to false positives in endemic areas.
	- **Molecular methods (PCR)** offer high sensitivity for early detection and identifying resistance genes.
	- **Carriers** excrete _S. Typhi_ in faeces or urine; diagnosis involves stool/bile/urine culture or Vi antibody detection.
2. Widal test-uses and interpretations
	
	
	### **Widal Test: Uses & Interpretations (High-Yield Notes)**
	
	The Widal test is one of the oldest and most widely used serological tests for the diagnosis of **enteric fever** (typhoid and paratyphoid fever), caused by _Salmonella Typhi_ and _S. Paratyphi_ [34, II.IV.1]. It was discovered by Fernand Widal in 1896.
	
	**I. Principle & Antigens Used**
	
	- **Principle**: It is an **agglutination test** that detects **O (somatic) and H (flagellar) antibodies** in patient serum against _Salmonella Typhi_ and _S. Paratyphi_ antigens [II.IV.1, 29].
	- **Antigens**: The test uses H antigens of _S. Typhi_, _S. Paratyphi A_, and _S. Paratyphi B_.
	
	**II. Timing & Clinical Utility**
	
	- **Timing**: The Widal test is useful in the **second/third week of illness** (febrile phase), as antibodies typically appear during this period [II.II.2, 31].
	- **Purpose**: Primarily used for **serodiagnosis of enteric fever** when bacterial isolation (e.g., blood culture) is no longer consistently positive or accessible [II.II.2].
	
	**III. Antibody Characteristics & Agglutination Patterns**
	
	The interpretation relies on differentiating characteristics of O and H antibodies:
	
	1. **O (Somatic) Antibodies**:
	    
	    - **Immunogenicity**: Less immunogenic.
	    - **Appearance/Disappearance**: Appear early in infection and disappear early.
	    - **Clinical Significance**: Suggestive of **recent infection**.
	    - **Agglutination**: Form **compact, granular, chalky clumps**. Agglutination takes place **slowly**.
	    - **Optimum Temperature**: 55°C.
	    - **Serogrouping**: Salmonellae are **serogrouped based on O antigen**.
	2. **H (Flagellar) Antibodies**:
	    
	    - **Immunogenicity**: More immunogenic.
	    - **Appearance/Disappearance**: Appear later in infection and disappear later.
	    - **Clinical Significance**: Suggestive of **convalescent stage**.
	    - **Agglutination**: Form **large, loose, fluffy clumps**. Agglutination takes place **rapidly**.
	    - **Optimum Temperature**: 37°C.
	    - **Serotyping**: Salmonellae **serotypes are differentiated based on H antigen**.
	
	**IV. Interpretation of Titres**
	
	- **Significant Titre (in endemic areas like India)**:
	    - **H agglutinin titre > 200** [II.IV.1].
	    - **O agglutinin titre > 100** [II.IV.1].
	- **Low titres** should generally be **ignored in endemic areas** due to background antibody levels from prior exposure or vaccination [II.IV.1].
	- A **four-fold rise** in antibody titre in paired sera (acute vs. convalescent) is highly indicative of active infection.
	
	**V. Limitations & False Results**
	
	- **Prone to False-Positive Results**:
	    - **Anamnestic (booster) response**: Can occur due to previous typhoid infection, vaccination, or other febrile illnesses (e.g., malaria, dengue) that cause cross-reacting antibodies [II.IV.1].
	    - **Non-fimbriae-free antigen suspensions** [II.IV.1].
	    - As a heterophile agglutination test, it can be non-specific.
	- **Prozone Phenomenon**: High antibody concentration in undiluted serum can lead to a false-negative result; therefore, serial dilutions are crucial.
	- **Delayed Seroconversion**: Antibodies may not be detectable in the first week of illness, leading to false negatives in early stages [II.II.2].
	- **Cannot differentiate between active infection and past exposure/vaccination** based on a single titre.
	- **Less sensitive and specific** than culture or molecular methods, especially in non-endemic areas.
3. Prophylaxis of enteric fever
	### **Enteric Fever: Prophylaxis (High-Yield Notes)**
	
	**Enteric fever** (_typhoid_ and _paratyphoid fever_) is a severe multisystem illness caused by _Salmonella Typhi_ and _S. Paratyphi A, B, and C_. Prophylactic measures are crucial, especially as the causative agents survive exclusively in human hosts and are primarily spread through contaminated food and water.
	
	---
	
	**I. General Principles of Prevention**
	
	- **Goal**: Control or elimination of enteric fever is theoretically possible given its human-only reservoir.
	- **Challenges**: In developing countries, this goal remains unrealistic due to a lack of adequate sewage disposal and water treatment.
	- **Multifaceted Approach**: Prophylaxis involves **three main lines** of measures:
	    1. **Control of Reservoir**
	    2. **Control of Cases**
	    3. **Vaccination**
	
	---
	
	**II. Key Prophylactic Measures**
	
	1. **Control of Reservoir**
	    
	    - This broadly involves improving **environmental sanitation**, **water treatment**, and **food hygiene**, as the disease is spread by contaminated water, food, and drinks.
	    - Addressing factors such as **lack of hand washing and toilet access** is also critical.
	2. **Control of Cases**
	    
	    - **Early Diagnosis & Prompt Effective Treatment**: This is fundamental for controlling the spread of infection. Prompt administration of appropriate antibiotics can prevent severe complications and reduce mortality.
	    - **Disinfection**: **Stool or urine-soiled clothes should be disinfected with 5% bleaching powder**.
	    - **Carrier Detection & Management**:
	        - **Carriers**: Untreated patients can become carriers, excreting _S. Typhi_ in faeces or urine. Fecal carriers (where bacilli multiply in the gallbladder) are more common than urinary carriers.
	        - **Diagnosis of Carriers**:
	            - **Culture**: Primarily by **stool and bile culture** (for fecal carriers) and **urine culture** (for urinary carriers).
	            - **Serology**: Detection of **Vi antibodies** by tube agglutination test using _S. Typhi_ suspension carrying Vi antigen (Bhatnagar strains). A titre of **1:10 is considered significant** in carriers.
	            - _Clinical Note_: While Vi antibody detection is useful for screening, the diagnosis of carriers **should always be confirmed by culture**.
	3. **Vaccination**
	    
	    - **Purpose**: Vaccination for _S. Typhi_ is an important prophylactic measure.
	    - **Impact**: Increased vaccination for _S. Typhi_ may be contributing to the observed increase in _S. Paratyphi A_ infections in areas like India.
	
	---
	
	**Clinical Relevance & Key Takeaways:**
	
	- **Integrated Strategy**: Effective control of enteric fever requires a comprehensive approach combining **improved sanitation, safe water, early case management, and targeted vaccination**.
	- **Carrier State**: The existence of **asymptomatic carriers** is a major public health challenge, making detection and management crucial for preventing further transmission.
	- **Vaccine Evolution**: While sources imply current vaccination for _S. Typhi_, ongoing development and implementation of newer vaccines (e.g., conjugate vaccines) are critical for sustainable control, particularly in endemic regions.
