**b. Pathogenesis of the Disease**

- **Etiological Agent:** Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), a retrovirus.
- **Primary Target:** HIV primarily infects and destroys **CD4 T cells**, which are crucial components of the host's immune system.
- **Replication Cycle:**
    - Upon entry, HIV uses its unique enzyme, **reverse transcriptase**, to convert its viral RNA into double-stranded DNA.
    - This viral DNA then integrates into the host cell's chromosome, becoming a **provirus**.
    - HIV establishes a **latent infection** for a variable period, during which it continues to replicate and remains infectious.
- **Immune Suppression:**
    - Initial infection leads to **primary viremia** and destruction of infected T cells in lymphoid tissues, causing an acute flu-like illness.
    - The host's immune response cannot fully clear the infection, and HIV-infected cells persist, leading to **ongoing viral replication**.
    - Progressive destruction of CD4 T cells results in **profound immune suppression**.
    - A rapid fall in CD4 T cell count (typically **below 200 cells/µL**) signifies the progression to AIDS, where lymphoid tissue is largely destroyed.
- **Clinical Manifestations:** Profound immune suppression leads to a high viral load and the development of **opportunistic infections** (such as persistent diarrhoea) and various neoplasias.

- **Primary Tests:**

        - **Rapid Tests**.
        - **ELISA** (Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay).

- **Disease Monitoring:**
    - **CD4 T cell count:** Essential for **staging the disease** and monitoring treatment response. A count of **<200 cells/µL** is a defining criterion for AIDS.
    - **Viral Load Measurement:** Used to monitor the effectiveness of antiretroviral therapy (ART).
- **Specimen Collection:** Blood is the primary specimen.

**d. Precautions for Prevention**

2. Opportunistic infections in HIV patients
	Opportunistic infections (OIs) are a significant concern in patients with Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection, occurring more frequently and severely due to their **weakened immune systems**. HIV infection leads to profound **immune suppression**, characterised by a rapid fall in **CD4 T cell count** (usually below 200 cells/µL) and a high viral load, which ultimately destroys lymphoid tissue.
	
	### Key Concepts
	
	- **Definition:** OIs are infections that occur more often or are more severe in individuals with compromised immune systems compared to those with healthy immune systems.
	- **Cause:** Profound immune suppression due to HIV infection, particularly the destruction of **CD4 T cells**.
	- **Prognosis:** The occurrence of OIs is a hallmark of the advanced, end-stage of HIV infection known as Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS).
	
	### Common Opportunistic Infections in HIV/AIDS Patients
	
	The specific OIs encountered often depend on the **CD4 T cell count**. Globally, **tuberculosis (TB)** is the most common opportunistic infection in HIV-infected individuals.
	
	#### Bacterial Opportunistic Infections
	
	- **Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Tuberculosis - TB)**:
	    - Most common OI globally.
	    - **Extrapulmonary TB (EPTB)** and **miliary (disseminated) TB** are much higher in HIV-positive patients.
	    - Blood culture may be indicated for disseminated TB.
	    - Often leads to **worsening of underlying TB** with a potential for false-negative Mantoux test results.
	- **Atypical Mycobacteria (Nontuberculous Mycobacteria - NTM)**:
	    - _Mycobacterium avium-intracellulare complex (MAC)_: Common in patients with **CD4 T cell counts less than 50/µL**. Can cause lymphadenitis, respiratory infection, and disseminated disease, including bone marrow infection.
	    - _Mycobacterium xenopi_: Rarely causes pulmonary disease.
	- **Recurrent Severe Bacterial Infections**:
	    - Includes **recurrent septicemia** (including non-typhoidal salmonellosis).
	    - Patients with hypogammaglobulinemia (which can be seen in HIV) are at higher risk for _C. difficile_ colitis and giardiasis.
	    - Gram-negative bacterial sepsis, pneumonia, or meningitis can result from enteric flora passage through disrupted mucosa, particularly in _Strongyloides_ co-infection (though _Strongyloides_ co-infection itself is not associated with disseminated disease in HIV).
	    - _Staphylococcus aureus_ can cause pyomyositis.
	    - _Haemophilus influenzae_: Less common invasive conditions include cellulitis, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, pericarditis, empyema, bronchiectasis, orbital cellulitis, endophthalmitis, UTI, and bacteremia without an identifiable focus.
	- _**Clostridioides difficile**_: Immunosuppression is a risk factor for _C. difficile_ infection leading to diarrhea.
	
	#### Viral Opportunistic Infections
	
	- **Cytomegalovirus (CMV)**:
	    - Common cause of **chorioretinitis** (leading to blindness) in AIDS patients with **CD4 T cell count <50/µL**.
	    - Also causes gastroenteritis, progressive dementia, cranial nerve defects, and other disseminated infections.
	    - Can infect bone marrow.
	    - CMV does **not respond to acyclovir**; treated with ganciclovir, foscarnet, or cidofovir.
	- **Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV)**:
	    - Frequent cause of **chronic HSV infection** and **mucosal lesions**.
	    - HSV infection of the oral cavity is more common in immunosuppressed patients.
	    - HSV encephalitis can be treated with acyclovir.
	- **Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV)**:
	    - **Recurrent or chronic zoster** is common in HIV-infected patients.
	    - PCR detecting VZV-specific genes is useful for diagnosis.
	- **Progressive Multifocal Leukoencephalopathy (PML)**:
	    - Caused by **JC virus**.
	    - Occurs in up to 5% of AIDS patients.
	    - Diagnosed by MRI (multifocal asymmetric lesions in white matter) and **PCR targeting JC virus DNA from CSF**.
	- **Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV)**:
	    - Can cause **cholecystitis**.
	    - Persistent EBV infection can induce malignant transformation of infected B cells and epithelial cells.
	    - **Lymphomas** (e.g., CNS lymphoma, Burkitt's lymphoma, Non-Hodgkin lymphoma) are associated with EBV, especially in immunocompromised individuals.
	
	#### Fungal Opportunistic Infections
	
	- **_Pneumocystis jirovecii_ pneumonia (PCP)**:
	    - Increasingly reported after HIV/AIDS discovery.
	    - **Cotrimoxazole** is the drug of choice for treatment and **prophylaxis** (primary and secondary) in HIV patients.
	- **Candidiasis**:
	    - **Oral thrush (oral candidiasis)** is a common fungal infection, especially in immunosuppressed patients like those with HIV.
	    - Also causes **esophageal candidiasis**.
	    - Can lead to **systemic candidiasis**.
	    - Cutaneous candidiasis or oral thrush treated with topical azoles or nystatin. Esophageal and vulvovaginal candidiasis treated with oral fluconazole or caspofungin.
	- **Cryptococcosis**:
	    - Caused by _Cryptococcus neoformans_, which produces **potentially fatal meningitis** in HIV-infected people.
	    - Risk factor: **advanced HIV infection with CD4 T cell counts less than 200/µL**.
	    - Can also cause **disseminated mycoses** including cutaneous cryptococcosis and bone marrow infection.
	    - Diagnosed by India ink stain of CSF to demonstrate the capsule.
	- **Disseminated Mycoses**:
	    - Includes **histoplasmosis**, **coccidioidomycoses**, and **blastomycosis**.
	    - _Histoplasma capsulatum_ can affect bone marrow.
	- **Aspergillosis**:
	    - Primarily affects the respiratory system, but can cause invasive infections in immunocompromised individuals.
	    - Post-TB aspergillosis (e.g., aspergilloma) can develop in residual TB cavities.
	- **_Penicillium marneffei_ (now _Talaromyces marneffei_)**:
	    - A thermally dimorphic fungus that causes opportunistic infection (e.g., wart-like skin lesions) in HIV-infected patients.
	- **Zygomycosis (Mucormycosis)**:
	    - Can occasionally cause cutaneous lesions.
	
	#### Parasitic Opportunistic Infections
	
	- **Toxoplasmosis**:
	    - One of the common opportunistic parasitic infections in AIDS patients (15–40%).
	    - Mainly targets the CNS, leading to **Toxoplasma encephalitis (TE)**, which is the most common manifestation in immunocompromised adults.
	    - Can also cause pulmonary infections and chorioretinitis.
	    - Often due to reactivation of latent infection.
	    - Diagnosed by PCR for _Toxoplasma_ DNA in CSF, blood, or other samples.
	    - Treatment with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (cotrimoxazole).
	- **_Cryptosporidium parvum_ diarrhea**:
	    - Causes **profuse watery diarrhea** in HIV-infected patients.
	    - Diagnosed by acid-fast oocysts in stool microscopy.
	- **Chronic Intestinal Cystoisosporiasis**:
	    - Can cause profuse watery diarrhea.
	- **Atypical Disseminated Leishmaniasis**:
	    - _Leishmania donovani_ (visceral leishmaniasis) amastigotes can be found in buffy coat smears in HIV patients.
	- **_Strongyloides stercoralis_ Hyperinfection Syndrome**:
	    - Can lead to **disseminated strongyloidiasis** where larvae invade the GIT and migrate to various organs including CNS, peritoneum, liver, and kidneys.
	    - Co-infection with HIV is common.
	- **Microsporidiosis**:
	    - Causes opportunistic infection in HIV-infected patients, especially when **CD4 T cell count falls below 100 µL**.
	    - Leads to enteric infections (e.g., _Enterocytozoon_, _Encephalitozoon_).
	- _**Acanthamoeba**_:
	    - Can cause granulomatous amoebic encephalitis (GAE), nasal ulcers, cutaneous ulcers, and musculoskeletal abscesses, particularly in HIV patients with **CD4 T cell <200/µL**.
	- **_Taenia solium_ (Neurocysticercosis - NCC)**:
	    - Co-infection with HIV is increasingly recognised.
	- **_Plasmodium falciparum_ (Malaria)**:
	    - Ocular disease (retinal hemorrhage) in cerebral malaria is a poor prognostic indicator.
	
	### Other HIV-associated Conditions
	
	- **Neoplasia**:
	    - Kaposi's sarcoma (associated with HHV-8).
	    - Invasive cervical cancer (associated with HPV).
	    - Lymphoma (cerebral, B cell, non-Hodgkin).
	    - Increased risk for EBV and HPV-associated malignancies due to immunosuppression.
	- **Direct HIV-induced Manifestations**:
	    - HIV encephalopathy.
	    - Symptomatic HIV-associated nephropathy or cardiomyopathy.
	
	### Management Implications
	
	- **Antiretroviral Therapy (ART)**: ART is crucial for treating HIV and reconstituting the immune system, which can allow for discontinuation of prophylaxis for some OIs (e.g., toxoplasmosis prophylaxis can be discontinued if CD4 > 200/µL for 3 months).
	- **Prophylaxis**: Specific prophylaxis is indicated for certain OIs, such as Cotrimoxazole for PCP.
	- **Drug Interactions**: Antiviral agents like tenofovir and emtricitabine are effective against both HIV and Hepatitis B virus (HBV), which is useful in co-infected patients.
	- **Diagnosis Challenges**: Some OIs (e.g., Measles) may present atypically in HIV patients (e.g., rashes may be absent). Lab diagnosis may rely more on molecular methods like PCR for early detection given the often low organism load.
