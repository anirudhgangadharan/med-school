	- **Lymphatic Filariasis (Bancroftian Filariasis)**.
	    - **Key Indicators:**
	        - **Fever on and off for one month:** Consistent with chronic infectious processes.
	        - **Unilateral swelling of the left lower limb:** A classic manifestation of chronic lymphatic obstruction leading to lymphedema or elephantiasis, commonly seen in filariasis.

	**3. Life Cycle of the Parasite (Wuchereria bancrofti)** [197, 198, Fig. 37.1]

	- _**W. bancrofti**_ is the most widely distributed filarial parasite of humans, accounting for over 90% of lymphatic filariasis cases.
	    
	- It is a **vector-borne disease** requiring **two hosts** to complete its life cycle: humans (definitive host) and mosquitoes (intermediate host).
	    
	    - **Human Cycle (Sexual Reproduction & Microfilaria Production):**
	        
	        1. **Infective Form:** Humans are infected when an infected mosquito (typically _Culex_ species, which bites at night) bites a human and **L3 (filariform) larvae** are deposited on the skin and enter through the bite wound.
	        2. **Maturation:** The L3 larvae migrate to the lymphatic vessels and local lymph nodes, where they molt twice and develop into **adult worms**.
	        3. **Reproduction:** Adult worms reside in the lymphatics or lymph nodes. Male and female worms undergo fertilization. Female worms are viviparous and discharge **L1 larvae (microfilariae)**, which circulate in the bloodstream.
	        4. **Prepatent Period:** This is the time from L3 larvae entry until microfilariae are detectable in blood, typically 82-142 days. Microfilariae have a lifespan of up to 1 year.
	        5. **Periodicity:** _W. bancrofti_ typically exhibits **nocturnal periodicity**, meaning microfilariae are most abundant in peripheral blood between **9 pm and 4 am**, co-adapting to the feeding habits of its _Culex_ vector.
	    - **Mosquito Cycle (Asexual Reproduction & Larval Development):**
	        
	        1. **Ingestion:** When a mosquito bites an infected human, it ingests microfilariae from the blood.
	        2. **Development:** Inside the mosquito, microfilariae shed their sheaths, penetrate the stomach wall, and migrate to the thoracic muscles. Here, they molt twice and develop into **infective L3 larvae**.
	        3. **Transmission:** These L3 larvae then migrate to the mosquito's proboscis, ready to infect another human host when the mosquito takes its next blood meal.
	
	**4. Laboratory Diagnosis**
	
	- **Microscopy (Detection of Microfilariae):**
	    - **Gold Standard:** Peripheral blood smear examination (thick and thin smears) is the **gold standard** for detecting microfilariae.
	    - **Specimen Collection:** Blood should ideally be collected during the **peak microfilarial periodicity** (between **9 pm and 4 am** for _W. bancrofti_).
	    - **Concentration Techniques:** For low parasite loads, **membrane filtration** or **Knott's centrifugation technique** can increase sensitivity.
	    - **Staining:** Smears are stained with **Giemsa or Leishman's stain** to observe the sheath and nuclei.
	    - **DEC Provocation Test:** If night blood samples are not feasible, a **Diethylcarbamazine (DEC) provocation test** can be performed during the day to stimulate nocturnal microfilariae to appear in peripheral blood.
	    - **Urine Analysis:** As seen in this case, direct microscopic examination of urine sediment can reveal microfilariae, particularly in cases of chyluria.
	- **Antigen Detection (ICTs/ELISA):**
	    - **Advantages:** These tests detect circulating filarial antigens, which are present throughout the day, thus overcoming the challenge of nocturnal periodicity. They are useful for screening and confirming active infection.
	    - **Method:** **Immunochromatographic tests (ICTs)** and **ELISA** using monoclonal antibodies against Og4C3 and AD12 antigens are commonly used.
	- **Antibody Detection:**
	    - Detection of parasite-specific **IgG4 antibodies** indicates active infection and is less cross-reactive. Useful for epidemiological studies and in non-endemic areas.
	- **Imaging Methods:**
	    - **Ultrasound (USG):** Can visualize dilated and tortuous lymphatic vessels and demonstrate the **"filarial dance sign"** (serpentine movement of adult worms in scrotal lymphatics).
	    - **X-ray:** May detect dead and calcified worms.

	- **Integration & History**:
	    - The **National Filariasis Control Programme** in India commenced in **1955**.
	    - This programme was subsequently **integrated** into the NVBDCP in **2006**. The NVBDCP oversees the control of six major vector-borne diseases in India, including filariasis.
	- **Elimination Goals**:
	    - India's efforts align with the **Global Programme to Eliminate Lymphatic Filariasis (ELF)**, which was launched by the WHO in **2000** with a global elimination target of 2020.
	    - In India, ELF has been operational since **2004**, in parallel with this global strategy.
	    - To aid in achieving a filaria-free India, a large-scale campaign called **"Hathipaon Mukt Bharat"** was launched in **2015** under the NVBDCP.

	**2. Classification of _Vibrio_ and Explanation of Pathogenesis & Laboratory Diagnosis of Cholera**
	
	### Classification of _Vibrio_
	
	_Vibrios_ are **curved gram-negative bacilli** that are **actively motile** by means of a single polar flagellum. The name 'Vibrio' comes from its characteristic vibratory motility. The most important species infecting humans is _V. cholerae_, which causes cholera.
	
	Classification is based on two main properties:
	
	1. **Salt Requirement**:
	    
	    - **Nonhalophilic Vibrios**: Can grow without salt, but 1% salt is optimal. They cannot grow at higher salt concentrations.
	        - Examples: _**V. cholerae**_ and _V. mimicus_.
	    - **Halophilic Vibrios**: Cannot grow in the absence of salt. They can tolerate and grow at higher salt concentrations (up to 7–10%).
	        - Examples: _V. parahaemolyticus_, _V. alginolyticus_, and _V. vulnificus_.
	2. **Serogrouping (Based on Somatic O Antigen)**:
	    
	    - _V. cholerae_ can be grouped into **over 200 serogroups**.
	    - **O1 Serogroup**: Responsible for all pandemics and most cholera epidemics. Strains are agglutinated by O1 antisera.
	    - **NAG Vibrios (Non-agglutinable/Non-cholera Vibrios)**: Serogroups other than O1 that were initially thought to be non-pathogenic, but many have since been found to be pathogenic.
	
	### Pathogenesis of Cholera
	
	Cholera pathogenesis is primarily **toxin-mediated**. Both _V. cholerae_ O1 and O139 produce **cholera toxin (CTX)**, leading to the disease.
	
	- **Mode of Transmission**: Ingestion of **contaminated water or food**.
	- **Infective Dose**: A **high infective dose of 10⁸ bacilli** is typically required to bypass the gastric barrier, as _V. cholerae_ is extremely acid-labile.
	- **Key Virulence Factor: Cholera Toxin (CTX)**:
	    - **Phage-coded**: The gene for CTX is encoded by a filamentous bacteriophage (CTX phage) integrated into the _V. cholerae_ chromosome.
	    - **Mechanism**: CTX binds to ganglioside GM1 receptors on intestinal epithelial cells. Its A subunit then ADP-ribosylates the Gs regulatory protein, leading to **constitutive activation of adenylate cyclase**. This causes a continuous increase in intracellular cyclic AMP (cAMP) levels.
	    - **Result**: High cAMP levels lead to **increased secretion of chloride and water** into the bowel lumen and decreased sodium absorption, resulting in the massive fluid loss characteristic of watery diarrhoea.
	- **Other Virulence Factors**:
	    - **Zona Occludens Toxin**: Disrupts tight junctions between mucosal cells.
	    - **Accessory Colonisation Factors**: Aid in adhesion and colonisation.
	    - **Bacterial Endotoxin (LPS)**: Unlike other gram-negative bacilli, _V. cholerae_ LPS does not directly contribute to cholera pathogenesis but is immunogenic and included in killed vaccines.
	- **Clinical Manifestations (Outcome of Pathogenesis)**:
	    - Ranges from asymptomatic infection (75% of cases) to mild diarrhoea (20% of cases).
	    - Characteristically, a **sudden onset of explosive, life-threatening watery diarrhoea** (often described as "rice water stool").
	    - Severe dehydration can lead to **shock and acidosis** (due to bicarbonate loss), potentially resulting in renal failure.
	    - Individuals with **blood group O** are at greater risk of severe disease.
	
	### Laboratory Diagnosis of Cholera
	
	Diagnosis relies on identifying the causative agent from patient samples.
	
	1. **Specimen Collection & Transport**:
	    
	    - **Specimen of choice**: **Freshly collected watery stool** for acute cases, ideally before antibiotics.
	    - **Rectal swab**: Preferred for convalescent patients or carriers.
	    - **Transport Media**: Specimens should be transported immediately. If delayed, use **Venkatraman-Ramakrishnan (VR) medium**, alkaline salt transport medium, or **Cary-Blair medium**.
	    - **Immediate processing** is crucial.
	2. **Direct Microscopy**:
	    
	    - **Gram Staining**: Of mucus flakes from faeces reveals **short, curved, comma-shaped gram-negative rods**, often arranged in parallel rows ("**fish in stream appearance**").
	    - **Motility Testing (Hanging Drop)**: Demonstrates characteristic **"darting motility"** (or "shooting star" / "swarming gnats" motility). This is a key preliminary clue.
	3. **Culture**:
	    
	    - _V. cholerae_ is **strongly aerobic and non-fastidious**, growing well on ordinary media.
	    - **Enrichment Broth**: Used to inhibit commensals and increase yield. Inoculate faeces into **Alkaline Peptone Water (APW)** or Monsur's taurocholate tellurite peptone water for 4–6 hours, then subculture.
	    - **Selective Media**:
	        - **TCBS (Thiosulfate Citrate Bile Salt Sucrose) agar**: Widely used. _V. cholerae_ ferments sucrose, producing **large yellow colonies**.
	        - Alkaline Bile Salt Agar (BSA): Produces translucent oil drop colonies.
	        - Monsur's Gelatin Taurocholate Trypticase Tellurite Agar (GTTTA): Produces translucent colonies with a grayish black center.
	        - MacConkey Agar: Colonies are translucent and pale (due to being non-lactose fermenting, NLF), may turn pink on prolonged incubation.
	4. **Identification (from Culture)**:
	    
	    - **Culture Smear**: Gram stain reveals short, curved gram-negative bacilli.
	    - **Motility**: Hanging drop confirms darting motility.
	    - **Biochemical Tests**:
	        - **Catalase and Oxidase positive**.
	        - **ICUT Test**: Indole (+), Citrate (+/–), Urease (–), TSI: Acid/Acid, no gas, no H₂S.
	        - **Hemodigestion**: Non-specific lysis of blood cells on blood agar, seen as greenish clearing.
	        - **String Test**: A colony forms a mucoid string when mixed with 0.5% sodium deoxycholate solution.
	    - **Automated Systems**: **MALDI-TOF and VITEK** can be used for rapid identification.
	    - **Biotyping**: Differentiates classical and El Tor biotypes based on biochemical tests, polymyxin B susceptibility, and bacteriophages.
	    - **Serogrouping**: Confirmed by **agglutination test with _V. cholerae_ polyvalent O antisera**. Serotyping (Ogawa, Inaba, Hikojima) further differentiates O1 serogroup.
	5. **Antigen Detection**:
	    
	    - **Cholera dipstick assay**: A point-of-care **immunochromatographic test (ICT)** is commercially available, useful in field settings.
	6. **Molecular Methods**:
	    
	    - **PCR**: Can directly detect _V. cholerae_ specific genes in stool.
	    - **BioFire FilmArray**: An automated multiplexed PCR assay, with a gastrointestinal (GI) panel that can detect _V. cholerae_ and other enteric pathogens directly from stool.
	7. **Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (AST)**:
	    
	    - **Essential** for guiding therapy due to increasing drug resistance. Performed by disk diffusion on Mueller Hinton agar.

1. Polymerase chain reaction
	**Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)**
	
	**Definition & Purpose**
	
	- **Amplifies a single or few copies of a DNA piece** to generate millions of copies.
	- Developed by Kary B Mullis (1983).
	
	**Principle of PCR**
	
	1. **DNA Extraction:** Lysis of organisms and release of DNA (e.g., boiling, enzymes like lysozyme, proteinase K). Commercial kits are available.
	2. **Amplification (in Thermocycler):** Repeated cycles (30-35) of three steps (3-4 hours total):
	    - **Denaturation (95°C):** Separation of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) into two single strands.
	    - **Primer Annealing (55°C):** Primers (short oligonucleotides) bind to complementary target ssDNA sequences.
	    - **Extension (72°C):** **Taq Polymerase** (from _Thermus aquaticus_) adds free nucleotides to the growing primer end, withstanding high temperatures.
	3. **Gel Electrophoresis & Visualization:** Amplified DNA migrated by agarose gel electrophoresis and visualized as clear bands under UV light.
	
	**Applications (Clinical Relevance)**
	
	- **Highly sensitive and specific:** Amplifies few DNA copies; primers target specific DNA sequences.
	- **Direct detection from samples** or **confirmation of cultured organisms**.
	- **Detects highly fastidious or non-cultivable organisms**.
	- **Drug Resistance Detection:** Detects genes responsible for drug resistance.
	    - **MRSA:** _mecA_ gene detection.
	    - **Vancomycin Resistance:** _van_ gene detection for VRSA and VRE.
	    - **Rifampicin Resistance in TB:** _rpoB_ gene (GeneXpert).
	    - **Anti-tubercular Drugs:** Line probe assay for resistance to various ATDs.
	- **Forensic Science, Paternity Testing, Genetic Diseases** (e.g., sickle cell anemia, phenylketonuria).
	- **Microbial Typing:** Characterizes organisms beyond species level, especially for genotypic methods.
	
	**Advantages of PCR** (compared to conventional methods)
	
	- **More Sensitive** (detects few copies of DNA).
	- **More Specific** (due to specific primers).
	- **Faster results** (e.g., 3-4 hours for conventional PCR).
	- Can detect **non-cultivable organisms**.
	- Useful for **drug resistance detection**.
	
	**Disadvantages/Limitations of Conventional PCR**
	
	- **Qualitative, not quantitative:** Detects presence/absence, not amount (Real-time PCR overcomes this).
	- **Cannot differentiate viable vs. non-viable organisms**.
	- **False-positives:** Due to environmental DNA contamination (requires strict asepsis).
	- **False-negatives:** Due to PCR inhibitors in specimens (e.g., blood, feces).
	- **Only detects DNA, not RNA** (RT-PCR overcomes this).
	
	**Modifications of PCR**
	
	- **Reverse Transcriptase PCR (RT-PCR):**
	    - Converts **RNA to DNA** using reverse transcriptase enzyme before amplification.
	    - **Essential for RNA viruses** (e.g., HIV RNA, Dengue RNA, Influenza RNA, Rabies RNA, Measles RNA, HCV RNA, SARS-CoV-2 RNA) and 16S rRNA genes.
	- **Nested PCR:**
	    - Two rounds of amplification with two different primers targeting same organism.
	    - **More sensitive and specific**.
	    - Applications: _M. tuberculosis_ (targeting IS6110 gene), _Acanthamoeba_ species, _Balamuthia_, _N. fowleri_ (18S rRNA), _E. histolytica_, _E. dispar_, _E. moshkovskii_ (small subunit rRNA).
	- **Multiplex PCR:**
	    - Uses **more than one primer** to detect multiple DNA sequences of several organisms in one reaction.
	    - **Syndromic approach:** Useful for diseases caused by multiple organisms.
	    - Examples: Common agents of pyogenic meningitis (_Pneumococcus_, _Meningococcus_, _H. influenzae_), viral meningitis, upper respiratory tract infections, atypical pneumonia (_M. pneumoniae_, _Chlamydophila pneumoniae_, _Legionella pneumophila_), genital ulcers (Treponema pallidum, _H. ducreyi_, HSV), diarrheal pathogens.
	- **Real-time PCR (rt-PCR):**
	    - **Quantifies DNA or RNA** in real-time.
	    - **Most sensitive and specific** with **faster turnaround time**.
	    - **Gold standard** for many viral diagnoses (e.g., influenza, COVID-19).
	    - Applications: Viral load monitoring (HIV, HBV, HCV), quantification of parasitic load (malaria, strongyloidiasis, fungal infections), speciation of parasites/fungi, detection of drug resistance.
	
	**Automated PCR Systems**
	
	- **BioFire FilmArray:**
	    - Completely automated multiplex nested PCR system.
	    - **Rapid results (approx. 1 hour)**.
	    - Syndrome-specific panels:
	        - **Meningitis/Encephalitis Panel:** 14 common agents (pyogenic and viral, including HSV-1/2, VZV).
	        - **Gastrointestinal Panel:** 22 enteric pathogens (bacterial, viral, parasitic e.g., _V. cholerae_, _Campylobacter_, _E. histolytica_, _Giardia_, _Cryptosporidium_, _Cyclospora_).
	        - **Respiratory Panel:** 22-33 respiratory pathogens (viruses and bacteria including Influenza A/B, _H. influenzae_, _M. pneumoniae_, _L. pneumophila_, RSV, Parainfluenza).
	- **GeneXpert (CBNAAT):**
	    - Automated real-time PCR system.
	    - **Rapid TB diagnosis (2 hours)** and **rifampicin resistance detection** (targeting _rpoB_ gene).
	    - Recommended initial test for **Extrapulmonary TB (EPTB)**.
	    - Used in India under RNTCP.
	- **Truenat:**
	    - Chip-based real-time PCR, battery operated.
	    - Detects MTB complex DNA and rifampicin resistance.
	    - Suitable for laboratories with less sample load.
2. Methods of antimicrobial susceptibility testing
	**Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (AST)**
	
	**I. Purpose of AST**
	
	- **Guides institution of appropriate therapy**.
	- Identifies the causative bacterial agent and provides **accurate antimicrobial therapy**.
	- Essential for **tailoring empirical antibiotic therapy to pathogen-directed therapy**.
	- Assesses disease burden for surveillance.
	- **Crucial for endocarditis isolates**, especially when reporting penicillin AST results.
	
	**II. Classification of AST Methods**
	
	1. **Phenotypic Methods**: Detect resistance by observing bacterial growth in the presence of antibiotics.
	2. **Genotypic Methods**: Detect specific drug-resistant genes in the organism.
	
	**III. Phenotypic Methods** A. **Disk Diffusion Method (Kirby-Bauer)** * **Principle**: Antibiotic from a disk diffuses into solid medium, creating a concentration gradient. Susceptibility is determined by the **zone of inhibition** of bacterial growth around the disk. * **Procedure**: * **Medium**: **Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA)** is standard; **Mueller-Hinton blood agar (MHBA) with 5% sheep blood** for fastidious organisms (e.g., _S. pyogenes_, _S. pneumoniae_). * **Inoculum**: Adjusted to **0.5 McFarland opacity standard** (approx. 1.5 x 10⁸ CFU/mL). * **Lawn culture**: Inoculum spread uniformly on MHA plate. * **Disks**: Standard antibiotic disks placed on surface, at least 24 mm apart (centre to centre). * **Incubation**: 37°C for 16-18 hours. * **Interpretation**: Zone diameter measured (e.g., by Vernier caliper) and interpreted as **sensitive, intermediate, or resistant** based on **CLSI or EUCAST guidelines**. * **Advantages**: Widely used, suitable for rapidly growing pathogenic bacteria. * **Limitations**: Not suitable for slow-growing bacteria. * **Direct Disk Diffusion Test**: * Performed when urgent results are needed, and a single pathogenic bacterium is suspected (e.g., from positively-flagged blood culture, sterile body fluids, or urine). * Specimen directly inoculated; results must be verified by AST from colony later. * Not useful for mixed growth specimens (pus, stool, sputum).
	
	B. **Dilution Tests** * **Principle**: Antimicrobial agent is serially diluted and tested against the organism to determine MIC. * **Types**: * **Broth Dilution (Macro/Micro)**: Serial dilutions of antibiotic in Mueller-Hinton broth. **MIC is the lowest concentration with no visible growth**. **Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC)** can be determined by subculturing from tubes showing no growth. * **Agar Dilution**: Serial dilutions prepared in molten agar and poured into Petri dishes. Multiple strains can be tested on the same plate, and it directly measures MBC. * **Role of MIC-based methods**: **Superior to disk diffusion** for reliability and accuracy. * **Confirms AST results** from disk diffusion. * Required for bacteria where disk diffusion is not standardized. * Used for **slow-growing bacteria** (e.g., tubercle bacilli). * Helps **select the most appropriate antibiotic**: Lower MIC (when compared to susceptibility breakpoint) generally indicates better therapeutic efficacy. * **MIC-guided therapy**: Essential in conditions like endocarditis, pneumococcal meningitis/pneumonia. * **Vancomycin for _S. aureus_**: **Avoided if MIC is >1 µg/mL** due to observed treatment failure.
	
	C. **Epsilometer (E-test)** * **Principle**: Quantitative method combining diffusion and dilution. Uses an absorbent strip with a predefined gradient of antibiotic concentration. * **Interpretation**: An elliptical zone of inhibition forms; **MIC value is where the ellipse edge intersects the strip**.
	
	D. **Automated AST Systems** * **Examples**: **VITEK 2 (bioMérieux)**, Phoenix System, Micro Scan Walk Away system. * **Principle**: Primarily based on **micro broth dilution** using commercially available panels with serial antibiotic dilutions. * **Advantages**: **Faster results** (e.g., 4-6 hours for identification, 8-10 hours for Gram-negative AST, 16-18 hours for Gram-positive AST), less labor-intensive, accurate, can identify a wide range of organisms.
	
	**IV. Genotypic Methods**
	
	- **Principle**: Detect **specific drug-resistant genes** within the bacterial genome.
	- **Examples**:
	    - **PCR for _mecA_ gene**: Detects Methicillin-Resistant _S. aureus_ (**MRSA**).
	    - **PCR for _van_ gene**: Detects Vancomycin-Resistant _S. aureus_ (**VRSA**) and Vancomycin-Resistant Enterococcus (**VRE**).
	    - **GeneXpert Assay**: An automated real-time PCR used for:
	        - **Detection of _M. tuberculosis_**.
	        - **Detection of Rifampicin resistance** (by targeting the _rpoB_ gene) in TB.
	    - **Line Probe Assay (LPA)**: Detects resistance to **first-line (e.g., isoniazid) and second-line anti-TB drugs**. Useful for rifampicin-sensitive cases to check for other first-line drug resistance, or for rifampicin-resistant cases to check for second-line drug resistance.
	
	**V. Interpretation of AST Results**
	
	- **Susceptible (S)**: Antibiotic is clinically effective at standard therapeutic doses.
	- **Intermediate (I)**: Antibiotic may be effective at increased doses, but should be avoided if alternatives exist.
	- **Susceptible Dose Dependent (SDD)**: Antibiotic is clinically active only if given in increased doses (available for few agents, e.g., cefepime for Enterobacteriaceae).
	- **Resistant (R)**: Antibiotic is **NOT clinically effective** at standard or increased doses, and should not be used.
	
	**VI. Selective or Cascade Reporting**
	
	- A strategy to encourage clinicians to use **narrow-spectrum antimicrobials** if effective, and to **reserve broad/extended-spectrum antimicrobials**.
	- **Method**: If an organism is susceptible to first-line agents, only those are reported. If resistant to first-line but susceptible to second-line/restricted, then only first and second-line are reported. This suppression of broad-spectrum options promotes judicious use.
	- **Not applicable** for organisms with limited therapeutic options or low resistance (e.g., _Salmonella_, _Burkholderia pseudomallei_).
3. Lab diagnosis of dermatophytes
	**Lab Diagnosis of Dermatophytes**
	
	**I. Introduction**
	
	- Dermatophytes are fungi causing **superficial mycoses** (infections of skin, hair, nail).
	- Diagnosis is crucial for **appropriate treatment** (e.g., oral terbinafine or itraconazole).
	
	**II. Specimen Collection**
	
	- Collected from the **active margin of the lesions**.
	- **Skin scrapings**: From scaling areas.
	- **Hair plucks**: Broken or scaly hairs are preferred.
	- **Nail clippings**: From infected parts of the nail.
	- Specimens are kept in **folded black paper**.
	- Hair should be **plucked, not cut**.
	
	**III. Direct Examination**
	
	- **Potassium Hydroxide (KOH) preparation**:
	    - **Procedure**: Specimen treated with 10% KOH (20-40% for nails) to digest keratin. Gentle heating and incubation (5-10 min) aid digestion. Glycerol (10%) can be added to prevent drying.
	    - **Findings**: **Thin septate hyaline hyphae with arthroconidia**.
	    - **Specific for Tinea Versicolor**: **Mixture of budding yeasts and short septate hyphae**, described as "**spaghetti and meatballs appearance**" [727, 726f].
	- **Calcofluor white stain**:
	    - **Principle**: Fluorescent stain that binds to chitin in fungal cell walls.
	    - **Findings**: Fungal elements appear **brilliant green or blue-white** under a fluorescence microscope.
	- **Hair involvement**: Arthroconidia may be seen **on the surface (ectothrix)** or **within the shaft (endothrix)** of the hair.
	
	**IV. Culture**
	
	- **Gold standard** for identification and speciation.
	- **Media**:
	    - **Sabouraud's Dextrose Agar (SDA)** with cycloheximide (to inhibit saprophytic fungi) and antibiotics (to inhibit bacteria).
	    - **Potato dextrose agar**: Used to stimulate sporulation.
	    - **CHROMagar Candida medium**: Can be used for selective and differential growth of _Candida_ species (though primary use for dermatophytes not explicitly stated as standard).
	- **Conditions**: Incubated at **26–28°C for up to 4 weeks**.
	- **Identification (Macroscopic)**:
	    - **Rate of growth**: Slow growth (1-4 weeks).
	    - **Pigmentation**: On the reverse side of the culture media.
	    - **Texture**: Glabrous (waxy/leathery), velvety, cottony, granular/powdery.
	    - **Topography**: Rugose (radial grooves), folded, verrucose, cerebriform (brain-like).
	- **Identification (Microscopic - LPCB mount)**:
	    - **Hyphae**: Thin septate hyaline hyphae.
	    - **Conidia (spores)**: [733, 737t]
	        - **Microconidia**: Small, unicellular (e.g., tear-drop shaped for _T. rubrum_).

4. Type IV hypersensitivity reaction
	**Type IV Hypersensitivity Reaction (Delayed-Type Hypersensitivity - DTH)**
	
	**I. Definition**
	
	- **Delayed hypersensitivity reaction** that occurs **after a few days** (typically 48-72 hours) of antigen contact.
	- It is **cell-mediated**, principally involving specific T cells called **TDTH cells** (delayed-type of hypersensitivity T cells).
	- Tissue injury predominantly occurs due to **activated macrophages**.
	
	**II. Mechanism** Type IV reactions occur through two phases: A. **Sensitization Phase (Initial Exposure)**: * An individual is exposed to a sensitizing allergen. * The allergen is processed by **antigen-presenting cells (APCs)** and presented as antigenic peptides to **CD4 helper T cells**. * These activated helper T cells differentiate into **TH1 cells**, which are responsible for DTH.
	
	B. **Effector Phase (Subsequent Exposure)**: * Upon re-exposure to the same allergen, TDTH cells (TH1 cells) are activated and **secrete various cytokines**: * **Interferon-gamma (IFN-$\gamma$)**: **Key cytokine** of Type IV reaction. It **activates resting macrophages** into highly microbicidal activated macrophages. * Increases MHC-II expression on macrophages, enhancing their APC function. * Increases TNF receptors, oxygen radicals, and nitric oxide. * **Interleukin-2 (IL-2)**: Stimulates the proliferation of TDTH cells in an autocrine manner. * **MCAF (Monocyte Chemotactic and Activating Factor) & TNF-$\beta$**: Promote migration of monocytes from blood to the DTH site, transforming them into tissue macrophages. * **MIF (Migration Inhibitory Factor)**: Inhibits further migration of macrophages away from the DTH site. * **IL-3 & GM-CSF**: Aid in local synthesis of monocytes. * **Activated macrophages** cause **nonspecific tissue destruction** by releasing lytic enzymes, especially when intracellular microbes escape killing mechanisms. * **Granuloma formation**: Continuous DTH reaction against persistent and/or nondegradable antigens leads to granuloma formation (e.g., tubercles in leprosy and tuberculosis). Macrophages transform into epithelioid cells, which may fuse to form multinucleated giant cells.
	
	**III. Clinical Relevance & Examples**
	
	- **Protection against Intracellular Microbes**: CMI (which includes Type IV DTH) provides effective immunity against:
	    - **All viruses**.
	    - **Some bacteria**: _Mycobacterium_ (e.g., _M. tuberculosis_), _Chlamydia_, _Rickettsia_, _Listeria_, _Salmonella_, _Yersinia_.
	    - **Some parasites**: _Plasmodium_, _Leishmania_, _Trypanosoma_, _Cryptosporidium_.
	    - **Some fungi**: _Histoplasma_, _Cryptococcus_, _Pneumocystis_.
	- **Tuberculin Test (Mantoux Test)**:
	    - **Prototype** of DTH.
	    - **Positive result** (induration >5 mm at 72 hours) indicates **prior exposure** to _M. tuberculosis_ antigens and intact cell-mediated immunity (e.g., in Tuberculoid Leprosy patients).
	- **Granuloma Formation**: Seen in chronic infections like **Tuberculosis** and **Leprosy**.
	- **Contact Dermatitis**: Caused by haptens (e.g., nickel, poison oak) complexing with skin proteins.
	- **Transplantation Immunity**: Plays a key role in **graft rejection**, particularly chronic graft rejection. Activated helper T cells differentiate into TDTH cells, and their cytokines activate macrophages to destroy graft cells.
	- **Graft-versus-Host (GVH) Reaction**: Another example of CMI.
	
	**IV. Factors Influencing Type IV Hypersensitivity**
	
	- **Genetic Makeup**: Host genetic factors play a significant role in determining the immune response to allergens.
	- **Allergen Dose**: Repeated small doses of allergen can induce a persistent DTH response.
	- **TH1 vs TH2 Response**: The balance between TH1 (promotes CMI, inhibitory to Type I hypersensitivity) and TH2 (promotes IgE-mediated allergic response) determines the individual's response.
	
	**V. Diagnosis**
	
	- Primarily by **skin tests** that demonstrate a **delayed reaction** (e.g., **Tuberculin test**). Small amounts of suspected allergens are introduced into the skin, and a wheal and flare response is observed after 48-72 hours.
5. Lab diagnosis of leprosy
	**Laboratory Diagnosis of Leprosy**
	
	**I. Introduction**
	
	- **Causative Agent**: _Mycobacterium leprae_ (Hansen’s bacillus).
	- **Obligate Intracellular Pathogen**: _M. leprae_ is an **obligate intracellular organism**.
	- **Non-cultivable**: Cannot be cultivated on artificial media or in tissue culture, posing a diagnostic challenge.
	
	**II. Specimen Collection**
	
	- **Slit Skin Smears**: Primary specimen collected from skin lesions.
	- **Nasal Specimens**: Nasal blow (early morning mucus) or nasal scraping (not routinely recommended).
	- **Biopsy**: From thickened nerves or nodular lesions in some cases.
	
	**III. Direct Smear Microscopy (Gold Standard for Bacteriological Confirmation)**
	
	- **Staining**: **Ziehl-Neelsen (ZN) technique** using **5% sulfuric acid** for decolorization (as _M. leprae_ is less acid-fast than _M. tuberculosis_).
	- **Appearance**:
	    - **Red, acid-fast bacilli (AFB)**.
	    - Arranged **singly or in groups (cigar-like bundles)**, bound by a lipid-like substance (glia) to form **globi** [666, 667f].
	    - Found predominantly **inside foamy macrophages** (Virchow's lepra cells) [666, 667f].
	- **Grading**: Smears are graded to quantify bacillary load.
	    - **Bacteriological Index (BI)**: Measures total number of bacilli (live and dead) per oil immersion field (OIF). Ranges from 0 (paucibacillary, PB) to 6+ (multibacillary, MB).
	    - **Morphological Index (MI)**: Percentage of **uniformly stained bacilli** (indicating viability) out of total counted. **Better marker for monitoring treatment response** (MI should fall with effective therapy).
	    - SFG percentage (Solid, Fragmented Granular rod percentage) also provides morphology.
	- **Clinical Relevance**: Provides **presumptive diagnosis**. Essential for **classifying leprosy** into paucibacillary (PB) or multibacillary (MB) for treatment regimens.
	
	**IV. Culture (Non-routine)**
	
	- _M. leprae_ **cannot be cultivated** on artificial culture media or in tissue culture.
	- **Mouse Footpad Cultivation**:
	    - The **only certain way to cultivate** _M. leprae_.
	    - Involves inoculating specimens into mouse footpads and incubating at 20°C for 6–9 months.
	    - Used for detecting drug resistance, evaluating drug potency, and assessing viability.
	    - **Limitations**: Time-consuming and ethical considerations.
	- **Nine-banded Armadillo** (_Dasypus novemcinctus_) can also be used for cultivation.
	
	**V. Antibody Detection (Serology)**
	
	- **FLA-ABS (Fluorescent Leprosy Antibody Absorption Test)**:
	    - Identifies **subclinical cases**.
	    - High sensitivity (>92%) and specificity (100%).
	- **ELISA for PGL-1 (Phenolic Glycolipid-1) IgM antibodies**:
	    - PGL-1 is an _M. leprae_-specific antigen.
	    - Detected in **95% of untreated lepromatous leprosy (LL) patients**; titre decreases with effective therapy.
	    - **Sensitivity is low (60%) in tuberculoid leprosy (TT) patients**.
	- **Limitations**: Antibodies play a minor role in disease control due to the intracellular nature of _M. leprae_. Serological tests **cannot differentiate current from past infection**.
	
	**VI. Cell-Mediated Immunity (CMI) Test / Lepromin Test (Mitsuda Test)**
	
	- **Principle**: Detects **delayed hypersensitivity reaction (DTH)** to lepra antigen, indicating **intact host CMI**.
	- **Utility**: **Not used for diagnosis of active infection**. Primarily for **classifying leprosy** (TT vs. LL) and as a **prognostic indicator**.
	- **Procedure & Interpretation**: Lepromin antigen injected intradermally, readings at two time points.
	    - **Early (Fernandez) Reaction (48 hours)**: Induration >10 mm indicates **prior exposure** to _M. leprae_ antigens.
	    - **Late (Mitsuda) Reaction (21 days)**: Nodule >5 mm indicates **intact CMI** (positive in TT cases, suggesting good prognosis); negative in LL cases (absence of CMI, poor prognosis).
	
	**VII. Molecular Methods**
	
	- The provided sources **do not explicitly mention PCR** as a routine diagnostic method for _M. leprae_ detection in clinical samples, unlike for _M. tuberculosis_.

	**Laboratory Diagnosis of Amoebic Liver Abscess (ALA)**
	
	**I. Introduction**
	
	- **Amoebic Liver Abscess (ALA)** is an important cause of space-occupying lesions in the liver.
	- It is caused by the protozoan parasite **_Entamoeba histolytica_**.
	- Occurs when _E. histolytica_ trophozoites spread from the large intestine to the liver, primarily via the hematogenous route.
	
	**II. Specimen Collection**
	
	- **Liver aspirate (pus)** is a primary specimen.
	    - Trophozoites, if present, are typically found in the **last portion of aspirated material from the abscess wall**, not in the central necrotic debris.
	    - **Diagnostic aspiration is generally NOT recommended** due to risks of fluid leakage, anaphylaxis, or dissemination. Aspiration is typically reserved for cases with impending rupture, large left-lobe abscesses (>10 cm), or lack of improvement after anti-protozoan therapy.
	
	**III. Direct Microscopy of Liver Pus**
	
	- **Appearance**: Liver abscess pus is characteristically **thick, chocolate brown in color** and often described as **"anchovy sauce pus"**.
	- **Cellularity**: It primarily consists of **necrotic hepatocytes and is typically devoid of pus cells**. This is a key distinguishing feature from pyogenic liver abscess.
	- **Parasite Detection**: Microscopy can detect _E. histolytica_ **trophozoites** (never cysts).
	    - Trophozoites are 15–20 µm, possess a single nucleus, are actively motile, with finger-like pseudopodia.
	    - **Sensitivity is low (<25%)**, but its presence **confirms the diagnosis**.
	
	**IV. Molecular Methods**
	
	- **PCR on amoebic liver pus**: Considered a highly reliable method.
	    - Approaches **100% sensitivity** and **90–100% specificity**.
	    - **Nested multiplex PCR** and **real-time PCR** can be performed, often targeting the 18S rRNA gene.
	
	**V. Imaging Methods**
	
	- **Ultrasonography (USG)**:
	    - Sensitivity: **75–80%**.
	    - Detects the site and extension of the abscess.
	- **Computed Tomography (CT scan)**:
	    - Sensitivity: **88–95%**.
	- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)**:
	    - Higher contrast resolution, providing clearer images of cysts.
	- **Limitation of USG/CT/MRI**: These methods **cannot differentiate ALA from pyogenic liver abscess** based on morphology alone.
	- **Nuclear Imaging Studies**:
	    - Useful for differentiating ALA from pyogenic abscesses.
	    - ALA appears as **"cold lesions"** (due to absence of leukocytes), in contrast to pyogenic abscesses which appear as "hot lesions" (due to leukocyte accumulation).
	
	**VI. Serology (Antibody Detection)**
	
	- **ELISA**: Used to detect antibodies in serum against _E. histolytica_.
	    - It is **highly sensitive (>90-95%)** for ALA.
	- **Clinical Note**: While sensitive for ALA, serological tests **cannot differentiate current from past infection** because antibodies can persist for years. Therefore, they are **not useful for monitoring treatment response**.
	
	**VII. Other Laboratory Findings**
	
	- **Blood Tests**:
	    - Increased **WBC count** (leukocytosis).
	    - Elevated **liver enzymes**.
	- **Stool Examination**: While not directly diagnostic for ALA, stool tests (e.g., ELISA detecting 170 kDa lectin antigen) can differentiate pathogenic _E. histolytica_ from non-pathogenic _E. dispar_ in cases of intestinal amoebiasis, which may precede ALA. 8. Graft versus host reaction
	**Graft Versus Host (GVH) Reaction**
	
	**I. Definition**
	
	- **Graft–versus–host (GVH) reaction** is a condition where the **graft mounts an immune response against the host (recipient)** and rejects the host.
	- This is **contrary to typical graft rejections** where the recipient rejects the graft.
	
	**II. Conditions for GVH Reaction** GVH reaction occurs when **three specific conditions** are met:
	
	1. The **graft must contain immunocompetent T cells** (e.g., from stem cells, bone marrow, or thymus transplants).
	2. The **recipient must possess transplantation antigens** that are absent in the graft.
	3. The **recipient must be immunologically suppressed**, rendering them unable to mount an immune response against the graft.
	
	**III. Types of GVH Disease**
	
	4. **Acute or Fulminant GVH Disease**:
	    - Occurs **within the first 100 days** post-transplantation.
	    - It is a **major challenge in bone marrow transplantation**.
	5. **Chronic GVH Disease**:
	    - A less severe form that occurs **after 100 days** post-transplantation.
	
	**IV. Clinical Manifestations**
	
	- **Acute GVH Disease**: Characterized by selective damage to:
	    - **Liver (hepatomegaly)**
	    - **Skin (rash)**
	    - **Mucosa**
	    - **Intestine (diarrhoea)**
	    - These effects are mediated by the **graft’s immunocompetent T cells**. Experimentally, it can produce "Runt disease" in mice.
	- **Chronic GVH Disease**: Attacks the same organs as acute GVH disease, but additionally causes damage to **connective tissues and exocrine glands**.
	
	**V. Treatment**
	
	- **Glucocorticoids** (administered intravenously) are the **standard treatment** for both acute and chronic GVH disease.

13. NK cell
	**NK Cells (Natural Killer Cells)**
	
	**I. Definition & Classification**
	
	- **Natural Killer (NK) cells** are a class of **large granular lymphocytes** that kill virus-infected cells and tumour cells.
	- They are a component of **innate immunity**, acting as a first line of defence.
	- They are **antigen-nonspecific** and do not require prior contact with the antigen.
	
	**II. Origin & Markers**
	
	- NK cells are derived from a **separate lymphoid lineage**.
	- They constitute 10–15% of peripheral blood lymphocytes.
	- NK cells **lack T cell markers** such as CD3, CD4, or CD8 molecules (hence, they are also called **null cells**).
	- They possess specific surface markers such as **CD16 and CD56**.
	
	**III. Functions**
	
	- **Primary role**: Destruction of **virus-infected cells** and **tumour cells**.
	- They act until cytotoxic T (TC) cells are activated and take over the function.
	- NK cells respond to IL-12 produced by macrophages and secrete **IFN-γ**, which in turn activates macrophages to phagocytose and kill microbes.
	- They play a key role in **tumour immunity**.
	
	**IV. Mechanism of Cytotoxicity**
	
	- **Receptor Interaction**: NK cells are **not MHC restricted** and directly recognize ligands (e.g., glycoproteins) on the surface of altered host cells (virus-infected or tumour cells).
	    - This is mediated by two types of receptors on the NK cell surface (opposing-signals model):
	        - **Activation Receptors (e.g., NKR-P1, CD16)**: When engaged with ligands, NK cells activate.
	        - **Inhibitory Receptors**: Recognize part of the **MHC class I molecule (HLA-E)** present on normal nucleated cells. Binding generates an inhibitory signal that overrides activation.
	    - In virus-infected cells and tumour cells, MHC-I expression is remarkably reduced, thus removing the inhibitory signal and allowing NK cell activation.
	- **Target Cell Destruction**: Similar to TC cells, NK cells destroy target cells by secreting **perforins** and **granzymes**.
	    - **Perforins** create pores in the target cell membrane.
	    - **Granzymes** are serine proteases that induce cell death via apoptosis through the caspase pathway.
	    - Notably, these enzymes are **constitutively expressed** in NK cell cytoplasm, meaning they are cytotoxic even without prior antigen exposure.
	- **Antibody-Dependent Cell-Mediated Cytotoxicity (ADCC)**: NK cells also mediate their function via ADCC.
	    - They express Fc receptors (FcR) on their surface that bind to the Fc region of antibodies coating a target cell, leading to target cell lysis.
	    - ADCC is important for immunity against **tumour cells and virus-infected cells** (mediated by NK cell-IgG) and **helminths** (mediated by eosinophil-IgE).
	
	**V. Distinguishing Features from TC Cells**
	
	- **MHC Restriction**: NK cells are not MHC restricted, while TC cells require antigen presentation with MHC class I.
	- **Memory**: NK cells do **not differentiate into memory cells**, unlike TC cells.
	- **Immunity Type**: NK cells are part of innate immunity; TC cells are part of acquired immunity.
	- **Enzyme Expression**: Lytic enzymes (perforins, granzymes) are constitutively expressed in NK cells, but are inducible in TC cells.
	
	**VI. Clinical Relevance**
	
	- Genetic defects causing impaired NK cells (e.g., beige mutant mouse strain, Chediak–Higashi syndrome in humans) are associated with an **increased incidence of certain types of cancer**.

This leads to inflammation, increased intestinal secretion of chloride and water, and decreased sodium absorption, resulting in fluid loss and diarrhea.

17. Name 3 viruses causing hepatitis
	**Viruses Causing Hepatitis**
	
	Hepatitis refers to **inflammation of the liver**. The most common causes globally are a diverse group of viruses collectively known as **Hepatitis viruses (HAV to HEV)**. These viruses are hepatotropic, meaning they primarily target the liver.
	
	Here are three significant viruses causing hepatitis:
	
	1. **Hepatitis A Virus (HAV)**
	    
	    - **Family & Genome**: Belongs to the family **Picornaviridae**, genus Hepatovirus. It is a **27-32 nm, spherical particle** with **icosahedral symmetry** and contains a **linear ssRNA**.
	    - **Transmission**: Primarily by **faecal-oral route** or ingestion of contaminated food/water. It is the most common cause of acute viral hepatitis in children.
	    - **Clinical Course**: Typically causes **acute viral hepatitis** with an abrupt onset. It is usually **self-limiting** and resolves completely.
	    - **Prognosis**: Excellent.
	    - **Chronic/Carrier State**: **No chronic infection or carrier state**. It also has no oncogenic potential.
	    - **Vaccine**: It is a **vaccine-preventable disease**; inactivated vaccines are available.
	2. **Hepatitis B Virus (HBV)**
	    
	    - **Family & Genome**: The **only DNA virus** among the hepatitis viruses, belonging to the family **Hepadnaviridae**, genus Orthohepadnavirus. It has a **partially double-stranded DNA** genome.
	    - **Transmission**: Primarily by **parenteral route** (e.g., blood/blood products, needle-prick injuries), **sexual transmission**, or **vertical transmission** (mother-to-fetus). HBV is highly infectious, with as little as 0.00001 mL of blood being infectious.
	    - **Clinical Course**: Onset is slow and insidious. Can lead to **chronic hepatitis**, **fulminant hepatitis**, **cirrhosis of liver**, and **hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)**. HBV accounts for 40–50% of HCC and 10–20% of cirrhosis in India.
	    - **Carrier State**: Can lead to a carrier state, which can be temporary or persistent/chronic (>6 months).
	    - **Vaccine & Prophylaxis**: A **recombinant subunit vaccine (HBsAg)** is available. Post-exposure prophylaxis guidelines exist.
	3. **Hepatitis C Virus (HCV)**
	    
	    - **Family & Genome**: Classified under the family **Flaviviridae**, genus Hepacivirus. It contains a **positive-sense ssRNA** genome.
	    - **Transmission**: Similar to HBV, primarily transmitted by **parenteral route** (e.g., blood/blood products) and can also be sexually or vertically transmitted. It is a common cause of post-transfusion hepatitis in developing countries.
	    - **Clinical Course**: Onset is insidious. A significant percentage (85%) of infected individuals develop **chronic hepatitis**. It can progress to **cirrhosis** and **hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)**.
	    - **Genetic Diversity**: Displays significant genetic diversity due to high mutation rates, which contributes to the **establishment of chronic infection** and **difficulty in vaccine development**.
	    - **Vaccine**: **No effective vaccine is available for HCV**.
	    - **Treatment**: Treatment has been revolutionized by **Directly-acting antiviral agents (DAAs)**, which can produce high sustained viral response (SVR) rates.
18. Weil-Felix test
	**Weil-Felix Test**
	
	**I. Definition & Classification**
	
	- The Weil-Felix test is a **non-specific serological test** used for the **diagnosis of rickettsial infections**.
	- It is a type of **heterophile agglutination test**.
	
	**II. Principle**
	
	- The test works on the principle of **antigenic cross-reactivity**.
	- Group-specific **LPS antigens** of certain rickettsiae share antigenic determinants with specific strains of _Proteus_.
	- The _Proteus_ strains used as antigens are non-motile strains of _P. vulgaris_ **OX19** and **OX2**, and _P. mirabilis_ **OXK**.
	
	**III. Procedure**
	
	- It is a **tube agglutination test**.
	- **Serial dilutions** of the patient's serum are mixed with suspensions of _P. vulgaris_ OX19, OX2, and _P. mirabilis_ OXK.
	
	**IV. Interpretation of Results**
	
	- The test should be carried out only **after 5–7 days of onset of fever**.
	- A **titre of 1:80** is generally considered indicative of a possible infection.
	- Specific patterns of agglutination are associated with different rickettsial diseases:
	    - In **epidemic and endemic typhus**: Sera primarily agglutinate with **OX19**, and sometimes with OX2.
	    - In **tick-borne spotted fever**: Antibodies to both **OX19 and OX2** are elevated.
	    - In **scrub typhus**: Antibodies to **OXK** are raised.
	- The test is **negative** in rickettsialpox and Q fever.
	
	**V. Clinical Relevance & Limitations**
	
	- Historically, it was a mainstay of diagnosis for rickettsial diseases.
	- However, it **lacks high sensitivity and specificity**.
19. **Nagler's Reaction**
	
	**I. Definition & Purpose**
	
	- Nagler's reaction is a **serological test** primarily used for the laboratory diagnosis of infections caused by _**Clostridium perfringens**_.
	- It detects the **lecithinase activity** of certain bacterial toxins.
	
	**II. Principle**
	
	- The test is based on the detection of **alpha-toxin**, a lecithinase, produced by _C. perfringens_.
	- Alpha-toxin acts on **lecithin** (a lipid present in egg yolk or human serum).
	
	**III. Procedure**
	
	- The test is performed on **egg yolk agar** or media containing 20% human serum.
	- The bacterium (_C. perfringens_) is streaked onto the medium.
	- A specific **anti-alpha-toxin** can be incorporated into one half of the agar plate to demonstrate inhibition.
	
	**IV. Interpretation**
	
	- A **positive result** is indicated by an **opalescence** (a zone of turbidity or haziness) surrounding the bacterial streak line. This opalescence is due to the breakdown of lecithin.
	- If anti-alpha-toxin is present in the medium, the **opalescence is inhibited**, confirming the specific lecithinase activity of alpha-toxin.
	
	**V. Other Organisms**
	
	- While characteristic of _C. perfringens_, the test can also be positive for other _Clostridium_ species that produce alpha-toxin, such as _**C. bifermentans, C. baratti, and C. sordellii**_.
	
	**VI. Clinical Relevance**
	
	- It serves as a **confirmatory test** in the laboratory diagnosis of **gas gangrene**.

20. **Prions**
	
	**I. Definition & Nature**
	
	- **Prions** are **infectious protein particles**.
	- They are incomplete viral particles that **lack nucleic acid**.
	- They are **extremely resistant** to most common sterilization procedures.
	
	**II. Pathogenesis (Mechanism of Disease)**
	
	- Prion diseases are caused by **misfolding of normal cellular prion proteins (PrPC)** into a disease-causing isoform, **PrPSc** (Prion protein scrapie).
	- Following infection, the infectious protein particles (PrPSc) are carried to the brain.
	- These PrPSc aggregates form **amyloid-like plaques** in the brain.
	- They are internalised by neurons and accumulate inside cytoplasmic vacuoles, leading to a **spongiform appearance** of the cells.
	- PrPSc differs from normal PrPC in its structure, being globular and richer in β-structure, and is resistant to proteases.
	
	**III. Clinical Manifestations**
	
	- Prions cause **neurodegenerative conditions of the brain** in humans.
	- These diseases typically have **long incubation periods** (years).
	- **Variant Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (vCJD)** is a human prion disease, mainly occurring in Britain, linked to consumption of beef with Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy (BSE) prions.
	- In the late stage of the disease, **abnormal EEG** (electroencephalogram) with high-voltage, triphasic sharp discharges may be observed.
	
	**IV. Diagnosis**
	
	- Diagnosis may involve observing **abnormal EEG** in the late stages of the disease.
	
	**V. Treatment & Prevention**
	
	- There is **no known effective therapy** for preventing or treating prion diseases.
	- Trials with drugs like quinacrine and anti-PrP antibodies have shown to eliminate PrPSc from cultured cells, but **failed in vivo**.
	- Due to their extreme resistance, recommended methods for sterilization of contaminated materials are necessary for decontamination.
	
	**VI. Key Distinctions (from Viruses)**
	
	- Lack nucleic acid (genome).
	- Are not immunogenic.
	- Have long incubation periods.
	- Are resistant to destruction by autoclaving at 121°C.
