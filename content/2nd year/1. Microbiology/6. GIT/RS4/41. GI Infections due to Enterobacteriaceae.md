1. A 32-year-old female was admitted with dysuria (burning micturition) and increased frequency of micturition from past 2 days. Culture of the urine specimen revealed lactose fermenting colonies on Mac Conkey agar.
	a. What is your clinical diagnosis and probable etiological agents?
	
	b. What are the risk factors associated, pathogenesis and clinical manifestations of this disease?
	
	c. How will you treat this clinical condition?

	**Clinical Scenario: 32-year-old female with Dysuria, Frequency, and Lactose-Fermenting Colonies on MacConkey Agar**
	
	**a. Clinical Diagnosis & Probable Etiological Agents**
	
	- **Clinical Diagnosis:** **Urinary Tract Infection (UTI)**. Specifically, based on symptoms (dysuria, frequency) without mention of systemic signs (fever, flank pain), this is likely a **Lower UTI**, such as Cystitis.
	- **Probable Etiological Agents:**
	    - The finding of **lactose-fermenting colonies on MacConkey agar** is a key clue.
	    - This strongly suggests organisms from the **Enterobacteriaceae** family.
	    - The **single most common etiological agent of UTI** is _**Escherichia coli**_ (an Enterobacteriaceae). _E. coli_ produces flat, pink, lactose-fermenting colonies on MacConkey agar.
	    - Another potential lactose-fermenting Enterobacteriaceae causing UTI is _**Klebsiella**_, which produces mucoid colonies on MacConkey agar.
	    - Thus, the probable agents are **lactose-fermenting Gram-negative bacilli**, primarily _**Escherichia coli**_, and potentially _**Klebsiella**_.
	
	**b. Risk Factors, Pathogenesis, & Clinical Manifestations**
	
	- **Risk Factors:**
	    - UTIs are more common in females.
	    - Sexual intercourse.
	    - Instrumentation (e.g., catheterization).
	    - Anatomical or functional obstruction of the urinary tract.
	    - Vesicoureteral reflux.
	    - Genetic factors (uroepithelial cell receptors).
	    - Decreased host defenses.
	- **Pathogenesis:**
	    - Mainly via the **ascending route**: Enteric bacteria colonize the periurethral area and ascend into the bladder.
	    - Bacterial virulence factors, such as **pili (fimbriae)**, aid in adhesion to uroepithelial cells.
	- **Clinical Manifestations:**
	    - The case describes the classical symptoms of **Lower UTI (Cystitis)**:
	        - **Dysuria** (pain while micturition - "burning micturition").
	        - **Increased frequency** of micturition.
	    - Other potential symptoms of cystitis include:
	        - Urgency.
	        - Suprapubic tenderness.
	        - Cloudy urine with bad odor.
	        - Hematuria (grossly bloody urine).
	    - **Absence of systemic manifestations** (like fever, flank pain) is typical for cystitis, differentiating it from Upper UTI (Pyelonephritis).
	
	**c. Treatment**
	
	- Treatment should be guided by **Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (AST)**.
	- Commonly preferred drugs for uncomplicated UTI (like cystitis) in outpatient settings include:
	    - Quinolones (e.g., norfloxacin).
	    - Nitrofurantoin.
	    - Cephalosporins.
	    - Aminoglycosides (typically for more severe cases or specific situations).
	- _E. coli_ can rapidly develop resistance, including **ESBL (extended spectrum β-lactamases)**. Hospital-acquired strains may be **multi-drug resistant (MDR)**.
	- For suspected resistant infections or healthcare-associated UTIs, **higher antibiotics** such as carbapenems (e.g., meropenem), β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations (e.g., piperacillin-tazobactam), or fosfomycin may be used.
	- If AST shows susceptibility to older drugs like penicillins, 3rd generation cephalosporins, quinolones, or cotrimoxazole, these may be preferred.
	- **Fluid intake** is also important in management.
2. A 16-year-old girl presented with fever, abdominal pain, loose stools with blood and mucus.
	
	 Microscopic examination of stool showed many pus cells and non-motile bacilli
	
	a. Enumerate the causative agents of dysentery.
	
	b. Discuss the pathogenesis and laboratory diagnosis of shigellosis.
	
	c. Compare and contrast amoebic dysentery and bacillary dysentery
	**Clinical Scenario:** 16-year-old girl with fever, abdominal pain, loose stools with blood and mucus. Stool microscopy showed many pus cells and non-motile bacilli [Query].
	
	**Probable Clinical Diagnosis:** Dysentery. The presentation of bloody/mucoid diarrhea, fever, and abdominal pain, along with the stool microscopy showing numerous pus cells (indicating inflammatory process) and non-motile bacilli, strongly suggests **Bacillary Dysentery**, with **Shigellosis** being the most likely cause.
	
	**a. Causative Agents of Dysentery:**
	
	Dysentery is characterised by diarrhea with increased blood and mucus, often with fever, abdominal pain, and tenesmus. Causative agents can be:
	
	- **Bacterial (Predominantly Dysentery):**
	    - _**Shigella species**_ - **Most common cause of bacillary dysentery**.
	    - _Campylobacter jejuni_
	    - _Enterohemorrhagic E. coli_ (EHEC)
	    - _Enteroinvasive E. coli_ (EIEC)
	    - _Vibrio parahaemolyticus_
	    - _Clostridioides difficile_ (can cause inflammatory diarrhea, listed under predominantly inflammatory but also associated with mucosal sloughing)
	- **Parasitic (Predominantly Dysentery):**
	    - _**Entamoeba histolytica**_ - Causes amoebic dysentery.
	    - _Balantidium coli_
	    - _Trichuris trichiura_ (_Trichuris dysentery syndrome_)
	    - _Schistosoma mansoni_
	    - _Schistosoma japonicum_
	
	**b. Pathogenesis and Laboratory Diagnosis of Shigellosis:**
	
	- **Pathogenesis:**
	    
	    - **Mode of Transmission:** Ingestion through contaminated fingers (most common), food, and water, or rarely flies. Can also be transmitted sexually.
	    - **Infective Dose:** As low as **10–100 bacilli**, due to ability to survive gastric acidity.
	    - **Entry & Invasion:** Bacilli enter mucosa via **M cells**, engulfed by macrophages which then release them.
	    - **Inflammation & Ulceration:** Released bacilli induce recruitment of inflammatory cells (cytokines) causing **acute colitis** (the hallmark). Invasion is mediated by a large virulence plasmid coding for **ipa proteins** and **type III secretion system**. Endotoxin also contributes by inducing intestinal inflammation and ulcerations.
	    - **Clinical Phases:** Incubation (1–4 days) -> Initial (watery diarrhea, fever, vomiting) -> **Dysentery** (frequent bloody/mucopurulent stools, tenesmus, cramps).
	    - **Key Features:** Shigella is **non-motile**. _S. dysenteriae_ type-1 is associated with high mortality and epidemics. Humans are the natural host. Drug resistance is increasing.
	- **Laboratory Diagnosis:**
	    
	    - **Specimen:** **Fresh stool** is ideal. Rectal swabs are less satisfactory.
	    - **Transport:** Transport immediately or in Sach’s buffered glycerol saline.
	    - **Direct Microscopy:** Wet mount of feces shows **large number of pus cells**, erythrocytes, and macrophages. Gram stain may show gram-negative bacilli.
	    - **Culture:** Inoculate into **enrichment broth** and **selective media** (e.g., MacConkey agar (MAC), Deoxycholate Citrate Agar (DCA), Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate (XLD)) to inhibit commensals. Incubate at 37°C.
	        - MAC or DCA: **Non-lactose fermenting (NLF) translucent colonies**.
	        - XLD: Red colonies without black center.
	    - **Identification:** Conventional biochemical tests or automated systems (MALDI-TOF, VITEK). Characteristically ferment fewer sugars (glucose and maltose only), are NLF, and produce H2S (-).
	    - **Serology:** Agglutinates with **Shigella polyvalent antisera** and specific monovalent antisera.
	    - **Molecular Methods:** PCR can detect specific genes. Automated multiplex PCR like **BioFire FilmArray** can detect Shigella directly from stool.

1. Lab diagnosis of bacillary dysentery (Shigella)
	**Laboratory Diagnosis of Bacillary Dysentery (_Shigella_)**
	
	- **Specimen Collection & Transport:**
	    
	    - **Fresh stool** is the ideal specimen. Rectal swabs are not satisfactory.
	    - Specimens should be transported **immediately** or sent in **transport media** such as:
	        - Sach’s buffered glycerol saline
	        - Cary-Blair medium (also useful for _Salmonella_).
	- **Direct Microscopy:**
	    
	    - **Wet mount** preparation of feces shows a **large number of pus cells, erythrocytes, and macrophages**.
	    - **Gram staining** of stool is **not routinely done** due to normal flora, but smears from colonies reveal **short, gram-negative bacilli**.
	- **Culture:**
	    
	    - To inhibit commensal flora, fecal specimens are inoculated simultaneously into **enrichment broth** and **selective media**.
	    - Culture media include:
	        - MacConkey agar (MAC) or Deoxycholate citrate agar (DCA): Produce **non-lactose fermenting (NLF) translucent colonies**. On MAC, colonies may become pink on prolonged incubation due to late lactose fermentation.
	        - Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate (XLD) agar: Produces **red colonies without a black center** (unlike _Salmonella_).
	    - Colonies reveal short, gram-negative bacilli. They are **non-motile**. They are non-capsulated and non-sporing.
	- **Identification:**
	    
	    - Identification from colonies can be made by **conventional biochemical tests** or **automated identification systems** such as VITEK or MALDI-TOF.
	    - Key Biochemical Characteristics:
	        - **Catalase positive** (except _S. dysenteriae_ serotype-1).
	        - **Oxidase negative**.
	        - **ICUT tests:** **Indole negative, Citrate negative, Urease negative**.
	        - **TSI (Triple Sugar Iron) agar:** Alkaline slant/acid butt, **gas absent, H2S absent**.
	    - **Serology:** Colonies agglutinate with _Shigella_ polyvalent antisera and specific monovalent antisera. Colicin typing can be done for _S. sonnei_.
	- **Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (AST):**
	    
	    - Done on Mueller-Hinton agar by disk diffusion test.
	    - **Crucial** because of the development of **drug resistance** among _Shigella_ strains.
	    - Required to choose appropriate antibiotics for treatment.
	
	_(Note: While BioFire FilmArray is mentioned for detecting common GI pathogens, the source excerpts explicitly list only Cryptosporidium, Cyclospora, E. histolytica, and Giardia for the GI panel, not Shigella, so PCR is not included as a specific diagnostic method for Shigella based solely on these excerpts)._
2. Pathogenesis of Shigella
	**Pathogenesis of Shigella:**
	
	- **Causative Agent:** _Shigella species_.
	    - Gram-negative bacilli [Source not explicitly stated, but implied by 'bacilli' in the context of typical stool microscopy findings and other bacterial agents listed].
	    - **Non-motile**.
	    - Drug resistance is increasing.
	- **Mode of Transmission:** Ingestion (contaminated food, water, fingers) [Source not explicitly stated, but standard knowledge for enteric pathogens].
	- **Infective Dose:** **Very low**, as low as **10–100 bacilli**.
	    - Survival in gastric acidity contributes to low infective dose.
	- **Mechanism of Invasion & Inflammation:**
	    - **Entry:** Bacilli enter the mucosa via **M cells**.
	    - **Macrophage Interaction:** Engulfed by macrophages. Subsequently, macrophages release the bacilli.
	    - **Invasion Factors:** Invasion is determined by a **large virulence plasmid**.
	        - Codes for important virulence factors such as **ipa proteins** and **type III secretion system**.
	    - **Inflammatory Response:** Released bacilli induce the recruitment of **inflammatory cells** to the infected site.
	        - Release of cytokines by inflammatory cells causes **acute colitis**—the **hallmark of shigellosis**.
	    - **Endotoxin:** Contributes to intestinal inflammation and ulcerations [Source not explicitly stated, but general bacterial knowledge].
	- **Host Factors:** People with **blood group O** show increased susceptibility.
	- **Clinical Manifestations (Pathological Basis):**
	    - Leads to inflammatory diarrhea / dysentery with blood, mucus, and pus [Clinical scenario].
	    - Direct microscopy of feces shows **large number of pus cells**, erythrocytes, and macrophages.
