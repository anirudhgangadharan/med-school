1. Enumerate various routes of drug administration with various examples. Write the advantages and disadvantages of the IV route.
	
	**Routes of Drug Administration**
	
	- Drugs can be administered via various routes, chosen based on drug properties and patient factors.
	- Routes are broadly divided into:
	    - **Local action:** Used for localized lesions at accessible sites with minimal or no systemic absorption. High concentrations are attained at the desired site, minimizing systemic side effects. If a drug is absorbed from these sites, it can also serve as a systemic route.
	        - Examples mentioned (not specifically listed as 'Local Routes' but described as such):
	            - Skin application (ointments, transdermal patches) - e.g., GTN patch for angina pectoris.
	            - Mucous membrane application (eye drops, nasal spray, inhalation). Nasal route can bypass the blood-brain barrier.
	            - Deep tissues (joint injections, intrathecal injection).
	            - Arterial supply (close intra-arterial injection) for localization, e.g., contrast media in angiography or anticancer drugs for limb malignancies.
	    - **Systemic action:** Drug is absorbed into the bloodstream and distributed throughout the body via circulation.
	        - **Oral (ingestion):** Oldest and commonest. Safer, more convenient, non-invasive, often painless, does not require sterile preparation, and is cheaper. Available in solid (tablets, capsules, etc.) and liquid (syrups, emulsions) dosage forms.
	            - **Limitations:** Slower action (not for emergencies), difficult for unpalatable drugs, may cause nausea/vomiting, cannot be used in uncooperative/unconscious/vomiting patients, variable/erratic absorption, some drugs destroyed by digestive juices (e.g., penicillin G, insulin) or liver (high first-pass metabolism, e.g., GTN, lidocaine).
	        - **Sublingual (s.l.) or Buccal:** Tablet under the tongue or on buccal mucosa. Requires lipid soluble, non-irritating drugs. Rapid absorption (minutes). **Key Advantage:** Bypasses liver (avoids first-pass metabolism). Examples: GTN, buprenorphine.
	        - **Transdermal:** Application to skin for systemic absorption. Example: Fentanyl patch for chronic pain.
	        - **Inhalation:** Gases or volatile liquids for systemic action (e.g., general anaesthetics). Absorption via alveoli is rapid, allowing controlled administration. Non-volatile liquids/solids can be aerosolized for inhalation into bronchioles (e.g., for asthma).
	        - **Nasal:** Mucous membrane of nose absorbs many drugs; bypasses digestive juices/liver. Used for peptide drugs like GnRH agonists, calcitonin, desmopressin.
	        - **Parenteral (by injection):** Bypasses enteral mucosa, drug directly into tissue fluid or blood. Circumvents oral limitations. Preparation must be sterile and is costlier. Invasive, painful, often needs assistance, local tissue injury risk, generally riskier than oral.
	            - **Subcutaneous (s.c.):** Drug deposited near capillaries. Slower absorption than i.m.. Depot preparations possible. Example: Insulin, Heparin.
	            - **Intramuscular (i.m.):** Drug deposited near capillaries. Generally faster and more predictable than oral. Depot preparations possible. Avoided in anticoagulant patients due to haematoma risk. Example: Aminoglycosides.
	            - **Intravenous (i.v.):** Drug injected as bolus or infused into superficial veins.
	            - **Intradermal:** Injection into the skin raising a bleb. Used for specific purposes like sensitivity testing or vaccines (e.g., BCG).
	
	**Intravenous (i.v.) Route**
	
	- Drug injected or infused directly into the bloodstream.
	    
	- **Advantages:**
	    
	    - **Fastest Action:** Effects are produced immediately (great value in emergency).
	    - **Surer Action:** Bioavailability is 100%.
	    - **Allows Rapid Titration:** Dose can be adjusted moment-to-moment based on response for short-acting drugs (e.g., sodium nitroprusside for BP).
	    - **Avoids First-Pass Metabolism:** Liver is bypassed. Lungs have minor first-pass metabolism for most drugs.
	    - **Suitable for Irritant Drugs:** Intima of veins is insensitive, drug gets diluted with blood (if injected slowly or into running i.v. line).
	    - **Can Administer Large Volumes:** Useful for fluids and electrolytes.
	    - **Suitable for Unconscious/Uncooperative/Vomiting Patients**.
	    - **Avoids Gastric Irritation**.
	    - **Continuous infusion:** Allows maintenance of steady drug levels, useful in critical care. Loading doses can be followed by continuous infusion.
	- **Disadvantages/Hazards:**
	    
	    - **Riskiest Route:** Vital organs (heart, brain) exposed to high drug concentrations.
	    - **Requires Sterilization:** Preparation must be sterile.
	    - **Costlier**.
	    - **Invasive and Painful**.
	    - **Requires Assistance** (though self-injection is possible).
	    - **Cannot be Used for Suspensions or Oils:** Particles/oils can cause embolism.
	    - **Risk of Thrombophlebitis:** Inflammation of the injected vein.
	    - **Risk of Necrosis:** If extravasation occurs.
	    - **Risk of Air Embolism**.
	    - **No Depot Preparations** for this route.
	- **Clinical Relevance/Examples of Drugs Used i.v.:**
	    
	    - **Emergencies:** Morphine for severe pain (e.g., MI), Sodium nitroprusside or Nicardipine for hypertensive emergencies, Vasodilators (GTN) for acute LVF, Antiarrhythmics (Lidocaine, Amiodarone, Esmolol, Verapamil, Diltiazem, Adenosine) for arrhythmias.
	    - **Severe Infections:** Vancomycin, Aminoglycosides, Amphotericin B. Parenteral antibiotics preferred for serious infections like meningitis, septicaemias. Antibiotics given i.v. or i.m. may be needed for severe pneumonia in a child.
	    - **Anaesthesia/Sedation:** Propofol for induction/maintenance, Fentanyl/Remifentanil/Morphine as components of anaesthesia, Midazolam for sedation in critical care, Dexmedetomidine for sedation.
	    - **Specific Conditions:** Terlipressin for bleeding oesophageal varices, Mannitol for increased intracranial/intraocular tension or impending renal failure.
	    - **Acute Coronary Syndromes (ACS)/MI:** GTN infusion for pain relief, Heparin, Fibrinolytic agents (alteplase/tenecteplase) for reperfusion in STEMI.
	    - **Heart Failure:** Furosemide for pulmonary edema/acute LVF, Dopamine/Dobutamine for emergency pump failure.
	    - **Reversing Toxicity:** Naloxone for opioid poisoning.
	    - **Surgical Prophylaxis:** Timed i.v. administration just before/after anaesthesia ensures effective blood levels during surgery.
2. Transdermal drug delivery system with examples
	**Transdermal Drug Delivery Systems (TDDS)**
	
	- **Definition:** These are adhesive devices, commonly known as patches, designed to **deliver a contained drug at a constant and predictable rate into the systemic circulation via the skin**.
	    
	- **Mechanism of Action:**
	    
	    - The drug, typically in solution or bound to a polymer, is held within a reservoir. This reservoir is positioned between an occlusive backing film and a rate-controlling micropore membrane.
	    - A priming dose of the drug is often incorporated into the adhesive layer for immediate effect.
	    - The drug is released from the patch and absorbed into the bloodstream through the **stratum corneum** via **diffusion**.
	    - The **lipid solubility** of the drug is a primary factor determining its penetration through intact skin.
	- **Key Advantages (Clinical Relevance):**
	    
	    - **Smooth Plasma Concentrations:** They provide **uniform and stable drug levels in the blood, avoiding fluctuations** often seen with intermittent oral dosing.
	    - **Bypasses First-Pass Metabolism:** Drugs absorbed transdermally largely **avoid metabolism in the intestinal wall and liver**, which is significant for drugs with high first-pass metabolism.
	    - **Minimises Inter-individual Variations:** Due to bypassing first-pass metabolism, there is **less variability** in drug levels among patients.
	    - **Enhanced Patient Compliance:** Patches are generally **more convenient** for patients than frequent oral tablets, leading to better adherence to treatment regimens.
	    - **Reduced Systemic Side Effects:** By delivering the drug directly to the site of absorption and avoiding high peak concentrations, systemic adverse effects can be minimised.
	    - **Prolonged Action:** Designed to release the drug over **extended periods** (e.g., 1-3 days), providing sustained therapeutic effects.
	    - **Non-invasive:** Offers a non-invasive alternative to injections for systemic drug delivery.
	- **Disadvantages/Considerations:**
	    
	    - **Cost:** Transdermal patches are generally **more expensive** than conventional oral formulations.
	    - **Local Reactions:** **Local irritation and erythema** (redness) can occur at the application site, though typically mild and manageable by rotating the patch location.
	    - **Drug Properties:** Only **highly lipid-soluble drugs** can penetrate the skin effectively enough for transdermal delivery.
	    - **Tolerance:** For certain drugs, continuous presence can lead to the rapid development of **tolerance**, necessitating drug-free intervals (e.g., Nitroglycerine patches require an 8-10 hour daily break).
	- **Examples of Drugs Used (High-Yield for Exams):**
	    
	    - **Glyceryl Trinitrate (GTN)/Nitroglycerine:** Used for **angina pectoris** (chest pain) due to myocardial ischaemia. Provides steady delivery for 24 hours, but requires an 8-10 hour patch-free interval daily to prevent nitrate tolerance.
	    - **Fentanyl:** A potent opioid analgesic widely used for **chronic severe pain**, particularly cancer pain. Patches are typically changed every 3 days to maintain uniform blood levels and consistent analgesia.
	    - **Nicotine:** Used in **smoking cessation** programs as a nicotine replacement therapy.
	    - **Estradiol:** Used in hormone replacement therapy for menopausal symptoms.
	    - **Hyoscine:** Mentioned for its ability to penetrate intact skin, though not explicitly as a patch in all contexts.
	    - **Testosterone:** Mentioned as a drug that can penetrate intact skin, usually in ointment form.
	    - _Other drugs (marketed elsewhere):_ Isosorbide dinitrate, Clonidine.
3. Intravenous route of drug administration (advantages and disadvantages).
	   The **intravenous (i.v.) route of drug administration** involves injecting the drug directly into the bloodstream, typically into a superficial vein.
	
	### **Intravenous Route of Drug Administration**
	
	#### **Advantages (Clinical Relevance for Exams):**
	
	- **Rapid Onset of Action:** Effects are produced **immediately** as the drug directly enters the circulation, making it invaluable in **emergencies**.
	- **Bypasses First-Pass Metabolism:** The drug **avoids metabolism** in the intestinal wall and liver, ensuring **100% bioavailability**. This is crucial for drugs with high first-pass metabolism or those unstable to digestive juices.
	- **Precise Dose Control (Titratability):** Allows for **accurate and fine-tuned dosing**, as the drug concentration in the blood can be directly controlled. For short-acting drugs with measurable responses (e.g., blood pressure), the dose can be **titrated moment-to-moment** to the desired effect.
	- **Suitable for Uncooperative/Unconscious Patients:** It can be used when oral administration is not possible due to patient condition (e.g., vomiting, unconsciousness).
	- **Administration of Irritant Drugs:** The intima of veins is less sensitive, and the drug is rapidly diluted by blood, allowing for the administration of **highly irritant drugs** that would cause pain or tissue damage if given by other parenteral routes (e.g., intramuscular or subcutaneous).
	- **Large Volumes Possible:** **Even large volumes of fluid** (e.g., for rehydration or inotropic support) can be administered.
	- **No Interference by Food or Digestive Juices:** Drug efficacy is not affected by gastric contents or pH.
	
	#### **Disadvantages/Considerations:**
	
	- **Invasiveness and Pain:** It is an **invasive procedure**, requiring skill and typically the assistance of another person (though self-injection is possible for some drugs). The process of venipuncture can be painful.
	- **Preparation Requirements:** The drug preparation must be **sterile** and is generally **costlier** than oral forms.
	- **Irreversible Effects & Higher Risk:** This is the **most risky route** because effects are immediate and irreversible once administered. Vital organs (heart, brain) are exposed to **high concentrations** of the drug, increasing the risk of serious adverse effects or toxicity if not properly managed.
	- **Local Complications at Injection Site:**
	    - **Thrombophlebitis:** Inflammation and clotting of the injected vein can occur.
	    - **Necrosis:** Damage to adjoining tissues can occur if **extravasation** (leakage outside the vein) happens.
	    - These can be minimized by diluting the drug or injecting into a running i.v. line.
	- **Embolism Risk:** Only **aqueous solutions** are suitable for i.v. injection; suspensions or oily solutions should **never be injected intravenously** as drug particles or oil droplets can cause **embolism**. Air embolism is also a risk.
	- **No Depot Preparations:** The i.v. route does not allow for depot preparations that provide prolonged release, as the drug is immediately distributed.
	- **Specific Drug-Related Issues:**
	    - **Pain during injection:** Can occur even with i.v. drugs (e.g., propofol).
	    - **Histamine release:** Rapid i.v. injection of certain drugs (e.g., vancomycin) can cause histamine release, leading to reactions like "Red man syndrome".
	    - **Specific toxicities:** Some drugs have particular toxicities associated with i.v. administration (e.g., foscarnet damage to kidney, severe anaphylactoid reactions with IV phytonadione).
	    - **Tolerance:** Continuous IV infusion of some drugs (e.g., GTN) can lead to tolerance.
	
	---
	
	#### **Clinical Applications & Examples:**
	
	- **Emergencies:**
	    - **Severe Infections/Septicemia:** Parenteral antibiotics (e.g., cephalosporins, vancomycin, carbapenems, pazufloxacin) are preferred for serious infections like meningitis or septicemia.
	    - **Acute Heart Failure/Pulmonary Edema:** Furosemide or Glyceryl Trinitrate (GTN) are given i.v. for rapid relief.
	    - **Hypertensive Emergencies:** Nicardipine, sodium nitroprusside, labetalol, or esmolol are administered i.v. for rapid blood pressure reduction.
	    - **Acute Myocardial Infarction (STEMI):** Fibrinolytic agents (e.g., alteplase, reteplase) are given i.v. to achieve reperfusion.
	    - **Status Epilepticus:** Fast-acting benzodiazepines (e.g., lorazepam) or fosphenytoin are administered i.v. for emergency seizure control.
	    - **Severe Malaria:** Intravenous artesunate is the first-choice drug for severe malaria, being faster and safer than i.v. quinine.
	    - **Opioid Overdose:** Naloxone is given i.v. to rapidly reverse respiratory depression.
	- **Anaesthesia:** Fast-acting intravenous anaesthetics (e.g., propofol, thiopentone sodium) are commonly used for **induction of anaesthesia**, often followed by an inhalational agent for maintenance. Fentanyl and remifentanil are used as adjuncts for intraoperative analgesia.
	- **Critically Ill Patients:** Dexmedetomidine, midazolam, or propofol can be given by continuous i.v. infusion for sedation in intensive care units.
	- **Specific Conditions:**
	    - **Visceral Leishmaniasis (Kala-azar):** Conventional and liposomal amphotericin B (AMB) are given i.v.. Liposomal AMB is particularly suited for delivering the drug to reticuloendothelial cells in the liver and spleen where parasites reside.
	    - **Bleeding Esophageal Varices:** Terlipressin is specifically used via i.v. administration to stop bleeding.
	    - **Volume Overload/Cerebral Edema:** Mannitol is given i.v. for increased intracranial or intraocular tension and to maintain GFR in impending acute renal failure.

4. Define chemoprophylaxis. Give examples.
	**Chemoprophylaxis**
	- **Definition:**
	    
	    - The **prophylactic use of antimicrobial agents (AMAs)** to **prevent the setting in of an infection** or to **suppress a contacted infection before it becomes clinically manifest**.
	    - It is synonymous with "pre-emptive therapy".
	    - The primary goal is to **prevent the progression of latent infection to active disease** or to prevent infection in high-risk situations.
	- **Key Considerations/Clinical Relevance:**
	    
	    - **Timeliness:** Administration must be appropriately timed to achieve therapeutic drug levels at the site of potential infection.
	    - **Risk vs. Benefit:** Prophylactic AMA use can be wasteful or harmful if indiscriminate, as it can lead to **resistant organisms** and **superinfections**.
	    - **Rational Use:** Should be targeted against specific organisms or used in high-risk situations where the benefit clearly outweighs the risk.
	- **Examples:**
	    
	    - **Rheumatic Fever Recurrences:**
	        
	        - **Benzathine penicillin** to prevent infection by Group A streptococci.
	    - **Tuberculosis (Latent Infection):**
	        
	        - **Isoniazid (INH)** for 6 months in high-risk individuals (e.g., children, HIV-positive contacts of open cases, immunocompromised patients).
	    - **HIV Infection:**
	        
	        - **Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP):** **Tenofovir + Emtricitabine** (often with a protease inhibitor like lopinavir or atazanavir) for healthcare workers after needle-stick injury or other significant exposure. The aim is to suppress local viral replication.
	        - **Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP):** **Tenofovir (± Emtricitabine)** for high-risk adults.
	        - **Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission:** **Tenofovir + Lamivudine + Efavirenz** for pregnant women, and **syrup nevirapine** for the neonate for 6 weeks.
	        - **Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) in HIV/AIDS:** **Azithromycin** or **Clarithromycin** (or **Rifabutin** as an alternative) in patients with low CD4 counts.
	    - **Bacterial Meningitis (Meningococcal):**
	        
	        - **Rifampin**, **Ciprofloxacin**, or **Ceftriaxone** for contacts during an epidemic.
	    - **Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs):**
	        
	        - **Gonorrhoea/Syphilis:** **Ampicillin** or **Ceftriaxone** before or immediately after contact.
	    - **Recurrent Genital Herpes Simplex:**
	        
	        - **Acyclovir** prophylaxis in patients experiencing frequent recurrences (e.g., > 4 per year).
	    - **Infective Endocarditis (Dental/Surgical Procedures):**
	        
	        - **Amoxicillin** or **Clindamycin** for patients with valvular defects undergoing dental extractions or endoscopies.
	        - **Ampicillin**, **Gentamicin**, or **Vancomycin** for patients with cardiac valvular lesions during catheterization.
	    - **Urinary Tract Infections (UTIs):**
	        
	        - **Cotrimoxazole** or **Nitrofurantoin** for long-term prevention of recurrences in patients with tract abnormalities (often due to _E. coli_). **Norfloxacin** may also be used.
	    - **Surgical Site Infection (SSI):**
	        
	        - Generally recommended only when there is a clear risk.
	        - **Timing is critical:** Oral drugs 1 hour before incision, IV drugs just before/after anaesthesia, ensuring peak levels during wound closure.
	        - **Postoperative administration** is typically limited to contaminated or dirty surgeries (up to 5 days).
	        - **Commonly used drugs:**
	            - **Cefazolin** (first-generation cephalosporin) for most surgeries, especially with prosthesis insertion.
	            - **Vancomycin** in areas with high MRSA prevalence or for penicillin-allergic patients.
	    - **Malaria:**
	        
	        - **Causal Prophylaxis:** Targets the pre-erythrocytic (liver) stage.
	        - **Intermittent Preventive Treatment in Pregnancy (IPTp):** **Pyrimethamine + Sulfadoxine** in the 2nd and 3rd trimesters.
	        - **Travellers to Endemic Areas:**
	            - **Mefloquine** for multi-resistant _P. falciparum_.
	            - **Doxycycline** for short-term travellers to CQ-resistant _P. falciparum_ areas (contraindicated in children and pregnant women).
	            - **Atovaquone + Proguanil** (fixed-dose combination, e.g., Malarone).
	            - _Note:_ Artemisinins are generally _not_ used for prophylaxis due to the risk of resistance development and short action.
