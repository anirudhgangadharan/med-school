1. Define pharmacodynamics. Describe the factors modifying drug action with suitable examples.
	### **Pharmacodynamics**
	
	- **Definition:** **Pharmacodynamics** is the study of **what the drug does to the body**. It describes the **physiological and biochemical effects of drugs** and their **mechanism of action** at various levels (organ system, subcellular, macromolecular).
	    - It elucidates the **complete action-effect sequence** and the **dose-effect relationship**.
	    - Drug action refers to the initial interaction of the drug with its **receptor** leading to a conformational change (for agonists) or preventing it (for antagonists).
	    - Drug effect is the **ultimate change in biological function** brought about as a consequence of drug action, through intermediate steps (transducer).
	    - Drugs generally **alter the pace of ongoing activity** in a system, organ, or cell, rather than imparting new functions.
	
	### **Factors Modifying Drug Action**
	
	**Individual variability in drug response** is common, affecting patients differently and even in the same patient at different times. These variations can be **quantitative** (altering plasma concentration or drug action, often managed by dose adjustment) or **qualitative** (altering the type of response, such as allergy or idiosyncrasy, which may preclude future drug use).
	
	1. **Body Size:** Influences the drug concentration achieved at the site of action. Doses for exceptionally obese or lean individuals, and for children, may be calculated based on **body weight**.
	    
	2. **Age:**
	    
	    - **Newborn/Infants:**
	        - **Chloramphenicol:** Inefficient conjugation and excretion can lead to **gray baby syndrome** with larger doses.
	        - **Sulfonamides:** Displace bilirubin from protein binding sites, potentially causing **kernicterus** due to a more permeable blood-brain barrier.
	        - **Tetracyclines:** Deposit in developing teeth and bones, causing discoloration and weakening; contraindicated below 6 years of age.
	        - **Renal Function:** Tubular transport mechanisms are not well developed, prolonging the action of drugs like penicillin, cephalosporins, and aspirin.
	        - **Morphine:** Infants are more susceptible to its respiratory depressant action.
	        - **Nevirapine:** Given as syrup to neonates for 6 weeks to prevent mother-to-child HIV transmission [HIV chemoprophylaxis example, prior conversation].
	    - **Elderly (>50 years):**
	        - **Aminoglycosides:** Prolonged half-life, increasing risk of VIII nerve toxicity.
	        - **Renal Function:** Progressively declines after 50 years, leading to substantially lower renal clearance of most drugs.
	        - **Morphine:** More susceptible to respiratory depression.
	        - **Digoxin:** Half-life prolonged in elderly patients, requiring dose reduction.
	        - **Theophylline:** Dose reduction needed for patients >60 years.
	        - **General Anaesthetics:** Minimal alveolar concentration (MAC) of inhalational GAs declines progressively after 50 years.
	3. **Sex:**
	    
	    - **Pregnancy:** Marked physiological changes, especially in the third trimester, alter drug disposition:
	        - **Reduced GI motility:** Delayed oral absorption.
	        - **Expanded plasma/ECF volume:** Increased drug volume of distribution.
	        - **Altered plasma proteins:** Unbound fraction of acidic drugs increases (plasma albumin falls), while that of basic drugs decreases (α1 acid glycoprotein increases).
	        - **Increased renal blood flow:** Faster elimination of polar drugs.
	        - **Hepatic microsomal enzyme induction:** Faster metabolism of many drugs.
	        - **Teratogenicity:** Drugs given during pregnancy can affect the foetus. The US-FDA categorizes risk (A, B, C, D, X). Methyldopa and Hydralazine have been safely used in pregnancy for hypertension.
	    - **Fat:Lean Body Mass Ratio:** Can vary with age and sex, influencing drug distribution.
	4. **Species and Race:**
	    
	    - **Species differences:** E.g., rabbits are resistant to atropine, rats/mice to digitalis, rat more sensitive to curare than cat.
	    - **Racial differences:** Blacks require higher and Mongols lower atropine/ephedrine concentrations for pupil dilation. Beta-blockers are less effective antihypertensives in Afro-Caribbeans. Chloramphenicol-induced aplastic anaemia and quiniodochlor-related SMON showed lower incidence in India compared to Western/Japanese populations despite widespread use.
	5. **Genetics (Pharmacogenetics/Pharmacogenomics):**
	    
	    - **Definition:** **Pharmacogenetics** is the study of the **genetic basis for variability in drug response**. **Pharmacogenomics** uses genetic information to guide drug choice and dose on an individual basis.
	    - All key determinants of drug response (transporters, metabolizing enzymes, ion channels, receptors) are genetically controlled, leading to individual variability.
	    - **Monogenic traits** show better genotype-to-phenotype predictability.
	        - **Atypical pseudocholinesterase:** Leads to prolonged succinylcholine apnoea.
	        - **G-6PD deficiency:** Causes haemolysis with primaquine and other oxidizing drugs (e.g., chloroquine, dapsone, sulfonamides, aspirin, nitrofurantoin). More common in Mediterranean, African, Southeast Asian races.
	        - **CYP2C9 variants:** Low activity variants metabolize warfarin slowly, increasing bleeding risk.
	        - **CYP2D6 polymorphism:** Codeine fails to produce analgesia in individuals who cannot demethylate it to morphine due to polymorphic CYP2D6.
	        - **CYP3A4/2B6 induction by Rifampin:** Induces metabolism of HIV protease inhibitors, NNRTIs, and methadone, potentially causing contraceptive failures (with oral contraceptives) or withdrawal symptoms (with methadone).
	    - **Polymorphisms (SNPs):** Single nucleotide polymorphisms (frequency >1%) are often encountered at different frequencies among ethnic/geographical groups.
	6. **Route of Administration:** Influences speed and intensity of drug response.
	    
	    - **Magnesium sulfate:** Orally causes purgation, topically reduces swelling, intravenously causes CNS depression and hypotension.
	    - **Oral vs. Parenteral:** Oral is generally safer and more convenient, but parenteral routes (e.g., i.v.) offer more rapid, pronounced, and predictable action, crucial in emergencies (e.g., meningitis, septicaemias, spreading cellulitis).
	    - **Bioavailability:** IV route provides 100% bioavailability. Oral bioavailability can be lower due to incomplete absorption or first-pass metabolism.
	    - **Drug-specific route requirements:** Aminoglycosides, penicillin G, many cephalosporins, vancomycin must be given by injection only. Daptomycin is not used for pneumonia because pulmonary surfactant inactivates it. Furosemide may require parenteral administration in severe CHF due to reduced oral bioavailability.
	7. **Environmental Factors and Time of Administration:**
	    
	    - **Enzyme Induction:** Exposure to insecticides, carcinogens, tobacco smoke, and consumption of charcoal-broiled meat can induce drug metabolism.
	    - **Food:** Can alter drug absorption; food interferes with ampicillin absorption, but a fatty meal enhances griseofulvin and lumefantrine absorption. Most drugs are absorbed better on an empty stomach.
	    - **Circadian Rhythm/Timing:** Statins cause greater inhibition of cholesterol synthesis when taken in the late evening. Corticosteroids taken as a single morning dose cause less pituitary-adrenal suppression. Hypnotics work more easily when taken at night in quiet, familiar surroundings.
	8. **Psychological Factors (Placebo/Nocebo Effect):**
	    
	    - **Placebo:** An inert substance given as medicine, working by psychodynamic means. Can produce physiological responses (e.g., endorphin release causing analgesia, blocked by naloxone). It can supplement pharmacological effects but is variable and has a limited role in practical therapeutics.
	    - **Nocebo:** Negative psychodynamic effect from pessimistic attitude, opposing therapeutic effects.
	    - A nervous and anxious patient may require more general anaesthetic.
	9. **Pathological States:** Disease conditions can significantly influence drug disposition and action.
	    
	    - **Gastrointestinal Diseases:** Can alter oral drug absorption (e.g., amoxicillin absorption decreased, cephalexin/cotrimoxazole increased in coeliac disease; gastric stasis in migraine retards absorption; achlorhydria decreases aspirin absorption). NSAIDs can aggravate peptic ulcer disease.
	    - **Liver Disease (e.g., cirrhosis):**
	        - **Increased Bioavailability:** For drugs with high first-pass metabolism due to loss of hepatocellular function and portocaval shunting.
	        - **Reduced Protein Binding:** Serum albumin is reduced, increasing free acidic drugs (e.g., diclofenac, warfarin).
	        - **Decreased Metabolism/Elimination:** For drugs like morphine, lidocaine, propranolol, requiring dose reduction; oxazepam or lorazepam preferred over diazepam; atenolol preferred as beta-blocker.
	        - **Theophylline:** Dose reduction needed in liver failure.
	        - **Pefloxacin:** Dose needs reduction in liver disease.
	        - **Moxifloxacin:** Primarily metabolized in liver; contraindicated in liver disease patients.
	        - **Bosentan:** Contraindicated in liver disease due to risk of hepatic injury and elevation of aminotransferases.
	        - **Sulfonamides/Acetazolamide:** Contraindicated in liver disease; may precipitate hepatic coma by interfering with urinary elimination of H+ due to alkaline urine.
	    - **Kidney Disease (Renal Insufficiency/Failure):**
	        - **Reduced Clearance:** For drugs primarily excreted unchanged (e.g., aminoglycosides, digoxin, phenobarbitone). Loading dose generally not altered, but maintenance doses should be reduced or dose interval prolonged proportionally.
	        - **Altered Protein Binding:** Plasma proteins may be low or altered, reducing binding of acidic drugs but not much affecting basic drugs.
	        - **Theophylline:** Dose reduction needed in renal failure.
	        - **Darbepoetin α:** Indications, efficacy, adverse effects similar to epoetin; dose adjusted based on Hb level.
	        - **Rivaroxaban:** Use not recommended in severe renal impairment (CrCl < 30 ml/min).
	        - **Unfractionated Heparin (UFH):** Preferred in kidney disease patients over LMW heparins/fondaparinux, as its clearance is less affected by renal failure.
	        - **Pefloxacin/Moxifloxacin:** No dose modification needed in renal disease.
	        - **Furosemide/Digoxin:** Half-lives prolonged in renal insufficiency.
	        - **Bosentan:** No dose adjustment needed in renal disease.
	    - **Congestive Heart Failure (CHF):**
	        - **Decreased Absorption:** From GI tract due to mucosal edema and splanchnic vasoconstriction (e.g., procainamide, hydrochlorothiazide).
	        - **Altered Volume of Distribution:** Can increase (due to ECF expansion) or decrease (due to decreased tissue perfusion) (e.g., lidocaine, procainamide loading doses should be lowered).
	        - **Theophylline:** Dose reduction needed in CHF.
	        - **Furosemide:** Oral bioavailability may be markedly reduced in severe CHF, necessitating parenteral administration.
	        - **Lidocaine:** Half-life is prolonged in CHF due to decrease in volume of distribution and hepatic blood flow, requiring reduced infusion rate.
	    - **Other Examples:**
	        - **Antipyretics:** Lower body temperature only when fever is present.
	        - **Thiazides:** Induce more marked diuresis in edematous patients.
	        - **Myocardial Infarction:** Patients more prone to adrenaline and digitalis-induced cardiac arrhythmias.
	        - **Myasthenia Gravis:** Patients very sensitive to curare; weakness aggravated by quinine.
	        - **Schizophrenics:** Tolerate large doses of phenothiazines.
	        - **Head Injury:** Patients prone to respiratory failure with normal morphine doses.
	        - **Prostatic Hypertrophy:** Atropine, tricyclic antidepressants, furosemide can cause urinary retention.
	        - **Severe Pain:** Hypnotics given to a patient in severe pain may cause mental confusion and delirium.
	        - **AIDS:** Cotrimoxazole produces higher incidence of adverse reactions in AIDS patients.
	10. **Other Drugs (Drug Interactions):** Drugs can modify each other's response via pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic interactions. This can be synergistic or antagonistic.
	    
	11. **Cumulation:** Occurs if the rate of drug administration exceeds its elimination rate. Slowly eliminated drugs are particularly prone to cumulative toxicity (e.g., prolonged chloroquine use causing retinal damage). Digoxin loading dose should be adjusted if patient received it recently.
	    
	12. **Tolerance, Tachyphylaxis, and Drug Resistance:**
	    
	    - **Tolerance:** Requirement of a **higher dose** of a drug to produce a given response. It's an adaptive biological phenomenon, often leading to loss of therapeutic efficacy with prolonged/intensive use (e.g., sulfonylureas in type 2 diabetes, nitrates, morphine, barbiturates).
	        - **Pharmacokinetic/Drug Disposition Tolerance:** Effective drug concentration at the site of action decreases, usually due to enhanced drug elimination (e.g., barbiturates and carbamazepine induce their own metabolism; accelerated renal excretion of amphetamine). Rifampin also induces its own metabolism (autoinduction) and that of other drugs. Nevirapine requires dose doubling after 2 weeks due to autoinduction.
	        - **Pharmacodynamic/Cellular Tolerance:** Target cells become less responsive (e.g., morphine, barbiturates, nitrates) due to **desensitization/downregulation of receptors** or weakening of response effectuation.
	    - **Tachyphylaxis:** **Rapid development of tolerance** when doses are repeated in quick succession, leading to marked reduction in response. Typically seen with indirectly acting drugs (e.g., ephedrine, tyramine, nicotine) that deplete neurotransmitter stores. Also linked to receptor desensitization/internalization/downregulation.
	    - **Drug Resistance:** Refers to the **tolerance of microorganisms to antimicrobial inhibitory action** (e.g., Staphylococci to penicillin). Chemoprophylaxis can lead to resistant organisms [Chemoprophylaxis definition, prior conversation].
2. Fixed dose combination of drugs with advantages, disadvantages and rational examples
	Fixed dose combinations (FDCs) are pharmaceutical preparations containing **two or more drugs in a fixed dose ratio**. They are designed to simplify treatment regimens and improve patient outcomes in specific clinical situations.
	
	### Advantages of Fixed Dose Combinations
	
	1. **Improved Patient Convenience and Compliance**: FDCs reduce the pill burden, making it easier for patients to adhere to their prescribed regimen. This is particularly important when multiple medications are required.
	2. **Enhanced Therapeutic Synergism**: Certain drug combinations exert a supra-additive effect, meaning the combined effect is greater than the sum of their individual effects.
	    - **Example**: **Sulfamethoxazole and trimethoprim** show optimal synergy, significantly reducing the Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) of each component.
	    - **Example**: **Levodopa with carbidopa/benserazide** achieves synergism by inhibiting the peripheral breakdown of levodopa, allowing more of the drug to reach the brain.
	3. **Prevention of Drug Resistance**: In chronic infections, combining drugs reduces the likelihood of resistant strains emerging, as it would require multiple independent mutations for resistance to all components.
	    - **Example**: **Antitubercular drug combinations** (e.g., Isoniazid, Rifampin, Pyrazinamide, Ethambutol) are crucial for eradicating bacilli and preventing resistance, especially in prolonged therapy.
	    - **Example**: **Antiretroviral therapy (ART) for HIV/AIDS** involves combinations of drugs from different classes (e.g., Emtricitabine + Tenofovir + Efavirenz) to achieve sustained viral suppression and prevent resistance.
	    - **Example**: **Artemisinin-based Combination Therapies (ACTs)** for malaria reduce recrudescence rates and prevent the development of resistance by combining a rapidly acting artemisinin with a longer-acting partner.
	    - **Example**: **Multi-drug therapy (MDT) for leprosy** with rifampin, dapsone, and clofazimine effectively manages dapsone-resistant strains and eliminates microbial persisters.
	4. **Reduced Side Effects**: When components of an FDC act by different mechanisms, their therapeutic effects can be additive, while their distinct side effects may not add up, leading to better tolerability.
	    - **Example**: A **thiazide diuretic combined with a potassium-sparing diuretic** can counteract the potassium loss caused by the thiazide.
	5. **Improved Efficacy/Broader Spectrum**: FDCs can provide a broader spectrum of activity, which is useful in severe infections where the causative organism is not yet identified (empirical therapy) or in mixed infections.
	    - **Example**: Initial treatment of severe infections often involves combinations like **penicillin + gentamicin** to cover both Gram-positive and Gram-negative organisms.
	
	### Disadvantages of Fixed Dose Combinations
	
	6. **Risk of Unnecessary Drug Exposure**: Patients may receive drugs they do not need, leading to additional side effects and increased cost.
	7. **Difficulty in Dose Individualisation**: Doses of individual drugs often need to be adjusted based on patient-specific factors (e.g., age, renal/hepatic function, response). FDCs make this difficult without altering the dose of other components.
	8. **Different Pharmacokinetic Profiles**: Components may have different absorption rates, half-lives, or metabolic pathways, making a single dosing schedule inappropriate for all drugs in the combination.
	9. **Complications with Organ Dysfunction**: Renal or hepatic impairment can affect the pharmacokinetics of components differently, necessitating cautious use and potential for accumulation of one drug over another.
	10. **Challenges in Identifying Adverse Drug Reactions (ADRs)**: If an ADR occurs, it is harder to pinpoint which specific drug in the combination is responsible.
	11. **Contraindications to One Component**: A contraindication or allergy to any single drug in the FDC automatically contraindicates the entire combination product.
	12. **Fosters Irrational Prescribing**: FDCs can lead to a casual approach to diagnosis and drug selection, promoting a false sense of superiority of combinations over single drugs.
	    - **Example**: **Ampicillin + cloxacillin** or **amoxicillin + cloxacillin** combinations were considered irrational and have been banned in India because cloxacillin is ineffective against Gram-negative bacteria and ampicillin is ineffective against staphylococci, making one component useless in most common infections and potentially fostering resistance.
	    - **Example**: Fixed dose combinations of centrally acting antitussives with bronchodilators or highly atropinic antihistamines have been banned in India due to indiscriminate use and lack of appropriate time course.
	
	### Rational Examples of FDCs (Clinical Relevance)
	
	- **Antimicrobials**:
	    - **Cotrimoxazole (Sulfamethoxazole + Trimethoprim)**: Classic example of **synergistic antimicrobial action** by sequential blockade of bacterial folate metabolism.
	    - **Antitubercular Fixed Dose Combinations (FDCs)**: Essential for **preventing resistance** due to the long duration of therapy and high mutation rates of _M. tuberculosis_. Examples include RHZE (Rifampin, Isoniazid, Pyrazinamide, Ethambutol) in the intensive phase.
	    - **Antiretroviral FDCs (e.g., Tenofovir/Emtricitabine/Efavirenz)**: Improve **adherence and reduce resistance** in HIV treatment.
	    - **Artemisinin-based Combination Therapies (ACTs)**: Crucial for **high cure rates and preventing resistance** in falciparum malaria.
	    - **MDT in Leprosy (Rifampin + Dapsone + Clofazimine)**: Address **dapsone resistance** and shorten treatment duration.
	- **Cardiovascular Drugs**:
	    - **Antihypertensives (e.g., ACE inhibitor/ARB + CCB or Diuretic)**: Rational combinations can achieve better BP control with lower doses of individual components and potentially fewer side effects.
	    - **Antiplatelets (e.g., Aspirin + Clopidogrel)**: Used in acute coronary syndromes (ACS) and after stent placement to provide **potent platelet inhibition** and reduce thrombotic events.
	    - **Hydralazine + Isosorbide Dinitrate**: Beneficial in severe CHF, providing both preload and afterload reduction, particularly for African-American patients with severe CHF.
	- **Parkinsonism**:
	    - **Levodopa + Carbidopa/Benserazide**: Carbidopa/benserazide inhibit peripheral decarboxylation of levodopa, **increasing its bioavailability** to the brain and reducing peripheral side effects.
	- **Other**:
	    - **Oral Contraceptives (combination)**: Provide **enhanced efficacy and convenience**.
	
	### Prescribing Considerations for FDCs
	
	According to the WHO and National List of Essential Medicines (NLEM), FDCs are justified when:
	
	- Each ingredient's dosage meets the requirements of a defined population group.
	- The combination offers **proven advantages** in therapeutic effect, safety, or adherence.
	- The combination helps in **decreasing the emergence of drug resistance**.
	
	In summary, while FDCs offer significant advantages in terms of convenience, compliance, and targeted therapeutic benefits, prescribers must critically evaluate their necessity, the individual patient's needs, and potential drawbacks, avoiding their indiscriminate use.
3. What is pharmacogenomics-give examples
	### Pharmacogenomics
	
	**Pharmacogenomics** is the **use of genetic information to guide the choice of drug and dose on an individual basis**. It is the study of the genetic basis for variability in drug response, aiming to identify individuals who are more or less likely to respond to a drug, or who require an altered dose. The ultimate goal is **'personalized medicine'**.
	
	**Key Concepts**:
	
	- **Genetic Basis of Variability**: All key determinants of drug response, including transporters, metabolising enzymes, ion channels, and receptors, are genetically controlled. This leads to significant individual variability in drug response, often varying by 4-6 fold among individuals.
	- **Polymorphisms**: Genetic variability can be due to single gene mutation or polygenic causes. When a single base pair substitution is found in a population at a frequency greater than 1%, these are called **'Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms' (SNPs)**. These polymorphisms can differ in frequency among different ethnic/geographical groups.
	- **Predictability**: Genotype-to-phenotype predictability is better for monogenic phenotypic traits (e.g., G-6PD, CYP2D6) than for multigenic traits.
	- **Practical Application**: While considerable pharmacogenomic data has accumulated, its routine application is currently limited due to the need for multiple drug-specific genotypic screening. Simple spot tests for some conditions, like G-6PD deficiency, are in use.
	
	**Rational Examples (Clinical Relevance)**:
	
	1. **Drug Metabolism Enzyme Polymorphisms**:
	    
	    - **CYP2C9 Variants and Warfarin**: Individuals with low activity CYP2C9 variants metabolise **warfarin** (an anticoagulant) slowly and have a **higher risk of bleeding**. This necessitates careful dose adjustment.
	    - **CYP2D6 Abnormality**: This leads to a 'poor metaboliser' status for drugs like **metoprolol** and **debrisoquin**. Patients with this deficiency are more likely to experience toxicity from certain **antidepressants and antipsychotics** that are also substrates of CYP2D6.
	        - **Codeine**: Fails to produce analgesia in CYP2D6 deficient individuals because this enzyme is responsible for generating morphine from codeine.
	    - **N-acetyltransferase 2 (NAT2) Polymorphism**: Results in rapid and slow acetylator statuses.
	        - **Isoniazid neuropathy**: Occurs mainly in **slow acetylators**.
	        - **Procainamide and Hydralazine-induced lupus**: Also occur predominantly in slow acetylators.
	2. **Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (G-6PD) Deficiency**:
	    
	    - This X-linked monogenic trait is responsible for **haemolysis** when exposed to certain **oxidising drugs**. It is more common in Mediterranean, African, and Southeast Asian populations.
	    - **Drugs that can cause haemolysis in G-6PD deficient individuals** (higher risk italicized):
	        - _**Primaquine**_
	        - _**Nalidixic acid**_
	        - _Chloroquine_
	        - _Fluoroquinolones_
	        - _Quinine/Quinidine_
	        - _Chloramphenicol_
	        - _Proguanil_
	        - _Nitrofurantoin_
	        - _Pyrimethamine_
	        - _Furazolidone_
	        - _**Dapsone**_
	        - _Menadione_
	        - _Sulfonamides (including Cotrimoxazole, Sulfasalazine)_
	        - _Methyldopa_
	        - _Hydralazine_
	        - _Procainamide_
	        - _Sulfonylureas_
	        - _Probenecid_
	        - _Thiazide diuretics_
	        - _Colchicine_
	        - _Aspirin_
	        - _Methylene blue_
	3. **Other Genetic Defects**:
	    
	    - **Atypical Pseudocholinesterase**: Leads to **prolonged succinylcholine apnoea**.
	    - **Acute Intermittent Porphyria**: Precipitated by **barbiturates** due to a genetic defect in repressing porphyrin synthesis.
	
	**Impact on Drug Development**: Pharmacogenomics plays a role in the "rational approach" to new drug invention, where knowledge of the target biomolecule's structure (which can be influenced by genetic variations) allows for the design of targeted compounds. This includes producing individualised drugs according to pharmacogenomic suitability.
4. Define tachyphylaxis. Mention drugs exhibiting it
	### Tachyphylaxis
	
	**Tachyphylaxis** refers to the **rapid development of tolerance** when doses of a drug are repeated in quick succession, resulting in a **marked reduction in response**.
	
	**Key Characteristics & Mechanisms:**
	
	- **Rapid Onset:** It is distinct from conventional tolerance in its swift development.
	- **Mechanism (Commonly):**
	    - Often observed with **indirectly acting drugs**.
	    - These drugs act by **releasing endogenous substances** (e.g., catecholamines) in the body.
	    - The rapid rate of release leads to **depletion of these endogenous stores**, as their synthesis is unable to match the rate of release.
	- **Other Mechanisms:**
	    - Slow dissociation of the drug from its receptor.
	    - Desensitization/internalization or down-regulation of receptors.
	    - Compensatory homeostatic adaptation by the body.
	
	**Drugs Exhibiting Tachyphylaxis:**
	
	- **Ephedrine**
	- **Tyramine**
	- **Nicotine**
	
	**Note on Angiotensin II:**
	
	- In contrast, Angiotensin II (Ang II) does **not** exhibit tachyphylaxis in its pressor action.
