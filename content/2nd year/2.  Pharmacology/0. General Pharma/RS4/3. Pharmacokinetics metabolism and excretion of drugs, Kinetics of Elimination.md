1. Methods to prolong drug action
	
	**Methods to Prolong Drug Action**
	
	Prolonging drug action is advantageous for:
	
	- Reducing the frequency of administration, which is **more convenient**.
	- Improving **patient compliance**.
	
	However, not all drugs need to be made long-acting, especially those used for brief effects (e.g., hypnotics, headache remedies) or those that already have an inherently **long duration of action** or half-life (t½). Drugs with a t½ of ≤ 4 hours are particularly **suitable for controlled release formulations**.
	
	Methods utilized for prolonging drug action include:
	
	1. **By Prolonging Absorption from Site of Administration**:
	    
	    - **Oral:**
	        - Use of **Sustained Release (SR) tablets, spansule capsules**, etc..
	        - Drug particles are coated with resins, plastics, or other substances that **temporally disperse the release** in the gastrointestinal tract.
	        - Use of **Controlled Release (CR) tablets/capsules** utilizing a **semipermeable membrane** to control drug release.
	        - These preparations can prolong action by **4 to 8 hours**, but duration is limited as particles reach the colon. Oral morphine in **continuous release formulation** and isosorbide dinitrate in **sustained release formulation** are mentioned as examples for chronic pain/prophylaxis.
	    - **Parenteral (s.c. or i.m.):**
	        - Injection of drugs in **insoluble form**. Examples: **benzathine penicillin, lente insulin**.
	        - Injection as **oily solutions**. Example: **depot progestins**.
	        - Use of solid implants like **pellet implantation, sialastic, and biodegradable implants**. These can provide absorption over **days to several months or even years**.
	        - Inclusion of a **vasoconstrictor** (e.g., **adrenaline with local anaesthetics**) also delays absorption.
	    - **Transdermal Drug Delivery Systems:**
	        - Drug is impregnated in **adhesive patches, strips, or used as ointment** applied on skin. This is used in some cases to prolong drug action.
	        - Examples: **GTN (glyceryl trinitrate)** applied as ointment or transdermal patch for angina pectoris. **Transdermal fentanyl** is a suitable option for chronic pain. **Buprenorphine** is also available as a transdermal patch.
	2. **By Increasing Plasma Protein Binding**:
	    
	    - Drug **congeners** can be prepared which are highly bound to plasma proteins.
	    - The drug is then **slowly released in the free active form**.
	    - Example: **sulfadoxine**.
	3. **By Retarding Rate of Metabolism**:
	    
	    - A **small chemical modification** can markedly affect the rate of metabolism without affecting biological action.
	    - Example: Addition of an **ethinyl group to estradiol (ethinyl estradiol)** makes it longer acting and suitable for use as an oral contraceptive.
	    - **Inhibition of a specific enzyme by one drug can prolong the action of another drug**.
	    - Examples: **allopurinol inhibits the degradation of 6-mercaptopurine**; **ritonavir boosts the levels of other HIV protease inhibitors (PIs) like atazanavir, lopinavir, and saquinavir** by potent CYP3A4 inhibition; **cilastatin protects imipenem** from degradation in the kidney. Note: This mechanism involves drug interactions or auto-inhibition, resulting in prolonged action of the co-administered drug or the drug itself.
	4. **By Retarding Renal Excretion**:
	    
	    - Tubular secretion of drugs, being an active process, can be **suppressed by a competing substance**.
	    - Example: **probenecid prolongs the duration of action of penicillins**, including **ampicillin and amoxicillin**.
	    - **Targeted drug delivery devices**, such as liposomes, can localize and prolong drug delivery to a specific target organ.
	    - Example: **Liposomal amphotericin B** is used in Kala azar and some serious cases of systemic mycosis, where the drug incorporated in liposomes gets selectively delivered to reticuloendothelial cells, potentially affecting its elimination and action duration at the target site.
2. Prodrug and examples
	**Prodrugs**
	
	- **Definition:** Based on the sources, a prodrug is an administered drug that is converted **metabolically** or **chemically** within the body (often at the site of action) into the **active pharmacological agent**. This conversion to an active form or an active metabolite contributes to the drug's overall effect.
	- The administered form itself may have little or no pharmacological activity.
	
	**Examples from Sources:**
	
	- **Ethionamide:** Converted by mycobacteria into an **active intermediate** that interferes with mycolic acid synthesis. It is nearly completely absorbed orally.
	- **Sodium Stibogluconate (SSG):** A pentavalent antimonial. A specific reductase enzyme present in _Leishmania_ amastigotes **reduces pentavalent-Sb of SSG to the toxic trivalent form**. This trivalent form is the active entity.
	- **Pefloxacin:** Highly metabolized, **partly to norfloxacin which contributes to its activity**. Pefloxacin is completely absorbed orally and penetrates tissues better than norfloxacin.
	- **Methyldopa:** While not explicitly called a prodrug in the source, it is transported actively by an intestinal amino acid carrier and has a primary central site of action. Its action on sympathetic tone and metabolism are consistent with conversion to an active central metabolite (alpha-methylnorepinephrine), although this metabolite isn't named in the source excerpts.
	
	**Clinical Importance:**
	
	- **Improved Pharmacokinetics:** Prodrugs can be designed to have better absorption, distribution (e.g., better tissue penetration), or other pharmacokinetic properties compared to the active drug. Pefloxacin, a methyl derivative of norfloxacin, is more lipid soluble, completely absorbed orally, and penetrates tissues better. Ethionamide is nearly completely absorbed orally.
	- **Selective Toxicity:** Activation at the target site can enhance selective toxicity. SSG is activated _within the Leishmania amastigote_ to its toxic form, which then disrupts parasite metabolism.
	- **Extended Action:** A parent drug and its active metabolite can contribute to the overall duration and intensity of effect. Pefloxacin's longer half-life contributes to its effectiveness in systemic infections.
	- **Influence on Dosing:** If a significant portion of a drug's activity comes from an active metabolite, dosage adjustments may be necessary in conditions affecting metabolism (e.g., liver disease for pefloxacin).
	- **Impact on Resistance:** For some prodrugs (like ethionamide), resistance can develop if the organism loses the capacity to activate the drug. For SSG, resistance may involve reduced capacity to convert it to the trivalent form or alterations in parasite thiol metabolism.
3. Define plasma half life and mention its significance
	**Plasma Half-Life (t½)**
	
	- **Definition:** The **time taken** for a drug's **plasma concentration to be reduced to half** of its original value.
	- Represents the time for plasma concentration to decay during the **elimination phase** (beta slope) after drug distribution.
	- The clinically relevant t½ is typically calculated over the **steady-state plasma concentration range**.
	- For drugs following **first-order kinetics**, t½ is inversely proportional to the elimination rate constant (t½ = 0.693/k).
	
	**Significance (Clinical Importance):**
	
	- **Key Pharmacokinetic Parameter:** It is a fundamental characteristic influencing how a drug behaves in the body over time.
	- **Dosing Frequency:** Determines the **appropriate interval** between drug administrations to maintain therapeutic levels. Drugs with shorter t½ generally require more frequent dosing (e.g., lidocaine, furosemide) or continuous infusion (e.g., lidocaine, midazolam). Drugs with longer t½ allow less frequent dosing (e.g., clofazimine allowing intermittent therapy, darbepoetin alpha allowing weekly injection).
	- **Time to Steady-State:** **Steady-state plasma concentration (Cpss)**, where the rate of administration equals the rate of elimination, is typically reached after **4-5 half-lives** when a drug is given at a constant dose and interval.
	- **Drug Accumulation & Fluctuation:** Determines the extent of drug accumulation with repeated dosing before the previous dose is fully eliminated. The t½ relative to the dose interval affects the amplitude of fluctuations in plasma concentration at steady-state; shorter dose intervals relative to t½ result in less fluctuation.
	- **Loading & Maintenance Doses:** Essential for calculating loading doses (to rapidly achieve therapeutic Cpss) and maintenance doses (to maintain Cpss), especially for drugs needed urgently. Examples include digoxin and lidocaine.
	- **Influence of Organ Function:** t½ is affected by the body's ability to eliminate the drug (metabolism and excretion). It is **prolonged** in patients with impaired renal function (e.g., aminoglycosides, digoxin) and/or hepatic function (e.g., furosemide, morphine, lidocaine, propranolol), necessitating dose adjustments to avoid accumulation and toxicity. This connects to our previous discussion about liver disease affecting the elimination of certain drugs, including those with high first-pass metabolism.
	- **Therapeutic Drug Monitoring (TDM):** Knowledge of t½ is critical for interpreting plasma drug concentrations and timing blood sample collection, particularly for dose adjustment purposes, by guiding when to measure trough or peak levels relative to the dose interval.
	- **Non-linear Kinetics:** For drugs following capacity-limited (Michaelis-Menten) kinetics (e.g., phenytoin), the t½ is not constant but can **increase as the plasma concentration rises** and metabolizing enzymes become saturated.
