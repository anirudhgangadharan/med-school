1. Pharmacological basis of use of Digoxin in heart failure
	**Digoxin in Heart Failure**
	
	- **Drug Class:** Cardiac Glycoside. Possesses cardiac inotropic property, increasing myocardial contractility and output in a hypodynamic heart without a proportionate increase in O2 consumption.
	- **Historical Context:** Historically, cardiac glycosides were the primary drugs for congestive heart failure (CHF) until the 1970s, derived from 'foxglove' (Digitalis).
	- **Current Role:** No longer a first-line drug, used in conjunction with other drugs in selected cases. Provides symptomatic relief, primarily in systolic dysfunction or when CHF is due to atrial fibrillation (AF) with rapid heart rate. Ineffective in reversing or retarding the pathological changes of heart failure or affording prognostic benefit. However, large studies like the DIG trial found no evidence that digoxin decreases overall mortality in CHF patients, although episodes of decompensation and heart failure deaths are reduced, but incidence of sudden death may increase. It lacks effect on neurohumoral contributors to the pathogenesis of heart failure.
	- **Mechanism of Action (Cellular):**
	    - Partial inhibition of **Na+-K+ATPase**.
	    - Reduces transmembrane gradient of Na+.
	    - Reduces the efficiency of the **Na+-Ca2+ exchanger (NCX)**, which normally extrudes Ca2+.
	    - Excess Ca2+ remains in the cytosol and is taken up into the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR).
	    - SR progressively loads with more Ca2+, augmenting subsequent calcium transients.
	    - This leads to increased interaction between actin and myosin, causing a dose-dependent increase in the force of contraction (**positive inotropic action**). This is especially pronounced in the failing heart.
	- **Effects on the Heart (beyond Inotropy):**
	    - **Increased Contractility:** Systole is shortened, diastole is prolonged. Increases cardiac output and reduces end-diastolic volume in failing ventricles.
	    - **Decreased Heart Rate:** Bradycardia, more marked in CHF patients due to restored vagal tone and abolished sympathetic overactivity. Slows heart rate via vagal (increased tone) and extravagal (direct depression of SA and AV nodes) actions.
	    - **Electrophysiological Properties:**
	        - Reduces action potential duration (APD), primarily at phase-2.
	        - Decreases effective refractory period (ERP) in atria and ventricles by direct action. Vagal action normally predominates in atria, causing inhomogeneity and allowing response at a higher rate/asynchronously.
	        - Increases ERP of the A-V node and bundle of His by direct, vagomimetic, and antiadrenergic actions, reducing the maximum rate of impulse transmission.
	        - Slows A-V conduction at therapeutic doses. High doses depress intraventricular conduction in Purkinje fibres.
	        - ECG changes occur at therapeutic doses, accentuated at high doses, potentially leading to arrhythmias.
	- **Effects on Circulation & Kidney in CHF:**
	    - In CHF patients, indirect effect of improved circulation (withdrawal of reflex sympathetic overactivity) leads to a net decrease in peripheral resistance, compensating for weak direct vasoconstriction.
	    - Diuresis occurs in CHF patients secondary to improved circulation and renal perfusion, excreting retained salt and water. No diuresis in normal individuals or edema from other causes.
	- **Pharmacokinetics:**
	    - Oral absorption: 60-80%. Absorption delayed by food. Bioavailability varies between manufacturers.
	    - Plasma protein binding: 25%.
	    - Time course of action (after i.v.): Onset 15-30 min, Peak 2-5 hr, Duration 2-6 days.
	    - Plasma t½: 40 hr. Prolonged in elderly and those with renal insufficiency; dose must be reduced.
	    - Route of elimination: Primarily excreted unchanged by kidney, mainly via glomerular filtration. Excretion rate parallels creatinine clearance.
	    - Therapeutic concentration: 0.5-1.4 ng/ml.
	    - Toxic concentration: > 2 ng/ml. Low therapeutic index.
	- **Uses in Heart Failure:**
	    - Most effective drug for relieving symptoms of systolic failure and restoring cardiac compensation, especially in patients with dilated heart and low ejection fraction (EF).
	    - Patients with preserved EF (diastolic dysfunction) usually do not respond to digoxin or other inotropic drugs.
	    - All patients with low EF who remain symptomatic despite ACE inhibitor/ARB + diuretic ± β-blocker should receive digoxin.
	    - Continued digoxin is the best course for CHF patients with atrial fibrillation needing ventricular rate control.
	- **Dosage:**
	    - Now initiated with estimated maintenance doses (0.125-0.25 mg/day) without a loading dose; full response takes 5-7 days.
	    - Dose increased at weekly intervals if response inadequate (to 0.375 and 0.5 mg/day).
	    - Reduction in heart rate and symptom relief are the best guides.
	    - Loading dose (0.75-1.25 mg over 24-48 hrs) possible if early response needed, but requires close monitoring.
	- **Adverse Effects (Toxicity):**
	    - Extracardiac: Anorexia, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, fatigue, headache, mental confusion, visual disturbances (often precede cardiac toxicity in ~2/3 patients).
	    - Cardiac: Almost any arrhythmia possible. Common: pulsus bigeminus, nodal/ventricular extrasystoles, ventricular tachycardia/fibrillation. Partial to complete A-V block possible, with or without other arrhythmias. Severe bradycardia, atrial extrasystoles, AF/AFl noted.
	- **Management of Toxicity:**
	    - Stop digoxin immediately.
	    - For tachyarrhythmias (especially with K+ depletion from chronic use/diuretics): IV KCl infusion (measure serum K+ first; contraindicated if high or with A-V block).
	    - For ventricular arrhythmias: IV Lidocaine is drug of choice (suppresses automaticity, doesn't worsen A-V block). Quinidine, procainamide, propafenone are contraindicated.
	    - For supraventricular arrhythmias: Propranolol (IV or oral).
	    - For A-V block and bradycardia: Cardiac pacing preferred; Atropine may help.
	    - Cardioversion (DC shock) is contraindicated due to risk of severe conduction defects/ventricular asystole.
	    - Digoxin-specific antibody (Fab fragment) is highly effective in severe toxicity (e.g., DIGIBIND, DIGIFAB).
	- **Contraindications/Precautions:**
	    - Contraindicated in **Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) syndrome** because it can decrease the ERP of the bypass tract, allowing rapid atrial impulses to ventricles and potentially causing VF.
	    - Avoid concurrent use with verapamil or diltiazem and β-blockers as their depressant effects on SA and A-V nodes may additive.
	    - Caution with diuretics due to hypokalemia risk, which increases digitalis arrhythmia risk; K+ supplements may be needed.
	    - Many drugs can affect digoxin absorption (e.g., reduced by metoclopramide, sucralfate, antacids, neomycin, sulfasalazine; increased by atropinic drugs).
	
	**Clinical Relevance Notes:**
	
	- Digoxin is primarily a **symptomatic** treatment in CHF with systolic dysfunction, particularly when accompanied by AF.
	- Its role in improving prognosis is limited compared to ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and β-blockers.
	- **Monitor serum digoxin levels** and **potassium levels** due to low therapeutic index and risk of hypokalemia-induced toxicity.
	- Recognise signs of digoxin toxicity, especially gastrointestinal, neurological, and cardiac arrhythmias.
	- Lidocaine is the preferred drug for digitalis-induced ventricular arrhythmias.
2. Aldosterone antagonists (spironolactone) are beneficial in heart failure , Give reasons
	
	**Aldosterone Antagonists (Spironolactone) in Heart Failure**
	
	- **Drug Class:** Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonists. Examples: Spironolactone, Eplerenone.
	- **Role in Heart Failure (HF):**
	    - Not primary therapy initially; added to conventional therapy (ACE inhibitor/ARB ± β-blocker ± diuretic) in **moderate-to-severe CHF**.
	    - Provide **symptomatic relief** and, crucially, **retard disease progression** and **lower mortality** in these selected patients.
	    - Benefit demonstrated in patients with **reduced Ejection Fraction (EF)**. Role in preserved EF is uncertain.
	- **Mechanism of Benefit (Antagonizing Aldosterone Effects):**
	    - Aldosterone production is increased in HF.
	    - Aldosterone acts on the kidney (distal tubule/collecting duct) to promote **Na+ reabsorption** and **K+/H+ excretion**. This expands ECF volume.
	    - Spironolactone **binds to the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR)**, preventing aldosterone's action.
	    - Antagonizing aldosterone combats its pathological effects which include:
	        - **ECF Volume Expansion:** Aldosterone increases cardiac preload. Antagonism contributes to diuresis/natriuresis, helping to reduce preload and pulmonary congestion.
	        - **Myocardial & Vascular Fibrosis/Remodeling:** Aldosterone promotes fibroblast proliferation and fibrotic change in the myocardium, worsening systolic dysfunction and causing pathological remodeling. Antagonists attenuate these complications. They also influence vascular hypertrophy.
	        - **Electrolyte Imbalance:** Aldosterone-induced K+ and Mg2+ loss increases risk of ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death. Aldosterone antagonists are K+ conserving.
	- **Key Clinical Outcomes:**
	    - **Increased Survival:** Large trials (RALES, EPHESUS) demonstrated **mortality benefit** (including sudden cardiac deaths) in moderate-to-severe HF with reduced EF.
	    - **Reduced Hospitalizations:** Reduce episodes of decompensation.
	    - **Aid Diuretic Response:** May help restore diuretic response to furosemide when refractoriness develops.
	- **Dosage and Monitoring:**
	    - Used at **low doses** (e.g., Spironolactone 12.5-25 mg/day) to avoid hyperkalaemia.
	    - Risk of **hyperkalaemia** is significant, especially when combined with ACE inhibitors/ARBs. Serum K+ must be monitored.
	    - Spironolactone can cause gynaecomastia; Eplerenone avoids this.
	- **Clinical Relevance:**
	    - Essential component of modern HF therapy guidelines for symptomatic patients with **reduced EF**, offering prognostic benefits **beyond** ACE inhibitors and β-blockers.
	    - Careful patient selection and monitoring for hyperkalaemia are crucial.
	    - They address neurohumoral pathways involved in HF progression, unlike older symptomatic treatments like diuretics alone or digoxin.
