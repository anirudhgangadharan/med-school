1. A 50-year-old woman visited the general practitioner for annual health screening. Her Blood pressure was 152/90 mm Hg. She is diagnosed to be suffering from hypertension and is prescribed angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors (ACE inhibitors). Explain the mechanism of action, therapeutic uses, adverse effects, contraindications of ACE inhibitors
	
	**Angiotensin Converting Enzyme (ACE) Inhibitors (e.g., Captopril, Enalapril, Lisinopril, Ramipril)**
	
	**Mechanism of Action:**
	
	- ACE Inhibitors prevent the conversion of inactive **Angiotensin I** (a decapeptide) to active **Angiotensin II** (an octapeptide).
	- This conversion is mediated by **Angiotensin Converting Enzyme (ACE)**, a dipeptidyl carboxypeptidase located primarily on vascular endothelial cells, especially in the lungs.
	- By reducing Angiotensin II levels, ACE inhibitors diminish its effects mediated mainly via **AT1 receptors**:
	    - Reduced **Vasoconstriction** (direct and sympathetically mediated). Angiotensin II is a potent pressor agent.
	    - Reduced **Aldosterone Secretion**, leading to decreased Na+ and water retention. Basal aldosterone levels are somewhat decreased, and reflex postural changes are abolished.
	    - Reduced enhancement of sympathetic activity (central and peripheral).
	    - Reduced increase in myocardial contraction force and cardiac work (due to decreased afterload/preload).
	    - Reduced induction of **myocardial and vascular hypertrophy and remodeling**.
	- ACE inhibitors also inhibit the breakdown of **bradykinin**, a potent vasodilator. Elevated bradykinin levels may contribute to beneficial effects (cardioprotective, vasodepressor).
	- Increased Angiotensin I levels may be diverted to produce more **Angiotensin (1-7)**, which has vasodilator properties and may contribute to BP lowering.
	
	**Therapeutic Uses:**
	
	ACE inhibitors are considered **first-line drugs** for many conditions.
	
	- **Hypertension:** Effective in all grades of essential and renovascular hypertension (except bilateral renal artery stenosis). Control BP in ~50% patients as monotherapy and ~90% in combination with diuretics or beta-blockers. Develops gradually over 2-3 weeks. **Suitable for:**
	    - Younger (<55 years) non-black patients (often higher plasma renin activity).
	    - Diabetics (reduce CV complications, improve endothelial function, retard retinopathy).
	    - Patients with Nephropathy (diabetic and non-diabetic CKD) - prevent/delay end-stage renal disease, reduce proteinuria, retard progression.
	    - Patients with Left Ventricular Hypertrophy (LVH) - reverses it.
	    - Patients with Angina.
	    - Patients with Peripheral Vascular Disease (PVD).
	- **Congestive Heart Failure (CHF):** **Sheet anchor of drug therapy**. First-line for symptomatic and asymptomatic LV inadequacy. Retard progression of LV systolic dysfunction, prolong survival (all grades I-IV), reduce mortality (~20%). Reduce afterload and preload, improve haemodynamics, cause diuresis, enhance exercise capacity. Attenuate hypertrophy, remodeling, apoptosis, fibrosis.
	- **Myocardial Infarction (MI):** Oral therapy started within 24 hours reduces early and long-term mortality. Beneficial in unstable angina/NSTEMI (reduce recurrent MI/need for angioplasty). All patients benefit if no contraindications, especially with associated HTN/diabetes. Reduce ventricular dysfunction, HF, mortality in post-MI patients.
	- **Prophylaxis in High Cardiovascular Risk Subjects:** Reduce cardiac death, MI, stroke, and risk of developing HF or diabetes, even without associated HTN or LV dysfunction.
	- **Scleroderma Crisis:** Produce dramatic, life-saving improvement.
	- **Compelling Indications:** Heart failure, Post-MI, High coronary artery disease risk, Diabetes, Chronic kidney disease, Recurrent stroke prevention.
	
	**Adverse Effects:**
	
	Generally well tolerated, especially at lower doses. Adverse effect profile similar across the class.
	
	- **Dry, persistent cough:** The **most common** side effect, often requiring discontinuation. Believed to be related to elevated bradykinin and substance P levels. Less common with ARBs.
	- **Angioedema:** Rare but potentially life-threatening. Less common with ARBs.
	- **Hypotension:** Can occur, especially with initial doses or in patients with activated RAS (Na+ depletion, CHF, renovascular/accelerated/malignant HTN). Can lead to weakness, nausea, sweating, syncope. Severe hypotension risks impairing vital organ perfusion (acute renal failure, myocardial ischaemia, stroke).
	- **Acute Renal Failure:** Risk is increased, especially in patients with **bilateral renal artery stenosis**. Risk also increased with drastic BP reduction or combination with ARBs.
	- **Hyperkalemia:** Risk increased, particularly when combined with potassium-sparing diuretics or aldosterone antagonists. Also increased with ARB combination.
	- **Taste Disturbance (Dysgeusia), Rashes, Urticaria:** Rare.
	- Fatigue, minor G.I. symptoms.
	
	**Contraindications:**
	
	- **Bilateral Renal Artery Stenosis:** Significant risk of acute renal failure.
	- **History of Angioedema** (especially with previous ACE inhibitor use).
	- **Pregnancy:** ACE inhibitors are considered unsafe in pregnancy and should be avoided. (Note: This is a critical medical school exam point, although not explicitly listed as a contraindication within the provided sources' dedicated sections. Source lists 'drugs to be avoided' in pregnancy hypertension context).
	- **Severe Hypotension:** Avoid initiating if BP is very low.
	- **Existing Hyperkalemia.**
	- Intolerance (e.g., intractable cough).
	- **Severe Aortic Stenosis/Hypertrophic Obstructive Cardiomyopathy** (not mentioned in provided sources, but standard clinical contraindication).
2. A 50-year-old man visits his general practitioner for routine health screening. His blood pressure was 150/90 mm Hg. He has no other comorbidities. He was prescribed ramipril. Explain the mechanism of action of ramipril in this patient? Enumerate the therapeutic uses, advantages and adverse effects of ramipril.
	**Ramipril in a 50-year-old Male with Hypertension**
	
	**Patient Profile:**
	
	- 50-year-old male [Query]
	- BP: 150/90 mm Hg (Stage 1 Hypertension) [Query, 116]
	- No other comorbidities [Query]
	- Prescribed Ramipril [Query]
	
	**Clinical Relevance:**
	
	- Hypertension (BP ≥ 140/90 mm Hg) is a major risk factor for cardiovascular mortality and morbidity.
	- Lowering BP in patients with BP above 140/90 mm Hg is beneficial.
	- For patients <60 years, the target BP is <140/90 mm Hg.
	- For young (<55 years) non-black patients with essential hypertension (cause unknown), ACE inhibitors/ARBs are recommended as one of the first-line options for initiating therapy, as they often have higher plasma renin activity and respond better. Ramipril is an ACE inhibitor.
	
	**Mechanism of Action (Ramipril is an ACE Inhibitor):**
	
	- ACE inhibitors like ramipril act on the Renin-Angiotensin System (RAS).
	- RAS involves the conversion of Angiotensin I (Ang I) to Angiotensin II (Ang II) by the Angiotensin Converting Enzyme (ACE).
	- ACE inhibitors **block the conversion of Ang I to active Ang II**.
	- **Reduced Ang II levels lead to:**
	    - **Vasodilation:** Ang II is a potent vasoconstrictor (arterioles > venules) acting directly and by enhancing sympathetic outflow and responsiveness to noradrenaline (NA). Blocking its formation reduces peripheral resistance.
	    - **Decreased Aldosterone Secretion:** Ang II stimulates aldosterone release, which promotes sodium and water retention. Lower Ang II leads to decreased aldosterone and thus natriuresis and diuresis, reducing blood volume.
	    - **Reduced Sympathetic Activity:** Ang II enhances sympathetic activity centrally and peripherally. Inhibition reduces this influence.
	    - **Increased Kinins (Bradykinin):** ACE is also kininase II, which breaks down kinins (like bradykinin). Inhibiting ACE **increases the level of kinins**, which are vasodilators (via NO and PGs) and may contribute to beneficial effects.
	    - **Retardation/Prevention of Hypertrophy & Remodeling:** Ang II contributes to cardiac and vascular hypertrophy and remodeling. ACE inhibitors mitigate these changes.
	- In essential hypertension, RAS contributes to vascular tone in over 80% of cases, and its inhibition lowers BP.
	
	**Therapeutic Uses of ACE Inhibitors:**
	
	- **Essential Hypertension:** First-line drugs in various guidelines, effective alone or combined.
	- **Renovascular Hypertension:** Effective and first-choice drugs (except bilateral renal artery stenosis).
	- **Congestive Heart Failure (CHF):** Symptomatic and asymptomatic left ventricular dysfunction (LVD). Retard progression and prolong survival.
	- **Post-Myocardial Infarction (MI):** Reduce early and long-term mortality, especially in high-risk patients or those with LVD/CHF.
	- **High Cardiovascular Risk Subjects:** Prophylaxis to reduce cardiac death, MI, stroke, and risk of developing heart failure or diabetes (Ramipril shown effective in HOPE study).
	- **Diabetic Nephropathy / Non-diabetic CKD:** Reduce albuminuria and slow disease progression. Appropriate in diabetic hypertensives, reducing cardiovascular complications.
	- **Scleroderma Crisis:** Life-saving in this condition where Ang II mediates severe BP rise and renal deterioration.
	- _Note:_ ACE inhibitors are specifically recommended for patients with compelling indications like Heart Failure, Post-MI, High CAD risk, Diabetes, CKD, and Recurrent stroke prevention.
	
	**Advantages of ACE Inhibitors:**
	
	- High efficacy in lowering BP.
	- Effective in renovascular and resistant hypertension.
	- Improve endothelial function.
	- Retard diabetic nephropathy.
	- Regress left ventricular/vascular hypertrophy.
	- Afford symptomatic and disease-modifying benefits in CHF, prolonging survival.
	- Reduce mortality early and long-term post-MI.
	- Reduce cardiovascular events in high-risk patients.
	- Can be combined synergistically with diuretics and beta-blockers.
	- Generally well tolerated at therapeutic doses.
	
	**Adverse Effects of ACE Inhibitors:**
	
	- **Dry Persistent Cough:** Most common side effect, often requiring discontinuation. Likely related to increased kinin levels.
	- **Hypotension:** Can occur, especially with initial doses, in Na+ depleted states (e.g., diuretics, dietary restriction), or in conditions with high RAS activity like severe CHF. Dose initiation should be lower in these conditions.
	- **Angioedema, Urticaria, Taste Disturbance:** Rare, but possible.
	- **Hyperkalemia:** Can occur due to reduced aldosterone secretion.
	- **Acute Kidney Injury:** Risk increased in bilateral renal artery stenosis (ACE inhibition reduces glomerular filtration rate in this condition).
	- Side effects are generally similar across all ACE inhibitors.
3. Angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors (enalpril)-name two/four, therapeutic uses, two adverse effects and three/two contraindications giving reasons.
	
	**Angiotensin Converting Enzyme (ACE) Inhibitors**
	
	**Relevance to Patient:** Ramipril, prescribed to the 50-year-old male with hypertension (150/90 mm Hg), is an ACE inhibitor. ACE inhibitors are often first-line for hypertension, especially in younger non-black patients.
	
	**Examples of ACE Inhibitors:**
	
	- Enalapril
	- Captopril
	- Ramipril
	- Lisinopril
	- (Others include benazepril, fosinopril, quinapril, trandolapril, imidapril, perindopril).
	
	**Mechanism of Action (Recall from previous turn; class effect):**
	
	- Block the conversion of Angiotensin I to Angiotensin II by inhibiting ACE.
	- Reduced Ang II -> **Vasodilation** (decreased peripheral resistance), **Decreased Aldosterone** (natriuresis, diuresis), **Reduced Sympathetic Activity**, **Increased Kinins** (like bradykinin).
	- Contribute to lowering BP by inhibiting RAS activity, which maintains vascular tone in >80% of essential hypertension cases.
	- Retard/reverse cardiac and vascular hypertrophy and remodeling.
	
	**Therapeutic Uses:**
	
	- **Essential Hypertension:** First-line, effective alone or combined. Particularly suitable for **younger (<55 yrs) and white races**. Improve renal blood flow, retard diabetic nephropathy, regress LVH/vascular hypertrophy.
	- **Congestive Heart Failure (CHF):** **Sheet anchor** of therapy. Provide symptomatic and disease-modifying benefits. Cause arteriolar and venodilatation, reduce afterload and preload. **Retard progression of LVD** and **prolong survival** in all grades (I-IV). Recommended for symptomatic and asymptomatic LVD. Reduce mortality by ~20%. Titrate to near highest recommended doses for maximum protection.
	- **Post-Myocardial Infarction (MI):** Administered early (within 24hr) and continued reduce **early and long-term mortality**. Especially beneficial in high-risk patients, those with LVD/CHF, associated hypertension/diabetes. Reduce recurrent MI in UA/NSTEMI.
	- **High Cardiovascular Risk Subjects:** (e.g., history of MI, stroke, PVD) **Reduce major cardiovascular events** (cardiac death, MI, stroke) and risk of developing CHF or diabetes, even without hypertension or LVD (HOPE study with Ramipril). Protective effect independent of BP lowering.
	- **Diabetic Nephropathy / Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD):** **Prevent or delay end-stage renal disease** in Type I/II diabetics. Reduce albuminuria and slow progression in non-diabetic CKD. Reduce proteinuria. Recommended for **all patients with diabetic nephropathy**, whether hypertensive or normotensive. Retard retinopathy deterioration.
	
	**Adverse Effects (Two with reasons):**
	
	1. **Dry, Persistent Cough:** Most common side effect, often requires discontinuation. Likely due to **increased levels of kinins** (like bradykinin), as ACE is also kininase II which breaks down kinins. Interference with substance P degradation may also contribute.
	2. **Hypotension:** Especially marked with initial doses in Na+-depleted states (diuretics, dietary restriction) or conditions with high RAS activity (severe CHF). Starting at lower doses is necessary in these situations. Enalapril is less likely to cause abrupt first dose hypotension than captopril due to slower onset.
	    - _(Other potential adverse effects mentioned include angioedema, urticaria, taste disturbance, hyperkalemia, acute kidney injury)_.
	
	**Contraindications (Two with reasons):**
	
	3. **Bilateral Renal Artery Stenosis:** ACE inhibitors are first choice for renovascular hypertension _except_ this condition. Reason: ACE inhibition reduces glomerular filtration rate (GFR) in this specific situation, increasing the risk of **acute kidney injury**.
	4. **Severe Renal Impairment OR Significant Deterioration of Renal Function with use:** Use is contraindicated if renal function deteriorates. Reason: Risk of **acute kidney injury** and potentially **dangerous hyperkalemia**, especially when combined with potassium-sparing diuretics or potassium supplements.
	
	_(Note: While other conditions like previous angioedema are standard contraindications, they are not explicitly listed as such with reasons in the provided excerpts)._
4. Rationale of using angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors (ACE inhibitors) in heart failure?
	**Rationale of Using ACE Inhibitors in Heart Failure**
	
	**Clinical Relevance (Linking to Patient Profile):**
	
	- Our 50-year-old patient was prescribed Ramipril (an ACE inhibitor) for hypertension [Query].
	- Hypertension is a major risk factor for cardiovascular mortality and morbidity, including Congestive Heart Failure (CHF) [Query, 116].
	- ACE inhibitors are used both to treat hypertension and to prevent/manage its complications, including heart failure.
	
	**Pathophysiology of Heart Failure & Role of RAS:**
	
	- In heart failure, reduced cardiac output leads to compensatory neurohumoral activation, including the **Renin-Angiotensin System (RAS)**.
	- **RAS activation is pivotal to the development of symptoms and disease progression in heart failure**.
	- Key components of the RAS pathway:
	    - Renin (from kidney) acts on Angiotensinogen to produce Angiotensin I (Ang I).
	    - Angiotensin Converting Enzyme (ACE) converts Ang I to Angiotensin II (Ang II).
	    - Ang II then exerts multiple effects.
	- **Adverse effects of chronic RAS activation (mediated by Ang II) in heart failure:**
	    - **Vasoconstriction:** Increases peripheral resistance (afterload), making it harder for the failing heart to pump. Constriction of venous side (venules) increases preload, contributing to congestion.
	    - **Aldosterone Secretion:** Ang II stimulates aldosterone, leading to sodium and water retention, increasing blood volume, preload, and contributing to edema and congestion. This also contributes to potassium and hydrogen excretion, risking hypokalemia and alkalosis.
	    - **Sympathetic Activation:** Ang II enhances sympathetic outflow (central and peripheral). Increased sympathetic activity leads to vasoconstriction, increased heart rate, and contractility (initially compensatory, but ultimately detrimental long-term).
	    - **Cardiac and Vascular Remodeling:** Ang II directly promotes **myocardial cell growth, fibrosis, apoptosis, and abnormal remodeling** in the heart (ventricular hypertrophy, dilation, fibrosis of infarcted area) and blood vessels (wall thickening). These structural changes worsen systolic dysfunction and contribute to disease progression. Aldosterone also contributes to myocardial fibrosis independent of sodium retention.
	    - **Increased Workload:** Increased preload and afterload increase cardiac work.
	
	**Mechanism of Benefit (Rationale for Use):**
	
	- ACE inhibitors are the **sheet anchor of drug therapy in CHF**.
	- They **block the conversion of Ang I to Ang II** by inhibiting ACE.
	- By **reducing Ang II levels** and activity, ACE inhibitors counteract the detrimental effects of RAS activation:
	    - **Vasodilation:** Cause arteriolar and venodilatation, **reducing afterload and preload** on the heart. This improves haemodynamic parameters, increases stroke volume and cardiac output.
	    - **Decreased Aldosterone:** Reduced aldosterone release leads to **natriuresis and diuresis**, reducing blood volume, clearing edema, and reducing congestion. Prevents potassium loss.
	    - **Reduced Sympathetic Activity:** Attenuation of Ang II-mediated sympathetic enhancement.
	    - **Retard/Prevent Remodeling:** **Retard/reverse ventricular hypertrophy, myocardial cell apoptosis, fibrosis, and remodeling**. This disease-modifying benefit is crucial.
	    - **Increased Kinins:** ACE inhibition leads to **increased levels of kinins** (like bradykinin), which stimulate generation of cardioprotective NO and PGs, contributing to vasodilation and potential cardioprotective effects.
	
	**Therapeutic Uses & Benefits in Heart Failure:**
	
	- **Symptomatic Relief:** Improve all haemodynamic parameters, leading to improved exercise capacity, reduced dyspnoea, clearing of edema, and restoration of cardiac compensation. Improve NYHA functional class.
	- **Disease Modification & Survival:** **Retard the progression of left ventricular systolic dysfunction**. **Prolong survival** in CHF patients of all grades (I to IV) with reduced EF. Reduce mortality by ~20% in symptomatic CHF patients.
	- **Asymptomatic LVD:** Prevent progression to overt heart failure even in asymptomatic cases.
	- **Post-MI:** Effective in reducing development of ventricular dysfunction, heart failure, and related mortality in post-MI patients, especially those with LVD/CHF. Administered early (within 24hr) and continued.
	- **High Cardiovascular Risk:** Reduce risk of developing heart failure in high-risk patients even without existing LVD or hypertension.
	- **Combination Therapy:** Used as **add-on therapy** to other drugs like diuretics, beta-blockers, and aldosterone antagonists in moderate to severe CHF. ACE inhibitors/ARBs + beta-blockers + aldosterone antagonists + possibly sacubitril/valsartan is the current standard of care for many.
	- **Diastolic Dysfunction:** Have **no prognostic benefit or improved outcome** in heart failure patients with preserved EF (diastolic dysfunction).
	
	**Dosing:**
	
	- ACE inhibitor therapy in heart failure is generally started at **low doses** to avoid initial hypotension and gradually increased to near the highest recommended doses for maximum benefit and protection.
	
	**Contraindications:**
	
	- Severe renal impairment or significant deterioration of renal function with use (risk of hyperkalemia and acute kidney injury) [Contraindications section in previous turn].
	- Bilateral renal artery stenosis (risk of acute kidney injury) [Contraindications section in previous turn, 119].
	- History of angioedema (not explicitly stated with reason in sources, but a standard contraindication - _information outside sources_).
