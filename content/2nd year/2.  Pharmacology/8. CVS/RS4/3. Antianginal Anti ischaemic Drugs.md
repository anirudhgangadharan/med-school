1. Organic nitrates (glyceryl trinitrate/ nitroglycerine)- pharmacological actions, mechanism of action, adverse effects and uses
	**Organic Nitrates (Glyceryl Trinitrate/Nitroglycerine)**
	
	Organic nitrates are a class of drugs that are essential in cardiovascular pharmacology, primarily used for their vasodilatory properties. Glyceryl trinitrate (GTN), also known as nitroglycerine, is a prototype drug in this class.
	
	### Pharmacological Actions
	
	GTN and other organic nitrates primarily affect smooth muscles, particularly those of blood vessels:
	
	- **Vascular Smooth Muscle Relaxation:**
	    - **Venodilatation (Predominant Effect):** Nitrates cause significant pooling of blood in systemic capacitance vessels. This **reduces ventricular end-diastolic pressure and volume (preload)**, thereby decreasing cardiac work and myocardial oxygen demand. This action is crucial in relieving angina and acute left ventricular failure.
	    - **Arteriolar Dilatation:** They also dilate resistance vessels, leading to a **reduction in peripheral vascular resistance (PVR) and aortic impedance (afterload)**. This allows the heart to pump blood more easily, improving cardiac output and tissue perfusion.
	    - **Coronary Artery Dilatation:** Nitrates dilate large coronary vessels and collateral vessels, which can **increase blood flow to ischaemic areas** by redistributing coronary blood flow. This is particularly beneficial in variant angina by counteracting coronary spasm. However, they do not significantly increase total coronary flow in angina patients.
	- **No Direct Cardiac Action:** Nitrates have **no direct stimulant or depressant action on the heart** itself.
	- **Other Smooth Muscles:** They mildly relax smooth muscles of the bronchi, oesophagus, and biliary tract. Effects on the intestine, ureter, and uterus are variable and generally insignificant.
	- **Platelets:** Nitrates produce a **mild anti-aggregatory effect**.
	
	### Mechanism of Action
	
	Organic nitrates are **prodrugs** that exert their effects by releasing nitric oxide (NO). The process involves:
	
	1. **NO Generation:** Organic nitrates are **enzymatically denitrated** within the smooth muscle cells to release the reactive free radical **nitric oxide (NO)**. For GTN, the most important enzyme involved is mitochondrial aldehyde dehydrogenase, which is abundantly expressed in veins, explaining GTN's preferential venodilator action.
	2. **Guanylyl Cyclase Activation:** The released NO then **activates soluble guanylyl cyclase (sGC)** in the cytosol of smooth muscle cells.
	3. **cGMP Production:** Activation of sGC leads to a **rise in cytosolic cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP)** concentration.
	4. **Protein Kinase G Activation:** Increased cGMP activates **cGMP-dependent protein kinase (PKG)**.
	5. **MLCK Dephosphorylation:** PKG dephosphorylates the **myosin light chain kinase (MLCK)**, reducing the availability of its active (phosphorylated) form.
	6. **Muscle Relaxation:** This interference with actin-myosin interaction ultimately results in **smooth muscle relaxation**.
	
	### Adverse Effects
	
	Most adverse effects of nitrates are directly related to their vasodilatory actions:
	
	- **Headache:** A common and often throbbing headache occurs, especially over the face and neck, due to the dilation of meningeal blood vessels. Tolerance to this effect may develop with continued use.
	- **Postural Hypotension:** Can lead to dizziness, weakness, sweating, palpitation, and even fainting. This is exacerbated by standing still or alcohol consumption.
	- **Reflex Tachycardia:** Due to the fall in blood pressure, the body's sympathetic nervous system may react, causing an increase in heart rate.
	- **Flushing:** Particularly in the face and neck area.
	- **Methaemoglobinaemia:** A rare but serious complication, especially with large doses, where haemoglobin is converted to methaemoglobin, reducing the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity.
	- **Rashes:** Infrequent, but more common with pentaerythritol tetranitrate.
	- **Nitrate Tolerance:** Occurs with continuous presence of the drug in the body, leading to attenuation of its haemodynamic and anti-ischaemic effects. This is believed to be due to reactive oxygen species inhibiting mitochondrial aldehyde dehydrogenase, which is crucial for NO generation.
	    - **Cross-tolerance** occurs among all nitrates.
	    - **Prevention:** The most practical way to prevent tolerance is to provide **nitrate-free intervals (e.g., 6-8 hours daily)**.
	- **Dependence:** Sudden withdrawal after prolonged exposure can lead to coronary and peripheral vasospasm, myocardial infarction, and even sudden death. Withdrawal should be gradual.
	- **Drug Interactions:**
	    - **PDE-5 Inhibitors (e.g., Sildenafil):** Concurrent use can cause **dangerous potentiation of nitrate action, leading to severe hypotension, myocardial infarction, and fatalities**. Nitrates are contraindicated for 24 hours after sildenafil intake.
	    - Other vasodilators may cause additive hypotension.
	
	### Uses (Clinical Relevance)
	
	Nitrates have various important clinical applications:
	
	- **Angina Pectoris:**
	    - **Acute Attack:** **Sublingual GTN tablet or spray** (e.g., 0.5 mg) is the **drug of choice** for rapid relief, acting within 1-2 minutes. The patient is advised to spit out the tablet once pain is relieved to minimize side effects. Isosorbide dinitrate can also be used sublingually.
	    - **Chronic Prophylaxis:** Longer-acting oral formulations (e.g., isosorbide mononitrate) or transdermal GTN patches are used on a regular schedule to decrease the frequency of attacks and improve exercise tolerance. A nitrate-free interval is crucial to prevent tolerance.
	- **Acute Coronary Syndromes (ACS):** Including unstable angina (UA) and non-ST segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). GTN, initially sublingual, then by **intravenous infusion** if pain persists, is used to relieve pain and reduce myocardial stress. While they relieve pain, their impact on mortality is not robust.
	- **Myocardial Infarction (MI):** IV GTN is often used during evolving MI to relieve chest pain, pulmonary congestion, and potentially limit infarct size by reducing preload. However, it should be avoided if systolic BP is < 90 mmHg, heart rate is abnormal, or right ventricular infarction is suspected.
	- **Congestive Heart Failure (CHF)/Acute Left Ventricular Failure (LVF):** Intravenous GTN provides rapid relief in acute LVF, especially with raised central venous pressure and dilated cardiomyopathy, by causing venous pooling of blood and reducing preload.
	- **Hypertensive Emergencies:** IV infusion of GTN acts rapidly and can lower blood pressure, but it is not as potent as some other agents and may need to be combined with other antihypertensives for substantial effects. It is particularly suitable for hypertension associated with MI or ACS and acute heart failure.
	- **Biliary Colic and Esophageal Spasm:** Sublingual GTN can promptly relieve pain by relaxing smooth muscles.
	- **Cyanide Poisoning:** Nitrates and nitrites (like sodium nitrite) are used as part of the treatment to generate methaemoglobin, which can bind to cyanide, forming a less toxic complex (cyanomethaemoglobin). Sodium thiosulfate is then given to convert this to thiocyanate for excretion.
	1. Nitrates- non cardiac uses
	Nitrates, widely recognised for their effects on the cardiovascular system, also exert several non-cardiac actions primarily due to their **smooth muscle relaxant properties**. This effect is mediated by the **release of nitric oxide (NO)**, which activates soluble guanylyl cyclase, increasing cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP), and ultimately leading to smooth muscle relaxation.
	
	**Key Non-Cardiac Therapeutic Applications:**
	
	- **Biliary Colic:**
	    
	    - Nitrates are effective in **relieving spasm of the biliary tract** and associated pain.
	    - **Sublingual glyceryl trinitrate (GTN)** or **isosorbide dinitrate** can be used.
	- **Oesophageal Spasm and Achalasia:**
	    
	    - They provide **prompt symptomatic relief** in cases of oesophageal spasm.
	    - In oesophageal achalasia, nitrates taken before a meal can **facilitate feeding by reducing oesophageal tone**.
	- **Cyanide Poisoning:**
	    
	    - Nitrates and nitrites (e.g., **amyl nitrite**, **sodium nitrite**) are crucial antidotes for cyanide poisoning.
	    - **Mechanism:** They function by converting **haemoglobin into methaemoglobin**. This methaemoglobin then has a **high affinity for cyanide ions (CN-)**, forming a non-toxic complex called **cyanomethaemoglobin**. This action effectively **protects vital cellular oxidative enzymes** (like cytochrome oxidase) from cyanide's inhibitory effects.
	    - **Combined Therapy:** To ensure complete detoxification, **sodium thiosulphate** is administered alongside, as it converts cyanomethaemoglobin into **thiocyanate**, which is then slowly excreted in the urine.
	    - **Clinical Relevance:** Prompt administration is critical for efficacy.
	- **Bronchial Smooth Muscle Relaxation:**
	    
	    - Nitrates can induce **mild relaxation of bronchial smooth muscles**. However, this effect is **not pronounced enough** for them to be considered primary bronchodilators in the management of conditions like asthma.
	- **Effects on Other Smooth Muscles:**
	    
	    - Their effects on the **intestine, ureter, and uterus** are generally **variable and clinically insignificant**.
2. Calcium channel blockers-non cardiac uses and adverse effects
	**Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs): Non-Cardiac Uses & Adverse Effects**
	
	CCBs primarily block voltage-sensitive **L-type Ca2+ channels**, which are abundant in cardiac and smooth muscle cells. While widely known for their cardiovascular indications (angina, hypertension, arrhythmias), their smooth muscle relaxant and other properties provide several crucial non-cardiac applications and a distinct set of adverse effects.
	
	### Non-Cardiac Uses
	
	- **Migraine Prophylaxis:**
	    - **Verapamil** and **flunarizine** are used to reduce the frequency of migraine attacks.
	    - Flunarizine specifically **selectively dilates cerebral calcium channels** and also has Na+ channel-blocking effects.
	- **Cerebral Vasospasm:**
	    - **Nimodipine** is highly lipid-soluble, allowing it to **freely cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB)**.
	    - It **selectively dilates cerebral blood vessels** and is used to **prevent cerebral vasospasm** and subsequent neurological deficits in patients with subarachnoid haemorrhage.
	- **Peripheral Vascular Disease (PVD):**
	    - CCBs are **effective** in PVD, particularly when **vasoconstriction is a prominent feature** (e.g., Raynaud's phenomenon, acrocyanosis).
	    - They are **preferred by some** due to fewer side effects compared to α-blockers.
	    - CCBs can be **safely given** to patients with obstructive lung disease and peripheral vascular disease where β-blockers are contraindicated.
	- **Diabetic Nephropathy:**
	    - CCBs **decrease albuminuria** and can **slow the progression of nephropathy in diabetes patients**.
	- **Tocolytic (to postpone labour):**
	    - **Nifedipine** acts by **inhibiting the influx of Ca2+ ions into myometrial cells**, thereby relaxing uterine smooth muscle. It is now preferred over sympathomimetics for this use.
	- **General Hypertensive Management (especially in specific populations):**
	    - Particularly useful in **elderly patients** and those with **renal dysfunction**.
	
	### Adverse Effects
	
	The adverse effects of CCBs are largely related to their vasodilatory actions and vary between the different classes (Dihydropyridines vs. Phenylalkylamines/Benzothiazepines).
	
	- **Common Dihydropyridine (DHPs) Adverse Effects (e.g., Nifedipine, Amlodipine):**
	    - **Headache**.
	    - **Flushing**.
	    - **Ankle oedema**. This effect is less pronounced with cilnidipine, which blocks N-type calcium channels in addition to L-type.
	    - **Palpitation**.
	    - **Reflex tachycardia**. This can be minimized by using sustained-release preparations or by co-administering a β-blocker. Cilnidipine is also claimed not to cause reflex tachycardia.
	    - Fatigue, dizziness, and sedation.
	    - **Short-acting DHPs** (e.g., immediate-release nifedipine) may **aggravate myocardial ischaemia** and have been linked to increased mortality in MI patients due to reflex tachycardia and potential for **coronary steal**.
	- **Common Phenylalkylamine (Verapamil) and Benzothiazepine (Diltiazem) Adverse Effects:**
	    - **Constipation** (especially with verapamil).
	    - **Sinus bradycardia**.
	    - **Oedema**.
	    - May **precipitate congestive heart failure (CHF)** in patients with low cardiac reserve due to their cardiac depressant effects.
	    - **AV block** (occurs rarely).
	    - Headache and hypotension can occur but are generally less marked compared to DHPs.
	    - Should be **avoided in patients with pre-existing sinus, AV nodal, or myocardial disease**.
	    - Can **increase plasma digoxin levels**.
	- **General Considerations for All CCBs:**
	    - Most CCBs are well absorbed orally, highly bound to plasma proteins, extensively metabolized in the liver, and excreted in urine.
	    - The problem of rebound worsening of angina on abrupt withdrawal after chronic use is less marked with CCBs compared to β-blockers.
