1. Aminoglycoside antibiotics-shared properties, toxicities and adverse effects
	**AMINOGLYCOSIDE ANTIBIOTICS**
	
	**Shared Properties:**
	
	- **Origin/Class:** A group of natural (e.g., Streptomycin from _Streptomyces griseus_) and semisynthetic antibiotics. Produced by soil actinomycetes.
	- **Mechanism of Action (Bactericidal):**
	    - Require **energy-dependent transport** into the bacterial cell. This transport can be impaired in anaerobic environments.
	    - Bind **irreversibly** to the **30S ribosomal subunit** [56, Previous conversation notes].
	    - Inhibit **initiation complex** formation [56, Previous conversation notes].
	    - Cause **misreading of mRNA code**, leading to the production of abnormal, non-functional proteins [56, Previous conversation notes].
	    - Block **translocation** [Commonly taught alongside misreading, although not explicitly detailed in this specific source excerpt].
	    - Cidal action likely involves the incorporation of defective proteins into the cell membrane, disrupting integrity. This damage may augment further antibiotic entry.
	- **Pharmacokinetics:**
	    - Highly **ionized**, poorly absorbed orally. Must be given **parenterally (IM or IV)** for systemic infections.
	    - Distributed **extracellularly**. Poor penetration into CSF (unless meninges inflamed - noted for vancomycin, less reliable for general aminoglycosides), eye, prostate, or intracellularly (except high levels in renal cortex and endolymph).
	    - Eliminated **unchanged** primarily by **glomerular filtration**.
	    - Have a relatively **short plasma t½** (2-4 hours).
	    - Exhibit **concentration-dependent killing** [18, 61, Previous conversation notes]. Efficacy correlates with the ratio of peak concentration to MIC.
	    - Show a significant **Post-Antibiotic Effect (PAE)** [18, 61, Previous conversation notes].
	    - **Single daily dosing** (extended interval dosing) is often preferred in patients with normal renal function based on concentration-dependent killing/PAE and potentially reduced nephrotoxicity due to drug washout from kidney/ear during trough periods. Divided doses are conventional but may be less effective for cidal action. Single daily dosing safety not established in renal insufficiency or children. Not for synergistic cidal effect (e.g., endocarditis combination therapy).
	- **Spectrum:** Primarily active against **aerobic Gram-negative bacilli**. Synergy with cell-wall active agents (beta-lactams, vancomycin) against certain gram-positive organisms like Enterococci or _Pseudomonas_ is noted.
	- **Resistance:** Mechanisms include decreased ribosomal affinity (mutation) and decreased transport efficiency. Acquisition of resistance to one may not extend to others (incomplete cross-resistance).
	
	**Shared Toxicities & Adverse Effects:**
	
	- **General:** Aminoglycosides exhibit **dose-related and predictable** organ toxicities. They have a **low therapeutic index**, limiting their use to serious infections.
	- **Nephrotoxicity:**
	    - Damage to **renal tubules** (Acute Tubular Necrosis - ATN) [59, Previous conversation notes].
	    - Drug concentrates in the **renal cortex** [59, 60, Previous conversation notes].
	    - Related to the **total drug exposure (AUC)** [59, Previous conversation notes].
	    - Manifestations: Decreased GFR, nitrogen retention, albuminuria, casts.
	    - Risk Factors: **Pre-existing renal disease**, increasing **age (>60 years)**, prolonged therapy, concurrent nephrotoxic drugs (e.g., vancomycin, some cephalosporins [Previous conversation notes, 90]), high dose.
	    - Generally **reversible** upon discontinuation.
	    - Requires **dose adjustment** in renal insufficiency. Monitoring renal function is essential [Previous conversation notes].
	    - Streptomycin is noted as the **least nephrotoxic**. Single daily dosing may be less nephrotoxic than divided doses in patients with normal renal function.
	- **Ototoxicity:**
	    - Damage to the **VIII cranial nerve** (vestibular and cochlear branches) [59, Previous conversation notes].
	    - Drug concentrates in the **endolymph** of the inner ear [60, Previous conversation notes].
	    - Manifestations: Can be **vestibular** (vertigo, nystagmus, ataxia) or **auditory** (hearing loss, tinnitus) [59, Previous conversation notes]. Propensity differs among agents (Gentamicin often more vestibulotoxic, Amikacin/Kanamycin/Neomycin often more audiotoxic) [Previous conversation notes, 66, 68].
	    - Can be **irreversible** [Previous conversation notes].
	    - Risk factors: High dose, prolonged therapy, **renal failure** (leading to higher/persistent levels) [59, 60, Previous conversation notes], concurrent ototoxic drugs (e.g., loop diuretics [Previous conversation notes, 97], vancomycin), increasing age.
	    - **Contraindicated in pregnancy** due to risk of **foetal ear damage**.
	    - No dosing regimen appears to be less ototoxic than another.
	- **Neuromuscular Blockade:**
	    - Rare but potentially serious [59, Previous conversation notes].
	    - Reduce **acetylcholine (ACh) release** and decrease sensitivity of muscle end-plates to ACh [59, Previous conversation notes].
	    - Risk increased by **rapid IV infusion**, **instillation into cavities** (e.g., peritoneal/pleural after surgery, especially if residual neuromuscular blockers used), or use with other neuromuscular blocking agents [59, Previous conversation notes].
	    - Can cause **apnoea** [59, Previous conversation notes].
	- **Other:**
	    - Pain at injection site (especially IM) can occur.
	    - Superinfections are not generally significant compared to broad-spectrum agents.
	    - General systemic toxicity mentioned.
	
	**Clinical Relevance:** Due to their toxicity, aminoglycosides are generally **reserved for serious infections** caused by susceptible aerobic Gram-negative bacteria, particularly when less toxic agents are ineffective or contraindicated. Their use often requires therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) of serum levels (peak and trough) to optimize efficacy and minimize toxicity, especially nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity [Previous conversation notes, 50]. Dose adjustments are crucial in patients with impaired renal function. Their synergistic activity with cell-wall agents makes them valuable in combinations for specific challenging infections.

	**GENTAMICIN: MECHANISM & ADVERSE EFFECTS**
	
	Gentamicin is an **aminoglycoside antibiotic**.
	
	**1. Mechanism of Action (Bactericidal):**
	
	- **Entry:** Requires **energy-dependent transport** into the bacterial cell [5, 6, Previous conversation notes]. This process is less efficient in acidic or anaerobic environments.
	- **Ribosomal Binding:** Binds **irreversibly** to the **30S ribosomal subunit** of bacteria [56, Previous conversation notes].
	- **Protein Synthesis Inhibition (Multifaceted):**
	    - Inhibits the formation of the bacterial **initiation complex** (mRNA, tRNA, 30S subunit) [56, Previous conversation notes].
	    - Causes **misreading of the mRNA code**, leading to the production of abnormal or non-functional proteins [56, Previous conversation notes].
	    - Blocks **translocation** (movement of the ribosome along mRNA) [Commonly taught alongside misreading, though not explicitly detailed in these sources].
	- **Cidal Effect:** The production and incorporation of defective proteins into the cell membrane likely disrupts bacterial cell integrity, enhancing further drug uptake and leading to cell death [Mechanism inferred from cidal action and defective protein incorporation, not explicitly stated as the cidal mechanism in the sources].
	- **Pharmacokinetic Action:** Exhibits **concentration-dependent killing**, meaning the killing effect is enhanced by higher peak concentrations [11, Previous conversation notes]. Also has a significant **Post-Antibiotic Effect (PAE)** [Inferred from concentration-dependent killing drugs like gentamicin having PAE, 11, Previous conversation notes]. Efficacy correlates with the ratio of peak concentration to the Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) [11, Previous conversation notes]. Single daily dosing can achieve higher peak concentrations and is often preferred in patients with normal renal function [11, Previous conversation notes].
	- **Spectrum:** Primarily active against **aerobic Gram-negative bacilli**, including _E. coli_. Effective against most urinary pathogens including _Pseudomonas_. Not effective against _M. tuberculosis_.
	- **Activity in Urine:** Gentamicin acts better in **alkaline urine**.
	
	**2. Adverse Effects of Gentamicin (Shared Aminoglycoside Toxicities):**
	
	- Aminoglycosides have a **low therapeutic index** and exhibit **dose-related and predictable** toxicities [Inferred from need for precise dosing and monitoring].
	- **Nephrotoxicity:**
	    - Damage to the **renal tubules** (often Acute Tubular Necrosis - ATN) [59, Previous conversation notes].
	    - The drug concentrates heavily in the **renal cortex** [59, 60, Previous conversation notes].
	    - Risk is related to **total drug exposure (AUC)** and duration of therapy [59, Previous conversation notes].
	    - Risk factors include **pre-existing renal disease**, increasing **age (>60 years)** [5, 59, Previous conversation notes], prolonged therapy, and concurrent use of other nephrotoxic drugs (e.g., vancomycin, some cephalosporins [92, although primary source link is re: Amphotericin B]).
	    - Requires **dose adjustment** in renal insufficiency based on creatinine clearance (CLcr) [48, 57, Previous conversation notes]. Guidelines exist for dose reduction based on CLcr.
	    - Generally **reversible** upon drug discontinuation [Common knowledge, not explicitly in sources but implied by monitoring/adjustment].
	    - **Avoided in UTI** when the patient has **renal impairment** if possible.
	- **Ototoxicity:**
	    - Damage to the **VIII cranial nerve** (vestibular and/or auditory branches) [59, Previous conversation notes].
	    - The drug concentrates in the **endolymph** of the inner ear [60, Previous conversation notes].
	    - Can manifest as **vestibular dysfunction** (vertigo, nystagmus, ataxia) or **auditory dysfunction** (hearing loss, tinnitus) [59, Previous conversation notes]. Gentamicin is often considered more **vestibulotoxic** [Previous conversation notes].
	    - Can be **irreversible** [Previous conversation notes, implied by foetal damage risk].
	    - Risk factors include high dose, prolonged therapy, **renal failure** (leading to persistent high levels) [59, 60, Previous conversation notes], increasing age [5, 59, Previous conversation notes], and concurrent ototoxic drugs (e.g., loop diuretics [97, Previous conversation notes], vancomycin).
	    - **Contraindicated in pregnancy** due to the risk of **foetal ear damage**.
	- **Neuromuscular Blockade:**
	    - A rare but serious adverse effect [59, Previous conversation notes].
	    - Occurs due to reduced **acetylcholine release** at the neuromuscular junction and decreased sensitivity of the muscle end-plate to ACh [59, Previous conversation notes].
	    - Risk is increased by **rapid IV infusion**, instillation into body cavities (e.g., peritoneal, pleural), or concurrent use of other neuromuscular blocking agents [59, Previous conversation notes].
	    - Can potentially cause **apnoea** [59, Previous conversation notes].
	- **Other:**
	    - **Pain at the injection site** can occur, especially with intramuscular administration.
	    - **Superinfections** are **not generally significant** compared to broad-spectrum agents. _C. difficile_ pseudomembranous enterocolitis has been associated with aminoglycosides.
	
	**Clinical Relevance (UTI context):**
	
	- Gentamicin is effective for treating _E. coli_ UTI when sensitivity is confirmed.
	- It must be administered **parenterally** (IM or IV) because it is poorly absorbed orally.
	- Used in more severe UTIs like acute pyelonephritis, often in combination with a β-lactam like amoxicillin-clavulanate, especially for empirical treatment before sensitivity results are available.
	- Monitoring of **renal function** is crucial, and dose adjustment is required in patients with impaired kidney function [48, 57, Previous conversation notes]. Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) of serum levels (peak and trough) is often performed to optimize efficacy and minimize toxicity [Previous conversation notes].
3. Advantages of Amikacin
	**AMINOGLYCOSIDES - ADVANTAGES OF AMIKACIN**
	
	- **Resistance to Modifying Enzymes:**
	    - The **outstanding feature of amikacin** is its **resistance to bacterial aminoglycoside modifying enzymes**.
	    - This confers activity against many bacteria resistant to gentamicin and tobramycin.
	- **Widest Spectrum:**
	    - Amikacin has the **widest spectrum of activity** among the classical aminoglycosides, including **many organisms resistant to other aminoglycosides**.
	- **Use in Resistant Infections:**
	    - Recommended as a **reserve drug** for empirical treatment of **hospital acquired gram-negative bacillary infections where gentamicin/tobramycin resistance is high**.
	    - Many streptomycin resistant and **Multidrug-Resistant (MDR) strains of _M. tuberculosis_ remain sensitive to Amikacin**. It is mostly included in the regimen for **MDR-TB** during the intensive phase.
	- **Toxicity Profile (relative):**
	    - Compared to some other aminoglycosides (like Streptomycin or Gentamicin), Amikacin typically causes **more hearing loss than vestibular disturbance** in cases of ototoxicity.
	- **Pharmacokinetic/Dynamic Benefit:**
	    - Like other aminoglycosides, Amikacin exhibits **concentration-dependent bactericidal action** and a significant **Post-Antibiotic Effect (PAE)**. Higher peak concentrations achieved with single daily dosing can be more effective than divided doses.
	
	**Clinical Relevance:** Amikacin's resistance to enzymatic inactivation makes it a crucial agent for treating severe infections caused by Gram-negative bacteria that have developed resistance to more commonly used aminoglycosides like gentamicin or tobramycin. Its activity against resistant _M. tuberculosis_ strains is vital in managing MDR-TB. It is generally reserved for these specific indications due to the overall toxicity profile shared with other aminoglycosides.
