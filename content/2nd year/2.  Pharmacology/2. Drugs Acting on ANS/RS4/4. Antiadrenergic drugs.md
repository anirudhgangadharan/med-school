1. Alpha blockers/ prazoin (mechanism of action uses and adverse effects)
	
	### **Alpha Blockers: Prazosin (Mechanism, Uses, Adverse Effects)**
	
	**I. Mechanism of Action:**
	
	- **Prazosin** is a **highly selective α1 adrenergic receptor antagonist**.
	    - Its α1:α2 selectivity ratio is approximately 1000:1.
	    - It blocks all subtypes of α1 receptors (α1A, α1B, α1D) equally.
	- **Primary Effect:** Blocks sympathetically mediated **vasoconstriction**.
	- **Haemodynamic Changes:**
	    - Reduces **total peripheral resistance (TPR)** and mean **blood pressure (BP)**.
	    - Causes a minor decrease in venous return and cardiac output (CO).
	    - Dilates arterioles more than veins.
	    - **Crucially, unlike non-selective α blockers, it causes only mild reflex tachycardia**. This is because it does **not** block presynaptic α2 receptors, thus allowing autoregulation of noradrenaline (NA) release to remain intact, preventing an increase in NA release.
	- **Other Actions:**
	    - Inhibits phosphodiesterase, which may contribute to its vasodilator action by increasing smooth muscle cAMP.
	    - Decreases central sympathetic tone.
	
	**II. Therapeutic Uses:**
	
	- **Hypertension:**
	    - Prazosin is a **moderately potent antihypertensive**.
	    - While historically a first-line drug, it's **not currently recommended as a first-line monotherapy** due to potential fluid retention and tolerance development, necessitating dose increases and increasing the risk of side effects and CHF.
	    - More commonly used as an **add-on drug** to a diuretic and/or β-blocker when target BP is not achieved.
	- **Benign Hypertrophy of Prostate (BHP):**
	    - Prazosin **blocks α1 receptors in the bladder trigone and prostatic smooth muscle**.
	    - This action **improves urine flow** and **reduces residual urine** in the bladder in patients with BPH.
	    - Newer, longer-acting α1 blockers (e.g., terazosin, doxazosin, alfuzosin, tamsulosin) are often preferred for BHP due to once-daily dosing and potential apoptosis-promoting effects on the prostate.
	- **Raynaud's Phenomenon/Peripheral Vascular Diseases:**
	    - Affords **good symptomatic relief** when vasoconstriction is a prominent feature, such as in Raynaud's phenomenon or acrocyanosis.
	- **Pheochromocytoma (General Alpha Blockers):**
	    - While prazosin is an alternative, **phenoxybenzamine** (an irreversible α blocker) is often used for definitive therapy in inoperable/malignant cases, and **pre-operatively** to normalize blood volume and prevent marked BP rises during surgery.
	    - **Phentolamine** (a rapidly acting, short-duration α blocker) is used for diagnosis and intraoperative management of pheochromocytoma, and for local infiltration to counteract vasoconstriction from extravasated NA/DA.
	
	**III. Adverse Effects & Precautions:**
	
	- **"First-Dose Effect" (Syncope):**
	    - Marked **postural hypotension** and fainting can occur, particularly with the first dose or rapid dose escalation.
	    - **Minimised by starting with a low dose (e.g., 0.5-1 mg) at bedtime**. Tolerance to this effect develops with continued treatment due to haemodynamic adjustments.
	- **Other Common Side Effects:**
	    - **Headache, drowsiness, dry mouth, weakness, palpitation, nasal blockade, blurred vision, and rash**.
	    - **Impaired ejaculation** in males, especially with higher doses.
	    - Increased intestinal motility (less common, but alpha blockers can cause loose motion due to partial inhibition of relaxant sympathetic influences).
	    - **Miosis** (due to α receptor blockade in radial muscles of iris).
	- **Fluid Retention:**
	    - **Prazosin monotherapy can cause fluid retention**, which may **precipitate Congestive Heart Failure (CHF)**. This is due to reduced renal blood flow and increased Na+ and water reabsorption in tubules, reinforced by reflex increase in renin release. This is why it's not a first-line monotherapy.
	    - Doxazosin monotherapy has been linked to a **doubled incidence of CHF** and a higher stroke risk compared to thiazide diuretics.
	- **Contraindications/Cautions:**
	    - Although not an absolute contraindication, caution is advised in patients at risk of fluid retention or heart failure, especially when used as monotherapy.
	    - Prazosin does not impair carbohydrate tolerance and may have a small favourable effect on lipid profile.
	    - The plasma half-life of prazosin is 2-3 hours, but its effect lasts 6-8 hours, necessitating multiple daily doses. Longer-acting congeners (terazosin, doxazosin) allow once-daily dosing.
2. Beta blockers (Propanolol) uses, adverse effects and contraindications
	### **Propranolol (A Non-Selective Beta-Adrenergic Blocker)**
	
	**I. Mechanism of Action & Properties:**
	
	- **Non-selective $\beta$-adrenergic antagonist** blocking $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ receptors, with weak $\beta_3$ activity.
	- Acts as an **inverse agonist**, reducing resting heart rate.
	- At high doses, exerts **direct membrane stabilizing** (quinidine-like) action.
	- Highly **lipid-soluble**, penetrates the brain relatively well.
	
	**II. Pharmacological Actions:**
	
	- **Cardiovascular System (CVS)**:
	    - **Heart**: Decreases **heart rate (HR)**, **force of contraction**, and **cardiac output (CO)**. Prolongs systole and retards conduction. Reduces **cardiac work** and **oxygen consumption**. Improves $\text{O}_2$ supply/demand in angina. Decreases automaticity of ectopic foci. Slows A-V conduction, abbreviates refractory period.
	    - **Blood Vessels**: Blocks vasodilation mediated by $\beta_2$ receptors. No direct acute effect on BP, but prolonged use lowers BP in hypertensives.
	    - **Antihypertensive Mechanisms** (long-term): Reduced Noradrenaline (NA) release from sympathetic terminals, decreased renin release from kidney ($\beta_1$-mediated), possible central action reducing sympathetic outflow.
	- **Respiratory Tract**:
	    - Increases **bronchial resistance** by blocking dilator $\beta_2$ receptors. **Consistently worsens asthma** and can precipitate severe attacks.
	- **Central Nervous System (CNS)**:
	    - At usual doses, causes **restlessness, apprehension, and tremor** (due to poor brain penetration of adrenaline). High doses can lead to **excitement, mental confusion, disorientation, tremors, convulsions, delirium, hallucinations**.
	    - Subtle behavioural changes, **forgetfulness, increased dreaming, nightmares** with long-term high doses.
	    - Suppresses anxiety in short-term stressful situations (e.g., public speaking) primarily due to **peripheral action** (blocking physical symptoms like palpitation, tremor).
	- **Metabolic Effects**:
	    - Blocks adrenergically induced **lipolysis** ($\downarrow$ plasma free fatty acids).
	    - **Increases plasma triglyceride** and **LDL/HDL-cholesterol ratio**.
	    - Attenuates glycogenolysis in heart, skeletal muscles, and liver.
	    - **Delays recovery from insulin hypoglycaemia** and suppresses warning signs (tachycardia, tremor). May impair carbohydrate tolerance.
	- **Skeletal Muscle**:
	    - Inhibits **adrenergic-provoked tremor** ($\beta_2$-mediated peripheral action).
	    - Reduces **exercise capacity** by attenuating $\beta_2$-mediated blood flow and limiting fuel supply (glycogenolysis, lipolysis).
	- **Ocular**: Reduces **aqueous humour secretion**, leading to **lowered intraocular tension (IOT)**. Minimal effect on pupil or accommodation.
	- **Local Anaesthetic**: Possesses **membrane-stabilizing property** (Na+ channel blocking), making it a potent local anaesthetic, but **not clinically usable** due to irritant action.
	
	**III. Therapeutic Uses:**
	
	- **Hypertension**:
	    - Mild antihypertensive, but **no longer first-line monotherapy** due to inferior efficacy in stroke/MI prevention compared to other agents.
	- **Angina Pectoris**:
	    - **First-line drug** for **stable angina of effort**. Decreases attack frequency, increases exercise tolerance.
	    - **Prolongs life expectancy** in Coronary Artery Disease (CAD) patients.
	    - **Not suitable for variant (vasospastic) angina**.
	- **Cardiac Arrhythmias**:
	    - **Very useful** in **inappropriate sinus tachycardia** and **atrial/nodal extrasystoles**.
	    - Controls **ventricular rate in Atrial Fibrillation (AF) and Atrial Flutter (AFl)**.
	    - Prevents recurrences of **Paroxysmal Supraventricular Tachycardia (PSVT)**.
	    - Highly effective in **sympathetically mediated arrhythmias** (e.g., pheochromocytoma, halothane anaesthesia).
	    - Suppresses **digitalis-induced** and **thyrotoxicosis-associated tachyarrhythmias**.
	    - **Reduces mortality** in **post-MI patients** as prophylactic treatment.
	    - May terminate **torsades de pointes**.
	- **Myocardial Infarction (MI)**:
	    - **Secondary prophylaxis**: Long-term use post-MI **decreases subsequent mortality by 20%**.
	    - **Myocardial salvage**: Early i.v. administration (within 4-6 hours) may **limit infarct size** and **prevent arrhythmias**.
	- **Congestive Heart Failure (CHF)**:
	    - **Selected $\beta_1$ blockers** (e.g., metoprolol, bisoprolol, carvedilol, nebivolol) **retard disease progression** and **prolong life** in mild-to-moderate dilated cardiomyopathy with systolic dysfunction. (Note: Propranolol is non-selective; specific cardioselective $\beta_1$ blockers are generally preferred in CHF).
	- **Dissecting Aortic Aneurysm**: Reduces cardiac contractile force and aortic pulsation.
	- **Pheochromocytoma**: Controls tachycardia and arrhythmia; **must always be administered after an $\alpha$-blocker** to prevent dangerous BP rise.
	- **Thyrotoxicosis**: Rapidly controls sympathetic symptoms (palpitation, nervousness, tremor, fixed stare, myopathy, sweating) and **inhibits peripheral conversion of T4 to T3**. Valuable during thyroid storm.
	- **Migraine**: **Most effective drug for chronic prophylaxis**.
	- **Essential Tremor**: Effective in suppressing essential tremor (non-selective $\beta$-blockers).
	- **Glaucoma (Ocular)**: Lowers IOT by reducing aqueous humour formation when instilled as eye drops.
	
	**IV. Adverse Effects:**
	
	- **Cardiovascular**:
	    - Can **accentuate myocardial insufficiency**, precipitating **CHF/oedema**.
	    - **Bradycardia** is the most common side effect.
	    - **Exacerbates variant (vasospastic) angina**.
	    - Can worsen classical angina at high doses.
	    - **Rebound hypertension, worsening angina, and sudden death** upon abrupt withdrawal after chronic use (due to $\beta$-receptor upregulation).
	- **Respiratory**: **Life-threatening bronchoconstriction/asthma attacks**.
	- **Metabolic**:
	    - **Delays recovery from hypoglycaemia** and **masks warning signs** (tachycardia, tremor) in diabetics.
	    - Can **impair carbohydrate tolerance**.
	    - **Increases plasma triglycerides** and **LDL/HDL-cholesterol ratio**.
	- **CNS**:
	    - **Fatigue, lethargy, decreased exercise capacity**.
	    - Subtle CNS effects: **nightmares, forgetfulness, mental depression**, lack of drive.
	- **Peripheral**:
	    - **Cold hands and feet** (due to $\beta_2$ blockade).
	    - **Worsening of peripheral vascular disease**.
	- **Gastrointestinal**: G.I. upset.
	- **Sexual Dysfunction**: Sexual distress reported in male patients.
	- **Ocular (topical use)**: Stinging, redness, dry eye, corneal hypoesthesia, allergic blepharoconjunctivitis, blurred vision. Systemic absorption via nasolacrimal duct can lead to systemic adverse effects.
	
	**V. Contraindications:**
	
	- **Bronchial Asthma / Severe COPD**: **Absolute contraindication** due to risk of life-threatening bronchospasm.
	- **Partial or Complete Heart Block**: **Absolute contraindication** (e.g., 2nd or 3rd degree A-V block) due to risk of cardiac arrest.
	- **Cardiogenic Shock / Decompensated CHF**: Can acutely worsen heart failure by blocking sympathetic support.
	- **Variant (Prinzmetal's) Angina**: May exacerbate due to unopposed $\alpha$-mediated coronary constriction.
	- **Severe Bradycardia**.
	- **Hypotension**.
	- **Halothane Anaesthesia**: Risk of arrhythmias.
	- **Patients on $\alpha$-agonists (e.g., in cold remedies)**: Risk of marked BP rise due to unopposed $\alpha$ action.
	- **Patients receiving other drugs that depress A-V conduction** (e.g., verapamil, digoxin).
	
	**VI. Drug Interactions:**
	
	- **Additive cardiac depression** with **digoxin** and **verapamil** (risk of cardiac arrest).
	- **Delays recovery from hypoglycaemia** when co-administered with **insulin or oral antidiabetics**.
	- **Marked rise in BP** if given with **phenylephrine, ephedrine, or other $\alpha$-agonists** (e.g., in cold remedies) due to unopposed $\alpha$ action.
	- **NSAIDs** (e.g., indomethacin) **attenuate its antihypertensive action**.
	- **Retards lidocaine metabolism** by reducing hepatic blood flow.
3. Cardioselective beta blockers-uses and their advantages

	### **Cardioselective Beta Blockers**
	
	**I. Definition & Mechanism:**
	
	- These are **β-adrenergic receptor antagonists** that are **more potent at blocking cardiac β1 receptors** than bronchial β2 receptors.
	- **Key Concept:** Cardioselectivity is **relative** and can be **lost at higher doses**.
	- **Examples:** Metoprolol, Atenolol, Bisoprolol, Acebutolol, Nebivolol, Celiprolol.
	    - _Note: Carvedilol and Labetalol are mixed α+β blockers, also with significant β-blocking properties and are often grouped with β-blockers in CHF management due to similar benefits._
	
	**II. Advantages of Cardioselective Beta Blockers:** Stem from their relative selectivity for β1 receptors, leading to fewer β2-mediated adverse effects:
	
	1. **Respiratory System:**
	    
	    - **Lower propensity to cause bronchoconstriction**.
	    - Generally **safer in asthmatics and COPD patients**, though _usually still avoided_ unless compelling cardiac conditions necessitate their use under supervision.
	2. **Metabolic Profile:**
	    
	    - **Less interference with carbohydrate metabolism**.
	    - **Reduced inhibition of glycogenolysis** during hypoglycaemia, making them **safer in diabetics** (though tachycardia response to hypoglycaemia is still blocked).
	    - **No/less deleterious effect on blood lipid profile** (e.g., less increase in total triglycerides or fall in HDL-cholesterol).
	    - Nebivolol specifically _increases insulin sensitivity_.
	3. **Peripheral Vascular Effects:**
	    
	    - **Lower incidence of cold hands and feet**.
	    - **Less chance of precipitating Raynaud's phenomenon**.
	4. **Novel (Third Generation) Agents (e.g., Nebivolol, Celiprolol, Carvedilol):**
	    
	    - **Nebivolol:** Activates endothelial **Nitric Oxide (NO) synthase**, enhancing NO production leading to **vasodilation** and potential improvement in endothelial function, which may delay atherosclerosis.
	    - **Celiprolol:** Possesses **weak β2 agonistic activity**, contributing to **vasodilation** and potentially enhanced safety in asthmatics.
	    - **Carvedilol:** Provides **vasodilation via α1 blockade** and exhibits **antioxidant properties**.
	
	**III. Therapeutic Uses:**
	
	5. **Hypertension:**
	    
	    - Used as **antihypertensives**.
	    - While not always first-line for _initial monotherapy_ (JNC8, NICE guidelines), they are effective and their use in specific patient subsets with **compelling indications** (e.g., coexisting CHF, post-MI) is strongly recommended.
	    - Vasodilating β-blockers (e.g., carvedilol, nebivolol) are favored by some experts.
	6. **Angina Pectoris:**
	    
	    - Effective in **classical angina of effort**, decreasing frequency and severity of attacks, and increasing exercise tolerance.
	    - **Preferred over nonselective β-blockers** in classical angina due to less risk of worsening variant (vasospastic) angina (which is mediated by unopposed α-receptor activity).
	    - Also used in **unstable angina/NSTEMI** (unless contraindicated), to reduce myocardial O2 demand and risk of impending MI/sudden cardiac death.
	7. **Myocardial Infarction (MI):**
	    
	    - **Secondary Prophylaxis:** **Long-term use** (e.g., metoprolol, propranolol) **reduces subsequent mortality by ~20%** after MI, especially in high-risk patients. Other β-blockers with partial agonistic action are considered less suitable for this purpose.
	    - **Myocardial Salvage:** Administered early (within 4-6 hours of attack) in evolving MI, they may **limit infarct size** and **prevent arrhythmias** (e.g., metoprolol).
	8. **Congestive Heart Failure (CHF):**
	    
	    - **Crucial for survival benefit:** Selected β1 blockers (metoprolol, bisoprolol, nebivolol) and carvedilol **retard disease progression and prolong life** in mild to moderate CHF with reduced ejection fraction (EF).
	    - Benefit arises from antagonizing deleterious effects of sympathetic overactivity on myocardium (apoptosis, fibrosis, remodeling).
	    - **Important clinical principle:** Therapy should be started at **very low doses** and _gradually titrated upwards_ once the patient is compensated to avoid precipitating failure.
	9. **Cardiac Arrhythmias:**
	    
	    - Suppress **adrenergically mediated ectopic activity**.
	    - **Esmolol** (ultra-short acting β1 blocker) is highly useful for **emergency control of ventricular rate in atrial fibrillation (AF)/atrial flutter (AFl)**, and for arrhythmias during anaesthesia.
	    - Control ventricular rate in AF/AFl.
	
	**IV. Clinical Considerations/Precautions:**
	
	- **Relative selectivity:** Cardioselectivity is **dose-dependent**; at high doses, β2 receptors may also be blocked.
	- **Asthma/COPD:** Despite selectivity, **caution is warranted**, and these drugs are often avoided unless cardiac indications are compelling.
	- **Hypoglycaemia:** Can mask the symptoms of hypoglycaemia (tachycardia).
	- **CHF Initiation:** Absolute contraindication in _decompensated_ CHF; must be initiated _gradually_ and _after compensation is restored_.
	- **Abrupt Withdrawal:** May precipitate severe angina or MI due to receptor supersensitivity (rebound phenomenon).
	1. Drug treatment for acute congestive glaucoma
	Acute congestive glaucoma, also known as angle-closure glaucoma, is a **medical emergency** characterised by a rapid and significant rise in intraocular tension (i.o.t.), typically to 40-60 mmHg. Failure to promptly lower i.o.t. can lead to irreversible loss of sight. Vigorous, multi-pronged drug therapy is immediately instituted to reduce the i.o.t..

4. **Drug Treatment for Acute Congestive Glaucoma**
	
	**I. Condition Overview (Clinical Relevance):**
	
	- **Acute onset** with marked eye congestion and severe headache.
	- Occurs in individuals with a **narrow iridocorneal angle** and shallow anterior chamber, often precipitated by mydriasis.
	- **Emergency status**: Rapid lowering of i.o.t. is crucial to prevent permanent vision loss.
	- Drugs are used to **terminate the acute attack**; definitive treatment often involves surgical or laser iridotomy.
	
	**II. Therapeutic Measures (Pharmacological Actions & Dosing):**
	
	1. **Hyperosmotic Agents (e.g., Mannitol, Glycerol):**
	    
	    - **Mechanism:** Decongest the eye by **osmotic action**, drawing water out of the eye. They transiently raise plasma osmolarity.
	    - **Drugs:**
	        - **Hypertonic Mannitol (20% solution):** 1.5-2 g/kg infused intravenously (i.v.) over 1 hour.
	        - **Glycerol (10% solution):** Infused i.v.. A retention enema of 50% glycerine may also be used.
	    - **Clinical Use:** Initial measure to rapidly lower dangerously high i.o.t.. Also used before and after ocular/brain surgery to prevent acute i.o.t./intracranial pressure rise.
	2. **Carbonic Anhydrase Inhibitors (CAIs):**
	    
	    - **Mechanism:** Reduce **aqueous humour formation** by inhibiting the generation of bicarbonate ion in the ciliary epithelium.
	    - **Drug:**
	        - **Acetazolamide:** **0.5 g i.v.** (to act rapidly) **followed by oral twice daily** (for sustained effect).
	    - **Clinical Use:** Started concurrently with hyperosmotic agents. Used as adjuvant for short-term indications like angle closure glaucoma.
	    - **Adverse Effects:** Systemic side effects (e.g., paraesthesias, anorexia, hypokalaemia, acidosis, malaise, depression) generally restrict long-term use.
	3. **Miotics (Parasympathomimetics):**
	    
	    - **Mechanism:** Increase the tone of the **sphincter pupillae and ciliary muscle**, which pulls the iris away from the lens, removes the pupillary block, and **restores aqueous drainage** through the iridocorneal angle.
	    - **Drug:**
	        - **Pilocarpine (1-4% eye drops):** Applied once the i.o.t. starts to fall due to i.v. therapy.
	    - **Clinical Use:** Essential for reversing the angle closure mechanism once initial i.o.t. reduction has begun. While generally third-line for open-angle glaucoma, it's crucial here.
	    - **Adverse Effects (Ocular):** Stinging, painful spasm of accommodation, and dim vision (due to constricted pupil) are frequent side effects.
	4. **Alpha-2 Adrenoceptor Agonists:**
	    
	    - **Mechanism:** Primarily decrease **aqueous production** by action in the ciliary body (alpha-2 and subsidiary alpha-1 action).
	    - **Drug:**
	        - **Apraclonidine (1% eye drops):** May be added to the regimen.
	        - **Brimonidine:** May be used for short-term (prophylaxis of i.o.t. spikes post laser/surgery) as well as long-term use in glaucoma.
	    - **Clinical Use:** Used for add-on therapy.
	5. **Prostaglandin F2α Analogues:**
	    
	    - **Mechanism:** Primarily **increase uveoscleral outflow** (and minor effect on trabecular outflow).
	    - **Drug:**
	        - **Latanoprost (0.005% eye drops):** Instillation may be added.
	    - **Clinical Use:** While often first-line for open-angle glaucoma due to good efficacy and once-daily application, their role in _acute_ angle closure is adjunctive, as seen by their "may be added" recommendation.
	
	**III. Contraindications:**
	
	- **Atropinic drugs** (anticholinergics) are **absolutely contraindicated** in individuals with a narrow iridocorneal angle, as they can precipitate acute congestive glaucoma by causing mydriasis and further closing the angle.

	### **Rationale for Mannitol in Acute Congestive Glaucoma**
	
	Acute congestive glaucoma (also known as angle-closure glaucoma) is a **medical emergency** characterised by a rapid and significant rise in intraocular tension (i.o.t.), often to 40-60 mmHg, necessitating prompt intervention to prevent irreversible vision loss [conversation history, 314]. **Mannitol is a crucial first-line drug** in this emergency [conversation history, 314].
	
	**I. Mechanism of Action (Pharmacology):**
	
	- **Class:** Mannitol is a **hyperosmotic agent** [conversation history, 314, 366].
	- **Core Mechanism:** It is a pharmacologically inert, non-electrolyte compound that, when administered intravenously, **transiently raises plasma osmolarity**.
	- **Ocular Effect:** This increased plasma osmolarity creates an **osmotic gradient** [conversation history, 315] between the blood and the intraocular fluids. As a result, mannitol effectively **draws water out of the eye** [conversation history], specifically from the **aqueous humour** and vitreous body, into the systemic circulation.
	- **Reduction of IOP:** This withdrawal of fluid from the ocular compartments leads to a rapid and marked **reduction in intraocular tension** (i.o.t.) [conversation history, 314, 369].
	- **Renal Handling:** Mannitol is freely filtered at the glomerulus and undergoes limited reabsorption, thus acting as an osmotic diuretic in the kidney as well.
	
	**II. Clinical Rationale & Benefits:**
	
	1. **Rapid Reduction of IOP:** Mannitol provides a **prompt** and **powerful** effect in lowering dangerously high i.o.t., which is critical in this sight-threatening emergency [conversation history, 314].
	2. **Decongests the Eye:** By drawing out water, it physically "decongests" the eye [conversation history, 314, 315], reducing the bulk that contributes to the angle closure.
	3. **Route of Administration:** It is administered **intravenously** (e.g., 20% solution) [conversation history, 314, 369], ensuring rapid systemic delivery and onset of action. A typical dose is **1.5-2 g/kg infused over 1 hour** [conversation history, 314, 369].
	4. **Adjuvant Therapy:** While effective, mannitol is typically part of a **vigorous, multi-pronged drug therapy** along with carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (e.g., acetazolamide) and miotics (e.g., pilocarpine) [conversation history, 315]. It is also used before and after ocular surgery to prevent acute i.o.t. rise.
