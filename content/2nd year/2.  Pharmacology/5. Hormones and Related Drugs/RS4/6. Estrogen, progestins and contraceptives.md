1. Clomiphene citrate-mechanism of action, uses and adverse effects
	**Clomiphene Citrate**
	
	Clomiphene citrate is a synthetic non-steroidal agent primarily used in reproductive medicine.
	
	**1. Mechanism of Action**:
	
	- **Binds to both oestrogen receptors (ERα and ERβ)**.
	- The **trans isomer (enclomiphene)** acts as a **pure oestrogen antagonist** in all human tissues.
	- The **cis isomer (zuclomiphene)** and the **racemate** exhibit **weak agonistic action in rats**.
	- **Key Action**: Induces **gonadotropin (Gn) secretion** in women by **blocking the oestrogenic negative feedback inhibition of the pituitary**.
	    - This leads to an **increased release of LH/FSH at each secretory pulse**.
	    - Consequently, the **ovaries enlarge, and ovulation occurs** if the ovaries are responsive to Gn.
	- Its peripheral anti-oestrogenic actions can cause **hot flashes**.
	- It may also modify the endometrium and cervical mucus.
	
	**2. Therapeutic Uses**:
	
	- **Infertility due to failure of ovulation (Chief use)**:
	    - Dose: **50 mg orally once daily for 5 days**, starting from the 5th day of the menstrual cycle. This is repeated monthly.
	    - If no conception after 1-2 months, the daily dose may be doubled to 100 mg for up to 2-3 cycles.
	    - **Maximum of 6 treatment cycles** is recommended.
	    - **Enhancement**: Adding menotropins or chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) on the last 2 days of the course can improve success rates.
	- **To aid in vitro fertilisation (IVF)**:
	    - Used in conjunction with gonadotropins (Gns) to promote **synchronous maturation of several ova**, facilitating their harvesting for assisted reproductive techniques.
	- **Oligozoospermia (Male Infertility)**:
	    - Increases Gn secretion in men, which promotes spermatogenesis and testosterone secretion if they are deficient.
	    - Dose: 25 mg daily for 24 days in a month with 6 days rest, for up to 6 months.
	    - _Clinical Note_: Success rates in male infertility are low.
	
	**3. Adverse Effects**:
	
	- **Polycystic ovaries**.
	- **Multiple pregnancy**.
	- **Hot flashes** (due to peripheral anti-oestrogenic effects).
	- Gastrointestinal upset.
	- Vertigo.
	- Allergic dermatitis.
	- **Increased risk of ovarian tumour** may occur.
	- _Clinical Note_: Caution is advised, and a lower dose should be used in women with enlarged ovaries.
	- Its anti-oestrogenic effect on the developing follicle, endometrium, or cervical mucus can be **counterproductive**.
	- Luteal phase dysfunction has also been implicated in therapeutic failures.
	
	**Pharmacokinetic Profile (Brief)**:
	
	- Well absorbed orally.
	- Deposited in adipose tissue.
	- **Long elimination half-life (~6 days)**.
	- Largely metabolised and excreted in bile.
	
	
	1. A 28 year old lady having a 4 year old child is requesting a contraceptive pill, which type of oral contraceptive pill will you presibe? What are the contraindications and adverse effects of the contraceptives pills?
		For a 28-year-old lady with a 4-year-old child requesting a contraceptive pill, the most appropriate choice would generally be a **Combined Oral Contraceptive (COC) Pill**.
		
		Here's a high-yield summary for medical school exams:
		
		---
		
		### **Choice of Oral Contraceptive Pill**
		
		- **Recommended Choice: Combined Oral Contraceptive (COC) Pill**.
		    - **Reasoning:**
		        - It is the **most popular and most efficacious method**, offering nearly 100% confidence in preventing pregnancy with a low failure rate (0.3% with perfect use).
		        - Provides **complete return of fertility** upon discontinuation.
		        - Given her age (28 years) and having already had a child (implying she is likely not breastfeeding, which would favour a progestin-only pill), the benefits and reliability of COCs make them the preferred first-line option.
		    - **Composition:** Contains a **fixed dose of an estrogen** (e.g., Ethinylestradiol 30 µg) and a **progestin** (e.g., Levonorgestrel 0.15 mg, a 19-nortestosterone derivative).
		    - **Mechanism of Action (Primary): Inhibition of Ovulation**.
		        - The progestin reduces the frequency of LH secretory pulses, while the estrogen primarily reduces FSH secretion.
		        - Both hormones synergise to **inhibit the midcycle LH surge**, preventing follicular development and rupture.
		    - **Other Contraceptive Mechanisms:**
		        - **Thick cervical mucus** secretion, hostile to sperm penetration.
		        - **Endometrial changes** (hyperproliferative, hypersecretory, or atrophic) making it unsuitable for blastocyst implantation.
		        - Possible modification of uterine and tubal contractions to disfavour fertilisation.
		- **Alternative (Less Preferred): Progestin-Only Pill (Mini-pill)**.
		    - Lower efficacy (96-98%) compared to COCs.
		    - Often leads to irregular menstrual cycles, which may be less acceptable to patients.
		    - Primarily used when estrogen is contraindicated, e.g., in breastfeeding mothers or those with high risk of thromboembolism.
		
		---
		
		### **Contraindications of Oral Contraceptive Pills**
		
		(Primarily for Combined Oral Contraceptive Pills)
		
		- **Absolute Contraindications**:
		    - **Thromboembolic, coronary, and cerebrovascular disease** (or history of).
		    - **Moderate-to-severe hypertension**; hyperlipidaemia.
		    - **Active liver disease, hepatoma** or history of jaundice during past pregnancy.
		    - Suspected or overt **malignancy of genitals/breast**.
		    - Porphyria.
		    - Impending major surgery (COCs should be stopped 4 weeks prior to reduce risk of postoperative thromboembolism).
		- **Relative Contraindications** (requiring cautious use and close supervision):
		    - **Diabetes** (can vitiate glycaemic control).
		    - Obesity.
		    - **Smoking** (especially in women >35 years due to increased cardiovascular risk).
		    - Undiagnosed vaginal bleeding.
		    - Uterine leiomyoma (fibroids) – may enlarge with estrogenic preparations.
		    - Mentally ill patients.
		    - **Age above 35 years**.
		    - Mild hypertension.
		    - Migraine.
		    - Gallbladder disease.
		
		---
		
		### **Adverse Effects of Oral Contraceptive Pills**
		
		(Primarily for Combined Oral Contraceptive Pills; low-dose preparations have reduced risks compared to older high-dose versions).
		
		- **Common (Non-Serious) Adverse Effects**:
		    - **Weight gain** and fluid retention.
		    - **Breast discomfort** (tenderness, enlargement).
		    - Nausea and vomiting (especially in early cycles).
		    - Headache.
		    - **Breakthrough bleeding** (can be managed by adjusting estrogen dose).
		    - Mood swings and mental depression.
		    - Abdominal distension.
		- **Serious Complications**:
		    - **Venous Thromboembolism (VTE)**: Increased risk (2-3 patients per 1000 women-years with newer low-dose pills), especially in women >35 years, diabetics, hypertensives, and smokers. Risk normalises after cessation.
		    - **Arterial Thrombosis (MI or Stroke)**: Small increase in incidence with low-dose pills without other risk factors. Higher risk for women >35 years.
		    - **Genital Carcinoma**: Long-term epidemiological data generally show no increase in cervical or breast cancers in the general population, but risk is increased in predisposed individuals, and growth of existing hormone-dependent tumours may be hastened.
		    - **Drug Interactions**: Can lead to contraceptive failure.
		        - **Enzyme inducers** (e.g., phenytoin, carbamazepine, rifampin, ritonavir) increase the metabolism of both estrogenic and progestational components.
		        - **Suppression of intestinal microflora** by antibiotics (e.g., tetracyclines, ampicillin) interrupts enterohepatic circulation of estrogens, leading to lower blood levels.
2. Oral (hormonal) contraceptive pills (types, MOA, action, adverse effects, contraindications)
	  **Oral (Hormonal) Contraceptive Pills**
	
	**1. Types of Oral Contraceptive Pills (OCPs)**
	
	- **Combined Oral Contraceptive (COC) Pill**
	    - Contains an **estrogen** (e.g., Ethinylestradiol) and a **progestin** (e.g., Levonorgestrel) in a fixed dose [Previous Answer, 408].
	    - Taken daily for 21 days, followed by a 7-day pill-free interval during which bleeding occurs.
	    - Most popular and efficacious method, with a failure rate of **0.3% with perfect use** [Previous Answer, 411].
	- **Phased Pill (Triphasic)**
	    - Designed to reduce total steroid dose by mimicking the normal hormonal pattern; estrogen dose constant (or slightly varied), progestin dose progressively higher through the cycle.
	    - Recommended for women >35 years or those with breakthrough bleeding on monophasic pills.
	- **Progestin-Only Pill (Mini-pill)**
	    - Contains only a **low-dose progestin**.
	    - Taken daily continuously without a gap.
	    - Lower efficacy (96-98%) compared to COCs [Previous Answer, 413].
	    - Often leads to irregular menstrual cycles [Previous Answer, 413].
	- **Emergency (Postcoital) Pill**
	    - Used after unprotected intercourse to prevent unwanted pregnancy.
	    - Most commonly **Levonorgestrel** (0.75 mg x 2 doses 12 hours apart, or 1.5 mg single dose within 72 hours) or **Mifepristone** (600 mg single dose within 72 hours).
	    - Higher failure rate and side effects than regular low-dose combined pills; reserved for unexpected/accidental exposure.
	
	**2. Mechanism of Action (MOA)** Hormonal contraceptives interfere with fertility through multiple mechanisms, with relative importance varying by type.
	
	- **Primary MOA for Combined Oral Contraceptive (COC) Pills: Inhibition of Ovulation** [Previous Answer, 417].
	    - **Progestin**: Reduces frequency of LH secretory pulses.
	    - **Estrogen**: Primarily reduces FSH secretion.
	    - **Synergistic effect**: Both hormones inhibit the **midcycle LH surge**, preventing follicular development and rupture [Previous Answer, 417].
	- **Other Contraceptive Mechanisms (for COCs, Mini-pills, and Postcoital pills)** [Previous Answer, 419]:
	    - **Thick cervical mucus**: Progestin action evokes secretion of cervical mucus hostile to sperm penetration.
	    - **Endometrial changes**: Endometrium becomes unsuitable for blastocyst implantation (hyperproliferative, hypersecretory, or atrophic and out of phase with fertilization).
	    - **Uterine and tubal contractions**: May be modified to disfavour fertilization (uncertain, but contributes to mini-pill/postcoital efficacy).
	    - **Postcoital pill specific**: May dislodge a just-implanted blastocyst or interfere with fertilization/implantation.
	
	**3. Actions (Non-Contraceptive Benefits/Effects)**
	
	- **Return of Fertility**: Full return within 1-2 months after discontinuing OCs (delayed with injectables).
	- **Menstrual Benefits**:
	    - Reduced menstrual blood loss and associated anaemia.
	    - Cycles become regular if irregular.
	    - Premenstrual tension, dysmenorrhoea, and menorrhagia are ameliorated.
	- **Gynaecological Benefits**:
	    - Reduced risk of **endometrial and ovarian carcinoma**; possibly colorectal cancer.
	    - Reduced incidence and symptomatic relief of **fibrocystic breast disease** and **ovarian cysts**.
	    - Endometriosis and pelvic inflammatory disease tend to subside.
	- **Metabolic/Cardiovascular (Estrogen Component)**:
	    - Decreases plasma **LDL cholesterol** while raising **HDL and triglyceride levels** (though progestin may nullify this benefit).
	    - Increases blood coagulability by inducing synthesis of clotting factors (II, VII, IX, X).
	
	**4. Adverse Effects** (Primarily for Combined Oral Contraceptive Pills; low-dose preparations have reduced risks).
	
	- **Common (Non-Serious) Adverse Effects** (especially in first 1-3 cycles):
	    - **Nausea and vomiting**.
	    - **Headache** (migraine may be precipitated or worsened).
	    - **Breakthrough bleeding** or spotting (especially with progestin-only preparations; switch to higher estrogen dose or triphasic pill if persistent).
	    - Breast discomfort.
	- **Later-Appearing Effects**:
	    - **Weight gain**, acne, increased body hair (less with newer progestins like desogestrel).
	    - **Chloasma** (pigmentation of cheeks, nose, forehead).
	    - **Mood swings and mental depression**.
	    - Abdominal distension.
	    - Carbohydrate intolerance and precipitation of **diabetes** in few subjects (with high-dose preparations, unlikely with present pills).
	- **Serious Complications**:
	    - **Venous Thromboembolism (VTE)**: Increased risk (2-3 patients per 1000 women-years with newer low-dose pills).
	        - Risk is due to the **estrogen component** and normalizes after stopping OCs.
	        - Higher risk in women **>35 years of age, diabetics, hypertensives, and smokers**.
	        - Mechanisms: Increased clotting factors, decreased antithrombin III, decreased plasminogen activator in endothelium, increased platelet aggregation.
	    - **Arterial Thrombosis (Myocardial Infarction or Stroke)**: Small increase in incidence with low-dose pills without other risk factors. Higher risk for women **>35 years**.
	    - **Rise in Blood Pressure (BP)**: Less frequent/magnitude with low-dose pills. If BP rises, stopping OCs usually normalizes it. Both estrogen and progestin contribute.
	    - **Genital Carcinoma**: No increase in general population; risk increased in predisposed individuals; may hasten growth of existing hormone-dependent tumours.
	    - **Teratogenicity**: If pregnancy occurs during use, there is an increased risk of malformations (e.g., masculinization of female foetus by progestins), and termination by suction-aspiration is advised.
	
	**5. Contraindications** (Primarily for Combined Oral Contraceptive Pills).
	
	- **Absolute Contraindications**:
	    - **Thromboembolic, coronary, and cerebrovascular disease** (or history of).
	    - **Moderate-to-severe hypertension**; hyperlipidaemia.
	    - **Active liver disease, hepatoma** or history of jaundice during past pregnancy.
	    - Suspected or overt **malignancy of genitals/breast**.
	    - Porphyria.
	    - **Impending major surgery**: COCs should be stopped **4 weeks prior** to reduce risk of postoperative thromboembolism.
	- **Relative Contraindications** (requiring cautious use and close supervision):
	    - **Diabetes** (can vitiate glycaemic control).
	    - Obesity.
	    - **Smoking** (especially in women >35 years due to increased cardiovascular risk).
	    - Undiagnosed vaginal bleeding.
	    - Uterine leiomyoma (fibroids) – may enlarge with estrogenic preparations (progestin-only pills may be used).
	    - Mentally ill patients.
	    - **Age above 35 years**.
	    - Mild hypertension.
	    - Migraine.
	    - Gallbladder disease.
	- **Drug Interactions leading to Contraceptive Failure**:
	    - **Enzyme inducers** (e.g., phenytoin, phenobarbitone, primidone, carbamazepine, **rifampin**, ritonavir) increase metabolism of both estrogenic and progestational components.
	    - **Antibiotics** (e.g., tetracyclines, ampicillin) suppress intestinal microflora, interrupting enterohepatic circulation of estrogens, leading to lower blood levels.
	    - _Clinical note_: With interacting drugs, advise switching to a preparation with 50 µg ethinylestradiol or using an alternative contraceptive method (e.g., rifampin requires alternative contraception due to potent enzyme induction).

	- **Pre-prescription Assessment**:
	    - **History**: Screen for **all absolute and relative contraindications**, especially family history of thromboembolism, hypertension, smoking status, liver disease, migraine, and any current medications (for potential drug interactions).
	    - **Physical Examination**: Measure **blood pressure** (crucial due to cardiovascular risks).
	    - **Investigations**: Generally, extensive investigations are not routinely required for OCP prescription in healthy women, but blood pressure, and potentially a lipid profile if risk factors exist, are important.
	- **Patient Education**:
	    - Discuss the **benefits** (contraception, regular periods, reduced menstrual symptoms, reduced risk of certain cancers/cysts).
	    - Inform about **common, non-serious side effects** (nausea, headache, breakthrough bleeding, breast tenderness) and advise that these often subside after the first few cycles.
	    - Explain the **serious but rare risks** (VTE, MI, stroke), emphasizing factors that increase risk (age >35, smoking, hypertension, diabetes).
	    - Provide clear instructions on **how to take the pill** (daily, consistently), what to do if a dose is missed, and the importance of using backup contraception if interacting drugs are started.
	    - Advise on **warning signs** that require immediate medical attention (e.g., severe headache, chest pain, vision changes, leg swelling) as these can indicate serious complications.
