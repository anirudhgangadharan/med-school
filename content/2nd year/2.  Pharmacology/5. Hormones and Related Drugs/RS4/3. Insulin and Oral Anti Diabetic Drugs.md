1. Metformin (MOA, uses and side effects)
	**Metformin**
	
	**Mechanism of Action (MOA)**:
	
	- **Does not cause insulin release**; its action is dependent on the **presence of insulin**.
	- **Activates AMP-dependent protein kinase (AMPK)**, which plays a crucial role in mediating its actions.
	- **Primary actions** responsible for blood glucose lowering:
	    - **Suppresses hepatic gluconeogenesis and glucose output from the liver**.
	    - **Enhances insulin-mediated glucose uptake and disposal** in skeletal muscle and fat, thereby **overcoming insulin resistance**.
	    - Interferes with the mitochondrial respiratory chain and promotes peripheral glucose utilization through anaerobic glycolysis.
	- Also **retards intestinal absorption of glucose**, other hexoses, amino acids, and Vitamin B12.
	
	**Uses**:
	
	- **First-choice drug for all Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (DM) patients**, unless contraindicated or not tolerated. Current recommendations advise initiating metformin therapy right at diagnosis, along with lifestyle modifications.
	- It is a **'euglycaemic' agent**, meaning it causes little or no hypoglycaemia in non-diabetic subjects, and hypoglycaemia is rare even in diabetics.
	- **Promotes weight loss**.
	- Has **potential to prevent both macrovascular and microvascular complications** of diabetes.
	    - Specifically, it decreased the risk of death and other diabetes-related endpoints in overweight patients. This may be because it decreases insulin resistance, a pathogenic factor in Type 2 DM, unlike sulfonylureas and exogenous insulin which increase insulin supply.
	- **No acceleration of β-cell exhaustion/failure** in Type 2 DM.
	- Antihyperglycaemic efficacy (HbA1c reduction by 0.8-1.2%) is equivalent to other oral drugs.
	- **Can be combined with any other oral or injectable antidiabetic**.
	- Can **prevent new onset Type 2 DM** in obese, middle-aged subjects with impaired glucose tolerance.
	- **Clinical Relevance:** Found to **improve ovulation and fertility** in some infertile women with **polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)**, likely by mitigating insulin resistance and lowering circulating insulin levels.
	
	**Side Effects**:
	
	- **Frequent, but generally not serious**.
	- **Gastrointestinal disturbances** are common:
	    - **Abdominal pain, anorexia, bloating, nausea, metallic taste, mild diarrhoea, and tiredness**. These complaints usually subside with time. Gastrointestinal intolerance is a limiting feature, especially at higher doses.
	- **Lactic acidosis**:
	    - A rare but serious complication (incidence <1 per 10,000 patient years).
	    - Risk is **increased in renal insufficiency**, as metformin accumulates.
	    - **Contraindicated** in: **renal insufficiency**, **hypotensive states, heart failure, severe respiratory/hepatic disease, and in alcoholics**.
	    - Alcohol ingestion can precipitate lactic acidosis.
	- **Vitamin B12 deficiency** can occur with high doses due to interference with its absorption.
	- Drugs like cimetidine and furosemide may compete with metformin excretion and enhance its toxicity.

2. Sulfonylurea (mechanism of action, adverse effects and therapeutic uses) as antidiabetic drugs.
	**Sulfonylureas (SUs) as Antidiabetic Drugs**
	
	**Mechanism of Action (MOA)**:
	
	- Sulfonylureas (SUs) are a class of oral antidiabetic drugs primarily used in **Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (DM)**.
	- Their sole significant action is to **lower blood glucose levels** in normal subjects and Type 2 diabetics.
	- They are dependent on the presence of **functional pancreatic β-cells**; they are ineffective in pancreatectomized animals and Type 1 diabetics.
	- **Key Mechanism**: SUs bind to a specific 'sulfonylurea receptor' (SUR1) located on the **pancreatic β-cell membrane**.
	    - This binding causes **closure of ATP-sensitive K+ channels (KATP channels)** on the β-cell membrane.
	    - The restriction of outward K+ flow leads to an **increase in intracellular K+ concentration and partial depolarisation of the membrane**.
	    - This depolarisation augments **Ca2+ channel opening** and release of Ca2+ from intracellular stores.
	    - Increased intracellular Ca2+ promotes **exocytotic release of insulin-storing granules**, thus stimulating insulin secretion.
	- SUs primarily augment the **second phase insulin secretion** with little effect on the first phase.
	- **Minor actions**: They may also reduce glucagon secretion and slow hepatic degradation of insulin.
	- **Extrapancreatic action (long-term)**: After several months, the direct insulinaemic action may decline, but glucose tolerance improves due to **sensitisation of target tissues (especially liver) to insulin's action**. This is hypothesised to occur by reversing the down-regulation of insulin receptors due to overall lower circulating insulin concentration, or by improving post-receptor action.
	**Adverse Effects**:
	- Adverse effects are relatively common, occurring in 3-7% of patients.
	- **Hypoglycaemia**: This is the **commonest and potentially most serious problem**, occasionally severe and rarely fatal.
	    - It is more frequent in the **elderly, patients with liver or kidney disease**, and when potentiating drugs are co-administered.
	    - **Tolbutamide carries the lowest risk** due to its lower potency and shorter duration of action.
	    - Treatment involves giving glucose orally (15-20g) or intravenously (30-50ml of 50% glucose) in severe cases; glucagon or adrenaline may be expedient measures.
	- **Weight Gain**: Most diabetics initiated on SUs tend to gain 1-3 kg, likely a consequence of the insulinaemic action.
	- **Gastrointestinal disturbances**: Mild and infrequent, including nausea, vomiting, flatulence, diarrhoea or constipation, headache, and paresthesias.
	- **Hypersensitivity reactions**: Rashes, photosensitivity, purpura, and rarely agranulocytosis. Serious reactions like exfoliative dermatitis and Stevens-Johnson syndrome have been reported, especially with long-acting agents.
	- **Disulfiram-like reaction**: Flushing and a disulfiram-like reaction can occur after alcohol intake in some individuals.
	- **Iodide uptake**: Tolbutamide reduces iodide uptake by the thyroid, but hypothyroidism is not typically observed.
	- **Vitamin B12 deficiency**: _Information on Vitamin B12 deficiency specifically linked to sulfonylureas is not found in the provided sources, but it is a known side effect of metformin._
	- **Use in pregnancy/lactation**: Safety during pregnancy is not established; change to insulin is advised. SUs are secreted in milk and should not be given to nursing mothers.
	- **Interactions**:
	    - **Drugs that enhance SU action (precipitating hypoglycaemia)**: Phenylbutazone, sulfinpyrazone, salicylates, sulfonamides (by displacing from protein binding); cimetidine, ketoconazole, sulfonamides, warfarin, chloramphenicol, acute alcohol intake (by inhibiting metabolism/excretion); salicylates, propranolol (less with cardioselective β1 blockers), sympatholytic antihypertensives, lithium, theophylline, alcohol (by inhibiting gluconeogenesis).
	    - **Drugs that decrease SU action (vitiating diabetes control)**: Phenobarbitone, phenytoin, rifampicin, chronic alcoholism (by inducing metabolism); corticosteroids, thiazides, furosemide, oral contraceptives (by opposite action/suppressing insulin release).
	
	**Therapeutic Uses**:
	
	- **First-line for Type 2 DM (historically)**: Sulfonylureas were among the first clinically acceptable oral antidiabetic drugs introduced, following the observation that early sulfonamides caused hypoglycaemia.
	- **Current Role**: While metformin is now established as the first-choice drug for most Type 2 DM patients, SUs are very commonly selected as the **second drug** when metformin alone fails to achieve target glycaemic control (HbA1c < 7%).
	- **Advantages**: Good patient acceptability, convenient dosing, and high efficacy.
	- **Efficacy**: Their antihyperglycaemic efficacy (HbA1c reduction by 0.8-1.2%) is comparable to other oral drugs.
	- **Limitations**:
	    - They are **not used in Type 1 DM** because they require functional β-cells.
	    - **Risk of β-cell exhaustion**: Some evidence suggests that long-term SU use (2-10 years) may expedite β-cell apoptosis and failure, and they tend to lose efficacy over years (5-10% per year failure rate).
	    - They increase insulin supply rather than overcoming insulin resistance, which is a pathogenic factor in Type 2 DM.
	- **Combination therapy**: SUs can be combined with any other oral or injectable antidiabetic agent. They are often continued with insulin, especially when a single daily injection of long-acting insulin is used for basal control, with the oral drug checking postprandial glycaemia.
	- **Choice among SUs**: There is no significant difference in the clinical efficacy of various second-generation SUs, but differences exist in dose, onset, and duration of action, influencing regimen flexibility.

3. A 16-year-old presents with complaints of weight loss and polyuria. His HbA1C was 9 g%. His fasting blood sugar was 178 mg/dL and postprandial blood glucose was 240 mg/dL. He was diagnosed as type 1 diabetes mellitus.
	a. Classify the insulin preparations based on their duration of action.
	b. What are the advantages of rapid acting insulins?
	Given the patient's diagnosis of Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus, understanding insulin preparations is crucial for effective management.
	
	### a. Classification of Insulin Preparations (based on duration of action)
	
	Insulin preparations are broadly classified based on their pharmacokinetic profiles, particularly their **onset of action, peak effect, and total duration of action**.
	
	- **Rapid-Acting Insulins**:
	    
	    - **Examples:** Insulin lispro, Insulin aspart, Insulin glulisine.
	    - **Appearance:** Clear.
	    - **Onset:** 0.2–0.4 hours.
	    - **Peak:** 1–2 hours.
	    - **Duration:** 3–5 hours.
	- **Short-Acting Insulins**:
	    
	    - **Example:** Regular (soluble) insulin.
	    - **Appearance:** Clear.
	    - **Onset:** 0.5–1 hour.
	    - **Peak:** 2–3 hours.
	    - **Duration:** 6–8 hours.
	- **Intermediate-Acting Insulins**:
	    
	    - **Examples:** Insulin zinc suspension (Lente), Neutral Protamine Hagedorn (NPH) or isophane insulin.
	    - **Appearance:** Cloudy.
	    - **Onset:** 1–2 hours.
	    - **Peak:** 8–10 hours.
	    - **Duration:** 12–20 hours.
	- **Long-Acting Insulins**:
	    
	    - **Examples:** Insulin glargine, Insulin detemir.
	    - **Appearance:** Clear.
	    - **Onset:** 1–4 hours.
	    - **Peak:** Peakless (glargine) or 20-24 hours (detemir), offering a smooth profile.
	    - **Duration:** 20–24 hours.
	- **Ultra Long-Acting Insulins**:
	    
	    - **Example:** Insulin degludec.
	    - Provides a **flat plasma glucose lowering effect lasting for approximately 40 hours**.
	    - Suitable for meeting basal insulin requirements.
	
	### b. Advantages of Rapid-Acting Insulins
	
	Rapid-acting insulin analogues (e.g., insulin lispro, insulin aspart, insulin glulisine) offer several advantages over traditional regular insulin, particularly in mimicking physiological insulin release and improving glycemic control:
	
	- **Mimics Physiological Release:** They more closely **mimic the body's natural insulin release pattern after a meal**.
	- **Faster Onset:** They have a **quick onset of action** (0.2-0.4 hours), allowing for injection closer to mealtime (0-20 minutes before a meal), which is a significant improvement over regular insulin needing to be injected 1 hour before a meal.
	- **Defined Peak and Shorter Duration:** They achieve a **more defined and rapid peak concentration**, and have a **shorter total duration of action** (3-5 hours). This helps cover the postprandial glucose rise effectively.
	- **Improved Postprandial Glycemia:** This profile leads to **better control of meal-time glycemia**.
	- **Reduced Late Postprandial Hypoglycemia:** The shorter duration reduces the risk of **late postprandial hypoglycaemia**, which can occur with longer-acting regular insulin due to a mismatch between insulin availability and glucose absorption.
	- **Minimized Variability:** Individual variability in absorption is minimised.
	- **Reduced Hypoglycemic Episodes:** Compared to regular insulin, regimens using rapid-acting insulins have been associated with **fewer overall hypoglycaemic episodes**.
	- **Flexibility:** Their rapid action allows for greater flexibility in meal timing, as patients can inject closer to, or even immediately after, eating.
	- **Improved HbA1c:** They can lead to a slightly greater reduction in HbA1c compared to regular insulin.
	- **Suitability for Pumps:** Fast-acting insulin analogues are used in insulin pumps for continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII).

5. A 52-year-old obese male attended endocrinology department with polyuria, polyphagia, polydipsia and generalized weakness. His random blood sugar is 200 mg%, HbA1c is 8%. He is not on any other medication. He was diagnosed to be suffering from type-2 diabetes mellitus.

	The patient's presentation of polyuria, polyphagia, polydipsia, weight loss, random blood sugar of 200 mg%, and HbA1c of 8% is consistent with a diagnosis of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus. Effective management relies on understanding various pharmacotherapeutic options.
	
	### a. Classification of Oral Antidiabetic Drugs (OADs)
	
	Oral antidiabetic drugs are primarily used to treat Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus. They are classified based on their main mechanisms of action:
	
	- **I. Enhance Insulin Secretion (Insulin Secretagogues)**: These drugs stimulate the pancreatic β-cells to release insulin.
	    
	    - **Sulfonylureas (SUs)**:
	        - **Mechanism**: Bind to specific "sulfonylurea receptors" (SUR1) on pancreatic β-cells, closing ATP-sensitive K-channels (K$_\text{ATP}$) leading to depolarization, Ca$^{2+}$ influx, and insulin release.
	        - **Examples**: Tolbutamide (first generation), Glibenclamide (Glyburide), Glipizide, Gliclazide, Glimepiride (second generation).
	    - **Meglitinide/Phenylalanine Analogues (K$_\text{ATP}$ Channel Blockers)**:
	        - **Mechanism**: Stimulate insulin secretion by closing K$_\text{ATP}$ channels, similar to SUs, but with faster onset and shorter duration.
	        - **Examples**: Repaglinide, Nateglinide.
	    - **Dipeptidyl Peptidase-4 (DPP-4) Inhibitors (Gliptins)**:
	        - **Mechanism**: Inhibit the enzyme DPP-4, which degrades endogenous glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1). This potentiates GLP-1's action, boosting postprandial insulin release and decreasing glucagon secretion, particularly at high glucose concentrations, thus having a low risk of hypoglycaemia.
	        - **Examples**: Sitagliptin, Vildagliptin, Saxagliptin, Teneligliptin, Alogliptin, Linagliptin.
	- **II. Overcome Insulin Resistance**: These drugs improve the body's sensitivity to insulin or reduce glucose production.
	    
	    - **Biguanides (AMPK Activator)**:
	        - **Example**: **Metformin**.
	    - **Thiazolidinediones (Glitazones) (PPARγ Activator)**:
	        - **Mechanism**: Act as selective agonists for nuclear peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ), mainly in fat cells. They enhance transcription of insulin-responsive genes, reverse insulin resistance (e.g., by increasing GLUT4 expression), and suppress hepatic gluconeogenesis.
	        - **Example**: Pioglitazone. (Rosiglitazone was banned in India and withdrawn in Europe due to cardiac risk, though restrictions lifted in USA).
	    - **α-Glucosidase Inhibitors**:
	        - **Mechanism**: Competitively inhibit α-glucosidase enzymes in the small intestinal brush border, slowing down and decreasing the digestion and absorption of polysaccharides and sucrose. This reduces postprandial glycaemia.
	        - **Examples**: Acarbose, Miglitol, Voglibose.
	- **III. Miscellaneous Drugs**:
	    
	    - **Sodium-Glucose Cotransport-2 (SGLT-2) Inhibitors (Gliflozins)**:
	        - **Mechanism**: Inhibit SGLT-2, the major transporter responsible for glucose reabsorption in the proximal renal tubules, leading to glucosuria and lower blood glucose levels, and also weight loss.
	        - **Examples**: Dapagliflozin, Canagliflozin.
	    - **Dopamine D2 Agonist**:
	        - **Example**: Bromocriptine (quick release oral formulation).
	    - **Bile Acid Sequestrant**:
	        - **Example**: Colesevelam.

	### c. Drug Treatment of Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)
	
	Diabetic ketoacidosis is a severe complication of insulin deficiency, primarily seen in Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus, though it can occur in Type 2 DM. Treatment principles remain the same, regardless of severity, with close monitoring of vital signs, plasma glucose, blood pH, and electrolytes.
	
	1. **Insulin**:
	    
	    - **Regular insulin** is used to rapidly correct metabolic abnormalities.
	    - **Dosage**: A bolus dose of **0.1-0.2 U/kg intravenously (i.v.)** is followed by a continuous infusion of **0.1 U/kg/hour i.v.**.
	    - The infusion rate is doubled if no significant fall in blood glucose occurs within 2 hours.
	    - A fall in blood glucose level by 10% per hour is considered an adequate response.
	2. **Fluid and Electrolyte Management**:
	    
	    - **Fluid Replacement**: Patients are typically dehydrated.
	        - Initial management involves i.v. infusion of **0.9% NaCl (saline)**.
	        - Once blood glucose levels fall below 250-300 mg/dL, the i.v. fluid should be changed to **5% glucose in saline** to prevent hypoglycaemia and allow for continued insulin administration.
	    - **Potassium (K$^+$) Replacement**:
	        - Despite elevated serum K$^+$ initially, total body K$^+$ is depleted.
	        - As insulin therapy is instituted and ketosis subsides, K$^+$ is driven back intracellularly, which can lead to dangerous hypokalaemia.
	        - After 2-3 hours of treatment, it is appropriate to add **10-20 mEq/hour KCl to the i.v. fluid**.
	        - K$^+$ is contraindicated if higher degrees of AV block are present.
	3. **Sodium Bicarbonate**:
	    
	    - **Not routinely needed**, as acidosis usually subsides as ketosis is controlled.
	    - Only given if arterial blood pH is **below 7.0**, or if acidosis is not corrected spontaneously, or if hyperventilation is exhausting.
	    - **Dosage**: **50 mEq** of sodium bicarbonate is added to the i.v. fluid.
	    - Bicarbonate infusion is discontinued once the blood pH rises above 7.1.
	4. **Phosphate**:
	    
	    - When serum PO$_4$ is in the low-normal range, **3-4 mmol/hour of potassium phosphate infusion** is advocated.
	    - Routine use is generally not required, and faster infusion can precipitate tetany.
	5. **Antibiotics and Other Supportive Measures**:
	    
	    - Antibiotics and other supportive measures, as well as treatment of any precipitating cause (e.g., infection), must be instituted simultaneously.
