1.  25-year-old lady presents with symptoms of anxiety, tremors, weight loss, protrusion of eyes and palpitations. A diagnosis of Grave's disease Was made. She was prescribed propylthiouracil. Write the mechanism of action, adverse effects and uses of the propylthiouracil.
	Here's a concise, high-yield summary of Propylthiouracil (PTU), relevant for medical school examinations, specifically in the context of Graves' disease:
	
	**Propylthiouracil (PTU) in Graves' Disease**
	
	**Background:** The patient's symptoms – anxiety, tremors, weight loss, eye protrusion (exophthalmos), and palpitations – are classic manifestations of **thyrotoxicosis** caused by Graves' disease, an autoimmune disorder where thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulins activate TSH receptors, leading to excessive thyroid hormone secretion.
	
	**1. Mechanism of Action:** Propylthiouracil (a thioamide antithyroid drug) acts primarily by:
	
	- **Inhibiting Thyroid Peroxidase**: This enzyme is crucial for:
	    - Preventing the **oxidation of iodide**.
	    - Inhibiting the **iodination of tyrosine residues** in thyroglobulin.
	    - Inhibiting the **coupling of iodotyrosine residues** (MIT and DIT) to form triiodothyronine (T3) and thyroxine (T4).
	- **Inhibiting Peripheral T4 to T3 Conversion**: PTU specifically inhibits the Type I deiodinase enzyme, which **reduces the conversion of T4 (less active) to T3 (more active)** in peripheral tissues. This action is not seen with carbimazole.
	- **Delayed Onset**: PTU does not affect the release of pre-formed T3 and T4. Therefore, its effects become apparent only after the thyroid gland depletes its stored hormone content, typically **1-2 weeks or more**.
	
	**2. Adverse Effects:**
	
	- **Hypothyroidism & Goitre**: Can occur with overtreatment due to excessive TSH release from reduced feedback inhibition. This is generally **reversible** upon dose adjustment or discontinuation.
	- **Gastrointestinal (Common)**: Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, G.I. intolerance, loose motions.
	- **Skin Reactions**: Skin rashes, pruritus, photosensitivity, rarely loss or graying of hair.
	- **Musculoskeletal**: Joint pain, muscle cramps, body ache.
	- **Hepatic Toxicity (Serious)**: **Liver damage, including severe hepatitis, is a significant risk**, especially with PTU.
	- **Haematologic (Serious)**: **Agranulocytosis** (rare, 1 in 500-1000 cases, but potentially fatal; mostly reversible). Partial cross-reactivity exists between PTU and carbimazole.
	- **Other**: Fever, loss of taste, headache, dizziness, fatigue, malaise, confusion.
	
	**3. Uses:**
	
	- **Definitive Therapy for Thyrotoxicosis (Graves' Disease)**: PTU is used to control hyperthyroidism. Remission can occur in up to 50% of Graves' disease patients after 1-2 years of treatment, allowing for drug withdrawal. It is **preferred in young patients with a short history of Graves' disease and a small goitre**.
	- **Preoperative Preparation for Thyroidectomy**: Used to render hyperthyroid patients euthyroid before surgery to reduce surgical risk.
	- **Conjunction with Radioactive Iodine (131I)**: Administered before and after 131I therapy to control severe hyperthyroidism and prevent an initial flare-up of the disease due to hormone release after radioiodine administration.
	- **Thyroid Storm (Thyrotoxic Crisis)**: A critical indication for PTU due to its ability to **rapidly reduce hormone synthesis and, importantly, inhibit the peripheral conversion of T4 to T3**, which is crucial in this life-threatening emergency.
	- **Early Pregnancy**: PTU is often **preferred in early pregnancy** due to its lower placental transfer compared to carbimazole, minimizing foetal exposure.

	**Radioactive Iodine (Iodine 131)**
	
	**Mechanism of Action**:
	
	- Radioactive iodine (I-131) behaves chemically similar to stable iodine.
	- It is **selectively concentrated by the thyroid gland** and subsequently **incorporated into the colloid**.
	- I-131 emits **β particles (electrons)**, which are primarily responsible for its therapeutic (destructive) effect.
	- These β particles have a **short tissue penetration range of only 0.5-2 mm**, ensuring that the radiation primarily affects the **thyroid follicular cells from within**.
	- This highly localized radiation induces **pyknosis and necrosis of the thyroid cells, followed by fibrosis**. This process effectively leads to **partial ablation of the thyroid tissue without causing significant damage to neighbouring tissues**.
	- I-131 also emits γ-rays, which are utilized for diagnostic tracer studies.
	
	**Precautions**:
	
	- To mitigate a potential **initial flare-up of hyperthyroidism** caused by the release of stored thyroid hormones (T4) following I-131 administration, it is often necessary to **pre-treat the patient with antithyroid drugs (e.g., carbimazole)**.
	- The antithyroid medication should typically be discontinued 1-2 weeks before the I-131 dose and then resumed 5-7 days afterwards, gradually tapering off over approximately 3 months as the therapeutic effect of I-131 manifests.
	- This combined therapeutic approach is particularly favoured for **older patients and those with pre-existing heart disease** who require rapid control of severe hyperthyroidism.
	
	**Indications**:
	
	- The most common indication for therapeutic I-131 is **hyperthyroidism**, specifically in cases of **Graves' disease** or **toxic nodular goiter**.
	- It is also employed as **palliative therapy for metastatic thyroid carcinoma** (particularly papillary or follicular types that retain the ability to concentrate iodine), typically **following thyroidectomy**. This application often necessitates much higher doses of I-131 and may require prior thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) stimulation.
	
	**Merits (Advantages)**:
	
	- **Simplicity and convenience**: The treatment is straightforward and can often be administered on an **outpatient basis**, making it very convenient for patients.
	- **Cost-effectiveness**: It is generally an **inexpensive** treatment modality.
	- **Avoidance of surgical risks**: I-131 therapy eliminates the need for surgery, thus avoiding surgical risks, scarring, or potential injury to adjacent structures like the parathyroid glands or recurrent laryngeal nerves.
	- **Permanent cure**: Once hyperthyroidism is successfully controlled, I-131 often leads to a **permanent resolution** of the condition.
	
	**Demerits (Disadvantages)**:
	
	- **Slow onset of action**: The clinical response to I-131 is not immediate; it typically **begins to appear after 2 weeks** and **reaches its peak effect around 3 months** post-administration.
	- **Need for repeat doses**: Approximately **20-40% of patients may require one or more repeat doses** to achieve adequate control.
	- **High incidence of delayed hypothyroidism**: This is a significant limitation.
	    - Around **5-10% of patients with Graves' disease treated with I-131 develop hypothyroidism each year** following treatment.
	    - **Over 50% of these patients may ultimately necessitate lifelong supplemental thyroxine therapy**.
	    - The overall incidence of **eventual hypothyroidism is common, potentially affecting up to 80% of patients after 15 years**.
	- **Dose variability**: Higher doses of I-131 are generally required to treat toxic multi-nodular goiter compared to Graves' disease.

3. Drug treatment of Thyrotoxic crisis
	**Thyrotoxic Crisis (Thyroid Storm)**
	
	**Clinical Context:**
	
	- A **life-threatening medical emergency** due to **decompensated hyperthyroidism**.
	- Requires **vigorous and immediate treatment**.
	
	**Drug Treatment Modalities:**
	
	1. **Propylthiouracil (PTU)**
	    
	    - **Mechanism of Action**:
	        - **Inhibits thyroid peroxidase** enzyme, thereby **blocking the synthesis** of thyroid hormones (T3 and T4). This involves preventing iodide oxidation, iodination of tyrosine residues, and coupling of iodotyrosines.
	        - **Crucially, it inhibits the peripheral conversion of T4 (thyroxine) to T3 (triiodothyronine)** by inhibiting Type I deiodinase. This direct reduction of the more active T3 is a **key advantage** of PTU in thyroid storm compared to carbimazole.
	    - **Dosage**: **200-300 mg orally every 6 hours**.
	    - **Clinical Relevance**: Preferred in early pregnancy due to lower placental transfer.
	2. **Beta-Adrenergic Blockers (e.g., Propranolol)**
	    
	    - **Mechanism of Action**:
	        - Rapidly **alleviate the manifestations of sympathetic overactivity** common in thyrotoxicosis, such as **palpitation, nervousness, tremor, severe myopathy, and sweating**.
	        - **High doses of propranolol can reduce the peripheral conversion of T4 to T3**.
	        - They have **minimal direct effect on thyroid hormone synthesis or metabolism**.
	    - **Dosage**: Propranolol **1-2 mg slow intravenous (i.v.)**, followed by **40-80 mg orally every 6 hours**.
	    - **Clinical Relevance**: Considered the **most valuable measure** for **dramatic symptomatic relief** in thyroid storm.
	3. **Iodine and Iodides (e.g., Lugol's solution, Iopanoic acid/Ipodate)**
	    
	    - **Mechanism of Action**:
	        - Acts as the **fastest-acting thyroid inhibitor**.
	        - **Inhibits the release of pre-formed thyroid hormones** (T3 and T4) from the gland – often termed "thyroid constipation".
	        - Also **decreases peripheral T4 to T3 conversion**.
	        - Causes the thyroid gland to **shrink, become firmer, and less vascular**, which is beneficial for surgical preparation.
	    - **Dosage**: Lugol's iodine **6-10 drops** orally or iodine-containing radiocontrast media (iopanoic acid/ipodate **0.5-1 g orally once daily**, given one hour after propylthiouracil).
	4. **Corticosteroids (e.g., Hydrocortisone, Prednisolone)**
	    
	    - **Mechanism of Action**:
	        - Help patients **tide over the crisis**.
	        - **Address potential concomitant adrenal insufficiency**, which is common in thyroid storm.
	        - **Reduce peripheral T4 to T3 conversion**.
	    - **Dosage**: **Hydrocortisone 100 mg i.v. every 8 hours**, followed by oral prednisolone.
	5. **Diltiazem** (Calcium Channel Blocker)
	    
	    - **Mechanism of Action**: Can be added if **tachycardia is not adequately controlled by propranolol alone**, or if beta-blockers are **contraindicated**.
	    - **Dosage**: **60-120 mg orally twice daily**.
	
	**Supportive Measures**:
	
	- **Rehydration**.
	- **Anxiolytics** to manage severe anxiety and agitation.
	- **External cooling** to reduce hyperthermia.
	- **Appropriate antibiotics** if an infection is a precipitating factor.
