1. A 64-year-old woman is treated for carcinoma of breast with metastases to bone and liver. Now, she complaints of severe pain in her limbs and requests for pain medication. The attending doctor decided to prescribe morphine. 
	1. a. Explain the rationale of using morphine in this patient; outline its mechanism of action. 
	2. b. Enumerate the therapeutic uses and adverse effects of morphine. 
	3. c. Discuss the drug treatment of morphine dependence.
	**Clinical Scenario:** 64-year-old female with metastatic breast carcinoma (bone, liver) and severe limb pain. Morphine is prescribed.
	
	**a. Rationale for Morphine Use & Mechanism of Action**
	
	- **Rationale:**
	    - Patient has **severe pain** due to **metastatic cancer**.
	    - Opioid analgesics, including morphine, are indicated for **severe pain of any type**.
	    - For **pain of terminal illness (e.g., cancer pain)**, opioids **should not be restricted**, and dependence risk is a lesser concern.
	    - Morphine is a **strong analgesic** with **high ceiling efficacy**, effective in **mitigating even severe pain**.
	    - Oral morphine in **continuous release formulation** is often used for **chronic terminal illness pain**. Transdermal fentanyl is also frequently used.
	- **Mechanism of Action:**
	    - Morphine acts by interacting with **specific opioid receptors** on neurones in the Central Nervous System (CNS) and peripheral tissues.
	    - It is a **full agonist**, primarily at the **µ (mu) opioid receptor**, for which it has the **highest affinity**.
	    - Also has **weak agonist activity** at **K (kappa) and δ (delta) receptors**, but effects are **primarily due to µ receptor activation**.
	    - **Cellular Level:**
	        - Opioid receptors are coupled to **Gi proteins**, inhibiting **adenylyl cyclase** and decreasing cAMP.
	        - They **block N type Ca++ channels**, decreasing the release of excitatory neurotransmitters (like glutamate and neuropeptides) from primary nociceptive afferents in the spinal cord.
	        - On postjunctional neurons, they activate **K+ channels**, causing hyperpolarization and neuronal inhibition.
	    - **Sites & Analgesia:**
	        - Acts at both **spinal and supraspinal sites**.
	        - **Spinal:** In the **substantia gelatinosa of the dorsal horn**, inhibiting pain impulse transmission. Mediated by µ2, δ, and K1 receptors.
	        - **Supraspinal:** In areas like periaqueductal gray, thalamus, medulla, limbic, and cortical areas, altering pain processing and augmenting descending inhibitory pathways (aminergic, GABAergic). Mediated by µ1 receptors.
	        - **Beyond Perception:** Suppresses not just pain perception, but also the **emotional component** (suffering, distress, anxiety), allowing the patient to **tolerate pain better**.
	
	**b. Therapeutic Uses & Adverse Effects of Morphine**
	
	- **Therapeutic Uses:**
	    - **Analgesia for Severe Pain:** Trauma, visceral pain, ischaemic pain (MI), postoperative pain, burn pain, **cancer pain**, renal colic. Use is liberal in terminal illness pain.
	    - Preanaesthetic medication (selected cases).
	    - Component of balanced anaesthesia and surgical analgesia.
	    - Relief of anxiety and apprehension (e.g., in MI). (Note: Not for general anxiety/insomnia).
	    - Acute Left Ventricular Failure / Acute Pulmonary Edema (IV route).
	    - Cough suppression (Codeine/substitutes more common).
	    - Diarrhoea control (Codeine, Loperamide, Diphenoxylate).
	    - Epidural or Intrathecal injection for segmental analgesia in surgery, labour, postoperative, **cancer**, or intractable pain.
	- **Adverse Effects:**
	    - **Common:**
	        - **Sedation, mental clouding, lethargy**.
	        - **Constipation** (very common and distressing; tolerance does NOT develop).
	        - Nausea and vomiting (especially with ambulation).
	        - **Respiratory depression** (dose-dependent, most serious side effect, risk increased in elderly, respiratory/liver/kidney disease). Death in overdose due to respiratory failure.
	        - **Miosis (pinpoint pupils)** (tolerance does NOT develop).
	        - Urinary retention (especially in elderly males).
	        - Hypotension, particularly postural hypotension and fainting (due to vasodilation/histamine release/impaired reflexes, risk increased in hypovolaemia).
	        - Pruritus (itching).
	    - **Less Common/Serious:**
	        - Histamine release: Urticaria, swelling, local reaction, generalized itching, **bronchoconstriction** (dangerous in asthmatics).
	        - Bradycardia (usual effect due to vagal stimulation).
	        - Increased intracranial tension (due to CO2 retention causing cerebral vasodilation; contraindicated in head injury).
	        - Truncal rigidity/immobility (high IV doses).
	        - Lowers seizure threshold, may precipitate fits/convulsions (especially high doses/poisoning).
	        - Biliary spasm and colic (increases intrabiliary pressure).
	        - Increased tone of urinary detrusor/sphincter and ureter contractions.
	        - Prolonged labour (occasionally).
	        - Apnoea of the newborn if given during labour.
	
	**c. Drug Treatment of Morphine Dependence**
	
	- Morphine produces **pronounced addiction and dependence**, with a **high abuse liability**.
	- Withdrawal of morphine in a dependent subject leads to a **marked withdrawal syndrome** (including drug-seeking, lacrimation, sweating, yawning, anxiety, muscle spasms, GI symptoms, BP changes).
	- Opioid antagonists like naloxone can **precipitate acute withdrawal** in dependent subjects.
	- Treatment of morphine dependence involves:
	    - **Withdrawal of morphine**.
	    - **Substitution with oral methadone**.
	    - Methadone is a **long-acting, orally effective** opioid.
	    - After substitution, methadone is **gradually withdrawn**. The methadone withdrawal syndrome is prolonged but typically more bearable than abrupt morphine withdrawal.
	    - An alternative approach is **long-term methadone maintenance therapy** (typically 40-80 mg/day orally) in opioid addicts. This creates a high level of tolerance that blocks the euphoric effects of injected morphine or heroin, helping the individual discontinue illicit use.
	    - **Buprenorphine**, a partial µ agonist/K antagonist, is also used as an alternative to methadone for the treatment of morphine dependence.
	- Craving for the opioid may persist long after withdrawal, and relapse rates are high.

2. Classify the opioid analgesics. Write the pharmacological actions (on CNS and smooth muscles with their clinical implications) therapeutic uses, adverse effects and contraindications of morphine
	**Opioid Analgesics: Classification, Morphine Actions, Uses, Adverse Effects & Contraindications**
	
	**Classification of Opioid Analgesics**
	
	- **A. Opioid/narcotic/morphine-like analgesics**
	- **B. Nonopioid/non-narcotic/aspirin-like/antipyretic or antiinflammatory analgesics** (not discussed here)
	- **C. Adjuvant analgesics** (e.g., anticonvulsants, antidepressants - used for neuropathic pain, not typical opioid indications)
	
	Opioid analgesics are further categorised based on origin and receptor interaction:
	
	1. **Natural opium alkaloids:** Morphine, Codeine
	2. **Semi synthetic opioids:** Diacetylmorphine (Heroin), Pholcodine, Ethylmorphine
	3. **Synthetic opioids:** Pethidine, Methadone, Fentanyl, Remifentanil, Tramadol, Tapentadol
	4. **Complex Action Opioids (Agonist-Antagonists & Partial Agonists):** Nalorphine, Pentazocine, Butorphanol, Nalbuphine, Buprenorphine
	5. **Pure Opioid Antagonists:** Naloxone, Naltrexone, Nalmefene, Methyl naltrexone, Alvimopan
	
	**Morphine: Pharmacological Actions**
	
	Morphine is the **prototype** opioid, acting primarily as a **full agonist at the µ (mu) opioid receptor**. It also has **weak agonist activity** at K (kappa) and δ (delta) receptors, but µ receptor activation is primary. Opioid receptors are **Gi protein-coupled**, inhibiting adenylyl cyclase, decreasing cAMP, blocking N type Ca++ channels, and opening K+ channels, leading to neuronal inhibition and reduced neurotransmitter release.
	
	- **CNS Actions:**
	    - **Analgesia (Strong):** Relieves severe pain, including visceral and somatic pain. Acts at **spinal** (dorsal horn, inhibiting excitatory transmitters like glutamate) and **supraspinal** sites (altering pain processing, augmenting descending inhibitory pathways). Suppresses pain perception and its **emotional component** (suffering, distress, anxiety), improving pain tolerance. Peripheral action on primary afferents also contributes in trauma/burns.
	    - **Sedation:** Drowsiness, mental dullness, indifference. Dose-dependent, can lead to sleep and coma in higher doses. Differs from hypnotic sedation.
	    - **Subjective Effects/Euphoria:** Calming, detachment, lack of initiative, warm/heavy sensation, mental clouding. Perceived as pleasurable ("high") in pain/anxiety patients and addicts, especially with rapid IV injection ("kick" or "rush"). Euphoric effects likely mediated by DA release in nucleus accumbens.
	    - **Respiratory Depression:** **Dose-dependent** depression of the respiratory centre (rate and tidal volume decreased). **Most serious side effect**. Can be marked with other sedatives or in patients with respiratory/liver/kidney disease. **Death in overdose is due to respiratory failure**. Suppresses neurogenic, hypercapnic, and hypoxic drives.
	    - **Cough Suppression:** Depresses cough centre, which is more sensitive than the respiratory centre. (Note: Codeine and substitutes are more commonly used clinically).
	    - **Temperature Regulation:** Depresses hypothalamic thermostatic centre; hypothermia possible in cold environments.
	    - **Vasomotor Centre Depression:** Contributes to BP fall at higher doses.
	    - **CTZ Stimulation:** Sensitises medullary Chemoreceptor Trigger Zone (CTZ), causing **nausea and vomiting** (especially with ambulation or full stomach). Larger doses directly depress vomiting centre.
	    - **Miosis (Pinpoint Pupils):** Stimulates Edinger-Westphal nucleus. **Tolerance does NOT develop** to this action; addicts have constricted pupils. Useful sign of opioid effect/poisoning.
	    - **Vagal Centre Stimulation:** Usually causes **bradycardia**.
	    - **Cortical/Hippocampal Stimulation:** Certain areas stimulated. Truncal rigidity can occur at high IV doses, potentially hampering respiration. **Lowers seizure threshold**; convulsions possible in poisoning.
	- **Neuro-endocrine Actions:** Reduces hypothalamic influence on pituitary. Lowers FSH, LH, ACTH, sex hormones, and cortisol. Raises prolactin and GH. Can release ADH, reducing urine volume.
	- **Peripheral Actions (Smooth Muscles & CVS):**
	    - **GIT:** Markedly affects motility and fluid dynamics. **Constipation** is a **prominent and very common feature**. **Tolerance does NOT develop** to constipation. Increases tone/segmentation, decreases propulsive movements. Increases tone of pyloric, ileocaecal, and anal sphincters. Decreases GI secretions and increases fluid absorption.
	    - **Sphincter of Oddi Spasm:** Increases intrabiliary pressure, potentially causing biliary colic. Counteracted by naloxone or nitrates. (Note: Pentazocine, buprenorphine less likely to cause this).
	    - **Urinary Tract:** Increases tone of detrusor and bladder sphincter, causing urgency and difficulty urinating. Enhances ureter contractions.
	    - **Uterus:** Generally insignificant effect on uterine muscle, but may occasionally prolong labour.
	    - **Bronchioles:** Causes **bronchoconstriction** by **histamine release** (non-immunological). **Dangerous in asthmatics**.
	    - **CVS:** **Vasodilatation** (histamine release, vasomotor centre depression, direct action). Little BP change in recumbent, normovolaemic patients, but **postural hypotension and fainting** can occur due to venodilatation and impaired reflexes. Cardiac rate usually decreases (vagal stimulation) but can reflexly increase if BP falls. Reduces cardiac work (decreased peripheral resistance). **Increased intracranial tension** due to CO2 retention and cerebral vasodilation.
	
	**Morphine: Therapeutic Uses**
	
	- **Analgesia for Severe Pain:** **Primary indication**. Used in trauma, visceral pain, ischaemic pain (especially MI), postoperative pain, burn pain, **cancer pain**, renal colic. **Liberal use in terminal illness pain (e.g., cancer) is indicated and dependence risk is a lesser concern**.
	- **Preanaesthetic medication** (selected cases).
	- Component of **balanced anaesthesia and surgical analgesia**. (Fentanyl/remifentanil more common).
	- Relief of **anxiety and apprehension** (e.g., in MI). (Note: Not for general anxiety/insomnia).
	- **Acute Left Ventricular Failure / Acute Pulmonary Edema (IV route):** Provides dramatic relief by reducing preload (vasodilation/pooling), shifting blood from pulmonary to systemic circulation, allaying dyspnoea (depressing respiratory centre), and reducing cardiac work (calming patient, reducing sympathetic stimulation).
	- Cough suppression (Codeine/substitutes preferred).
	- Diarrhoea control (Codeine, Loperamide, Diphenoxylate preferred).
	- **Epidural or Intrathecal Injection:** For **segmental analgesia** in surgery (abdominal, limb, pelvic), labour, postoperative pain, **cancer pain**, intractable pain.
	
	**Morphine: Adverse Effects**
	
	- **Common:**
	    - **Sedation, mental clouding, lethargy**.
	    - **Constipation** (very common and distressing; tolerance does NOT develop).
	    - Nausea and vomiting.
	    - **Respiratory depression** (dose-dependent, most serious; tolerance develops).
	    - **Miosis (pinpoint pupils)** (tolerance does NOT develop).
	    - Urinary retention (especially elderly male).
	    - Hypotension, particularly postural hypotension and fainting (due to vasodilation/impaired reflexes, increased risk in hypovolaemia).
	    - Pruritus (itching, due to histamine release and/or spinal pruritoceptive neuron activation).
	- **Less Common/Serious:**
	    - Histamine release: Urticaria, swelling, local reaction, generalized itching, **bronchoconstriction** (dangerous in asthmatics).
	    - Bradycardia (usual effect).
	    - Increased intracranial tension (due to CO2 retention causing cerebral vasodilation; **contraindicated in head injury**).
	    - Truncal rigidity/immobility (high IV doses).
	    - Lowers seizure threshold, may precipitate fits/convulsions (especially high doses/poisoning).
	    - Biliary spasm and colic (increases intrabiliary pressure).
	    - Increased tone of urinary detrusor/sphincter and ureter contractions.
	    - Prolonged labour (occasionally).
	    - Apnoea of the newborn if given during labour. Naloxone is the treatment.
	    - Tolerance and Dependence: High degree of tolerance and pronounced addiction/dependence can develop with repeated use. Abuse liability is high.
	
	**Morphine: Contraindications and Precautions**
	
	- **Infants and the elderly:** More susceptible to respiratory depression.
	- **Respiratory insufficiency:** Dangerous in emphysema, pulmonary fibrosis, cor pulmonale; accentuates sleep apnoea.
	- **Bronchial asthma:** Can precipitate attack via histamine release. Fentanyl (lower histamine release) may be used if unavoidable.
	- **Head injury:** **Contraindicated** due to increased intracranial tension (CO2 retention) and interference with assessment of progress (vomiting, miosis, altered mentation).
	- **Hypotensive states and hypovolaemia:** Exaggerate BP fall.
	- **Undiagnosed acute abdominal pain:** Can aggravate conditions like diverticulitis, biliary colic, pancreatitis; can mask signs. Should be given **after** diagnosis is established.
	- **Elderly male:** High chance of urinary retention.
	- **Hypothyroidism, liver and kidney disease:** Increased sensitivity to morphine.
	- **Unstable personalities:** Higher risk of addiction.
	
	**Drug Interactions:** Potentiated by phenothiazines, tricyclic antidepressants, MAO inhibitors, amphetamine, neostigmine. Exaggerated CNS depression with sedative-hypnotics. Retards absorption of oral drugs by delaying gastric emptying.
	
	**Treatment of Morphine Dependence:**
	
	- Involves **withdrawal of morphine**.
	- **Substitution with oral methadone** (long-acting, orally effective opioid).
	- **Gradual withdrawal of methadone**. Methadone withdrawal is prolonged but more bearable than abrupt morphine withdrawal.
	- **Long-term methadone maintenance therapy** (40-80 mg/day orally) can be used in addicts to create tolerance and block euphoric effects of illicit opioids, aiding discontinuation.
	- **Buprenorphine** (partial µ agonist) is also used as an alternative to methadone for dependence treatment.
	- Craving and high relapse rates can persist.
	- Opioid antagonists (naloxone, nalorphine) precipitate acute withdrawal in dependent subjects. Naloxone is the specific antidote for acute overdose/poisoning.
3. A 70-year-old patient suffering from lung carcinoma with metastases is requesting for analgesics, the doctor decided to prescribe morphine for pain relief.
		a. Explain the mechanism of analgesic action of morphine in this patient.
		b. Mention the contraindications for morphine.
	**Morphine Analgesia & Contraindications**
	
	**a. Mechanism of Analgesic Action of Morphine in Cancer Pain**
	
	Morphine is a **strong opioid analgesic**. In this patient with lung carcinoma and metastases, morphine relieves severe pain primarily by acting as a **full agonist at the µ (mu) opioid receptor**. It also has weak agonist activity at K (kappa) and δ (delta) receptors, but µ receptor activation is dominant.
	
	The analgesic action involves multiple sites:
	
	- **Spinal Analgesia:** Morphine acts in the **substantia gelatinosa of the dorsal horn** of the spinal cord. It inhibits the release of excitatory neurotransmitters (e.g., **glutamate, substance P**) from primary afferents carrying pain impulses. This is mediated through **interneurones involved in pain gating**. Activation of **µ2 receptors** is primarily responsible for spinal analgesia. Intrathecal morphine demonstrates this **segmental analgesia**.
	- **Supraspinal Analgesia:** Morphine acts at sites like the medulla, periaqueductal gray, limbic, and cortical areas. This alters the processing and interpretation of pain impulses. Activation of **µ1 receptors** is primarily responsible for supraspinal analgesia. Supraspinal action also augments **descending inhibitory pathways** to the spinal cord, further suppressing pain.
	- **Emotional Component Relief:** Critically for cancer pain, morphine and other opioids suppress both the perception of pain AND its **emotional component** (suffering, distress, anxiety, fear). The patient tolerates the pain better. This is a key difference from non-opioid analgesics. Relief of anxiety and apprehension is a known therapeutic use, especially in conditions like myocardial infarction.
	- **Peripheral Action:** Opioids can also act on small primary afferent terminals in injured tissues, potentially contributing to analgesia in trauma or burns. While less prominent than central actions, this might play a role in pain from metastatic disease.
	
	Opioid receptors are **Gi protein-coupled receptors** located mostly on prejunctional neurons. Their activation inhibits adenylyl cyclase (decreasing cAMP), opens K+ channels, and closes voltage-gated Ca2+ channels. This leads to neuronal hyperpolarization and reduced release of neurotransmitters, including those involved in pain signaling (e.g., glutamate from nociceptive afferents). Postjunctional receptors also exist, causing inhibition via K+ channel opening.
	
	In summary, morphine works by reducing pain signaling at multiple levels of the nervous system and altering the emotional response to pain, which is highly relevant for managing the severe pain associated with advanced cancer. For terminal illness pain like cancer, liberal use of morphine is indicated without undue concern for dependence risk.
	
	**b. Contraindications for Morphine**
	
	While indicated for severe cancer pain in this patient, certain conditions or factors serve as **contraindications or require precautions** for morphine use.
	
	- **Infants and the elderly:** The elderly are **more susceptible to respiratory depression**. This 70-year-old patient's age increases this risk.
	- **Respiratory insufficiency:** Morphine is **dangerous** in patients with conditions like emphysema, pulmonary fibrosis, or cor pulmonale, and can accentuate sleep apnoea. Given the patient has lung carcinoma, underlying respiratory compromise is highly likely, making this a significant precaution.
	- **Bronchial asthma:** Morphine can precipitate an attack due to **histamine release**. A patient with lung carcinoma may have co-existing or related airway hyperreactivity. Fentanyl, with lower histamine release potential, might be considered if an opioid is unavoidable.
	- **Head injury:** **Contraindicated** due to increased intracranial tension (from CO2 retention causing cerebral vasodilation) and masking of neurological signs (vomiting, miosis, altered mentation).
	- **Hypotensive states and hypovolaemia:** These exaggerate the **fall in BP** caused by morphine's vasodilatory effects.
	- **Undiagnosed acute abdominal pain:** Morphine can mask symptoms and aggravate conditions like diverticulitis, biliary colic, or pancreatitis. In a patient with metastases, new abdominal pain could indicate such complications and requires diagnosis before opioid administration.
	- **Elderly male:** There is a **high chance of urinary retention**. The patient's age and gender increase this risk.
	- **Hypothyroidism, liver and kidney disease:** Patients with these conditions are **more sensitive** to morphine's effects. Liver disease would impair metabolism and kidney disease would impair excretion of morphine and its active metabolite, morphine-6-glucuronide, potentially leading to accumulation and increased side effects. Metastases to the liver or kidneys could thus increase sensitivity.
	- **Unstable personalities:** Considered at higher risk of addiction. While liberal use is acceptable in terminal illness, awareness of this exists.
	- **Drug Interactions:** Potentiation of CNS depression and other effects can occur with sedatives/hypnotics, phenothiazines, tricyclic antidepressants, MAO inhibitors, etc.
4. Rationale of using morphine in acute left ventricular failure

	
	1. **Reducing preload on the heart** [48a]. This occurs due to **vasodilatation** and **peripheral pooling of blood** [32, 48a]. Morphine causes vasodilatation through histamine release, depression of the vasomotor centre, and a direct action decreasing the tone of blood vessels. This venodilatation reduces venous return (preload), thereby decreasing ventricular end-diastolic pressure and volume. In a dilated ventricle, this improves the effectiveness of myocardial fibre shortening.
	2. **Tending to shift blood from the pulmonary to systemic circuit** [48b]. This is a consequence of greater vasodilatation in the systemic circulation compared to the pulmonary circulation. This action helps to relieve pulmonary congestion and edema [32, 48b].
	3. **Allaying air hunger and dyspnoea** [48c]. Morphine achieves this by **depressing the respiratory centre** [30d, 48c].
	4. **Cutting down sympathetic stimulation** [48d]. Morphine has a **prominent calming effect** and leads to a loss of apprehension and mental clouding [29c]. By calming the patient, it reduces sympathetic overactivity, which consequently reduces cardiac work [48d, 32].
	
	Morphine is also indicated to relieve pulmonary edema due to infarction of the lung. However, it is **contraindicated in bronchial asthma**.
	
	It's worth noting that intravenous furosemide, in conjunction with vasodilators, is mentioned as having "virtually obviated the need for i.v. digoxin" and providing rapid symptomatic relief in acute left ventricular failure. Intravenous glyceryl trinitrate (GTN) is also highlighted for rapid relief in acute LVF, particularly that due to myocardial ischaemia/infarction, and sodium nitroprusside, a high efficacy intravenous dilator, may be employed with a loop diuretic and inotropic drug in severely decompensated patients. Digoxin, while historically a primary drug for heart failure, is no longer first-line and affords only symptomatic relief without modifying the disease course.
