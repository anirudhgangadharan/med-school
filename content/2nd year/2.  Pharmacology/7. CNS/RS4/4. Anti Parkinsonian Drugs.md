1. Levodopa (L-Dopa)
	
	**Levodopa (L-Dopa)**
	
	- A **dopamine precursor** used in the treatment of Parkinson's disease (PD).
	- In PD, there is a degenerative lesion in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SN-PC) which decreases dopaminergic input to the striatum, causing an imbalance between dopamine (DA) and acetylcholine (ACh). This imbalance results in hypokinesia, rigidity, and tremor.
	- Levodopa is used to **make good this DA deficiency**, as DA itself does not cross the blood-brain barrier.
	
	**Mechanism of Action**
	
	- Levodopa is inactive by itself.
	- It is the **immediate precursor of the transmitter DA**.
	- About 1-2% of administered levodopa crosses to the brain.
	- In the brain, it is **taken up by surviving dopaminergic neurones**.
	- Converted to DA in these neurones.
	- DA is then **stored and released as a transmitter**.
	- In the striatum, DA stimulates both excitatory D1-like (D1, D5) and inhibitory D2-like (D2, D3, D4) receptors.
	- Stimulation of both D1 and D2 receptors achieves the same net effect of **smoothening movements and reducing muscle tone**.
	- Brains of parkinsonian patients treated with levodopa till death had higher DA levels than those not treated, supporting this mechanism.
	
	**Pharmacological Actions**
	
	- **CNS:**
	    - Hardly produces effects in normal individuals or those with other neurological diseases.
	    - Produces **marked symptomatic improvement in parkinsonian patients**.
	    - **Hypokinesia and rigidity resolve first**, then tremor.
	    - Secondary symptoms like posture, gait, handwriting, speech, facial expression, mood, self-care, and interest in life are gradually normalized.
	    - Therapeutic benefit is nearly complete in early disease but declines as disease advances.
	    - Can cause a "general alerting response" that may progress to excitement or frank psychosis.
	    - May cause a disproportionate increase in sexual activity.
	    - Dementia does not improve; it predisposes to psychiatric symptoms.
	    - Used for a non-specific 'awakening' effect in hepatic coma.
	- **CVS:**
	    - Peripherally formed DA can cause **tachycardia** by acting on β adrenergic receptors.
	    - **Postural hypotension is quite common**. This may be a central action.
	    - Rise in BP is not seen despite DA stimulating vascular adrenergic receptors.
	
	**Pharmacokinetics**
	
	- **Rapidly absorbed** from the small intestines via active transport for aromatic amino acids.
	- Oral absorption is affected by gastric emptying (slow emptying leads to less availability) and competition with amino acids from food.
	- Undergoes **high first pass metabolism** in g.i. mucosa and liver.
	- More than 95% of an oral dose is decarboxylated in peripheral tissues (mainly gut and liver).
	- Metabolized in the brain (1-2% reaches here) by the same transformation.
	- Pyridoxal is a cofactor for dopa-decarboxylase enzyme.
	- Metabolites mostly excreted in urine after conjugation.
	- Plasma t½ is **1-2 hours**.
	
	**Adverse Effects**
	
	- Frequent and often troublesome.
	- Most are **dose-related and limit the administered dose**.
	- Usually reversible.
	- **At the initiation of therapy** (minimized by starting with low dose):
	    - **Nausea and vomiting:** Occurs in almost every patient. Tolerance develops.
	    - **Postural hypotension:** Occurs in about 1/3 of patients, mostly asymptomatic; some have dizziness or fainting attacks. More common with antihypertensives. Tolerance develops.
	    - **Cardiac arrhythmias:** Due to β adrenergic action of peripherally formed DA. More common in patients with pre-existing heart disease.
	    - **Exacerbation of angina:** More common in patients with pre-existing heart disease.
	    - Alteration in taste sensation.
	- **After prolonged therapy** (appear after months/years):
	    - **Abnormal movements (dyskinesias):** Facial tics, grimacing, tongue thrusting, choreoathetoid limb movements. Appear after a few months at optimum dose, worsen with time, practically all patients involved after a few years. Intensity corresponds with levodopa levels. No tolerance develops, but dose reduction decreases severity. Can be as disabling as the original disease and are the most important dose-limiting side effects.
	    - **Behavioural effects:** Range from mild anxiety, nightmares to severe depression, mania, hallucinations, mental confusion, or frank psychosis. Probably due to excessive DA action in the limbic system. Levodopa is **contra-indicated in patients with psychotic illness**.
	    - **Fluctuation in motor performance:** Seen after 2-5 years of therapy.
	        - 'End of dose' deterioration ('wearing off'): Initially gradual, develops into rapid 'switches' or 'on-off' effect.
	        - 'All or none' response: Patient is alternately well and disabled.
	        - Abnormal movements may jeopardize the 'on' phase.
	        - Probably reflects disease progression and loss of ability to regulate DA storage/release.
	        - Dose fractionation/frequent administration may help temporarily.
	- **Caution is needed in the elderly; patients with ischaemic heart disease; cerebrovascular, psychiatric, hepatic, and renal disease; peptic ulcer (increased bleeding risk); glaucoma and gout**.
	
	**Therapeutic Uses**
	
	- **Specific salutary effect in Parkinson's disease**.
	- **Monotherapy:** Efficacy exceeds that of any other drug used alone. Benefit declines as disease advances.
	- **Combination with decarboxylase inhibitors (Carbidopa/Benserazide):** Practically always used together now.
	    - Reduces the peripheral decarboxylation of levodopa.
	    - Minimizes nausea and vomiting.
	    - Reduces cardiac complications.
	    - Prevents pyridoxine reversal of levodopa effect.
	    - Minimizes 'on-off' effect as cerebral DA levels are more sustained.
	    - Degree of improvement may be higher.
	    - Problems not resolved or accentuated: involuntary movements (may be more pronounced) and behavioural abnormalities (appear earlier).
	    - Standard therapy now, replaced levodopa alone.
	    - Full benefit lasts about 2-3 years, then declines.
	- **Used to supplement levodopa in advanced cases** (in combination with other drugs like MAO-B inhibitors or COMT inhibitors) to smoothen motor fluctuations and abnormal movements.
	- Used to produce 'awakening' effect in hepatic coma.
	
	**Drug Interactions**
	
	- **Pyridoxine:** Abolishes therapeutic effect (if not combined with carbidopa) by enhancing peripheral decarboxylation.
	- **Phenothiazines, butyrophenones, metoclopramide:** Reverse therapeutic effect by blocking DA receptors.
	- **Domperidone:** Blocks levodopa-induced nausea/vomiting (peripheral action) without affecting antiparkinsonian effect (as it doesn't cross blood-brain barrier).
	- **Reserpine:** Abolishes action by preventing DA entry into synaptic vesicles.
	- **Nonselective MAO inhibitors:** Can cause hypertensive crisis by preventing degradation of excess peripherally synthesized DA and NA.
	- **Antihypertensive drugs:** Postural hypotension caused by levodopa is accentuated; reduce antihypertensive dose.
	- **Atropine/Antiparkinsonian anticholinergics:** Additive therapeutic action with low levodopa doses, but retard absorption (more peripheral degradation, reduced efficacy - not when combined with carbidopa).
	
	**Preparations and Dose (in combination with Carbidopa/Benserazide)**
	
	- Preparations listed include Carbidopa + Levodopa tablets/capsules and Benserazide + Levodopa capsules.
	- Usual daily maintenance dose of levodopa is **0.4-0.8 g along with 75-100 mg carbidopa or 100-200 mg benserazide**, given in 3-4 divided doses.
	- Therapy starts at a low dose, gradually increased until adequate response, then maintained. Slow and careful initiation over 2-3 months recommended.
	- Sustained/continuous release formulations (e.g., MAZETOL SR, TEGRITAL CR) are preferred to avoid high peaks and low troughs in plasma concentration.
	
	**Clinical Considerations**
	
	- Levodopa has specific efficacy in PD, acting via conversion to DA in surviving neurones.
	- Combination with decarboxylase inhibitors significantly improves efficacy and reduces early side effects, but not later complications like dyskinesias and motor fluctuations.
	- Motor fluctuations ('wearing off', 'on-off') are a significant late complication.
	- Dyskinesias are the most important dose-limiting side effect after prolonged therapy.
	- Contraindicated in psychotic illness.
	- Carbidopa/levodopa ratio in common preparations allows sufficient carbidopa to inhibit peripheral decarboxylation.
	- Slow initiation and dose titration are crucial to manage early side effects.
