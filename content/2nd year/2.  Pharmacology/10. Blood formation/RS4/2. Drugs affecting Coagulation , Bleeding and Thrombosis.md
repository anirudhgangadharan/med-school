1. Warfarin Sodium-Mechanism of anticoagulant action, adverse effects, uses and antagonist
		**Warfarin Sodium: High-Yield Notes**
	
	- **Class:** Oral Anticoagulant (Vitamin K Antagonist - VKA).
	- **Source:** Synthetic coumarin derivative. Initially used as rat poison, now principal coumarin oral anticoagulant. Racemic mixture of R and S enantiomers, S form is more potent.
	
	**Mechanism of Anticoagulant Action**
	
	- Acts **indirectly** and **only _in vivo_** (not _in vitro_).
	- Interferes with the **synthesis of Vitamin K-dependent clotting factors** (Factors II, VII, IX, X) in the liver. Factor VII has shortest t½ (6hr), Prothrombin longest (60hr), influencing onset time.
	- Behaves as a **competitive antagonist of Vitamin K**.
	- Inhibits the enzyme **Vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKOR)**.
	- This blocks the **regeneration of the active hydroquinone form of Vitamin K**.
	- Active Vitamin K hydroquinone is essential as a cofactor for **γ-glutamyl carboxylase**, which carboxy lates glutamate residues on Factors II, VII, IX, X.
	- Carboxylation is necessary for factors to bind Ca2+ and phospholipid surfaces required for the coagulation cascade.
	- Results in lower plasma levels of _functional_ clotting factors.
	- **Delayed onset of action** (1-3 days) as anticoagulant effect depends on the decline of pre-existing clotting factors.
	
	**Adverse Effects**
	
	- **Bleeding:** The most important and common complication, an extension of pharmacological action. Can be ecchymosis, epistaxis, hematuria, GI bleeding; potentially fatal intracranial or internal haemorrhages can occur. Increased risk if therapy not monitored, INR > 4, or with interacting drugs.
	- **Treatment of Bleeding:**
	    - **Withhold anticoagulant**.
	    - **Fresh blood transfusion** (supplies clotting factors and replenishes lost blood) or **Fresh frozen plasma (FFP)** (source of clotting factors).
	    - **Vitamin K1 (Phytonadione)** is the specific antidote. **Takes 6-24 hours** for factors to be resynthesized. Dose depends on severity; avoid unnecessarily high doses which cause prolonged resistance to warfarin.
	- **Other Effects:** Alopecia, dermatitis, diarrhoea. Oral ulceration (with other coumarins). Cutaneous necrosis (rare).
	- **Teratogenicity:** **ABSOLUTELY CONTRAINDICATED in pregnancy**.
	    - Early pregnancy: Increases birth defects, especially skeletal abnormalities (Fetal warfarin syndrome: hypoplasia of nose, eye socket, hand bones, growth retardation).
	    - Later pregnancy: CNS defects, fetal haemorrhage, fetal death, accentuated neonatal hypoprothrombinemia.
	
	**Uses**
	
	- Standard oral anticoagulant for **maintenance therapy**.
	- Typically follows initial rapid-acting parenteral anticoagulation (e.g., Heparin, LMW Heparin).
	- Prevention and treatment of **Venous Thromboembolism (VTE)** (DVT, PE).
	- Prevention of **stroke and systemic embolism in Atrial Fibrillation (AF)** (nonvalvular). More effective than antiplatelet drugs for this indication.
	- Prevention of thrombus formation/embolism on **Prosthetic heart valves**. Often used with antiplatelet drugs (e.g., aspirin, dipyridamole) for this indication.
	- Treatment of TIAs.
	- Not typically used as primary therapy for **acute coronary syndromes (ACS)** or **thrombotic stroke**.
	- **Dose individualization** is essential, guided by repeated measurement of **Prothrombin Time (PT)**, reported as **International Normalized Ratio (INR)**.
	- **Target INR Ranges:**
	    - Prophylaxis of DVT: **2-2.5**.
	    - Treatment of DVT/PE, TIAs, hip surgery: **2-3**.
	    - Recurrent thromboembolism, arterial disease (MI), Prosthetic heart valves: **3-3.5**.
	
	**Antagonist**
	
	- The specific antagonist is **Vitamin K1 (Phytonadione)**.
	- Available as oral or injectable (i.m. or i.v., though i.v. carries risk of anaphylactoid reaction and should generally be avoided unless specific emulsified formulation is used).
	- Restores clotting factors over **6-24 hours**.
	- Note: Menadione (Vitamin K3) is not recommended due to lower efficacy and higher toxicity.
2. Rationale of using warfarin in Deep Vein Thrombosis
		**Warfarin in Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT): Rationale**
	
	- **DVT** is a form of Venous Thromboembolism (VTE).
	- **Venous thrombi** (like those in DVT) are mainly **fibrin thrombi**.
	- **Anticoagulants** are most effective for the prevention and treatment of VTE because venous thrombi are fibrin-based.
	- The aim of using anticoagulants is to **prevent thrombus extension and embolic complications** (e.g., Pulmonary Embolism) by reducing the rate of fibrin formation.
	- Anticoagulants **do not dissolve** already formed clots, but they prevent recurrences.
	- **Warfarin** is an **oral anticoagulant**.
	- Oral anticoagulants, including Warfarin, are suitable for **maintenance therapy**.
	- Therapy for DVT is typically **initiated with rapid, short-term parenteral anticoagulants** (like Unfractionated Heparin, Low Molecular Weight Heparins, or Fondaparinux).
	- Warfarin is then used for **subsequent maintenance therapy** for DVT.
	- Warfarin acts by interfering with the synthesis of Vitamin K-dependent clotting factors (II, VII, IX, X) in the liver, thereby lowering the plasma levels of functional clotting factors needed for coagulation. This reduces fibrin formation.
	- Dose is adjusted based on **INR**. The recommended therapeutic INR range for the **treatment of DVT** is **2-3**.
3. Antiplatelet agents (mention any three groups with examples, mechanism of action and uses)
		**Antiplatelet Agents (Antithrombotic Drugs): High-Yield Notes**
	
	- **Role:** Interfere with **platelet function** to prevent intravascular thrombosis and embolization. Primarily useful in **arterial thrombosis**, where thrombi are mainly composed of platelets.
	
	---
	
	**Group 1: Thromboxane Synthesis Inhibitor**
	
	- **Example:** **Aspirin**
	- **Mechanism of Action:**
	    - Irreversibly inhibits the enzyme **COX-1** and TX-synthase in platelets.
	    - Suppresses the formation of **Thromboxane A2 (TXA2)**, which is a major arachidonic acid product generated by platelets that activates platelets to change shape, release mediators, and promote aggregation.
	    - Because platelets lack nuclei, they cannot synthesize fresh enzyme, so the antiplatelet effect lasts for the life of the platelet (**5-7 days**).
	    - Low doses (e.g., 75-150 mg/day) are thought to selectively suppress platelet TXA2 formation.
	- **Uses (Clinical Relevance):**
	    - Standard for **long-term prophylaxis** of thrombosis (e.g., 75-162 mg/day recommended).
	    - Part of initial management for **Acute Coronary Syndromes (ACS)**, including Unstable Angina (UA) and NSTEMI (e.g., 325 mg oral soluble).
	    - Often used in **Dual Antiplatelet Therapy (DAPT)** with P2Y12 blockers for ACS, post-PCI with stent placement, and maintaining vascular recanalization.
	    - Prophylaxis for individuals at risk of **CAD or thrombotic stroke**.
	    - Secondary prevention of **stroke** (low dose synergistic with dipyridamole).
	    - Used with warfarin to reduce thromboembolism on **Prosthetic heart valves** (though increases bleeding risk).
	    - Prolongs patency of chronic **arteriovenous shunts** and **vascular grafts**.
	
	---
	
	**Group 2: P2Y12 Receptor Blockers**
	
	- **Examples:** **Clopidogrel, Prasugrel, Ticagrelor** (newer agents). Ticlopidine (older, less used due to side effects).
	- **Mechanism of Action:**
	    - Block the **P2Y12 type of purinergic receptor** on the platelet surface.
	    - This Gi-coupled GPCR mediates **ADP-induced platelet aggregation** by inhibiting adenylyl cyclase and decreasing cAMP.
	    - Clopidogrel and Prasugrel **irreversibly** block this receptor.
	- **Uses (Clinical Relevance):**
	    - Prevention of intravascular thrombosis and embolization.
	    - Used alone as an alternative if aspirin is contraindicated.
	    - Key component of **Dual Antiplatelet Therapy (DAPT)** with aspirin for **ACS** (UA, NSTEMI, STEMI), particularly in patients undergoing **PCI** or treated with thrombolysis.
	    - Used after stent placement to check **restenosis** (DAPT for up to 12 months recommended).
	    - Clopidogrel recipients had slightly lower risk of primary ischaemic events than aspirin recipients in one trial.
	    - Prasugrel is more potent and faster acting than clopidogrel, increasingly used in ACS and when strong antiplatelet action is needed, preferred in diabetics.
	    - Ticagrelor is used in ACS patients.
	
	---
	
	**Group 3: GP IIb/IIIa Antagonists**
	
	- **Examples:** **Abciximab, Eptifibatide, Tirofiban**.
	- **Mechanism of Action:**
	    - Block the **platelet surface Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa (GP IIb/IIIa) receptor**.
	    - This receptor, upon platelet activation, undergoes conformational change allowing it to bind fibrinogen and vWF.
	    - Fibrinogen and vWF then **cross-link platelets**, leading to **aggregation**.
	    - Blocking this receptor inhibits the final common pathway of platelet aggregation and is the **most powerful** antiplatelet action.
	    - Abciximab binds relatively non-specifically. Eptifibatide and Tirofiban are specific GP IIb/IIIa antagonists.
	- **Uses (Clinical Relevance):**
	    - Prevention of intravascular thrombosis and embolization.
	    - Used intravenously in combination with oral aspirin and s.c. heparin for **high risk patients undergoing PCI** for **ACS** (UA, NSTEMI, STEMI).
	    - Markedly reduce incidence of **restenosis** and subsequent MI after coronary angioplasty.
	    - Typically infused intravenously for a maximum of **72 hours**.
	
	---
	
	**Overall Key Considerations:**
	
	- Antiplatelet drugs are crucial in **arterial thrombotic events** like MI and stroke.
	- **Bleeding** is the most important adverse effect, an extension of their pharmacological action. Risk is increased with DAPT or combination with warfarin.
	- **Dose individualization** and careful patient selection are necessary, weighing thrombotic risk against bleeding risk.
4. Low molecular weight heparins -also mention the names and their advantages over heparin
		**Low Molecular Weight Heparins (LMWHs): High-Yield Notes**
	
	- **Description:** Fractionated forms of unfractionated heparin (UFH) with lower molecular weights (MW 3000-7000).
	- **Mechanism of Action (Key Differences from UFH):**
	    - Selectively inhibit **Factor Xa** with little effect on Factor IIa (Thrombin).
	    - Act by inducing a conformational change in Antithrombin III (AT III).
	    - Unlike UFH, they are insufficient to provide the long scaffolding needed to bind AT III and Thrombin (Factor IIa) simultaneously.
	    - Have less antiplatelet action than UFH.
	- **Examples (Names mentioned):**
	    - **Enoxaparin** (CLEXANE)
	    - **Reviparin** (CLIVARINE)
	    - **Nadroparin** (FRAXIPARIN, CARDIOPARIN)
	    - **Dalteparin** (FRAGMIN)
	    - **Parnaparin** (FLUXUM)
	    - **Ardeparin** (INDEPAPARIN)
	- **Advantages over Unfractionated Heparin (UFH):**
	    - **Pharmacokinetic Advantages:**
	        - **Better subcutaneous bioavailability** (70-90%) compared to UFH (20-30%). This minimizes variability in response.
	        - **Longer and more consistent monoexponential t½** (4-6 hours), allowing **once-daily subcutaneous administration**.
	        - Eliminated primarily by **renal excretion**. (Note: This is a disadvantage in renal failure).
	    - **Clinical Advantages:**
	        - Have smaller effect on aPTT and clotting time relative to anti-factor Xa activity.
	        - **Laboratory monitoring (aPTT/clotting time) is generally NOT needed**, as dose is calculated based on body weight. (Note: Though monitoring anti-Xa levels _is_ sometimes done in specific populations like renal impairment or extremes of weight, the sources state routine monitoring is not needed).
	        - Lower incidence of **Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT)** than UFH.
	        - Risk of **osteoporosis** after long-term use is much less.
	        - May have a lower incidence of **haemorrhagic complications**, especially major bleeding, compared to UFH.
	- **Uses (Clinical Relevance):**
	    - Prophylaxis of **Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT)** and **Pulmonary Embolism (PE)** in high-risk patients (surgery, immobilized patients).
	    - Treatment of **established DVT**.
	    - Management of **Unstable Angina (UA)** and **Myocardial Infarction (MI)**. They have largely replaced continuous infusion of UFH for these indications.
	    - Maintaining patency of cannulae and shunts in dialysis patients.
	    - Preferred parenteral anticoagulant for rapid/short-term action.
	    - Used with aspirin ± clopidogrel to prevent reocclusion following fibrinolytic therapy for STEMI/high-risk NSTEMI.
	- **Limitations/When UFH is still preferred:**
	    - **Renal failure patients:** LMWHs are primarily eliminated by the kidney and should not be used in these patients. UFH is safer.
	    - **Situations requiring rapid reversal:** Protamine sulfate effectively neutralizes UFH, but only partially reverses LMWHs and does not neutralize fondaparinux. UFH may be preferred in major surgery and high-risk cases where rapid reversal is needed.
	    - **Cardiopulmonary bypass surgery:** High dose UFH is preferred because LMWHs are less effective in preventing catheter thrombosis.
	- LMWHs and fondaparinux have almost replaced UFH, except in kidney disease patients.
5. Tranexemic acid-and its three uses
		**Tranexamic Acid: High-Yield Notes**
	
	- **Classification:** Antifibrinolytic agent.
	- **Mechanism of Action:** Binds to the lysine binding site on **plasminogen**, which prevents plasminogen combining with fibrin. This ultimately inhibits fibrinolysis.
	- **Potency:** 7 times more potent than EACA (aminocaproic acid).
	- **Primary Role:** Preferred for the **prevention/control of excessive bleeding**.
	
	---
	
	**Key Uses (Clinical Relevance):**
	
	1. **Bleeding due to Fibrinolytic Drugs:** Used to control excessive bleeding that occurs as a complication of using fibrinolytic agents (e.g., alteplase, reteplase, tenecteplase).
	2. **Surgical Bleeding:** Used in specific surgical contexts, such as **cardiopulmonary bypass surgery**, **tonsillectomy**, and **prostatic surgery** to reduce bleeding. Also used for **tooth extraction in haemophiliacs**.
	3. **Menorrhagia:** Used to manage excessive menstrual bleeding, especially that associated with **intrauterine contraceptive devices (IUCDs)**.
	
	_Other uses mentioned include recurrent epistaxis, hyphema due to ocular trauma, and peptic ulcer bleeding._
	
	---
	
	**Important Considerations:**
	
	- Main side effects include **nausea** and **diarrhoea**.
	- Less frequent side effects include thromboembolic events, disturbed colour vision, and allergic reactions.
	- Thrombophlebitis can occur at the injection site.
6. Aspirin is used in low doses for secondary chronic prophylaxis of myocardial infarction
		**Aspirin for Secondary Chronic MI Prophylaxis: High-Yield Notes**
	
	- **Agent:** **Aspirin**
	- **Class:** Antiplatelet Agent; Thromboxane Synthesis Inhibitor
	- **Mechanism:**
	    - Irreversibly inhibits platelet enzymes **COX-1** and TX-synthase.
	    - Suppresses formation of **Thromboxane A2 (TXA2)**, a platelet activator that promotes aggregation.
	    - Effect lasts for the **life of the platelet** (5-7 days) as platelets cannot synthesize new enzyme.
	    - Low doses (e.g., 75-150 mg/day) selectively suppress platelet TXA2 formation.
	- **Dose for Prophylaxis:**
	    - Standard low dose: **75-150 mg/day**.
	    - ACC/AHA guidelines recommend: **75-162 mg/day** for long-term prophylaxis.
	- **Uses & Clinical Relevance (Secondary Prevention of MI):**
	    - Recommended for **all individuals with evidence of coronary artery disease**.
	    - **Clearly prevents reinfarction** and reduces **mortality** in post-MI patients.
	    - Used in the management of **Acute Coronary Syndromes (ACS)** (UA, NSTEMI, STEMI) initially with a higher dose (e.g., 325 mg oral soluble), followed by ongoing therapy.
	    - Key component of **Dual Antiplatelet Therapy (DAPT)**, often combined with a P2Y12 blocker (Clopidogrel, Prasugrel, Ticagrelor), post-ACS, after PCI with stent placement, and to maintain vascular recanalization. DAPT is typically continued for up to 12 months post-stent.
	    - Prophylactic use may also involve mechanisms beyond aggregation inhibition, potentially related to improving coronary artery compliance, **stabilizing atheromatous plaques** via suppressing inflammation, improving endothelial function, and reducing LDL oxidation.
	- **Adverse Effects:**
	    - Most important: **Bleeding**. Risk increases with DAPT or concurrent anticoagulant use.
	    - Increases risk of cerebral haemorrhage, especially when used for primary prevention.
7. Clopidogrel and rationale in using it in acute Myocardial infarction
		**Clopidogrel in Acute Myocardial Infarction (MI): High-Yield Notes**
	
	- **Classification:** Antiplatelet Agent; P2Y12 Receptor Inhibitor; Thienopyridine (prodrug).
	- **Mechanism of Action:**
	    - Irreversibly blocks the **P2Y12 receptor** on the surface of platelets.
	    - This receptor mediates **ADP-induced platelet aggregation** by inhibiting adenylyl cyclase.
	    - Inhibits platelet function **irreversibly**. Effect lasts for the life of the platelet (similar to aspirin's effect on COX-1, though not explicitly stated for Clopidogrel in the source).
	- **Rationale in Acute MI (ACS):**
	    - Acute MI (part of Acute Coronary Syndromes - ACS) is caused by thrombus formation, often involving platelet aggregation, on a ruptured plaque.
	    - By **irreversibly blocking P2Y12 receptors**, Clopidogrel inhibits a key pathway of platelet activation and aggregation.
	    - This reduces the formation and propagation of the platelet-rich thrombus causing the MI.
	    - Combination with aspirin (acting via COX-1/TXA2 inhibition) provides **synergistic inhibition of platelet aggregation**.
	    - This Dual Antiplatelet Therapy (DAPT) is highly effective in preventing **reinfarction** and reducing **mortality**.
	- **Key Uses in MI/ACS (Clinical Relevance):**
	    - Management of **Acute Coronary Syndromes (ACS)**: Unstable Angina (UA), Non-ST Elevation MI (NSTEMI), and ST Elevation MI (STEMI).
	    - Usually used as part of **Dual Antiplatelet Therapy (DAPT)**, combined with **Aspirin**.
	    - Used in patients with **UA and NSTEMI** to reduce the risk of progression to MI and sudden death.
	    - Used in **STEMI** managed without PCI/thrombolysis.
	    - Routine component for ACS patients treated with **thrombolysis**.
	    - Recommended after **stent placement** during PCI to check restenosis and reduce reocclusion.
	    - Alternative to aspirin in symptomatic patients of ischaemia.
	- **Dosage:**
	    - **Loading dose:** **300 mg** orally.
	    - **Maintenance dose:** **75 mg** once daily (OD).
	- **Duration of Therapy:**
	    - Often continued for up to **12 months** post-ACS, especially after stent placement.
	    - Continued prophylaxis in post-MI patients clearly prevents reinfarction and reduces mortality.
	- **Key Evidence (Mentioned):**
	    - **CURE trial:** Reported a 20% reduction in MI, stroke, and cardiovascular death by adding Clopidogrel to aspirin in UA and NSTEMI.
	    - **CLASSICS study:** Found Clopidogrel safer and better tolerated than ticlopidine.
	- **Adverse Effects:**
	    - Most important: **Bleeding**. Risk increased when combined with aspirin.
	    - Neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, bone marrow toxicity: Rare compared to ticlopidine.
	    - Other: Diarrhoea, epigastric pain, rashes.
