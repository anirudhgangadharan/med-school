1. Oral iron therapy-preparation, indications and adverse effects
	**Oral Iron Therapy: Preparations, Indications, and Adverse Effects**
	
	**Preparations**
	
	- **Preferred route** of iron administration is oral.
	- **Dissociable ferrous salts** are inexpensive, have high iron content, and are better absorbed than ferric salts, especially at higher doses.
	- Gastric irritation and constipation, the most significant side effects of oral iron, are linked to the total quantity of **elemental iron** administered.
	- **Elemental iron content**, not the quantity of the iron compound, should be considered when dosing.
	- Simple Oral Preparations:
	    - **Ferrous sulfate**: Cheapest, 20% elemental iron (hydrated salt), 32% (dried salt). May leave a metallic taste.
	    - **Ferrous gluconate**: 12% elemental iron.
	    - **Ferrous fumarate**: 33% elemental iron, less water soluble, tasteless.
	    - **Carbonyl iron**: High purity metallic iron powder (particle size < 5 µM). Claimed better gastric tolerance but bioavailability is about 3/4 that of ferrous sulfate.
	- Other forms (e.g., Ferrous succinate, Ferric ammonium citrate, Ferrous aminoate) are claimed to be better absorbed or cause less bowel upset, primarily due to lower iron content, and are generally more expensive.
	- Many oral formulations combine iron with vitamins, yeast, amino acids, etc., but are **not better than simple ferrous salts**.
	- Recommendations exist (Technical Advisory Board, India) that B complex vitamins and zinc **should not** be included in iron and folic acid preparations.
	- Sustained release preparations are more expensive and not rational as most iron absorption occurs in the upper intestine. Bioavailability from these is variable.
	- Liquid formulations may stain teeth and are generally less satisfactory.
	
	**Indications**
	
	- **Iron deficiency anaemia** is the most important indication for medicinal iron.
	- Iron deficiency may be unmasked when brisk haemopoiesis is induced during **megaloblastic anaemia treatment** with Vitamin B12 or folate. Iron status should be evaluated and iron given accordingly.
	- **Prophylactic dose** of iron is 30 mg elemental iron daily.
	- Oral iron is the **normal route** of administration; parenteral therapy is reserved for special circumstances.
	
	**Administration and Factors Affecting Absorption**
	
	- A total of **200 mg elemental iron daily** in 3 divided doses produces the maximal haemopoietic response in adults (3-5 mg/kg in infants/children).
	- Absorption is much better when taken on an **empty stomach**, but side effects are also more pronounced. Taking after meals or smaller doses between meals can improve tolerance.
	- Factors facilitating absorption:
	    - **Acid**: Favours dissolution and reduction of ferric iron.
	    - **Reducing substances** (ascorbic acid, SH-containing amino acids): Reduce ferric iron and form absorbable complexes.
	    - **Meat**: Increases HCl secretion and provides haeme iron. Haeme iron absorption is better (upto 35%) and less affected by other foods than inorganic iron absorption (average 5%).
	- Factors impeding absorption:
	    - **Alkalies (antacids)**: Render iron insoluble, oppose reduction. (Relates to case study where Rabeprazole, a proton pump inhibitor reducing stomach acid, was used).
	    - **Phosphates** (in egg yolk), **Phytates** (in maize, wheat), **Tetracyclines**: Form complexes with iron.
	    - Presence of other foods.
	- Bioavailability from cereal-based diets is low.
	- The gut has a mechanism, the 'Ferritin curtain', where iron entering mucosal cells is stored as ferritin and lost when cells are shed, preventing excess entry. This mucosal block is regulated by body iron status and erythropoietic activity, allowing greater absorption during iron deficiency. However, the block can be overwhelmed by gross excess.
	
	**Adverse Effects**
	
	- Side effects are **common** at therapeutic doses and are related to the elemental iron content. Individual susceptibility varies.
	- Frequent complaints include: **Epigastric pain**, heartburn, nausea, vomiting, bloating, staining of teeth, metallic taste, colic.
	- **Constipation** is more common than diarrhoea. Constipation is believed due to the astringent action of iron, while diarrhoea may reflect irritant action. These symptoms may also result from altered intestinal flora.
	- Tolerance can be improved by starting therapy at a low dose and gradually escalating to the optimum dose.
2. Explain why orally administered Vit B12 is not effective in pernicious anemia
	**Oral Vitamin B12 in Pernicious Anaemia: Ineffectiveness**
	
	- **Pernicious anaemia** is primarily caused by an autoimmune disorder leading to the destruction of gastric parietal cells.
	- This destruction results in the **absence of intrinsic factor (IF)** in the gastric juice.
	- Intrinsic factor, a glycoprotein secreted by the stomach, is **essential** for the absorption of physiological amounts of Vitamin B12 from the intestine.
	- Normally, Vitamin B12 from food is released by gastric acid, combines with IF, and this complex binds to receptors on intestinal mucosal cells for absorption via an active carrier-mediated transport mechanism.
	- In pernicious anaemia, because the intrinsic factor is absent, the standard mechanism for intestinal absorption of Vitamin B12 is **totally non-functional**.
	- While a small fraction of _gross excess_ oral Vitamin B12 might be absorbed without IF, this route is **not dependable** for treating confirmed Vitamin B12 deficiency, especially when intrinsic factor is lacking.
	- Therefore, **injected Vitamin B12 is a must** for treating Vitamin B12 deficiency due to lack of intrinsic factor (e.g., pernicious anaemia), as it bypasses the defective intestinal absorption mechanism by being absorbed directly and completely after i.m. or deep s.c. injection.
