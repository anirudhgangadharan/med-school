1. A 49-year-old male presented with an ulcerated pigmented nodule in the foot with an inguinal mass.
	a. What is your probable diagnosis?
	b. Describe the mechanism of metastasis.
	c. Discuss the routes of spread of tumor.
	
	### Probable Diagnosis
	
	- **Malignant Melanoma** of the foot, with **regional (inguinal) lymph node metastasis**.
	    - The presence of an **ulcerated pigmented nodule** is a classic clinical feature of malignant melanoma.
	    - The location on the **foot** is consistent with an **acral lentiginous melanoma** variant, which is known for ulceration and early metastasis.
	    - The **inguinal mass** strongly suggests involvement of the regional lymph nodes, a common site for metastatic spread of malignant melanoma via lymphatics.
	
	### Mechanism of Metastasis (Metastatic Cascade)
	
	Metastasis is a **multistep process** by which a primary tumour spreads to distant sites and forms secondary growths. This **invasion-metastatic cascade** involves several key steps:
	
	- **Local Invasion:**
	    - Tumour cells detach from the primary mass and invade the surrounding **extracellular matrix** in the interstitial tissue, and the **basement membranes** of the tumour and vessel walls.
	    - This invasion is often via the route of **least resistance**.
	- **Intravasation:**
	    - Malignant cells penetrate the **vascular or lymphatic channels** to enter the circulation.
	    - **Capillaries and venules** are more easily invaded than thick-walled arteries.
	- **Survival in Circulation and Tumour Emboli Formation:**
	    - Once in the circulation, tumour cells are vulnerable to mechanical stress, apoptosis (anoikis), and immune defences.
	    - Survived tumour cells may clump with **platelets** to form **platelet-tumour aggregates**, which can enhance their survival and implantability.
	    - Tumour cells can also bind and activate **coagulation factors**, forming emboli.
	- **Extravasation:**
	    - The circulating tumour emboli adhere to the vascular endothelium at distant sites.
	    - They then penetrate the vessel wall and basement membrane to exit the circulation and invade the new tissue.
	- **Establishment of a Secondary Growth:**
	    - The tumour cells proliferate at the new site, forming a **discontinuous secondary tumour mass**.
	    - The selection of metastatic sites often follows the "**seed-soil theory**", where certain organs (e.g., liver, lungs, brain, bones, kidney, adrenals) provide a favourable environment ("good soil") for the growth of tumour cells ("good seeds").
	
	### Routes of Spread of Tumour
	
	Malignant tumours can spread through several pathways:
	
	- **1. Lymphatic Spread:**
	    
	    - This is a **common route for carcinomas**.
	    - Tumour cells enter the **lymphatic vessels** and are carried to **regional draining lymph nodes**.
	    - The tumour emboli typically lodge and grow in the **subcapsular sinus** of the lymph node.
	    - **Regional nodal metastasis** is common (e.g., breast carcinoma to axillary lymph nodes, gastric carcinoma to Virchow's lymph node in the supraclavicular region).
	    - **Skip metastasis** can occur where local lymph nodes are bypassed.
	    - **Retrograde metastasis** happens when tumour cells spread against the normal flow of lymph, often due to lymphatic obstruction.
	    - **Lymph node involvement** is a crucial prognostic indicator (e.g., in breast cancer).
	- **2. Haematogenous Spread (Blood-borne):**
	    
	    - This is the **usual route for sarcomas** and also frequently occurs with certain carcinomas (e.g., lung, breast, thyroid, kidney, liver, prostate, ovary).
	    - Tumour cells invade **capillaries and venules** more easily than thick-walled arteries.
	    - **Common sites** for blood-borne metastasis include the **liver, lungs, brain, bones, kidney, and adrenals**.
	    - Some organs (e.g., **spleen, heart, skeletal muscle**) are generally **resistant** to tumour metastasis.
	    - **Bone metastases** can be **osteolytic** (radiolucent, e.g., lung cancer, leading to pathological fractures and hypercalcemia) or **osteoblastic** (radiodense, e.g., prostatic cancer, leading to increased serum alkaline phosphatase).
	- **3. Spread along Body Cavities and Natural Passages (Transcoelomic Spread):**
	    
	    - Malignant tumours arising in organs adjacent to body cavities (e.g., ovaries, gastrointestinal tract, lung) may **exfoliate cells** into these cavities.
	    - Examples include **peritoneal seeding** from ovarian or appendiceal carcinomas, which can result in conditions like **pseudomyxoma peritonei**.
	    - Spread can also occur along epithelium-lined surfaces, or via **cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)**, as seen in "drop metastasis" of medulloblastoma to the spine.

2. Squamous cell carcinoma-etiology, morphology microscopy and gross features
	### Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC)
	
	**a. Probable Diagnosis:** While the initial query described an ulcerated pigmented nodule suggestive of melanoma, the request for SCC details is broader. Squamous Cell Carcinoma is a **malignant tumour of epithelial origin**. It arises from **stratified squamous epithelium**.
	
	**b. Etiology / Predisposing Factors:**
	
	- **Chronic Persistent Injury / Irritation:** SCC often arises from pre-existing chronic inflammatory and degenerative lesions. Metaplasia, a reversible adaptive response to chronic injury, can progress to dysplasia and neoplasia.
	    - **Tobacco Exposure:**
	        - **Heavy smoking/chewing tobacco** is a **strong association** for **oral cavity SCC** (can cause leukoplakia).
	        - **Heavy smoking** is a major etiologic factor for **laryngeal carcinoma** (99% SCC).
	        - **Cigarette smoking** is strongly related to **lung SCC**.
	        - **Industrial carcinogens** (e.g., coal tars, oils, soot in chimney-sweeps, Kangari cancer).
	    - **Alcohol Consumption:** Chronic excessive alcohol consumption is a strong associated factor for **oral cavity SCC** and **laryngeal carcinoma**.
	    - **Sunlight Exposure (UV Radiation):**
	        - Prolonged exposure to strong sunlight is a key factor for **skin SCC** (especially in light-skinned people, e.g., Australia, New Zealand).
	        - Can be a factor for **lip cancer**.
	    - **Human Papilloma Virus (HPV) Infection:**
	        - **High-risk HPV types (e.g., 16, 18, 33)** are strongly associated with **oral cavity SCC**, **cervical SCC**, **vaginal SCC**, and **vulval SCC**.
	        - HPV-induced epidermodysplasia verruciformis is a predisposing condition for **skin SCC**.
	    - **Chronic Inflammatory Conditions:**
	        - Chronic ulcers (e.g., Marjolin's ulcers from old burn scars), draining osteomyelitis for **skin SCC**.
	        - Chronic sinusitis for **sinonasal carcinoma**.
	        - Schistosomiasis for **bladder SCC**.
	    - **Pre-existing Lesions / Precancerous Conditions:**
	        - **Dysplasia**: Disordered cellular growth and maturation, primarily in epithelial tissues. Can progress to carcinoma. Common in squamous epithelium of uterine cervix, respiratory tract, skin.
	        - **Carcinoma In Situ (CIS)**: Pre-invasive stage where malignant cells are confined to epithelial layers above the basement membrane without invasion. Seen in uterine cervix, bronchus, skin (Bowen's disease, actinic keratosis), oral leukoplakia, Barrett's oesophagus.
	        - **Squamous Metaplasia**: Replacement of one mature cell type by another. Seen in bronchus of chronic smokers, uterine cervix in chronic infection. Can progress to dysplasia and neoplasia.
	        - **Oral Leukoplakia / Erythroplasia**: A background for oral cavity SCC. Erythroplasia has higher malignancy risk.
	        - **Actinic/Solar Keratosis**: Pre-malignant for skin SCC.
	    - **Genetic Factors:**
	        - **Xeroderma pigmentosum**: Inherited defect in DNA repair, leading to multiple skin malignancies including SCC.
	        - **Molecular Alterations**: p53 mutation due to DNA damage is common. Other molecular alterations in p16, p63, cyclin D, PTEN, EGFR are seen in oral cavity SCC.
	
	**c. Morphology (Gross and Microscopic Features):**
	
	**General Morphologic Characteristics of Malignant Tumours (relevant to SCC):**
	
	- **Grossly**: Usually **irregular in shape, poorly-circumscribed**, and extend into adjacent tissues. Secondary changes like haemorrhage, infarction, and **ulceration are seen more often**.
	- **Microscopically (Anaplasia)**: Lack of differentiation, characteristic of most malignant tumours.
	    - **Pleomorphism**: Variation in size and shape of cells and nuclei.
	    - **Abnormal Nuclear Morphology**:
	        - **Increased N:C ratio** (e.g., 1:1 vs. normal 1:4-1:6).
	        - **Hyperchromatism**: Dark-staining nuclei due to increased chromatin.
	        - **Anisonucleosis**: Variation in nuclear size and shape.
	        - **Prominent nucleoli**.
	    - **Mitotic Figures**: Increased number, and crucially, **atypical (abnormal) mitotic figures** (e.g., tripolar, quadripolar) are present.
	    - **Loss of Polarity**: Disordered cellular arrangement.
	    - **Invasion**: Tumour cells invade surrounding tissues.
	
	**Specific Gross and Microscopic Features of SCC by Site:**
	
	1. **Skin SCC**:
	    
	    - **Grossly**:
	        - Most commonly, an **ulcerated growth with elevated and indurated margins**.
	        - Less often, a raised **fungating or polypoid verrucous lesion without ulceration**.
	        - Can appear on the sole of the foot as a fungating and ulcerated growth, exophytic and endophytic.
	    - **Microscopically**:
	        - **Irregular downward proliferation of epidermal cells into the dermis**, invading through the basement membrane.
	        - Depending on grade: atypical features like variation in cell size/shape, nuclear hyperchromatism, absence of intercellular bridges, individual cell keratinisation, atypical mitoses.
	        - **Better-differentiated SCCs** show **whorled arrangements of malignant squamous cells forming "horn pearls" or "epithelial pearls"** (cell nests) with central laminated keratin material.
	        - Higher grades have fewer or no horn pearls and are highly atypical.
	        - Variants: Spindle cell carcinoma, adenoid squamous cell carcinoma, verrucous carcinoma.
	        - **Verrucous carcinoma**: Low-grade variant, resembles verruca superficially but has broad masses of well-differentiated squamous epithelium invading deeper, _lacking cellular atypia_.
	        - Inflammatory reaction often present.
	2. **Oral Cavity SCC**:
	    
	    - **Grossly**:
	        - **Ulcerative type**: most frequent, **indurated ulcer with firm everted or rolled edges**.
	        - **Papillary or verrucous type**: soft, wart-like growth.
	        - Nodular type: firm, slow-growing submucosal nodule.
	        - Scirrhous type: infiltration into deeper structures.
	        - May arise from leukoplakia or erythroplasia.
	    - **Microscopically**:
	        - Ranges from **well-differentiated keratinising carcinoma to highly-undifferentiated neoplasm**.
	        - Often show epithelial dysplasia in surrounding areas.
	        - Lip carcinoma generally well-differentiated.
	        - **Verrucous carcinoma** composed of very well-differentiated squamous epithelium with minimal atypia.
	3. **Lung SCC (Bronchogenic Carcinoma)**:
	    
	    - **Grossly**:
	        - Frequently **hilar or central in location**.
	        - Begins as a small roughened area on bronchial mucosa, enlarging to a nodular or ulcerated surface, growing into a **friable spherical mass**, occluding the lumen.
	        - Cut surface is yellowish-white with foci of necrosis and haemorrhages, which may produce **cavitary lesions**.
	    - **Microscopically**:
	        - Diagnosed by identification of **intercellular bridges or keratinisation**.
	        - Shows varying histologic grades of differentiation (well, moderately, poorly).
	        - The edge of the growth and adjoining uninvolved bronchi often show squamous metaplasia, epithelial dysplasia, and carcinoma in situ.
	4. **Oesophageal SCC**:
	    
	    - **Grossly**:
	        - **Polypoid/fungating type**: cauliflower-like, friable mass protruding into the lumen.
	        - **Ulcerating type**: necrotic ulcer with everted edges.
	        - **Diffuse infiltrating type**: annular, stenosing narrowing due to infiltration into the wall.
	    - **Microscopically**:
	        - Majority are **well-differentiated or moderately-differentiated**.
	        - Show **prickle cells** and **keratin formation**.
	5. **Cervical SCC**:
	    
	    - **Grossly**: Most common pattern is **fungating or exophytic, appearing as a cauliflower-like growth** infiltrating the adjacent vaginal wall.
	    - **Microscopically**:
	        - Vast majority (~70%) are **moderately-differentiated non-keratinising large cell type**.
	        - Well-differentiated keratinising type also occurs (~25%).
	        - Tumour cells are anaplastic, varying in size, with abundant cytoplasm. Intervening stroma shows prominent inflammatory infiltrate.

	6. Basal cell carcinoma-etiology, morphology microscopy and gross features 
	### Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC)
	
	**a. Etiology / Predisposing Factors:** Basal Cell Carcinoma is typically a **locally invasive, slow-growing tumour** that **rarely metastasises**. It occurs exclusively on **hairy skin**, with the **face** being the most common location (90% of cases), usually above a line from the lobe of the ear to the corner of the mouth.
	
	Key predisposing factors include:
	
	- **Light-skinned people** with little melanin.
	- **Prolonged exposure to strong sunlight** (UV radiation), particularly in regions like Australia and New Zealand.
	- **Inherited defects in DNA repair mechanisms**, such as in **xeroderma pigmentosum**.
	- **Nevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome**: An autosomal dominant condition where multiple BCCs appear at a young age (under 20 years). This involves an inherited defective allele (PTCH gene on chromosome 9) and a subsequent mutation in the other allele due to sun exposure (Hudson's two-hit hypothesis).
	
	**b. Morphology:**
	
	**i. Gross Features:**
	
	- The most common pattern is a **nodulo-ulcerative basal cell carcinoma**.
	    - It typically presents as a **slow-growing small nodule** that undergoes **central ulceration**.
	    - The ulcer characteristically has **pearly, rolled margins**.
	    - The tumour enlarges by burrowing and destroying local tissues, which is why it's also known as "**rodent ulcer**".
	- Less commonly, it can appear as a **raised fungating or polypoid verrucous lesion without ulceration**.
	- Other less frequent variants include non-ulcerated nodular, pigmented, and fibrosing types.
	- On cut section, the growth is typically **grey-white** and can be both **exophytic and endophytic**.
	
	**ii. Microscopic Features:**
	
	- The most characteristic feature is the **proliferation of basaloid cells**, which resemble the basal layer of the epidermis.
	- These tumour cells are typically arranged in **irregular masses** within the dermis.
	- A hallmark feature is the **characteristic peripheral palisaded appearance of the nuclei** (cells aligned parallel at the periphery of cell nests).
	- The tumour cells are generally **basophilic** with **hyperchromatic nuclei**.
	- **Stromal shrinkage** away from the epithelial tumour nests, creating **clefts**, is a helpful diagnostic feature for differentiation from adnexal tumours.
	- Depending on the pattern, various arrangements may be seen, including solid masses, pigmented cell masses, strands and nests (morphea pattern), keratotic masses, or adenoid patterns.
	- In the **solid basal cell carcinoma** (most common microscopic pattern), the dermis is invaded by irregular masses of basaloid cells.
	- A **superficial multicentric variant** shows multiple foci of tumour cells within the dermis, often on the trunk.
	- Basal cell carcinoma cells typically show **cytokeratin positivity** on immunostaining (general epithelial marker).
	
	**Clinical Relevance:**
	
	- BCC is notable for its **local invasiveness** but a **very low metastatic potential**.
	- It is a **slow-growing tumour**.
	- The overall prognosis for BCC (especially those induced by actinic keratosis) is **excellent**. Prognosis for deeply invading tumours depends on the TNM staging.
	- It is a common diagnosis in older patients presenting with chronic facial lesions.
	
2. Malignant melanoma, sites and microscopy and gross and etiology
	Here's a concise, high-yield, note-style summary of Malignant Melanoma for medical school exams:
	## **Malignant Melanoma (Melanocarcinoma)**
		
	
	**Definition:** A malignant tumour arising from melanocytes. It is one of the most rapidly spreading malignant tumours of the skin.
	
	**1. Etiology / Predisposing Factors:**
	
	- **Cell of Origin:** Arises from **melanocytes**, which are neural crest-derived pigment epithelial cells.
	- **Main Risk Factor: Excessive Exposure of White Skin to Sunlight (UV radiation)**.
	    - Higher incidence in regions with high sun exposure (e.g., New Zealand and Australia).
	    - Radiation is a known carcinogen, with extremely long latent periods and cumulative effects, potentially additive or synergistic with other carcinogens.
	- **Genetic Factors:**
	    - **Inherited/Familial Cases:** Germline mutation in **CDKN2A gene** (encodes for cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor).
	    - Mutational loss of **PTEN gene** and mutations in other tumour suppressor genes (but not p53).
	    - Point mutations in **BRAF** are observed in melanoma.
	- **Pre-existing Lesions / Risk Factors (Clinically):**
	    - **Persistent change** in appearance of a mole.
	    - Presence of a **pre-existing naevus**, especially a **dysplastic naevus** (atypical naevi with increased risk of progression).
	    - **Family history** of melanoma.
	    - **Higher age** of the patient (>50 years for uveal melanoma).
	    - More than 50 moles **2 mm or more** in diameter.
	    - Many melanomas arise **_de novo_** (without a pre-existing naevus).
	
	**2. Sites:**
	
	- **Primary Cutaneous Sites:**
	    - Common sites: **Trunk (men), legs (women)**, face, soles, palms, and nail-beds.
	- **Other Mucosal/Extracutaneous Sites:**
	    - **Uvea** (most common primary ocular malignancy in white adults; posterior choroid, ciliary body, iris).
	    - **Oral and anogenital mucosa**.
	    - **Oesophagus**.
	    - **Conjunctiva, orbit**.
	    - **Leptomeninges**.
	    - Rarely, salivary glands.
	
	**3. Gross Features:**
	
	- **General Appearance:** Often appears as a **flat, macular, or slightly elevated, nodular lesion**.
	    
	- **Pigmentation:** Exhibits **variation in pigmentation** (shades of black, brown, grey, blue, or red).
	    
	    - Uveal melanoma is typically a **grey-black** pigmented mass.
	- **Borders:** **Irregular borders**.
	    
	- **Secondary Changes:** May show **ulceration, bleeding, and increase in size** (especially in rapidly growing or acral variants).
	    
	- **Clinical Differentiation (ABCD criteria):**
	    
	    - **A**symmetry
	    - **B**order irregularity
	    - **C**olour change
	    - **D**iameter > **6 mm**
	- **Gross Variants (Cutaneous):**
	    
	    - **Lentigo maligna melanoma:** Flat, slow-growing, often from pre-existing lentigo; essentially melanoma _in situ_.
	    - **Superficial spreading melanoma:** Slightly elevated, variegated colour, ulcerated surface; often from _in situ_ (pagetoid melanoma).
	    - **Acral lentiginous melanoma:** Occurs on **soles, palms, mucosal surfaces**; often ulcerates and metastasises early.
	    - **Nodular melanoma:** Elevated, deeply pigmented nodule, rapid growth, ulceration; **worst prognosis**.
	    - **Desmoplastic melanoma:** Fibrotic stroma, neural invasion, frequent local recurrences.
	
	**4. Microscopic Features:**
	
	- **Origin:** Marked **junctional activity** at the epidermo-dermal junction, with horizontal and downward growth into the dermis.
	- **Tumour Cells:**
	    - Usually **larger** than naevus cells, with **atypia** and **mitoses**.
	    - May be **epithelioid or spindle-shaped** (epithelioid more common).
	    - **Amphophilic cytoplasm**.
	    - **Large, pleomorphic nuclei** with **conspicuous nucleoli**.
	    - **Mitotic figures are often present** (including atypical mitotic figures).
	    - **Multinucleate giant cells** may occur.
	    - Arrangement: **Solid masses, sheets, islands, alveoli**.
	- **Melanin Pigment:**
	    - May be present (**melanotic**) or absent (**amelanotic melanoma**) without prognostic influence.
	    - If present, typically in **uniform fine granules** (unlike coarse, irregular clumps in benign naevi).
	    - May require **Fontana-Masson stain or dopa reaction** to reveal granules in amelanotic cases.
	- **Inflammatory Infiltrate:** Some lymphocytic infiltrate often present in invasive melanomas.
	- **Immunohistochemistry (IHC):** Melanoma cells are typically positive for:
	    - **HMB-45** (most specific).
	    - **S-100** (more sensitive).
	    - **Melan-A**.
	    - Usually **negative for cytokeratins** (differentiating from poorly differentiated carcinomas).
	- **Uveal Melanoma Specifics:**
	    - **Spindle A:** Uniform spindle cells, indistinct nucleoli, rare mitoses (most favourable prognosis).
	    - **Spindle B:** Larger, plumper spindle cells, ovoid nuclei, conspicuous nucleoli, few mitoses (worse prognosis).
	    - **Epithelioid:** Larger, irregular, pleomorphic cells, large nuclei, abundant acidophilic cytoplasm (most malignant, poor prognosis).
	    - **Mixed:** Features of both spindle and epithelioid types (intermediate prognosis).
	- **Depth of Invasion (Clark's Levels):**
	    - Level I: Confined to epidermis.
	    - Level II: Extension into papillary dermis.
	    - Level III: Extension to interface of papillary and reticular dermis.
	    - Level IV: Invasion of reticular dermis.
	    - Level V: Invasion of subcutaneous fat.
	
	**5. Clinical Relevance & Prognosis:**
	
	- **Metastatic Potential:** Malignant melanoma has a **very common and rapid metastatic spread**.
	    - Routes: **Lymphatics** (regional lymph nodes), **blood** (distant sites).
	    - Common distant sites: **Lungs, liver, brain, spinal cord, adrenals**.
	    - **Prognosis depends heavily on the stage** at presentation, including microscopic depth of invasion, ulceration, and nodal/distant metastasis.
	    - **Sentinel lymph node biopsy** is helpful for regional nodal status evaluation.
	- **Spontaneous Regression:** Rarely, malignant melanoma may disappear spontaneously from the primary site.
	- **Differential Diagnosis:** Clear cell sarcoma closely resembles malignant melanoma.
