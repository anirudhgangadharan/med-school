	**a. Probable Diagnosis**
	
	- Based on the sudden onset of severe, crushing chest pain (substernal or precordial), often radiating, and the characteristic ECG changes (ST segment elevation, T wave inversion), the probable diagnosis is **Acute Myocardial Infarction (MI)**.
	- This clinical presentation, especially with severe constricting chest pain, is typical of acute MI.
	- The term "acute coronary syndromes" includes acute myocardial infarction, unstable angina, and sudden cardiac death. The ECG findings of ST segment elevation strongly point towards an acute MI (STEMI).
	
	**b. Laboratory Diagnosis**
	
	- Laboratory diagnosis relies on serum cardiac marker determinations. These are proteins and enzymes released into the blood from necrotic heart muscle after acute MI.
	- Important myocardial markers currently in use include:
	    - **Creatine Kinase (CK)**.
	    - **Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH)**.
	    - **Cardiac Troponins T and I**. These are more specific.
	    - **Myoglobin** is the first marker elevated but lacks cardiac specificity and is rapidly excreted (levels normal within 24 hours).
	- Rapid assay of more specific cardiac proteins makes estimation of SGOT of historical importance only.
	- Specific ratios might be used for diagnosis: **CK-MB2: CK-MB1 ratio, LDH-1: LDH2 ratio**.
	- Other investigations listed in the clinical case analysis which support diagnosis and management include:
	    - Biochemical estimation of renal function tests (urea, creatinine, BUN), liver function tests, lipid profile, blood glucose, electrolytes.
	    - CBC (haemoglobin, counts, indices), ESR, PCV, blood smear.
	    - Urine examination.
	    - X-ray chest, Ultrasound abdomen (though less direct for acute MI diagnosis itself).
	
	**c. Gross and Microscopic Changes During Evolution**
	
	Myocardial infarction involves ischaemic coagulative necrosis. The changes vary according to the age of the infarct and are sequential.
	
	- **Gross Changes:**
	    
	    - **0-6 hours:** Usually no striking gross changes are discernible, maybe slightly paler and drier. Early infarcts (3-6 hours) can be detected by histochemical stains like triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) or nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) - infarcted areas fail to stain.
	    - **By about 24 hours:** The infarct develops **cyanotic, red-purple, blotchy areas of haemorrhage** due to stagnation of blood.
	    - **48-72 hours:** The infarct develops a **yellow border** due to neutrophilic infiltration, becoming more well-defined.
	    - **3-7 days:** Hyperaemic border, centre is **yellow and soft**.
	    - **By 10 days:** Periphery appears reddish-purple due to growth of granulation tissue.
	    - **With passage of time:** Necrotic muscle resorbed, infarct shrinks, becomes pale grey.
	    - **End of 6 weeks:** Infarcted area replaced by a thin, **grey-white, hard, shrunken fibrous scar**.
	    - **2-3 months:** Scar is well developed.
	    - **Reperfusion Injury:** Grossly appears **haemorrhagic** rather than pale.
	- **Microscopic Changes:** Ischaemia induces coagulative necrosis.
	    
	    - **First 6 hours:** Usually no detectable change on routine light microscopy. Maybe stretching and waviness of fibres. Oedema fluid and myocytolysis at the margin after 6 hours.
	    - **12 hours:** **Coagulative necrosis sets in** (loss of striations, eosinophilic, hyaline appearance of muscle fibers; nuclear changes like karyolysis, pyknosis, karyorrhexis). Neutrophils begin to appear at the margin.
	    - **First 24 hours:** Coagulative necrosis progresses (shrunken eosinophilic cytoplasm, pyknosis). Slight neutrophilic infiltrate.
	    - **First 48-72 hours:** Coagulative necrosis is complete (loss of nuclei). Neutrophilic infiltrate is well developed and extends centrally.
	    - **3-7 days:** Neutrophils disappear. **Macrophages begin resorbing necrosed muscle**. **Proliferation of capillaries and fibroblasts** begins from the margins (onset of granulation tissue).
	    - **Old infarct:** Replaced by **fibrous scar**.
	    - **Reperfusion Injury:** Myofibres show **contraction band necrosis** (transverse, thick eosinophilic bands).
	- **Changes in Early Infarcts (<30 mins) - Special Techniques:**
	    
	    - **Electron Microscopy:** Disappearance of perinuclear glycogen granules (5 mins), mitochondrial swelling (20-30 mins), sarcolemma disruption, nuclear chromatin clumping.
	    - **Chemical/Histochemical:** Glycogen depletion (30-60 mins), increased lactic acid, K+ loss, Na+ increase, Ca++ influx (irreversible injury).
	
	**Relevant Clinical Note:** Myocardial cells die after only 20-30 minutes of ischaemia [Definition of MI, not explicitly numbered but core concept]. Early diagnosis and intervention are crucial.

	**a. Provisional Diagnosis**
	
	- Based on the sudden onset of severe, crushing retrosternal pain radiating to the left arm, accompanied by dyspnea and sweating, in a patient with significant risk factors (age, smoking), the provisional diagnosis is **Acute Myocardial Infarction (MI)**.
	- The ECG findings of **ST segment elevation** are characteristic of a **STEMI** (ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction) [Clinical Case details, implicit from standard medical knowledge linking ST elevation to acute injury, supported by sources mentioning ECG in MI diagnosis].
	- Acute Myocardial Infarction is classified under **Acute Coronary Syndromes**, which also include unstable angina and sudden cardiac death.

	- **Gross Changes:**
	    
	    - **0-6 hours:** Usually **no striking gross changes**. May be slightly paler and drier. Early infarcts (3-6 hours) detectable by histochemical stains like **triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC)** or **nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT)**, which fail to stain the infarcted area.

	    - **By 10 days:** Periphery appears reddish-purple due to growth of granulation tissue. Necrotic muscle is gradually resorbed, infarct shrinks, becomes pale grey.
	    - **End of 6 weeks:** Infarcted area replaced by a thin, **grey-white, hard, shrunken fibrous scar**.
	    - **2-3 months:** Scar is well developed.
	    - **Reperfusion Injury:** Grossly appears **haemorrhagic**.
	- **Microscopic Changes:** Ischaemia induces coagulative necrosis.
	    
	    - **First 6 hours:** Usually **no detectable change** on routine light microscopy. May show stretching and waviness of fibres, oedema and myocytolysis at margin after 6 hours.
	    - **12 hours:** **Coagulative necrosis sets in**. Fibers lose striations, appear eosinophilic and hyaline; nuclei show karyolysis, pyknosis, karyorrhexis. Neutrophils appear at margin.
	    - **First 24 hours:** Coagulative necrosis progresses (shrunken eosinophilic cytoplasm, pyknosis). Slight neutrophilic infiltrate.
	    - **First 48-72 hours:** Coagulative necrosis complete (loss of nuclei). Neutrophilic infiltrate is well developed and extends centrally.
	    - **3-7 days:** Neutrophils disappear. **Macrophages begin resorbing necrosed muscle**. **Proliferation of capillaries and fibroblasts** starts from margins (granulation tissue formation).
	    - **Old infarct:** Replaced by **fibrous scar**.
	
	**c. Biochemical Markers Indicative of the Condition**
	
	Laboratory diagnosis of acute MI relies on **serum cardiac marker determinations**. Key markers include:
	
	- **Creatine Kinase (CK)** [Specific isoenzyme **CK-MB** is more indicative of cardiac muscle damage]. The **CK-MB2: CK-MB1 ratio** can be used for diagnosis.
	- **Cardiac Troponins T and I:** These are considered more specific markers for myocardial damage.
	- **Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH):** The **LDH-1: LDH2 ratio** can be used for diagnosis.
	- Other investigations listed as part of the workup include CBC, ESR, PCV, blood smear, urine examination, renal function tests, liver function tests, lipid profile, blood glucose, and electrolytes.
	
	**d. Complications of the Disease**
	
	Based on the sources, potential complications and long-term consequences of Myocardial Infarction and Ischaemic Heart Disease include:
	
	- **Chronic Ischaemic Heart Disease (CIHD) / Ischaemic Cardiomyopathy / Myocardial Fibrosis:** This represents the long-term damage and scarring post-MI, where the heart muscle is replaced by fibrous tissue. Morphologically, this involves patchy myocardial fibrosis, especially around blood vessels.
	- **Heart Failure:** Can present as congestive heart failure. Right heart failure (cor pulmonale) is mentioned as a consequence of severe lung disease, but general heart failure is a known outcome of damaged myocardium. Sustained cardiac issues can also lead to complications in other organs, like cardiac sclerosis/cirrhosis of the liver.
	- **Sudden Cardiac Death:** Listed as one of the acute coronary syndromes alongside acute MI.
	- **Arrhythmias and Conduction Disturbances:** Mentioned as potential presentations in cardiac conditions involving myocardial damage.
	- **Reperfusion Injury:** A consequence of intervention, manifesting as haemorrhage morphologically.

	**SUBACUTE BACTERIAL / INFECTIVE ENDOCARDITIS (SABE)**
	
	- **Definition:** A serious infection of the valvular and mural endocardium caused by microorganisms, characterised by typical infected and friable vegetations. It is a form of Infective Endocarditis (IE or BE). SABE is a clinical form of BE, traditionally distinguished from Acute Bacterial Endocarditis (ABE).
	- **Prevalence:** SABE is the **more common** form of Bacterial Endocarditis.
	- **Etiopathogenesis:**
	    - Occurs following conditions initiating transient **bacteraemia, septicaemia, and pyaemia**.
	    - Characterised by involvement of a **previously diseased heart**, particularly damaged valves.
	        - Commonly affects valves previously damaged by **Rheumatic Heart Disease (RHD)**.
	        - Can also occur on valves damaged by congenital heart disease or other conditions.
	    - Caused by **less virulent bacteria**.
	        - The **most common organism** is **Streptococcus viridans**.
	    - Impaired host defences can also contribute.
	- **Morphological Changes (Organ Involved - Heart Valves):**
	    - **Gross Appearance (Vegetations):**
	        - Vegetations are characteristic features of endocarditis.
	        - Found mainly on the valves of the **left heart**.
	        - Most frequent on the **atrial surface of the mitral valve** and ventricular surface of the aortic valve.
	        - Vegetations are typically **infected and friable**.
	        - Variable in size (few mm to several cm), grey to greenish, irregular, and may be flat, filiform, fungating, or polypoid.
	        - In SABE, they are generally medium-sized, multiple, and usually do not produce significant valvular deformity compared to RHD.
	    - **Microscopic Appearance (Vegetations):**
	        - Composed of **three zones**:
	            - **Outer layer/cap:** Eosinophilic material of **fibrin and platelets**.
	            - **Underneath layer:** **Basophilic zone containing colonies of bacteria** (in untreated cases).
	            - **Deeper zone:** Non-specific inflammation in the cusp. In SABE, the underlying endocardium typically shows **inflammatory granulation tissue**, and the lesion is usually not invasive or suppurative.
	- **Course:** Has a **gradual downhill course** typically lasting >6 weeks.
	- **Clinical Relevance & Complications:**
	    - Since vegetations are typically **friable**, they tend to get dislodged and cause **embolism**.
	    - Emboli can cause serious **extra-cardiac complications**.
	        - Emboli to vital organs like kidneys and brain causing **infarcts, abscesses, and mycotic aneurysms**. Mycotic aneurysms are often bacterial, not fungal, despite the name. Rupture of cerebral mycotic aneurysms is a cause of death.
	        - Emboli from the right side of the heart cause **pulmonary abscesses**.
	    - **Petechiae** (skin, conjunctiva).
	    - In SABE, characteristic painful, tender nodules on fingertips called **Osler's nodes** may appear.
	    - **Focal necrotising glomerulonephritis** is seen more commonly in SABE, related to circulating immune complexes (hypersensitivity). Diffuse glomerulonephritis can also occur.
	    - Other causes of death include cardiac failure, persistent infection, renal failure, and embolism to vital organs.
4. Cardiomyopathies-etiology and types.
	
	**CARDIOMYOPATHIES**
	
	- **Definition:** Disease of the heart muscle. Term _originally_ restricted to myocardial disease of **unknown cause**. World Health Organization (WHO) definition _also_ excludes heart muscle diseases of known etiologies.
	    
	- **Classification:** Resolved by classifying into two broad groups:
	    
	    1. **Primary Cardiomyopathies:** Heart muscle disease with **unknown underlying cause** (Idiopathic).
	    2. **Secondary Cardiomyopathies:** Heart muscle disease with a **known underlying cause**. Excludes well-defined entities like ischaemic, hypertensive, valvular, pericardial, congenital, and inflammatory heart diseases.
	- **Primary (Idiopathic) Cardiomyopathies:**
	    
	    - **1. Idiopathic Dilated (or Congestive) Cardiomyopathy:**
	        - Characterised by **dilatation of cardiac chambers**.
	        - May be associated with **chronic alcoholism**, possibly due to thiamine deficiency/beri-beri heart disease.
	        - **Morphology (Microscopic):** Non-specific and variable changes. May include hypertrophy/atrophy of fibres, degenerative changes, small areas of interstitial fibrosis, focal mononuclear inflammatory infiltrate.
	    - **2. Idiopathic Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy:**
	        - Synonyms: Asymmetrical hypertrophy, Hypertrophic subaortic stenosis, Teare’s disease.
	        - Typical Age: More frequent between 25-50 years.
	        - Clinical: Often asymptomatic, but can cause **dyspnoea, angina, congestive heart failure, sudden death**.
	        - Etiology/Pathogenesis: Contributory factors include increased circulating catecholamines, myocardial ischaemia (thickened vasculature), abnormally increased fibrous tissue. Stress causes increased production of myofilaments, myofibrils. Fibres hypertrophy (increase in size); sarcomeres may divide to increase width.
	        - **Morphology (Gross):** Cardiac enlargement, increased weight. Ventricular cavities normal or small. Myocardial hypertrophy is typically **asymmetrical**, affecting the **interventricular septum more** than free walls.
	            - Asymmetric septal hypertrophy: Can be confined to apical region (**non-obstructive type**) or extend to mitral valve causing outflow obstruction (**obstructive type/subaortic stenosis**).
	        - **Morphology (Microscopic):** Classical feature is **myocardial cell disorganisation** (irregular/haphazard arrangement) in the ventricular septum. Muscle cells show hypertrophy and large nuclei. Bundles separated by interstitial fibrous tissue.
	        - _Note: Old literature used "rhabdomyoma of the septum" for this form, but it is not a true neoplasm._
	    - **3. Idiopathic Restrictive (or Obliterative or Infiltrative) Cardiomyopathy:**
	        - Characterised by **restriction in ventricular filling** and **abnormal diastolic function** due to reduced ventricular volume.
	        - Heterogeneous group of conditions.
	        - **Etiologies (Source-listed):**
	            - **Cardiac amyloidosis** (Systemic or isolated amyloidosis of ageing). Gross: enlarged heart, pale, waxy, nodules/plaques. Micro: amyloid deposits in/around coronaries. Can cause arrhythmias.
	            - **Endocardial fibroelastosis** (Uncommon, predominantly infants/children, causes sudden death in infants). Gross: diffuse/patchy, **rigid, pearly-white thickening of mural endocardium** (mainly LV). Enlarged heart (LV hypertrophy) but **decreased chamber volume**. Often valves affected, mural thrombi common.
	            - Endomyocardial fibrosis.
	            - Löeffler’s endocarditis.
	            - Haemochromatosis.
	            - Myocardial sarcoidosis.
	            - Carcinoid syndrome.
	            - Scleroderma.
	            - Neoplastic infiltration.
	- **Secondary Cardiomyopathies:**
	    
	    - Myocardial diseases of **known etiologies** or with clinical associations. Excludes IHD, hypertensive, valvular, pericardial, congenital, and inflammatory heart diseases.
	    - **Etiologies (Source-listed categories & examples):**
	        - Nutritional disorders (e.g., chronic alcoholism, thiamine deficiency causing beri-beri heart disease).
	        - Toxic chemicals (e.g., cobalt, arsenic, lithium, hydrocarbons).
	        - Drugs (e.g., cyclophosphamide, adriamycin, catecholamines).
	        - Metabolic diseases (e.g., amyloidosis, haemochromatosis, glycogen storage diseases, hypo-/hyperthyroidism, hypo-/hyperkalaemia).
	        - Neuromuscular diseases (e.g., Friedreich’s ataxia, muscular dystrophies).
	        - Infiltrations (e.g., from leukaemia and carcinoma).
	        - Connective tissue diseases (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, systemic sclerosis, dermatomyositis, lupus erythematosus).
	- **Related Concept: Cardiac Hypertrophy** (often involved in or distinguished from cardiomyopathies):
	    
	    - Definition: Increase in size and weight of the myocardium/individual cells. Results in enlargement of the organ.
	    - Causes: Generally results from **increased pressure load** (e.g., systemic/pulmonary hypertension, valvular stenosis). Increased volume load (e.g., valvular insufficiency) results in hypertrophy _with dilatation_.
	    - Mechanisms: Increased synthesis of cellular proteins. Increased synthesis of contractile proteins. Induction/re-expression of embryonic/fetal genes (e.g., ANF). Myocardial fibres hypertrophy, sarcomeres may divide to increase width.
	    - Types:
	        - **Concentric:** Hypertrophy **without dilatation** of the chamber lumen. Lumen smaller than usual. Seen initially in pressure overload (e.g., hypertension).
	        - **Eccentric:** Hypertrophy **associated with dilatation** of the chamber lumen. Lumen dilated. Occurs with volume overload or when compensation fails in pressure overload.
	    - **Morphology (Gross):** Increased heart weight (>500 gm, can be 700-800 gm vs normal ~300-350 gm). Left ventricular wall thickness >15 mm indicates significant hypertrophy (>20 mm in severe hypertension). Papillary muscles/trabeculae carneae rounded/enlarged (pure hypertrophy), flattened (hypertrophy with dilatation).
	    - **Morphology (Microscopic):** Increase in size of individual muscle fibres; prominent nuclei. Minute foci of degenerative changes and necrosis may occur due to relative hypoxia. Fibrosis may be seen in advanced cases.
5. Myocardial infarction-laboratory diagnosis (enzyme/biochemical markers), consequences and complications.
	
	**MYOCARDIAL INFARCTION (MI)**
	
	- **Definition:** The most important and feared consequence of coronary artery disease. It is the necrosis of heart muscle due to prolonged ischaemia. Myocardial cells die after only 20 to 30 minutes of ischaemia.
	- **Aetiology & Pathogenesis:**
	    - Majority of cases (95%) are due to **coronary atherosclerosis** causing critical narrowing (more than 75% compromised lumen).
	    - Often precipitated by partial or complete **coronary thrombosis** superimposed on a fixed chronic atherosclerotic plaque with surface ulceration. The lipid core of the plaque is highly thrombogenic.
	    - Intramural haemorrhage in the plaque can also contribute.
	    - **Non-atherosclerotic causes** account for ~10% of acute MI cases. These include coronary vasospasm, arteritis, coronary ostial stenosis, embolism, thrombotic diseases, trauma, and outside compression.
	    - Reduced coronary perfusion without critical stenosis (e.g., in aortic stenosis or haemorrhagic shock) can cause subendocardial infarcts.
	    - High risk factors for coronary artery disease/acute MI include hypertension, family history of diabetes, hypercholesterolaemia, stress, and age.
	- **Types of Infarcts:**
	    - **According to Colour:**
	        - **Pale or Anaemic:** Due to arterial occlusion, seen in compact organs like the **heart**.
	        - **Red or Haemorrhagic:** Less common in the heart. Grossly, myocardial infarct following reperfusion injury appears haemorrhagic.
	    - **According to Thickness of Ventricular Wall Involved:**
	        - **Transmural (Full-thickness):** Involve the entire thickness of the ventricular wall. Most common type (95%). Distribution is specific to the area of coronary supply. Pathogenesis involves >75% coronary stenosis. Coronary thrombosis is common. Epicarditis is common. Transmural infarcts usually have a thin rim of preserved subendocardial myocardium.
	        - **Subendocardial (Laminar):** Occur when they occupy the inner subendocardial half or inner third to half of the myocardium. Less frequent. Distribution is circumferential. Pathogenesis involves hypoperfusion of the myocardium, making the subendocardium (normally least well perfused) vulnerable. Coronary thrombosis is rare. Epicarditis is none. Subendocardial infarcts produce less well-defined gross changes than transmural infarcts.
	    - **According to Age:** Acute/Recent/Fresh vs Old/Healed/Organised.
	- **Location:** Most often in the **left ventricle**. Less often, multifocal lesions occur.
	    - Stenosis of **Left Anterior Descending (LAD)** coronary artery: Most frequent (40-50%), involves anterior, lateral, and septal parts of LV, and apex.
	    - Stenosis of **Right Coronary** artery: Next most frequent (30-40%), involves posterior part of LV and posterior one-third of interventricular septum.
	    - Stenosis of **Left Circumflex Coronary** artery: Least frequent (15-20%), involves lateral wall of LV.
	- **Morphological Features:** Vary according to the age of the infarct.
	    - **Gross Changes (Sequential):**
	        - 0-6 hours: No change or pale. TTC/NBT test negative in infarcted area.
	        - 6-12 hours: Slightly paler and drier. Early infarcts (3-6 hrs) detectable by TTC/NBT staining (infarcted area fails to stain).
	        - 24 hours: **Cyanotic, red-purple, blotchy areas of haemorrhage**.
	        - 48-72 hours: Yellow border (due to neutrophilic infiltration), becomes more well-defined.
	        - 3-7 days: **Hyperaemic border, centre yellow and soft**.
	        - 10 days: Periphery appears reddish-purple due to growth of granulation tissue.
	        - With time: Necrotic muscle resorbed, infarct shrinks, becomes pale grey.
	        - 6 weeks: Replaced by a thin, grey-white, hard, **shrunken fibrous scar**. Well-developed in about 2-3 months.
	        - Reperfusion injury: Grossly appears haemorrhagic.
	    - **Microscopic Changes (Sequential):** Ischaemia induces ischaemic **coagulative necrosis**.
	        - First 6 hours: Usually no detectable change on routine light microscopy. Maybe stretching and waviness of fibres.
	        - After 6 hours: Oedema fluid between fibres, **myocytolysis** (vacuolar degeneration) at the margin.
	        - 12 hours: **Coagulative necrosis sets in** (loss of striations, eosinophilic, hyaline appearance; nuclear changes like karyolysis, pyknosis, karyorrhexis). Neutrophils begin to appear at the margin.
	        - First 24 hours: Coagulative necrosis progresses (shrunken eosinophilic cytoplasm, pyknosis). Slight neutrophilic infiltrate.
	        - First 48-72 hours: Coagulative necrosis complete (loss of nuclei). Neutrophilic infiltrate well developed and extends centrally.
	        - 3-7 days: Neutrophils disappear, **macrophages begin resorbing necrosed muscle**. **Proliferation of capillaries and fibroblasts** begins from the margins (onset of granulation tissue).
	        - Old infarct: Replaced by fibrous scar. Granulation tissue seen in healing stage.
	        - Reperfusion injury: **Contraction band necrosis** (transverse, thick eosinophilic bands).
	    - **Microscopic (Early Infarcts <30 mins) - Special Techniques:**
	        - Electron Microscopy: Disappearance of perinuclear glycogen granules (5 mins), mitochondrial swelling (20-30 mins), sarcolemma disruption, nuclear chromatin clumping.
	        - Chemical/Histochemical: Glycogen depletion (30-60 mins), increased lactic acid, K+ loss, Na+ increase, Ca++ influx (irreversible injury).
	- **Clinical Features:**
	    - Diagnosis is made on clinical features, ECG changes, and serum enzyme determinations.
	    - Typically has a **sudden onset**.
	    - **Pain:** Usually sudden, **severe, crushing and prolonged**, substernal or precordial, unrelieved by rest or nitroglycerin, often radiating to arms, neck, back.
	    - **Indigestion:** Epigastric/substernal discomfort ("heartburn") with nausea and vomiting.
	    - Massive MI can cause sudden reduction in cardiac output, systemic hypotension, **cardiogenic shock**, and cerebral hypoxia.
	    - If death is somewhat delayed, clinical features resemble MI (severe chest pain, dyspnoea, shock).
	- **Laboratory Diagnosis:**
	    - **ECG changes:** One of the most important parameters.
	        - Most characteristic: **ST segment elevation** (STEMI).
	        - Other changes: T wave inversion, appearance of **wide deep Q waves**.
	    - **Serum Cardiac Markers:** Proteins and enzymes released from necrotic heart muscle.
	        - Helpful for diagnosis and management.
	        - Rapid assay of more specific proteins available. SGOT estimation is of historical importance.
	        - Important markers include: Creatine Kinase (CK), Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH), **Cardiac Troponins T and I**.
	        - **Myoglobin:** First marker elevated, but lacks cardiac specificity and is rapidly excreted (levels return to normal within 24 hours).
	        - Clinical Case 9 lists specific markers for investigation: CK-MB2: CK-MB1 ratio, LDH-1: LDH2 ratio, cardiac troponins T and I.
	- **Consequences & Complications:**
	    - Only 10-20% cases do not develop major complications and recover. 80-90% develop one or more.
	    - Immediate mortality is about **25% (sudden cardiac death)**.
	    - Important complications:
	        - **Cardiac Arrhythmias:** Very common.
	        - **Cardiac Failure:** Impaired pump function due to muscle loss.
	        - **Cardiogenic Shock:** Occurs with massive MI.
	        - **Extension of infarct.**
	        - **Rupture:** Up to 5% cases, often fatal, usually in the first week. Sites: ventricular wall (causing haemopericardium and tamponade), interventricular septum, papillary muscle.
	        - **Cardiac Aneurysm:** About 5% of patients, often of the LV, occurs in healed infarcts (thin fibrous scar). Impairs function and is a common site for **mural thrombi**.
	        - **Pericarditis:** (May be related to a clinical case example provided, although not explicitly listed as a direct MI complication in the morphology section).
	        - Systemic hypotension.
	        - Cerebral hypoxia.
6. Pericarditis (morphological pattern).
	**PERICARDITIS**
	
	- **Definition:** Inflammation of the pericardial layers.
	- **Etiology & Classification:**
	    - Generally **secondary** to diseases elsewhere in the body or the heart. Primary pericarditis is rare.
	    - Classified into **Acute** and **Chronic** types based on morphology and exudate.
	    - **Acute Pericarditis Subtypes:**
	        - Serous
	        - **Fibrinous or Serofibrinous** (Most Common)
	        - Purulent or Fibrinopurulent
	        - Haemorrhagic
	    - **Chronic Pericarditis Subtypes:**
	        - Tuberculous Pericarditis
	        - Chronic Adhesive Pericarditis
	        - Chronic Constrictive Pericarditis
	        - Pericardial Plaques (Milk Spots, Soldiers’ Spots)
	- **Morphological Features:** Vary with the type of inflammation and exudate.
	    - **Acute Types:**
	        - Serous: Fluid accumulation (50-200 ml, can be more causing tamponade). Microscopically: Infiltration by neutrophils, lymphocytes, histiocytes.
	        - **Fibrinous/Serofibrinous:**
	            - **Gross:** Cardiac surface covered by dry or moist, shaggy, fibrinous exudate, giving a characteristic "**bread and butter**" appearance.
	            - **Microscopic:** Pink fibrinous exudate on the pericardial surface; space between layers shows inflammatory cells (neutrophils, lymphocytes, histiocytes).
	        - Purulent/Fibrinopurulent:
	            - **Gross:** Thick, creamy pus coating pericardial surfaces.
	            - **Microscopic:** Dense infiltration by neutrophils in the serosal layers.
	        - Haemorrhagic: Exudate mixed with blood.
	    - **Chronic Types:** Result from organisation and healing of acute forms.
	        - Chronic Adhesive:
	            - **Gross:** Fibrous adhesions between parietal and visceral layers, obliterating pericardial space.
	        - Chronic Constrictive:
	            - **Gross:** Dense fibrous or fibro-calcific thickening (0.5-1 cm) encasing the heart. Heart size is normal or smaller.
	        - Pericardial Plaques (Milk Spots):
	            - **Gross:** Opaque, white, shining, well-circumscribed fibrous areas (1-3 cm), most frequent on the anterior surface of the right ventricle.
	- **Clinical Relevance & Complications:**
	    - Acute pericarditis, especially serous or haemorrhagic, can cause **cardiac tamponade** if fluid accumulates rapidly.
	    - Fibrinous pericarditis: May manifest clinically as a **friction rub**. In advanced cases, heals by organisation leading to **adhesive pericarditis**.
	    - Purulent and Haemorrhagic pericarditis typically do not resolve completely but heal by organisation, potentially resulting in **adhesive** or **chronic constrictive pericarditis**.
	    - Chronic Adhesive Pericarditis: Generally **does not mechanically interfere with heart function**. Severe cases can lead to cardiac hypertrophy and dilatation due to increased workload.
	    - Chronic Constrictive Pericarditis: **Impairs diastolic filling** and reduces cardiac output by mechanically restricting heart expansion. Can obstruct venous return causing **right heart failure**. Often results from tuberculosis, purulent pericarditis, or haemopericardium.
	    - Pericardial Plaques: Generally of **no functional significance**.
