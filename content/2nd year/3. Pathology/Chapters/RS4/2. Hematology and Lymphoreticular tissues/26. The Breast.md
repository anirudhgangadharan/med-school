1. Benign fibroepithelial neoplasm of the breast

	### **Benign Fibroepithelial Neoplasm of the Breast (Fibroadenoma)**
	
	**1. Definition & Epidemiology:**
	
	- A **benign tumour** composed of both **fibrous (stromal) and epithelial elements**.
	- The **most common benign tumour of the female breast**.
	- Typically occurs during **reproductive life**, most commonly in patients aged **15 to 30 years**.
	- Rare in men.
	
	**2. Clinical Presentation:**
	
	- Usually presents as a **solitary, discrete, freely mobile, painless palpable lump** within the breast.
	- May occasionally be part of generalized fibrocystic disease, termed fibroadenomatosis.
	- Less commonly, can be a **"giant fibroadenoma,"** growing up to 15 cm in diameter.
	
	**3. Morphology:**
	
	**a. Gross Features:**
	
	- Typically a **small (2-4 cm diameter)**, **solitary**, **well-encapsulated**, spherical or discoid mass.
	- On **cut surface**, it is **firm, grey-white, slightly myxoid**.
	- May exhibit characteristic **slit-like spaces** (representing compressed ducts).
	
	**b. Microscopic Features:**
	
	- Composed of proliferating **fibrous tissue (stroma)** and benign **ductal epithelial elements**.
	- Characteristically shows two main patterns, which often coexist:
	    - **Intracanalicular pattern:** The proliferating stroma compresses the ducts, reducing them to **slit-like clefts** lined by ductal epithelium.
	    - **Pericanalicular pattern:** The fibrous stroma grows around and **encircles patent or dilated ducts**.
	- The fibrous stroma can vary in cellularity and may be hyalinised or myxomatous.
	- **Cytology (Fine Needle Aspiration Cytology - FNAC) features**:
	    - **Moderately cellular smears**.
	    - Presence of **large, branching monolayered sheets of uniform benign ductal epithelial cells** (often described as "antler horn configuration").
	    - Numerous **single bare bipolar nuclei** (representing benign myoepithelial cells).
	    - Fragments of loose fibromyxoid stroma.
	
	**4. Morphologic Variants:**
	
	- **Tubular adenoma:** Predominantly composed of closely-packed ductular or acinar proliferation with scanty fibrous tissue.
	- **Lactating adenoma:** Shows acini with secretory activity, typically seen during pregnancy or lactation.
	- **Juvenile fibroadenoma:** A larger, rapidly growing mass seen in adolescent girls, which generally has an excellent prognosis and does not recur after excision.
	
	**5. Clinical Relevance & Prognosis:**
	
	- Fibroadenomas are **benign** and generally cured by complete surgical excision.
	- **Rarely, an _in situ_ or invasive lobular or ductal carcinoma may arise within a fibroadenoma**, or an adjacent carcinoma may invade it.
	- Fibroadenoma can be a **long-term risk factor for invasive breast cancer (after >20 years),** with approximately twice the risk compared to controls.
	- FNAC is a valuable tool to differentiate fibroadenoma from malignant lesions.

2. Lobular carcinoma of breast morphology
	
	**Lobular Carcinoma of the Breast: Morphology**
	
	Lobular carcinoma (LC) constitutes approximately **10% of all breast cancers**. It is peculiar as it is **more frequently bilateral** and may have a **multicentric origin** within the same breast.
	
	**I. Lobular Carcinoma In Situ (LCIS)**
	
	- **Gross Morphology:**
	    - **Not palpable or grossly visible**.
	    - Typically an incidental microscopic finding.
	- **Microscopic (Histology):**
	    - Characterized by the **filling of terminal ducts and ductules or acini**.
	    - Tumour cells are **rather uniform**.
	    - Cells are **loosely cohesive**, with **small, rounded nuclei** and **indistinct cytoplasmic margins**.
	- **Clinical Relevance:**
	    - Associated with a **higher incidence of developing contralateral breast cancer (30%)** compared to intraductal carcinoma.
	    - Risk of developing **ipsilateral invasive cancer is about 25%** within 10 years.
	
	**II. Invasive Lobular Carcinoma (ILC)**
	
	- **Gross Morphology:**
	    - Appearance varies from a **well-defined scirrhous mass** to a **poorly-defined area of induration**.
	    - May be difficult to detect by inspection or palpation.
	- **Microscopic (Histology):**
	    - **Growth Pattern:**
	        - **Characteristic single file (Indian file) linear arrangement** of tumour cells infiltrating the stromal.
	        - Shows **very little tendency to gland formation**.
	        - Infiltrating cells may be arranged **concentrically around ducts in a a target-like pattern**.
	    - **Tumour Cytology:**
	        - Individual tumour cells resemble those of LCIS: **round and regular with very little pleomorphism and infrequent mitoses**.
	        - Some tumours may show **signet-ring cells distended with cytoplasmic mucin**.
	    - **Key Molecular Feature (High-Yield):**
	        - Often associated with the **loss of E-cadherin expression** (due to mutations in the CDH1 gene). This explains the characteristic loosely cohesive, single-file infiltration pattern. _(Note: While not strictly morphology, this molecular underpinning is critical for understanding the morphological appearance and is highly testable)_.
	- **Prognosis:**
	    - Generally considered to have a **more favourable prognosis** when it is of a low histological grade.
3. Gynaecomastia
	**Gynaecomastia (Hypertrophy of Male Breast)**
	
	**I. Definition**
	
	- **Unilateral or bilateral enlargement of the male breast**.
	- Primarily due to **proliferation of ducts and increased periductal stroma**, as male breast does not contain secretory lobules.
	- Occurs in response to **hormonal stimulation, mainly oestrogen**.
	
	**II. Etiology/Causes (Clinical Relevance)** Gynaecomastia is associated with **excessive oestrogenic activity** or **decreased androgenic activity** in males. It can be classified into:
	
	- **Physiological:**
	    - **Pubertal Gynaecomastia:** Seen in young boys (13-17 years old).
	    - **Senescent Gynaecomastia:** Occurs in men over 50 years.
	- **Pathological (Secondary):** Associated with underlying conditions or treatments:
	    - **Hepatic Cirrhosis:** Liver is not capable of metabolizing endogenous oestrogens, leading to their accumulation.
	    - **Testicular Tumours:** May elaborate oestrogen.
	    - **Pituitary Tumours**.
	    - **Carcinoma of the Lung**.
	    - **Exogenous Oestrogen Therapy:** E.g., for prostatic cancer.
	    - **Testicular Atrophy:** E.g., in Klinefelter's syndrome.
	- **Idiopathic:** Enlargement without any obvious cause.
	
	**III. Morphology**
	
	- **Gross Morphology:**
	    - One or both male breasts are **enlarged**.
	    - Tissue appears **smooth and glistening white**.
	- **Microscopic (Histology):** Two main features:
	    1. **Proliferation of branching ducts:** These may display epithelial hyperplasia with formation of papillary projections at places.
	    2. **Increased fibrous stroma:** Often with a myxoid appearance.
	
	---
	
	**Clinical Note:** Gynaecomastia should be distinguished from male breast cancer, though it is a benign condition. Any suspicious lump warrants thorough investigation.
4. Phyloides tumor
	**Phyllodes Tumour (Cystosarcoma Phyllodes)**
	
	**I. Definition & Nomenclature**
	
	- An **uncommon bulky breast tumour**.
	- Nomenclature: Formerly known as "Cystosarcoma phyllodes" (coined by Müller in 1838 due to its leaf-like gross appearance and aggressive behaviour), the WHO now prefers "**Phyllodes Tumour**" to avoid the misleading "sarcoma" term, as not all are malignant.
	- It is essentially a tumour with **stromal overgrowth** accompanied by **benign ductal structures**.
	
	**II. Clinical Relevance**
	
	- **Age:** Most commonly affects patients between **30 to 70 years of age**.
	- **Behaviour:** While some are malignant, **local recurrences are much more frequent than metastases**.
		
		**III. Morphology**
		
		- **A. Gross Morphology:**
		    
		    - **Size:** Generally **large**, typically **10-15 cm in diameter**.
		    - **Shape:** **Round to oval**, often **bosselated** (lumpy/knobby surface).
		    - **Encapsulation:** **Less fully encapsulated than a fibroadenoma**, though still somewhat delineated.
		    - **Cut Surface:** **Grey-white** with characteristic **cystic cavities**. May also show areas of **haemorrhages, necrosis, and degenerative changes**.
		    - **Distinction from Giant Fibroadenoma:** Grossly resembles a giant fibroadenoma, but lacks the specific _histologic_ features of cystosarcoma phyllodes.
		- **B. Microscopic (Histology):**
		    
		    - **Biphasic Composition:** Composed of two main components:
		        1. **Extremely hypercellular stroma:** This is the _distinguishing feature_ from fibroadenoma, which also has fibrous tissue but not to this degree of hypercellularity.
		        2. **Benign ductal structures:** These epithelial elements are typically benign.
		    - **Patterns:** The marked stromal overgrowth compresses the ducts, leading to characteristic "leaf-like" or cleft-like spaces.
		    - **Grading Criteria (for Benign, Borderline, Malignant):** The classification is based on the **histologic features of the stromal cells**, specifically assessing:
		        1. **Frequency of mitoses** (in the stromal component).
		        2. **Cellular atypia** (in the stromal component).
		        3. **Cellularity** (of the stroma).
		        4. **Infiltrative margins** (of the tumour).
		
		**IV. Prognosis**
		
		- Approximately **20% of phyllodes tumours are histologically malignant**.
		- Less than half of the malignant cases may metastasise.
5. Tumor markers  of carcinoma of breast

	### Tumour Markers in Carcinoma of the Breast
	
	Tumour markers for breast carcinoma are crucial for **prognosis and guiding therapy**, rather than primary diagnosis.
	
	1. **Hormone Receptors (ER/PR)**
	    
	    - **Type:** **Oestrogen Receptors (ER)** and **Progesterone Receptors (PR)**.
	    - **Detection:** Identified by **immunohistochemistry (IHC)** on tumour cells.
	    - **Clinical Relevance:**
	        - **Prediction of Response to Therapy:** Tumours with **high levels of receptor positivity** (ER-PR positive) respond **favourably to endocrine therapy** (e.g., oophorectomy or hormonal therapy). Receptor-positive recurrent tumours are more likely to respond to anti-oestrogen therapy.
	        - **Prognosis:** Patients with **ER-PR positive** breast cancer generally have a **slightly better prognosis**.
	2. **HER2/neu (ERBB2)**
	    
	    - **Type:** A transmembrane protein with **tyrosine kinase activity**, part of the epidermal growth factor receptor family.
	    - **Detection:** Detected by **immunohistochemistry (IHC)** or **fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH)**.
	    - **Clinical Relevance:**
	        - **Prognosis:** **Overexpression** of HER2/neu indicates a **poor prognosis**.
	        - **Prediction of Response to Therapy:** Overexpression of HER2/neu predicts a **favourable response to Herceptin (trastuzumab) therapy**. Underexpression is associated with a favourable prognosis.
	3. **Genetic Markers and Proliferation Indicators**
	    
	    - **BRCA1 and BRCA2 Genes:**
	        - **Type:** DNA repair genes.
	        - **Location:** BRCA1 on chromosome 17, BRCA2 on chromosome 13.
	        - **Clinical Relevance:**
	            - **Risk Factor:** Inherited mutations in these genes are found in **~10% of breast cancers**. Both copies of the gene must be inactivated for cancer development.
	            - **Prognosis/Therapy:** **BRCA-negative** cases tend to **respond better to chemotherapy and radiotherapy**, while **BRCA-positive** cases may **respond poorly**.
	    - **p53 (TP53) Tumour Suppressor Gene:**
	        - **Type:** A tumour suppressor gene.
	        - **Clinical Relevance:** Mutations account for **~40% of sporadic breast cancers**. **p53 positive** cases may **respond better to chemotherapy and radiotherapy**.
	    - **Ki-67 and PCNA (Proliferation Markers):**
	        - **Detection:** IHC.
	        - **Clinical Relevance:** **High mitotic count** (measured by these markers) indicates a **poor prognosis**.
	    - **DNA Content Analysis:**
	        - **Detection:** Flow cytometry.
	        - **Clinical Relevance:** Tumours with **aneuploid DNA content** (abnormal number of chromosomes) have a **worse prognosis** than purely diploid tumours.
	    - **Angiogenesis:**
	        - **Markers:** Vascular Endothelium Growth Factor (VEGF). Microvascular density can be counted.
	        - **Clinical Relevance:** **High angiogenic activity** in the tumour indicates a **poor prognosis**.
	    - **BCL2:**
	        - **Clinical Relevance:** In breast cancer, **BCL2 positive** is associated with a **good prognosis**, while **BCL2 negative** is associated with a **poor prognosis**.
	    - **Cathepsin D:**
	        - **Clinical Relevance:** **Absence** is associated with a **good prognosis**, while **presence** indicates a **poor prognosis**.
	4. **Biochemical Assays (Serum/Body Fluids)**
	    
	    - **CA 15-3:** A cancer-associated protein used as a tumour marker for breast cancer.
	    - **Carcinoembryonic Antigen (CEA):** An oncofoetal antigen that can appear in breast cancer.
	    - **Important Note:** These biochemical assays are primarily used as an **adjunct to pathologic diagnosis** and for **prognostic and therapeutic monitoring**, but **not for primary cancer diagnosis** due to lack of sensitivity and specificity.

2. Prognostic factors of carcinoma of breast
	## Prognostic Factors of Carcinoma of the Breast
	
	The prognosis and management of breast cancer are critically determined by several factors, which can be broadly categorised as routine histopathology criteria, hormone receptor status, and biological indicators.
	
	1. **Routine Histopathology Criteria:**
	    
	    - **Histologic Type:**
	        - **Favourable Prognosis:** Medullary carcinoma, tubular carcinoma, mucinous (colloid) carcinoma, and low-grade lobular carcinoma.
	        - **Poor Prognosis:** Inflammatory carcinoma.
	    - **Tumour Size:**
	        - **Favourable Prognosis:** Tumour size **less than 1 cm**; associated with a 10-year survival of 90% in node-negative cases.
	        - **Poor Prognosis:** Size **greater than 1 cm**.
	    - **Histologic (Nottingham) Grading:**
	        - Based on tubule formation, nuclear pleomorphism, and mitotic count (each scored 1-3).
	        - **Low Grade (Grade I):** Score 3-5 (favourable prognosis).
	        - **High Grade (Grade III):** Score 8-9 (poor prognosis).
	    - **Axillary Nodal Status (Lymph Node Metastasis):**
	        - This is considered the **most important prognostic factor**.
	        - **Node Negative:** Recurrence rate after 10 years is 10-30%; involves fewer than 4 lymph nodes; sentinel node negative (favourable prognosis).
	        - **Node Positive:** Recurrence rate after 10 years is 70%; involves more than 4 lymph nodes; sentinel node positive (poor prognosis).
	        - **Sentinel lymph node biopsy** is crucial for evaluating regional nodal status.
	    - **Lymphatic and/or Vascular Invasion (Extratumoural):**
	        - **Negative for both:** Good prognosis.
	        - **Positive for one or both:** Poor prognosis.
	    - **Other Factors:**
	        - **Good tumour circumscription**.
	        - **Presence of inflammatory reaction**.
	        - **Presence of stromal elastosis**.
	        - **Absence of intraductal component**.
	        - **Absence of skin involvement**.
	        - **Paget's disease** of the nipple is associated with a less favourable prognosis than ductal carcinoma without Paget's.
	2. **Hormone Receptor Status:**
	    
	    - **Oestrogen-Progesterone Receptors (ER/PR):**
	        - **ER-PR positive cases:** Respond better to adjuvant endocrine therapy and generally have a slightly better prognosis.
	        - **ER-PR negative cases:** Respond poorly to adjuvant hormonal therapy.
	    - **HER2/neu (C-erb B-2) Overexpression:**
	        - **Overexpression:** Indicates a **poorer prognosis**.
	        - However, HER2/neu positive tumours are **likely to respond to Herceptin (trastuzumab)** therapy, making it a good predictive marker for treatment response.
	3. **Biological Indicators:**
	    
	    - **Mitotic Index (Ki67, MIB-1):**
	        - **Low mitotic count:** Favourable prognosis.
	        - **High mitotic count:** Poor prognosis.
	    - **DNA Ploidy Analysis:**
	        - Tumour cell populations with **aneuploid DNA content** have a **worse prognosis** compared to purely diploid tumours.
	    - **Angiogenesis (VEGF, CD31, CD34, Microvessel Density Counts):**
	        - **Low angiogenic activity:** Favourable prognosis.
	        - **High angiogenic activity:** Poor prognosis.
	    - **Oncogene Disregulation:**
	        - **BRCA1/BRCA2:** BRCA-negative cases may have a better response to chemotherapy/radiotherapy, whereas BRCA-positive cases may respond poorly. (Note: BRCA1/2 mutations are also identified as familial risk factors for breast cancer).
	        - **p53:** p53-positive cases may respond better to chemotherapy/radiotherapy, while p53-negative cases may respond poorly.
	        - **BCL2:** BCL2-positive cases indicate a good prognosis, while BCL2-negative cases suggest a poor prognosis.
	        - **Cathepsin D:** Its absence indicates a good prognosis, while its presence suggests a poor prognosis.
	        - **N-MYC gene amplification** is also a poor prognostic feature in some cancers, including neuroblastoma.
	4. **Molecular Classification:**
	    
	    - Based on gene profiling (microarray), breast cancers can be classified into luminal types A or B, HER2/neu type, basal-like type, and normal breast-like type.
	    - **Basal-like type:** Has the **worst prognosis**.
	    - **Luminal type A:** Responds well to endocrine therapy and has a **good prognosis**.
	    - This classification is still gaining universal acceptance.
	
	**Overall Prognosis Summary:**
	
	- Localised breast cancer without axillary lymph node involvement typically has a survival rate of 84%, which drops to 56% with nodal metastases.
