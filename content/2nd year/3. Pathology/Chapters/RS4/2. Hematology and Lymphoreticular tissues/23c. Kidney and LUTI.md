1. Nephrotic syndrome and causes
**NEPHROTIC SYNDROME**

**Definition**: Nephrotic syndrome is a **constellation of clinical and biochemical features** resulting from increased glomerular permeability to plasma proteins. It is clinically characterised by a **tetrad** of:

- **Massive Proteinuria** (> 3 gm/24 hours in adults).
- **Hypoalbuminaemia** (plasma albumin < 3 gm/dl).
- **Generalised Oedema**.
- **Hyperlipidaemia**.

Associated features often include **Lipiduria** and a state of **Hypercoagulability**.

**Key Clinical Features & Pathogenesis**:

- **Massive Proteinuria**: This is the chief characteristic, involving protein loss typically exceeding 3 gm per 24 hours. It results from increased glomerular permeability to plasma proteins, overwhelming the reabsorptive capacity of the tubules. Proteinuria can be **highly selective** (mostly low molecular weight proteins like albumin) in conditions like Minimal Change Disease in children, or **non-selective** (loss of high molecular weight proteins) in other forms.
- **Hypoalbuminaemia**: Occurs primarily due to the urinary loss of albumin, exacerbated by increased renal catabolism and insufficient hepatic synthesis of albumin.
- **Generalised Oedema**: This is often the most common and earliest clinical feature. Hypoalbuminaemia leads to a significant **fall in plasma oncotic pressure**, causing fluid to shift from the intravascular space into the interstitial tissues. This fluid shift also activates the **renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system**, leading to secondary retention of sodium and water, which further exacerbates the oedema, setting up a vicious cycle. Oedema is typically **pitting**, starting in loose tissues like the periorbital region (especially common in children) and ankles, eventually becoming generalised.
- **Hyperlipidaemia**: Almost invariably present, characterised by elevated levels of total lipids, cholesterol, triglycerides, VLDL, and LDL, often with a decrease in HDL. This is hypothesised to be a compensatory response by the liver, which increases lipoprotein synthesis under the stress of massive protein loss.
- **Lipiduria**: The presence of fat globules in the urine occurs due to the excessive leakiness of the glomerular filtration barrier in hyperlipidaemic states.
- **Hypercoagulability**: Patients are at increased risk of spontaneous arterial or venous thrombosis, including renal vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism. This predisposition is due to various factors, such as increased urinary loss of antithrombin III, increased hepatic synthesis of fibrinogen, decreased fibrinolysis, and altered levels of protein C and S.

**Causes of Nephrotic Syndrome (Etiological Classification)**: The causes are diverse, encompassing both primary glomerular diseases and systemic disorders.

1. **Primary Glomerulonephritis**:
    
    - **Minimal Change Disease (Lipoid Nephrosis / Foot Process Disease)**:
        - The **most frequent cause in children**, accounting for approximately 80% of cases under 16 years of age.
        - Light microscopy reveals **no apparent abnormality** in the glomeruli ("nil lesion"), but electron microscopy shows characteristic **diffuse effacement or fusion of podocyte foot processes** without immune deposits.
        - Typically presents with **highly selective proteinuria** (mainly albumin) and shows an **excellent response to steroid therapy**.
    - **Membranous Glomerulonephritis (Epimembranous Nephropathy)**:
        - The **most common primary glomerular disease causing nephrotic syndrome in adults**, accounting for about 40% of cases.
        - Characterised by widespread and diffuse **thickening of the glomerular capillary wall**.
        - Electron microscopy shows characteristic **subepithelial immune complex deposits** (often described as "spikes" or "domes" along the GBM). Immunofluorescence confirms **granular deposits of IgG and C3**.
        - Often has an insidious onset and may progress to impaired renal function in a significant proportion of patients.
    - **Membranoproliferative Glomerulonephritis (Mesangiocapillary GN)**:
        - An important cause in **children and young adults**.
        - Light microscopy shows increased **mesangial cellularity and matrix**, with accentuation of the lobular pattern of the glomerular tuft and often a **"double contour" or "tram track" appearance** of the glomerular basement membrane (GBM). Electron microscopy differentiates subtypes based on deposit location (e.g., subendothelial in Type I, dense intramembranous in Type II).
    - **Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)**:
        - Currently responsible for approximately one-third of nephrotic syndrome cases in **adults**.
        - Characterised by **sclerosis and hyalinosis** affecting only a portion of some glomeruli (focal and segmental involvement).
        - Tends to have **non-selective proteinuria**, is often **steroid-resistant**, and carries a higher risk of progression to chronic renal failure.
    - **Focal and Diffuse Proliferative Glomerulonephritis**.
    - **IgA Nephropathy** (Berger Disease) can occasionally present with nephrotic syndrome.
2. **Systemic Diseases**:
    
    - **Diabetes Mellitus**: Through diabetic nephropathy, which involves diffuse and/or nodular glomerulosclerosis.
    - **Amyloidosis**: Especially primary generalised amyloidosis (AL type) or secondary reactive amyloidosis (AA type).
    - **Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)**: Specifically, lupus nephritis, with Class V (Membranous lupus nephritis) being a common pattern causing nephrotic syndrome.
    - **Malignancies**: Including carcinomas, lymphomas, and leukaemias.
    - **Drugs**: Such as NSAIDs, Gold compounds, Penicillamine, and Heroin.
    - **Infections**: Including post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, Hepatitis B and C, HIV, Malaria, and Syphilis.
    - **Hereditary Diseases**: Such as Alport’s syndrome, Fabry’s disease, and Nail-patella syndrome.
3. **Circulatory Disturbances**:
    
    - **Renal Vein Thrombosis**.
    - **Constrictive Pericarditis**.
4. **Pregnancy**:
    
    - **Toxaemia of Pregnancy**.

1. Acute post infections glomerulonepgritis- morphology gross and microscopy 
	**ACUTE POST-STREPTOCOCCAL GLOMERULONEPHRITIS (AGN)**
	
	**Definition**: Acute Glomerulonephritis is a form of glomerulonephritis that typically follows an acute infection and characteristically presents as an **acute nephritic syndrome**. It is the **most common form of GN in children** aged 6 to 16 years.
	
	**Gross Morphology**:
	
	- Kidneys are typically **symmetrically enlarged**, weighing one and a half to twice the normal weight.
	- The cortical and sectioned surfaces often show **tiny petechial haemorrhages**, giving a characteristic **"flea-bitten kidney"** appearance.
	
	**Microscopic Morphology**:
	
	1. **Light Microscopy (LM)**:
	    
	    - **Glomeruli**:
	        - **Diffusely affected**, appearing **enlarged and hypercellular**.
	        - Hypercellularity is due to **proliferation of mesangial, endothelial, and occasionally epithelial cells** (acute proliferative lesions).
	        - Also characterized by **infiltration of leucocytes**, chiefly **polymorphs** and sometimes **monocytes** (acute exudative lesions).
	        - **Small deposits of fibrin** may be present within capillary lumina and in the mesangium.
	    - **Tubules**:
	        - Generally **not very striking**, but may show **swelling of tubular lining cells**.
	        - **Hyaline droplets** may be present in tubular cells.
	        - Tubular lumina may contain **red cell casts**.
	    - **Interstitium**:
	        - Some degree of **interstitial oedema** and **leucocytic infiltration** may be observed.
	    - **Vessels**:
	        - Changes in arteries and arterioles are **seldom present**.
	2. **Electron Microscopy (EM)**:
	    
	    - Demonstrates characteristic **electron-dense, irregular deposits** on the **epithelial side of the glomerular basement membrane (GBM)**. These deposits are often referred to as **"humps"**.
	3. **Immunofluorescence Microscopy (IFM)**:
	    
	    - Reveals **irregular deposits along the GBM** that consist principally of **IgG and complement C3**.
	
	**Clinical Relevance**:
	
	- Typically presents with **acute nephritic syndrome** approximately **1-2 weeks following an episode of sore throat or skin infection**.
	- Key features of presentation include **microscopic or intermittent haematuria** (often described as "smoky urine"), **red cell casts**, **mild non-selective proteinuria** (less than 3 gm per 24 hours), **hypertension**, **periorbital oedema**, and variable **oliguria**.
	- In children, the prognosis is generally **excellent**, with almost all (95%) recovering completely.
	- In adults, features may be atypical, including sudden hypertension, oedema, and azotaemia, with hypertension being a poor prognostic sign.
	- Less commonly, it may present as nephrotic syndrome.
	- Complications are more frequent in adults and can include progression to rapidly progressive GN, chronic GN, or uraemia.
2. Chronic pyelonephritis (CPN) etiology and pathology (morphology)
	
	**CHRONIC PYELONEPHRITIS (CPN)**
	
	**Definition**:
	
	- Chronic Pyelonephritis is a **chronic tubulointerstitial disease** resulting from **repeated attacks of inflammation and scarring**.
	
	**Etiology & Pathogenesis**: Depending on the cause, two major types are described:
	
	1. **Reflux Nephropathy**:
	    
	    - The **major cause**, particularly common in **children**, especially girls.
	    - Results from **vesicoureteric reflux**, where urine flows back from the bladder into one or both ureters during micturition due to congenital absence or shortening of the intravesical ureter.
	    - This reflux forces urine into renal tubules, leading to **damage and scar formation**.
	    - Can occur with either **infected or sterile urine**.
	2. **Obstructive Pyelonephritis**:
	    
	    - **Obstruction to urine outflow** at different levels predisposes the kidney to infection.
	    - **Recurrent episodes of obstruction and infection** lead to renal damage and scarring.
	    - Less commonly, it may also result from **recurrent attacks of acute pyelonephritis**.
	    - Chronic pyelonephritis is 10 to 20 times more common in diabetics.
	
	**Morphology**:
	
	- **Gross Appearance**:
	    
	    - Kidneys are typically **small and contracted**, usually weighing **less than 100 gm** each, and show **unequal reduction** in size.
	    - The outer surface is **irregularly scarred** with variable-sized depressions, often described as **U-shaped scars**. This differentiates it from granular scars of chronic glomerulonephritis and V-shaped scars of benign nephrosclerosis.
	    - The **pelvis and calyces are dilated and blunted**.
	    - A **renal stone (staghorn stone)** may be present within the dilated pelvis and calyces.
	- **Microscopic Examination**: Predominant changes are seen in the **interstitium and tubules**.
	    
	    - **Interstitium**:
	        - Presence of **chronic inflammatory infiltrate**, chiefly **lymphocytes, plasma cells, and macrophages**.
	        - **Pronounced interstitial fibrosis** is characteristic.
	        - An uncommon variant, **xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis**, shows collections of **foamy macrophages** admixed with other inflammatory cells and giant cells.
	    - **Tubules**:
	        - Exhibit varying degrees of **atrophy and dilatation**.
	        - Dilated tubules frequently contain **eosinophilic colloid casts**, leading to a characteristic appearance known as **"thyroidisation"** of tubules.
	        - A few tubules may contain neutrophils.
	    - **Pelvicalyceal System**:
	        - The walls show **marked chronic inflammation and fibrosis**.
	        - **Lymphoid follicles with germinal centres** may be observed in the walls.
	        - The lining epithelium may undergo **squamous metaplastic change**.
	    - **Blood Vessels**:
	        - Blood vessels entrapped within the scarred areas demonstrate **obliterative endarteritis**.
	        - Features of **hypertensive hyaline arteriolosclerosis** may also be present.
	    - **Glomeruli**:
	        - Though often **intact in scarred regions**, they frequently show **periglomerular fibrosis**.
	        - In advanced stages, **hyalinisation of glomeruli** can be observed.
	
	**Clinical Relevance**:
	
	- Often has an **insidious onset**.
	- Patients typically present with symptoms of **chronic renal failure** or **hypertension**.
	- Alternatively, presentation may involve features of **acute recurrent pyelonephritis** (e.g., fever, loin pain, lumbar tenderness, dysuria, pyuria, bacteriuria, frequency of micturition).
	- Diagnosis commonly involves **intravenous pyelography (IVP)**.
	- Long-standing cases may lead to the development of **secondary systemic amyloidosis**.
	- It is a cause of decreased specific gravity of urine (isosthenuria).
3. Renal Calculi (nephrolithiasis/urolithiasis) types and Etiopathogenesis 
	**RENAL CALCULI (NEPHROLITHIASIS/UROLITHIASIS)**
	
	**Definition**:
	
	- **Nephrolithiasis** or **urolithiasis** is the formation of **urinary calculi** at any level of the urinary tract.
	
	**Clinical Relevance**:
	
	- Approximately **2% of the population** experiences renal stone disease.
	- It is more common in males, with a **male-female ratio of 2:1**.
	- Peak incidence is observed in the **2nd to 3rd decades of life**.
	- Clinically characterized by **colicky pain (renal colic)** as stones pass down the ureter, and can manifest as **haematuria**.
	- It is the **most common cause of obstructive uropathy**.
	- Long-standing cases of hypercalcemia can lead to **nephrocalcinosis**, which is diffuse deposition of calcium salts in renal tissue.
	
	**Types and Etiopathogenesis**: There are **4 main types** of urinary calculi, along with a few rare types:
	
	1. **Calcium Stones**:
	    
	    - **Incidence/Composition**: Constitute about **75%** of all urinary calculi. They can be pure calcium oxalate (50%), calcium phosphate (5%), or a mixture (45%).
	    - **Etiology**:
	        - **Idiopathic hypercalciuria** (without hypercalcaemia) in ~50% of patients.
	        - **Hypercalcaemia and hypercalciuria** in ~10% of cases, most commonly due to hyperparathyroidism, absorptive (bowel defect), or renal hypercalciuria (kidney defect).
	        - **Hyperuricosuria** (with normal blood uric acid) in ~15%.
	        - **Idiopathic calcium stone disease** in ~25%, where no abnormality in urinary excretion of calcium, uric acid, or oxalate is found.
	    - **Pathogenesis**:
	        - Involves an imbalance between the **supersaturation of ions** and the concentration of **inhibitors** in the urine.
	        - Crystals of calcium oxalate and/or calcium phosphate precipitate on the **tubular lining** or around debris (acting as a **nidus**).
	        - Stone grows as more crystals are deposited.
	        - Predisposing factors: **alkaline urinary pH**, **decreased urinary volume**, and increased excretion of oxalate and uric acid.
	    - **Morphology**: Usually small (<1 cm), ovoid, hard, with a granular rough surface, and dark brown due to old blood pigment.
	2. **Mixed (Struvite) Stones**:
	    
	    - **Incidence/Composition**: Account for about **15%** of urinary calculi, made of magnesium-ammonium-calcium phosphate (often called **struvite** or "triple phosphate stones").
	    - **Etiology**: Formed as a result of **urinary infection with urea-splitting organisms** (e.g., _Proteus_). These infections can lead to chronic pyelonephritis.
	    - **Pathogenesis**: Bacteria produce **urease**, which splits urea into ammonia, leading to an **alkaline urinary pH** that favors stone formation.
	    - **Morphology**: Often form **staghorn calculi**, which are large, branched stones that fill the renal pelvis and calyces.
	3. **Uric Acid Stones**:
	    
	    - **Incidence/Composition**: Approximately **6%** of urinary calculi. They are **radiolucent** (unlike calcium stones).
	    - **Etiology**:
	        - Frequently formed in cases with **hyperuricaemia and hyperuricosuria** (e.g., primary/secondary gout, myeloproliferative disorders on chemotherapy, uricosuric drugs).
	        - Other factors include **acidic urinary pH** (below 6) and low urinary volume.
	    - **Pathogenesis**: The solubility of uric acid significantly decreases in **acidic urine**, favoring precipitation of uric acid crystals. Hyperuricosuria is the most important factor.
	    - **Morphology**: Smooth, yellowish-brown, hard, often multiple, and show a laminated structure on cut section.
	4. **Cystine Stones**:
	    
	    - **Incidence/Composition**: Comprise less than **2%** of urinary calculi.
	    - **Etiology**: Associated with **cystinuria**, a genetically-determined defect in the transport of cystine and other amino acids across renal tubules and intestinal mucosa.
	    - **Pathogenesis**: Results from excessive excretion of cystine, which is the **least soluble** of naturally-occurring amino acids, leading to crystal and calculus formation.
	    - **Morphology**: Small, rounded, smooth, often multiple, yellowish, and waxy.
	5. **Other Types**:
	    
	    - Less than **2%** of urinary calculi, such as xanthine stones due to inherited enzyme metabolism abnormalities (e.g., hereditary xanthinuria).
4. Renal cell carcinoma (type and morphology - gross and microscopic features)
	**RENAL CELL CARCINOMA (RCC)**
	
	**Synonyms**: Renal Cell Carcinoma (RCC), Hypernephroma, Grawitz Tumour, Adenocarcinoma of Kidney.
	
	**Clinical Relevance & Incidence**:
	
	- Comprises **70-80% of all renal cancers**.
	- Most commonly occurs between **50 to 70 years of age**.
	- Often has an insidious onset, with the **classical clinical triad** of symptoms being:
	    1. **Gross painless haematuria** (occurs in ~60% of cases).
	    2. **Flank pain**.
	    3. **Palpable abdominal mass**.
	- Systemic symptoms like fatiguability, weight loss, cachexia, and intermittent fever may also be present.
	- May be associated with **paraneoplastic syndromes** due to ectopic hormone production (e.g., polycythaemia by erythropoietin, hypercalcaemia by parathyroid hormone, hypertension by renin, feminisation/masculinisation by gonadotropins, Cushing’s syndrome by glucocorticoids).
	
	**Classification**:
	
	- Based on cytogenetics of sporadic and familial tumours, RCC is reclassified into the following types:
	    - **Clear cell type** (70%)
	    - **Papillary type** (15%)
	    - **Granular cell type** (8%)
	    - **Chromophobe type** (5%)
	    - **Sarcomatoid type** (1.5%)
	    - **Collecting duct type** (0.5%)
	
	**Morphology**:
	
	- **Gross Appearance**:
	    
	    - Typically arises from a **pole of the kidney**, most often the **upper pole**.
	    - Usually **solitary and unilateral** (though ~1% can be bilateral).
	    - Generally **large, golden yellow, and circumscribed**.
	    - Cut surface commonly shows **large areas of ischaemic necrosis, cystic change, and foci of haemorrhages**.
	    - A significant characteristic is the frequent presence of a **tumour thrombus in the renal vein**, which may extend into the vena cava.
	    - Papillary tumours may be multifocal.
	- **Microscopic Examination**:
	    
	    - Tumour cells may be arranged in a variety of patterns: **solid, acinar, tubular, trabecular, cord, and papillary arrangements**.
	    - Stroma is typically **delicate and fibrous**.
	    - **Key Cell Types and Specific Features per Classification**:
	        - **1. Clear Cell Type RCC (70%)**:
	            - Most common pattern.
	            - Characterized by **large cells with well-defined borders**.
	            - **Abundant clear cytoplasm** (due to the removal of glycogen and lipid during tissue processing).
	            - Regular pyknotic nuclei.
	            - Majority are **well-differentiated**.
	        - **2. Papillary Type RCC (15%)**:
	            - Tumour cells arranged in a **papillary pattern** over fibrovascular stalks.
	            - Cells are cuboidal with small round nuclei.
	            - **Psammoma bodies may be seen**.
	        - **3. Granular Cell Type RCC (8%)**:
	            - Tumour cells have **abundant acidophilic granular cytoplasm**.
	            - Show more marked nuclear pleomorphism, hyperchromatism, and cellular atypia.
	        - **4. Chromophobe Type RCC (5%)**:
	            - Admixture of pale clear cells with perinuclear halo and acidophilic granular cells.
	            - Cytoplasm contains many vesicles.
	        - **5. Sarcomatoid Type RCC (1.5%)**:
	            - The most **anaplastic and poorly differentiated** form.
	            - Characterised by **whorls of atypical spindle tumour cells**.
	        - **6. Collecting Duct Type RCC (0.5%)**:
	            - A rare type that occurs in the medulla.
	            - Composed of a single layer of cuboidal tumour cells arranged in **tubular and papillary patterns**.
	
	**Prognosis & Spread**:
	
	- Generally a **slow-growing tumour**.
	- Spread often occurs via the **haematogenous route** to distant sites like the lungs, brain, and bone, and locally to the liver and perirenal lymph nodes.
	- Overall **5-year survival rate is about 70%**.
	- Presence of **metastases, renal vein invasion, and a higher nuclear grade** are predictors of poor prognosis.

5. Wilm's Tumor (Nephroblastoma)
	**WILMS' TUMOUR (NEPHROBLASTOMA)**
	
	**Definition**:
	
	- An **embryonic tumour** derived from **primitive renal epithelial and mesenchymal components**.
	- Also known as **nephroblastoma**.
	
	**Clinical Relevance**:
	
	- **Most common abdominal malignant tumour of young children**.
	- Typically seen between **1 to 6 years of age**, with equal sex incidence.
	- **Classical presentation**: A **palpable abdominal mass in a child**.
	- Other common signs/symptoms: **haematuria, pain, fever, and hypertension**.
	
	**Etiology and Pathogenesis**:
	
	- **Genetic predisposition**: Higher incidence in monozygotic twins and cases with family history.
	- **Congenital anomalies**: Often associated with other congenital anomalies, especially of the **genitourinary tract**.
	- **Syndromic Associations**:
	    - **WAGR syndrome**: Caused by deletion of the **WT1 gene (chromosome 11p13)**, leading to **Wilms' tumour, aniridia (absence of iris), genitourinary anomalies, and mental retardation**. The WT1 gene is crucial for kidney and gonad development.
	    - Individuals with **gonadal dysgenesis** are at higher risk due to common genetic mutations.
	- **Molecular Mechanisms**: Sporadic cases may involve synergism due to mutations in the **β-catenin pathway and WNT (wingless) signaling pathway**.
	- May be associated with a higher incidence of other malignancies like osteosarcoma, botryoid sarcoma, and retinoblastoma.
	
	**Morphology**:
	
	- **Gross Appearance**:
	    
	    - Usually **large and spheroidal**, often replacing most of the affected kidney.
	    - Generally **solitary and unilateral**, but 5-10% of cases can be bilateral.
	    - On cut section, shows a characteristic **variegated appearance**.
	    - **Soft, fish-flesh-like, grey-white to cream-yellow** with areas of **necrosis and haemorrhages**.
	    - May contain grossly identifiable **myxomatous or cartilaginous elements**.
	    - **Invasion into the renal vein is grossly evident in about half the cases**.
	- **Microscopic Features**:
	    
	    - Composed of a **mixture of primitive epithelial and mesenchymal elements**.
	    - Mainly consists of **small, round to spindled, anaplastic, sarcomatoid tumour cells**.
	    - Presence of **abortive tubules and poorly-formed glomerular structures** in these areas.
	    - Mesenchymal components like **smooth and skeletal muscle, cartilage, bone, fat cells, and fibrous tissue** may also be observed.
	
	**Prognosis & Spread**:
	
	- Rapidly spreads via **blood, particularly to the lungs**.
	- Prognosis has significantly improved with combined therapy (nephrectomy, post-operative irradiation, and chemotherapy).
	- The **5-year survival rate is now 80-90%**.

	### Renal Pathology: High-Yield Notes
	
	#### 1. Nephrotic Syndrome and Causes
	
	- **Definition:** A clinical syndrome characterized by a constellation of features due to increased glomerular permeability.
	- **Key Features:**
	    - **Massive Proteinuria (>3 gm/24 hrs):** Primarily albumin loss (albuminuria).
	    - **Hypoalbuminaemia:** Due to urinary loss, increased renal catabolism, inadequate hepatic synthesis.
	    - **Oedema:** Marked, generalized peripheral oedema due to decreased plasma oncotic pressure and sodium/water retention.
	    - **Hyperlipidaemia & Lipiduria:** Increased hepatic lipoprotein synthesis and decreased lipid catabolism.
	    - **Hypercoagulability:** Increased risk of thrombosis (e.g., renal vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism) due to various factors (e.g., loss of antithrombin III).
	- **Causes:**
	    - **Primary Glomerulonephritis (most common in children):**
	        - **Minimal Change Disease (most common in children)**.
	        - **Membranous Glomerulonephritis (most common in adults)**.
	        - Membranoproliferative GN, Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis, IgA Nephropathy.
	    - **Systemic Diseases (more frequent in adults):**
	        - **Diabetes Mellitus**.
	        - **Amyloidosis**.
	        - **Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)**.
	    - **Systemic Infections:** Viral (HBV, HCV, HIV), Bacterial (endocarditis, syphilis), Protozoal (malaria, filariasis).
	    - **Hypersensitivity Reactions:** Drugs (gold, mercury, penicillamine), bee stings.
	    - **Malignancy:** Carcinomas, Myeloma, Hodgkin's disease.
	    - **Hereditary Diseases:** Alport's disease, Fabry's disease.
	
	#### 2. Acute Post-Infectious Glomerulonephritis (APIGN) – Morphology
	
	- **Etiology:** Typically follows **acute infection** (e.g., sore throat, skin infection) by **Group A Beta-Haemolytic Streptococcus** (1-2 weeks prior).
	- **Pathogenesis:** Immune complex disease, where circulating immune complexes deposit in glomeruli, activating complement and causing inflammation.
	- **Gross Morphology:**
	    - Kidneys are **symmetrically enlarged**, often 1.5 to 2 times normal weight.
	    - Cortex and sectioned surface show **petechial haemorrhages**, giving a characteristic **"flea-bitten kidney"** appearance.
	- **Microscopic Morphology:**
	    - **Glomeruli:**
	        - **Diffusely affected, enlarged, and hypercellular**.
	        - Hypercellularity due to **proliferation of mesangial, endothelial, and occasional epithelial cells**, along with **infiltration of leucocytes** (polymorphs, monocytes).
	    - **Tubules:** May show swelling of lining cells, hyaline droplets, and **red cell casts** in lumina.
	    - **Interstitium:** Some oedema and leucocytic infiltration.
	    - **Electron Microscopy (EM):** Reveals characteristic **electron-dense irregular deposits ("humps") on the epithelial side of the Glomerular Basement Membrane (GBM)**. These represent immune complexes.
	    - **Immunofluorescence (IF):** Shows **irregular granular deposits of IgG and complement C3** along the GBM.
	
	#### 3. Chronic Pyelonephritis (CPN) – Etiology and Pathology (Morphology)
	
	- **Definition:** Chronic tubulointerstitial disease resulting from **repeated attacks of inflammation and scarring**.
	- **Etiology:**
	    - **Reflux Nephropathy (Vesicoureteric Reflux):** Most common cause. Allows urine (and bacteria) to reflux from bladder to kidney, leading to infection and scarring, often prominent at the poles ("U-shaped scars").
	    - **Obstructive Pyelonephritis:** Obstruction to urine flow (e.g., calculi, strictures, tumours, BPH) leads to urinary stasis, predisposing to infection and diffuse scarring of the kidney.
	- **Gross Morphology:**
	    - Kidneys are usually **small and contracted**, often **unequally reduced** in size and weight (<100 gm each).
	    - Outer surface is **irregularly scarred** with varying size and depth of depressions.
	    - Renal pelvis is **dilated**, and calyces are **blunted** and flattened.
	    - May contain **staghorn stones** (calculi taking the shape of the pelvicalyceal system).
	- **Microscopic Morphology:**
	    - **Interstitium:** Shows **chronic inflammatory infiltrate** (lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages) with **pronounced interstitial fibrosis**.
	    - **Tubules:** Exhibit varying degrees of **atrophy and dilatation**. Dilated tubules often contain eosinophilic colloid casts, a phenomenon known as **"thyroidisation of tubules"** due to their resemblance to thyroid follicles.
	    - **Glomeruli:** May show **periglomerular fibrosis** and hyalinisation.
	    - **Vessels:** Conspicuous arterial and arteriolar sclerosis in advanced cases.
	    - **Pelvicalyceal System:** Walls show marked chronic inflammation and fibrosis.
	
	#### 4. Renal Calculi (Nephrolithiasis/Urolithiasis) – Types and Etiopathogenesis
	
	- **Definition:** Formation of stones (calculi) anywhere in the urinary tract. Most common cause of obstructive uropathy.
	- **Etiopathogenesis (General Principles):** Primarily due to **supersaturation of urine** with stone-forming constituents, coupled with changes in urinary pH and reduced urine volume .
	- **Types of Urinary Calculi:**
	    - **1. Calcium Stones (Most Common, ~75%):**
	        - **Composition:** Calcium oxalate (most common) or calcium phosphate.
	        - **Associated Conditions (Hypercalciuria):** Idiopathic hypercalciuria (most common), **hyperparathyroidism**, hypervitaminosis D, sarcoidosis, milk-alkali syndrome.
	    - **2. Mixed (Struvite/Triple Phosphate) Stones (~15%):**
	        - **Composition:** Magnesium ammonium phosphate .
	        - **Associated Conditions:** Form in **alkaline urine** due to **bacterial urinary tract infections** (UTIs) by urea-splitting organisms (e.g., _Proteus_ spp.) . Often grow large, forming **staghorn calculi**.
	    - **3. Uric Acid Stones (~6%):**
	        - **Composition:** Uric acid.
	        - **Associated Conditions:** Seen in **hyperuricaemia** (e.g., **gout**, leukaemia), or idiopathic. Form in **acidic urine**.
	    - **4. Cystine Stones (<2%):**
	        - **Composition:** Cystine.
	        - **Associated Conditions:** Associated with **cystinuria**, a genetic defect in renal tubular reabsorption of amino acids.
	
	#### 5. Renal Cell Carcinoma (RCC) – Type and Morphology
	
	- **Type:** **Adenocarcinoma** arising from the **tubular epithelium** of the kidney. Also known as **hypernephroma** or **Grawitz tumour**. It is the most common primary malignant kidney tumour in adults.
	- **Gross Morphology:**
	    - Typically arises from a **pole (most often the upper pole)** as a **solitary and unilateral** tumour.
	    - Generally **large, golden yellow, and circumscribed**.
	    - Cut surface shows characteristic features: **large areas of ischaemic necrosis, cystic change, and foci of haemorrhages**.
	    - A significant characteristic is the **frequent presence of tumour thrombus in the renal vein**, which may extend into the inferior vena cava.
	- **Microscopic Morphology:**
	    - Composed of neoplastic tubular epithelial cells arranged in various patterns: **solid, acinar, tubular, trabecular, cord, and papillary** arrangements, supported by a delicate fibrous stroma.
	    - **Two main cell types (often mixed):**
	        - **Clear Cells (70% of RCCs):** Large, with **well-defined borders**, and **abundant clear cytoplasm** due to dissolved lipids and glycogen. Nuclei are typically regular and pyknotic. Most clear cell tumours are well differentiated.
	        - **Granular Cells:** Similar features to clear cells but possess a moderate amount of **pink granular cytoplasm**.
	    - Other histological types include Papillary RCC, Chromophobe RCC, Sarcomatoid RCC (highly anaplastic), and Collecting Duct Type RCC (rare).
	
	#### 6. Wilms' Tumour (Nephroblastoma)
	
	- **Definition:** An **embryonic tumour** of the kidney, primarily affecting **children (typically between 1 and 6 years of age)**. It arises from primitive renal epithelial and mesenchymal components. It is the second most common primary malignant kidney tumour in children.
	- **Gross Morphology:**
	    - Usually a **large, spheroidal mass**, often replacing most of the kidney.
	    - Generally **solitary and unilateral**, though 5-10% of cases may be bilateral.
	    - Cut surface shows a **characteristic variegated appearance**: soft, "fish-flesh-like" grey-white to cream-yellow tumour with **foci of necrosis and haemorrhages**.
	    - **Myxomatous or cartilaginous elements** may be grossly identifiable.
	    - Invasion into the renal vein is grossly evident in about half the cases.
	- **Microscopic Morphology:**
	    - The classic Wilms' tumour typically exhibits a **triphasic appearance**, meaning it contains elements of three basic cell components:
	        - **Blastemal Component:** Composed of small, round, blue undifferentiated cells with scant cytoplasm and hyperchromatic nuclei, often forming diffuse sheets or small nodules.
	        - **Epithelial Component:** Characterized by immature tubules or glomeruli, lined by cuboidal to columnar cells.
	        - **Stromal Component:** Consists of spindle cells (fibrous tissue), and may include heterologous elements such as skeletal muscle, fat, or cartilage.
	
	#### 7. Renal Lesions (Complications in Diabetes Mellitus, Lesions of Diabetic Nephropathy)
	
	- **Clinical Significance:** Renal involvement is a **common and leading cause of death** in patients with diabetes mellitus.
	- **Types of Renal Lesions (Diabetic Nephropathy):** Diabetic nephropathy encompasses four main types of renal lesions:
	    - **1. Diabetic Glomerulosclerosis:**
	        - **Diffuse Lesions:** Characterized by **thickening of the Glomerular Basement Membrane (GBM)** and a diffuse increase in the mesangial matrix with mild proliferation of mesangial cells.
	        - **Nodular Lesions (Kimmelstiel-Wilson Lesions):** Pathognomonic. Consist of one or more **hyaline nodules** within the lobules of the glomeruli, which are spherical, laminated, acellular, and PAS-positive. These nodules are peripherally surrounded by glomerular capillary loops with thickened GBM.
	        - **Exudative Lesions:** Include **capsular hyaline drops** (eosinophilic hyaline thickening of Bowman's capsule) and **fibrin caps** (homogeneous eosinophilic material on capillary walls).
	    - **2. Vascular Lesions:**
	        - **Atheroma of Renal Arteries:** Very common and severe in diabetes.
	        - **Hyaline Arteriolosclerosis:** Particularly affects the **afferent and efferent arterioles** of the glomeruli, often severe. These vascular changes are responsible for renal ischaemia, leading to tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis.
	    - **3. Diabetic Pyelonephritis and Necrotising Renal Papillitis:**
	        - Diabetics are more prone to infections. Pyelonephritis (bacterial infection of kidney) and necrotising papillitis (ischemic necrosis of renal papillae) are common complications.
	    - **4. Tubular Lesions (Armanni-Ebstein Lesions):**
	        - Characterized by **glycogen deposits as cytoplasmic vacuoles** in tubular epithelial cells.
	
	#### 8. Renal Stones – Types and Conditions Where They Occur (Consolidated from point 4 for conciseness)
	
	- **Calcium Stones (75%):**
	    - **Composition:** Calcium oxalate or calcium phosphate.
	    - **Associated Conditions (Hypercalciuria):** Idiopathic hypercalciuria, **hyperparathyroidism**, hypervitaminosis D, sarcoidosis, milk-alkali syndrome.
	- **Mixed (Struvite) Stones (15%):**
	    - **Composition:** Magnesium ammonium phosphate.
	    - **Associated Conditions:** Form in **alkaline urine** due to **bacterial UTIs** (urea-splitting organisms like _Proteus_). Often form **staghorn calculi**.
	- **Uric Acid Stones (6%):**
	    - **Composition:** Uric acid.
	    - **Associated Conditions:** Seen in **hyperuricaemia** (e.g., **gout**, leukaemias), or idiopathic. Form in **acidic urine**.
	- **Cystine Stones (<2%):**
	    - **Composition:** Cystine.
	    - **Associated Conditions:** Associated with **cystinuria** (genetic defect in amino acid transport).
	
	#### 9. Malignant Nephrosclerosis
	
	- **Definition:** A form of renal disease that occurs in **malignant or accelerated hypertension**. It is uncommon, often superimposed on pre-existing benign nephrosclerosis, but can occur in pure form, especially in younger males.
	- **Clinical Features:**
	    - Characterized by a **marked and sudden increase in blood pressure**, typically **≥200/140 mmHg**.
	    - **Severe headache, dizziness, impaired vision**.
	    - The presence of **papilloedema** is a distinguishing feature from benign hypertension.
	    - Urine often shows **microscopic haematuria and proteinuria**.
	    - Rapid deterioration of renal function, leading to **azotaemia and uraemia**.
	    - **Poor prognosis**; approximately 90% of patients die within one year if not treated aggressively.
	- **Gross Morphology:**
	    - Varies. If superimposed on benign nephrosclerosis, kidneys are small and shrunken.
	    - In its pure form, kidneys are **enlarged, oedematous, and show petechial haemorrhages on the surface**, giving a characteristic **"flea-bitten kidney"** appearance.
	    - Cut surface shows a mottled red and yellow appearance.
	- **Microscopic Morphology:** Primarily diffuse vascular changes with secondary parenchymal ischaemic changes.
	    - **Vascular Changes (most severe, affecting arterioles):**
	        - **Necrotising Arteriolitis:** Characterized by **fibrinoid necrosis** of the vessel wall, presence of a few acute inflammatory cells, and small haemorrhages.
	        - **Hyperplastic Intimal Sclerosis (Onion-Skin Proliferation):** Refers to concentric laminae of proliferated smooth muscle cells, collagen, and basement membranes, leading to severe narrowing of the arteriolar lumen.
	    - **Ischaemic Parenchymal Changes:** Result from the severe vascular narrowing and include tubular loss, fine interstitial fibrosis, and foci of infarction necrosis.
	
	#### 10. Morphological Features of Urothelial Carcinoma of Urinary Bladder
	
	- **Gross Morphology:**
	    - Can present as a **papillary (exophytic) growth** projecting into the bladder lumen . These can range from minute red excrescences to large, fungating masses .
	    - May also appear as **flat or sessile infiltrative lesions** within the bladder wall .
	    - Cut surface may show areas of necrosis and haemorrhage .
	- **Microscopic Morphology:**
	    - Composed of neoplastic urothelial (transitional) cells .
	    - Displays variable architectural patterns, including papillary, solid, nested, or inverted .
	    - **Histological Grading (Low-Grade vs. High-Grade):** Based on the degree of architectural and cytological atypia .
	    - **Key Diagnostic Criteria (General for Urothelial Tumours):**
	        - **Architectural Polarity:** Assessment of cellular arrangement and orientation.
	        - **Cytologic Atypia:** Evaluation of nuclear size and shape variation, chromatin pattern, presence and type of nucleoli, and mitotic figures.
	        - **Invasion:** Crucial for determining malignancy and prognosis. Assessed by penetration of the basement membrane and the presence of neoplastic cells in the muscularis propria layer of the bladder wall.

#### 12. Clinical Case Analysis: 60-year-old female with right flank pain, weight loss, fatigue, renal mass in upper pole, and plenty of RBCs in urine microscopy.

- **a. Diagnosis:** The classic triad of symptoms – **gross painless haematuria (implied by "plenty of RBCs"), flank pain, and a palpable abdominal mass (renal mass in upper pole)** – along with systemic symptoms like **weight loss and fatigue**, strongly indicates **Renal Cell Carcinoma (RCC) of the right kidney**.
    
- **b. Pathogenesis and Microscopic Variants:**
    
    - **Pathogenesis:** RCC is an **adenocarcinoma arising from the tubular epithelium** of the kidney. While the exact pathogenesis involves specific genetic mutations (e.g., in the VHL gene, although not explicitly detailed for RCC pathogenesis in the provided sources), it is generally understood to result from the malignant transformation of renal tubular cells.
    - **Microscopic Variants (Histological Types):** RCC is classified into several types based on their microscopic appearance :
        - **Clear Cell RCC (most common, ~70%):** Characterized by tumour cells with **abundant clear cytoplasm** (due to high lipid and glycogen content) and distinct cell borders. They can be arranged in solid, acinar, tubular, or papillary patterns.
        - **Papillary RCC (~10-15%):** Displays a papillary growth pattern.
        - **Chromophobe RCC (~5%):** Cells have pale, eosinophilic, somewhat granular cytoplasm and often irregular nuclei .
        - **Sarcomatoid Type RCC (~1.5%):** This is the **most anaplastic and poorly differentiated** form, composed of whorls of atypical spindle tumour cells.
        - **Collecting Duct Type RCC (rare, ~0.5%):** A rare variant that occurs in the medulla, composed of cuboidal tumour cells arranged in tubular and papillary patterns.
- **c. Spread of the Disease:**
    
    - **Local Invasion:** The tumour can **directly invade** adjacent organs such as the liver and regional perirenal lymph nodes.
    - **Haematogenous Spread:** This is the **most common route for distant metastasis**. RCC frequently spreads to sites with rich blood supply, including the **lungs, brain, and bone**.
    - **Tumour Thrombus:** A significant characteristic of RCC is the frequent presence of **tumour thrombus in the renal vein**, which can **extend into the inferior vena cava** and even reach the right side of the heart, from where it can embolize.
    - **Lymphatic Spread:** While haematogenous spread is more common for distant sites, RCC can also spread to regional lymph nodes via the lymphatic system.
