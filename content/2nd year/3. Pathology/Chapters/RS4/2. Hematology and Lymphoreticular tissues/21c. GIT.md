1. A 40-year-old police constable gives history of epigastric pain and it is relieved after eating certain food, for the last 6 months. Now he is presenting with dark coloured stools.
	a. What is your diagnosis?
	b. Describe the etiology, pathogenesis and microscopic feature of this condition.	
	c. Write its complications?
	**Clinical Case Analysis:** A 40-year-old male police constable with a history of chronic epigastric pain relieved after eating, now presenting with dark coloured stools.
	
	**a. What is your diagnosis?**
	
	- Probable Diagnosis: **Chronic Peptic Ulcer, likely Duodenal**.
	    - Reasoning: The pain pattern (relief after food) is characteristic of a **duodenal ulcer**. Dark stools indicate melaena, which is a common complication of peptic ulcers due to haemorrhage.
	
	**b. Describe the etiology, pathogenesis and microscopic feature of this condition.**
	
	- **Etiology & Pathogenesis:**
	    
	    - _H. pylori_ is implicated in the etiology.
	    - Use of **analgesics**, particularly NSAIDs, over an extended period are known to damage gastric mucosa. (While this source links NSAIDs directly to gastric mucosa damage, they are well-established causes of peptic ulcers generally).
	    - Stressful life and job may contribute.
	    - Pathogenesis involves an imbalance leading to mucosal damage and ulcer formation.
	- **Microscopic Features (Chronic Peptic Ulcer):**
	    
	    - Histologically, duodenal ulcers are **indistinguishable from gastric ulcers**.
	    - Chronic peptic ulcers have **4 histological zones** from within outside:
	        1. **Necrotic zone:** Fibrinous exudate, necrotic debris, few leucocytes. Lies in the floor of the ulcer.
	        2. **Superficial exudative zone**.
	        3. **Granulation tissue zone**.
	        4. **Cicatrisation zone:** Fibrosis and scarring.
	
	**c. Write its complications?**
	
	- Complications of Duodenal Ulcer:
	    - **Haemorrhage**: Presenting as melaena (dark stools) or haematemesis. This is likely causing the patient's current symptom.
	    - **Perforation**.
	    - **Obstruction** (at times).
	    - **Malignant transformation never occurs** in duodenal ulcers.

	- Probable Diagnosis: **Gastric Carcinoma (Adenocarcinoma)**.

The endoscopic finding of an **exophytic mass** in the stomach is a characteristic gross pattern of gastric carcinoma. The patient's age (60 years old) also fits the typical demographic (for oesophageal cancer, but GI cancers share similar age demographics generally).

	    - Association with **achlorhydria**.
	    - Development in patients who have undergone **partial gastrectomy** (stump carcinoma).

5. Polyps of large intestine,--etiology, pathogenesis, pathology (morphology) and complications, gross microscopy, clinical features
	**Large Intestinal Polyps**
	
	**Definition:**
	
	- Any growth or mass protruding from the mucous membrane into the lumen of the large intestine.
	- More common in the large intestine than the small intestine, particularly in the rectosigmoid colon.
	
	**Classification:**
	
	- Broadly classified into 2 groups:
	    - **Non-neoplastic Polyps**
	    - **Neoplastic Polyps (Adenomas)** (Includes epithelial tumours, both benign and malignant).
	
	**Etiology & Pathogenesis:**
	
	- **Non-neoplastic Polyps:**
	    - Often **regenerative, non-neoplastic lesions**.
	    - Hyperplastic polyps: Characterised by epithelial **hyperplasia** at the base of the crypts.
	    - Inflammatory polyps (Pseudopolyps): Appear due to **re-epithelialisation of undermined ulcers** and overhanging margins, typically in **inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)**, most frequently ulcerative colitis and sometimes Crohn's disease.
	    - Lymphoid polyps: Result from **reactive hyperplasia of lymphoid tissue**.
	    - Hamartomatous polyps (Peutz-Jeghers, Juvenile): Tumour-like lesions composed of an **abnormal mixture of normal tissues**. Juvenile polyps involve cystic dilation of glands.
	- **Neoplastic Polyps (Adenomas):**
	    - True epithelial neoplasms with potential for malignant transformation.
	    - Development related to the **adenoma-carcinoma sequence** (details not provided in sources).
	
	**Morphology (Pathology):**
	
	- **Gross Appearance (G/A):**
	    - Can be **sessile** or **pedunculated**.
	    - Size varies significantly (minute nodules to several centimetres).
	    - Hyperplastic: Generally multiple, sessile, smooth-surfaced, small (<0.5 cm).
	    - Juvenile: Spherical, smooth-surfaced, about 2 cm, often pedunculated.
	    - Villous adenoma: Usually large (1-10 cm+), often sessile, surface may be haemorrhagic or ulcerated.
	    - Inflammatory (Pseudopolyps): Multiple, cylindrical to rounded overgrowths.
	    - Familial polyposis coli: Mucosal surface straddled with multiple polypoid structures of varying sizes, many pedunculated.
	- **Microscopic Appearance (M/E):**
	    - **Hyperplastic:** Long, cystically dilated glands/crypts lined by normal epithelial cells, often with a serrated or saw-toothed luminal border. Centre is connective tissue core with inflammatory cells.
	    - **Hamartomatous (Peutz-Jeghers):** Characteristic **tree-like branching of muscularis mucosae**, lined by normal epithelium. Glands may be hyperplastic/cystic.
	    - **Hamartomatous (Juvenile):** Cystically dilated glands containing mucus, lined by normal mucus-secreting epithelium. Stroma may show inflammatory cells.
	    - **Inflammatory (Pseudopolyps):** Connective tissue core with inflammatory cell infiltrate, covered by regenerating epithelial cells and some cystic glands.
	    - **Lymphoid:** Prominent lymphoid follicles with germinal centres in submucosa/mucosa, covered by inflamed epithelium.
	    - **Neoplastic (Adenomas):**
	        - Generally show epithelial dysplasia (cells varying from benign to anaplastic).
	        - **Villous adenoma:** Characterised by many slender, finger-like **villi** arising from the muscularis mucosae area. Each papilla has a fibrovascular stromal core covered by the dysplastic epithelium. Excess mucus secretion sometimes seen.
	        - Tubular and Tubulovillous adenomas are other patterns.
	
	**Clinical Features:**
	
	- Often **symptomless** (e.g., Hyperplastic polyps).
	- Symptoms depend on type and size:
	    - **Rectal bleeding** common (e.g., Juvenile polyps, Villous adenomas).
	    - **Diarrhoea** and **mucus** may be features (e.g., Villous adenomas).
	    - Symptoms in inflammatory polyps are related to the underlying **inflammatory bowel disease**.
	
	**Malignant Potential & Complications:**
	
	- **Non-neoplastic Polyps:**
	    - Generally have **no malignant potential**.
	    - Exceptions: Malignancy can occur if a coexistent adenoma is present (e.g., in Hyperplastic or Peutz-Jeghers polyps). Cancer in long-standing ulcerative colitis arises from epithelial dysplasia, not the inflammatory polyps themselves.
	- **Neoplastic Polyps (Adenomas):**
	    - More significant due to their **possible malignant change**.
	    - Risk of malignancy is related to type and size (e.g., Villous adenomas frequently show severe atypia, carcinoma in situ, and invasive carcinoma reported in 30%).
	- **Familial Polyposis Syndromes:**
	    - Have a **very high malignant potential**. Colorectal cancer develops in virtually **100% of cases by age 50 years** if untreated (e.g., Familial polyposis coli).
	- Other Complications (generally for large polyps):
	    - Bleeding (leading to haematemesis in the case scenario, though this is gastric [a]) or rectal bleeding.
	    - Obstruction (if large or multiple) (mentioned as peptic ulcer complication but applicable to any obstructing lesion).

7. Crohn's disease--etiology, pathogenesis, pathology (morphology) and complications, gross microscopy, clinical features
	**Crohn's Disease (Inflammatory Bowel Disease)**
	
	**Definition:** A chronic inflammatory condition that can involve any portion of the gastrointestinal tract. It is distinguished from Ulcerative Colitis by specific morphologic features.
	
	**Aetiology & Pathogenesis:**
	
	- Develops in a **genetically predisposed individual**.
	- Results from the effects of **exogenous and endogenous host factors** leading to **dysregulation of mucosal immune function**.
	- Further modified by certain **environmental factors**.
	- Suggested mechanism: an **immune reaction against intestinal bacteria or self-antigens**.
	
	**Clinical Features:**
	
	- Specific typical symptoms (like pain, diarrhoea, weight loss) are not explicitly detailed in the provided sources, but complications may present clinically.
	
	**Pathology (Morphology):**
	
	- Morphologic features are **sufficiently distinctive** from Ulcerative Colitis.
	- **Gross Appearance (G/A):**
	    - Can involve **any portion of the gastrointestinal tract**.
	    - Characteristically **segmental lesions** with **intervening uninvolved 'skip areas'**.
	    - Bowel wall is **thickened** and the lumen **narrowed**, giving a **'hose-pipe appearance'**.
	    - Mucosa shows **serpiginous ulcers**.
	    - May have **deep fissures** into the bowel wall.
	    - Swollen intervening surviving mucosa gives a **'cobblestone appearance'**.
	    - Contrasts with Ulcerative Colitis which shows continuous involvement, superficial ulcers, inflammatory pseudopolyps, and loss of haustral folds ('garden-hose appearance').
	- **Microscopic Appearance (M/E):**
	    - Shows **transmural chronic inflammatory cell infiltration**.
	    - Presence of **deep fissures** into the bowel wall.
	    - **Submucosal widening** due to **oedema**.
	    - Some prominent **lymphoid follicles**.
	    - Presence of occasional **non-caseating epithelioid cell granulomas**.
	    - Leads to **fibrosis** in the affected bowel wall.
	
	**Complications:**
	
	- **Malabsorption**: Due to impaired absorption of fat, vitamin B12, proteins, and electrolytes from the diseased small bowel.
	- **Fistula formation**: May occur in long-standing cases; can be internal (between intestinal loops) or external (e.g., enterocutaneous, rectal, anal).
	- **Stricture formation**: May occur in chronic cases due to extensive **fibrosis** in the affected bowel wall.
	- **Malignancy**: Development of malignancy in the small intestine is rarer than in ulcerative colitis. Lymphoma may develop more often than adenocarcinoma.
	
	**Clinical Relevance:** Understanding the characteristic segmental, transmural morphology and the presence of non-caseating granulomas microscopically is key to differentiating Crohn's disease from Ulcerative Colitis. Pathologic assessment of biopsy specimens (gross and microscopic) is essential for diagnosis and predicting complications like strictures and fistulae.

Specifically for colorectal cancer, the Dukes' staging system has been **modified by Astler-Coller**. This modified system divides colorectal cancers into various stages, each with an associated overall 5-year survival rate [213, Table 18.12]:

- **Stage A**: Cancer confined to the mucosa [214, Table 18.12]. The 5-year survival rate for this stage is **90-100%** [214, Table 18.12].
- **Stage B1**: Cancer involves the muscularis propria but does not penetrate it, and there is no lymph node involvement [214, Table 18.12]. The 5-year survival rate is **65-85%** [214, Table 18.12].
- **Stage B2**: Cancer extends into the serosa [214, Table 18.12]. The 5-year survival rate is **50-70%** [214, Table 18.12].
- **Stage C1 (III Tx N1 M0)**: Cancer involves the muscularis propria with regional lymph node involvement [214, Table 18.12]. The 5-year survival rate is **35-65%** [214, Table 18.12].
- **Stage C2 (III Tx N1 M0)**: Cancer extends into the serosa with regional lymph node involvement [214, Table 18.12]. The 5-year survival rate is **20-35%** [214, Table 18.12].
- **Stage D (IV Tx Nx M1)**: Cancer with distant metastases [214, Table 18.12]. The 5-year survival rate is **5%** [214, Table 18.12].

In addition to Dukes' ABC staging, the **TNM staging** system is also used for colorectal cancer.
