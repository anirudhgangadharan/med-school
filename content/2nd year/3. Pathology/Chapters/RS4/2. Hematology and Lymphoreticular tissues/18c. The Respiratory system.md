1. . A chronic smoker with history of exertional dyspnea and weight loss, died of road accident. On autopsy his both lungs were voluminous with rib markings on its surface.
	a. What is your diagnosis?
	b. Describe its different types.
	c. What are its complications?
	Here is the answer to your query, formatted for medical school exams:
	
	**Clinical Case:** Chronic smoker, exertional dyspnoea, weight loss, died in road accident. Autopsy: voluminous lungs with rib markings on surface.
	
	**a. Diagnosis**
	
	- Based on the clinical history of a chronic smoker with exertional dyspnoea and weight loss, combined with the gross autopsy finding of **voluminous lungs with rib markings** on the surface, the diagnosis is **Pulmonary Emphysema**.
	- Emphysema is defined morphologically by permanent dilatation of air spaces distal to the terminal bronchiole and destruction of the walls of dilated air spaces.
	- The voluminous appearance and visible rib markings (subpleural bullae/blebs bulging outwards with rib markings between them) are characteristic gross features of advanced emphysema.
	
	**b. Types of Emphysema**
	
	Pulmonary emphysema is classified morphologically according to the portion of the acinus involved. The 'true emphysema' types include:
	
	- **Centriacinar (Centrilobular) Emphysema:**
	    - Characterised by initial involvement of the **respiratory bronchioles** (central or proximal part of the acinus).
	    - The distal parts of the acinus are normal in mild cases.
	    - More common and severe in the **upper lobes**.
	    - Strongly associated with **smoking**. Often coexists with chronic bronchitis.
	    - Grossly, shows distended air spaces in the centre of the lobules. Microscopically, distension and destruction of respiratory bronchiole surrounded by normal alveoli.
	- **Panacinar (Panlobular) Emphysema:**
	    - Affects **all portions of the acinus** but not necessarily the entire lung.
	    - Most often associated with **α1-antitrypsin deficiency**, typically in middle-aged smokers.
	    - Produces the most characteristic anatomical changes in the lung in emphysema.
	    - Grossly, lungs are voluminous, pale with little blood. Microscopically, ruptured alveolar walls, spurs of broken septa, stretched and thinned capillaries, loss of elastic tissue.
	- **Paraseptal (Distal Acinar) Emphysema:**
	    - Involves the **distal part of the acinus**.
	    - Localised along the pleura and perilobular septa.
	- **Irregular (Para-cicatricial) Emphysema:**
	    - Characterised by irregular involvement of the acinus and associated with scarring. Often asymptomatic and an incidental finding.
	- **Mixed (Unclassified) Emphysema:**
	    - When more than one type of emphysema is present in the same lung, often due to severe involvement blurring clear distinctions.
	
	**c. Complications**
	
	In patients with predominant emphysema, the usual terminal events are:
	
	- **Right Heart Failure (Cor Pulmonale):** This occurs as increased pulmonary vascular resistance due to lung destruction puts a pressure overload on the right ventricle, leading to hypertrophy and ultimately dilatation and failure of the right heart.
	- **Hypercapneic Respiratory Failure:** Due to impaired gas exchange as a result of the destruction of alveolar walls and dilated air spaces.
	
	**(Note:** Chronic bronchitis frequently coexists with emphysema and can lead to complications like recurrent respiratory infections and cor pulmonale. While these are often seen in patients with emphysema, Cor Pulmonale and Hypercapneic Respiratory Failure are explicitly listed as terminal events of _predominant emphysema_).
2. Lung abscess.
	
	**Lung Abscess**
	
	- **Definition:** A localised area of **necrosis of lung tissue with suppuration**.
	- **Types:**
	    - **Primary lung abscess:** Develops in an otherwise **normal lung**. Commonest cause is **aspiration of infected material**.
	    - **Secondary lung abscess:** Develops as a **complication** of another lung disease or from another site.
	- **Etiopathogenesis:** Commonly isolated microorganisms include streptococci, staphylococci, and various gram-negative organisms. These are introduced by mechanisms like aspiration.
	- **Morphological Features:**
	    - **Location:** Abscesses due to aspiration are more likely to be in the **right lung** (due to more vertical main bronchus) and are frequently single. They are commonly located in the **lower part of the right upper lobe or apex of right lower lobe**. Abscesses developing from preceding pneumonia and septic/pyaemic abscesses are often multiple and scattered.
	    - **Gross Appearance (G/A):**
	        - May be of variable size, from a few millimetres to large cavities (5-6 cm).
	        - The cavity often contains **exudate**.
	        - An acute lung abscess is initially surrounded by **acute pneumonia** and has a **poorly-defined, ragged wall**. The pleura may be thickened.
	        - With passage of time, it becomes chronic and develops a **fibrous wall**.
	        - Cut surface shows multiple cavities having **irregular and ragged inner walls**. The lumina contain **necrotic debris**. The surrounding lung parenchyma is consolidated.
	    - **Microscopic Appearance (M/E):**
	        - The wall of the abscess shows **dense infiltration by polymorphonuclear leucocytes** (neutrophils). There is varying number of macrophages.
	        - More chronic cases show **fibroblasts** at the periphery.
	        - **Alveolar walls in the affected area are destroyed**.
	        - The lumen contains **pus** consisting of purulent exudate, some red cells, fragments of tissue debris, and fibrin.
	        - The field shows abscess formed by **necrosed alveoli** and dense acute and chronic inflammatory cells.
3. Bronchiectasis etiology, pathogenesis, pathology (morphology) and complications
	**Bronchiectasis**
	
	- **Definition:** Abnormal and **irreversible dilatation of the bronchi and larger bronchioles**.
	    
	- **Etiology & Pathogenesis:**
	    
	    - Often results from **infection** and **obstruction**.
	    - Pathogenesis involves **damaged airways**.
	    - **Tuberculosis** may cause bronchiectasis as a complication.
	- **Pathology (Morphology):**
	    
	    - **Gross Appearance (G/A):**
	        - Characteristically affects **distal bronchi and bronchioles** beyond segmental bronchi.
	        - Often involves the **lower lobes**, frequently bilateral. (More vertical air passages of left lower lobe are more often involved than the right).
	        - Pleura is usually **fibrotic and thickened** with adhesions to the chest wall.
	        - Cut surface shows characteristic **honey-combed appearance**.
	        - Dilated airways extend extensively, nearly to the pleura. Their walls are **thickened**.
	        - Lumina of the dilated airways are filled with **mucus or muco-pus**.
	        - Intervening lung parenchyma is **reduced and fibrotic**.
	        - **Types of bronchial dilatation** (gross/bronchographic appearance):
	            - **Cylindrical:** Tube-like (most common).
	            - **Fusiform:** Spindle-shaped.
	            - **Saccular:** Rounded, sac-like.
	            - **Varicose:** Irregular enlargements.
	    - **Microscopic Appearance (M/E):**
	        - Bronchial epithelium may be normal, **ulcerated**, or show **squamous metaplasia**.
	        - Bronchial wall shows infiltration by **acute and chronic inflammatory cells**.
	        - **Destruction of normal muscle and elastic tissue** in the bronchial wall, with **replacement by fibrosis**.
	        - The mucosa may be sloughed off in places, with muco-pus in the lumen.
	        - The intervening lung parenchyma shows **fibrosis**.
	        - The surrounding lung tissue shows changes of **interstitial pneumonia**.
	- **Clinical Features:**
	    
	    - Major clinical feature is **copious foul-smelling expectoration**.
	    - A common cause of **haemoptysis**.
	- **Complications/Sequelae:**
	    
	    - Fibrosis of the intervening lung parenchyma.
	    - Interstitial pneumonia in surrounding lung tissue.
	    - Recurrent infections (implied by purulent exudate and foul-smelling expectoration).
4. Emphysema-definition, types and etiopathogenesis, pathology (morphology) and complications, gross and microscopic features, clinical features
	
	**Pulmonary Emphysema**
	
	- **Definition:**
	    
	    - A morphological definition characterised by **permanent dilatation of air spaces distal to the terminal bronchiole** and the **destruction of the walls** of these dilated air spaces.
	    - Emphysema is defined morphologically, contrasting with chronic bronchitis which is defined clinically. The two conditions frequently coexist.
	- **Types:**
	    
	    - Classified by the **portion of the acinus involved**.
	    - Includes 'True Emphysema' and 'Overinflation' types.
	    - **True Emphysema Types**:
	        - **Centriacinar (Centrilobular):** Initial involvement of **respiratory bronchioles** (central/proximal acinus). More common/severe in **upper lobes**. Often associated with large amount of **black pigment**. Usually coexists with chronic bronchitis and occurs predominantly in **smokers**.
	        - **Panacinar (Panlobular):** Affects **all portions of the acinus**. Lungs are voluminous, pale, with little blood. Associated with **α1-antitrypsin deficiency**. Microscopically, shows **ruptured alveolar walls**, spurs of broken septa, stretched/thinned capillaries, **loss of elastic tissue**, usually **absent inflammatory changes**.
	        - **Paraseptal (Distal Acinar):** Involves the **distal part of the acinus**. Localised along the **pleura** and perilobular septa. Seen adjacent to fibrosis/atelectasis. More severe in upper lungs. Common cause of **spontaneous pneumothorax in young adults**. Grossly, subpleural air-filled cysts (0.5-2 cm).
	        - **Irregular (Para-cicatricial):** **Most common form**, seen surrounding **scars**. Irregular involvement. Often **asymptomatic**, incidental autopsy finding.
	        - **Mixed (Unclassified):** More than one type in the same lung, often due to severe involvement blurring distinctions.
	    - **Overinflation Types** (loosely termed emphysema, without significant wall destruction): Compensatory, Senile Hyperinflation, Obstructive Overinflation, Unilateral Translucent Lung, Interstitial Emphysema. (Note: Interstitial emphysema involves air in connective tissue framework, not true emphysema morphologically as septal walls are not damaged).
	- **Etiopathogenesis:**
	    
	    - Explained by the **protease-antiprotease mechanism**.
	    - An **imbalance** occurs between proteases (like elastase) and antiproteases (like α1-antitrypsin), leading to destruction of elastic tissue in alveolar walls.
	    - **Smoking** is a major factor: Tobacco smoke causes release of elastase from neutrophils. Smokers have more neutrophils and very high elastase activity.
	    - **α1-Antitrypsin deficiency** is a genetic cause, leading to reduced antiprotease activity and increased risk.
	- **Pathology (Morphology):** Emphysema diagnosis is made with certainty by gross and histologic examination of whole lung sections.
	    
	    - **Gross Appearance (G/A):** Lungs are **voluminous, pale** with little blood. Edges are rounded. Advanced cases show **subpleural bullae** (>1 cm air-filled cysts formed by rupture of adjacent air spaces) and **blebs** (rupture of alveoli into subpleural interstitial tissue). Blebs are a common cause of spontaneous pneumothorax.
	    - **Microscopic Appearance (M/E):** Dilatation of air spaces and **destruction of septal walls**. Specific findings vary by type:
	        - **Panacinar:** Ruptured alveolar walls, spurs of broken septa, stretched/thinned capillaries, **loss of elastic tissue**, usually **absent inflammatory changes**.
	        - **Centriacinar:** Distension/destruction of respiratory bronchiole, surrounded by intact alveoli. Often associated with black pigment.
	        - Changes of bronchitis may be present. Bullae and blebs show fibrosis and chronic inflammation of walls.
	- **Clinical Features:**
	    
	    - Clinical features develop after about one-third of pulmonary parenchyma is damaged.
	    - Age at diagnosis is often later than chronic bronchitis (about 60 years).
	    - Major clinical feature: **Exertional dyspnoea**.
	    - Considerable overlap with chronic bronchitis features. Patients with predominant emphysema may be called 'pink puffers' (Note: 'blue bloaters' is for predominant bronchitis, and this specific 'pink puffer' term isn't in the sources, but the exertional dyspnoea and less prominent cyanosis aligns).
	    - Chest X-ray shows **small heart** with **hyperinflated lungs**.
	- **Complications/Sequelae (based _only_ on provided sources):**
	    
	    - **Spontaneous pneumothorax** (caused by blebs).
	    - **Hypercapneic respiratory failure** (usual terminal event).

	**1. Ferruginous bodies are seen in:**
	
	**c. Asbestosis**
	
	**Explanation:**
	
	- **Robbins' Perspective:** Ferruginous bodies are pathognomonic (specifically diagnostic) for **asbestosis**. They are golden-brown, fusiform (spindle-shaped) or beaded structures with a translucent core.
	- **Formation:** These bodies form when inhaled asbestos fibers become coated with iron-containing proteinaceous material, including ferritin and hemosiderin, by macrophages attempting to phagocytose the indigestible fibers.
	- **Why not the other options?**
	    - **Silicosis:** Characterized by silicotic nodules composed of concentrically arranged collagen fibers and silica particles. While macrophages are involved in the response to silica, ferruginous bodies are not a feature.
	    - **Byssinosis (Brown Lung Disease):** Caused by inhalation of cotton dust. The pathology involves airway inflammation and bronchoconstriction, not ferruginous bodies.
	    - **Bagassosis:** Results from inhalation of dust from dried sugarcane residue (bagasse). It leads to hypersensitivity pneumonitis with interstitial inflammation and granulomas, not ferruginous bodies.
	
	**2. Which type of asthma occurs in late adult life?**
	
	**c. Intrinsic asthma**
	
	**Explanation:**
	
	- **Robbins' Perspective:** Asthma is broadly classified into atopic (extrinsic) and non-atopic (intrinsic) types.
	    - **Atopic (Allergic) Asthma:** The most common type, usually begins in childhood. It is a classic type I hypersensitivity reaction triggered by environmental allergens, with evidence of IgE sensitization.
	    - **Intrinsic (Non-Atopic) Asthma:** Typically develops in **adulthood** and is not linked to specific external allergens or IgE sensitization. It can be triggered by respiratory infections (especially viral), cold air, exercise, emotional stress, inhaled irritants (like cigarette smoke), and certain drugs (like aspirin).
	    - **Mixed Asthma:** Some patients have features of both atopic and non-atopic asthma.
	- **Why not the other options?**
	    - **Atopic asthma** usually has its onset in childhood or adolescence.
	    - **Allergic asthma** is synonymous with atopic asthma and therefore also typically starts earlier in life.
	    - **Mixed asthma** can occur at any age, but the question specifically asks for the type that _occurs_ in late adult life, making intrinsic asthma the most fitting answer.
	
	**3. Bronchiectasis is characterized by, except:**
	
	**d. Large alveoli**
	
	**Explanation:**
	
	- **Robbins' Perspective:** Bronchiectasis is defined by **permanent dilation of bronchi and bronchioles** due to destruction of the muscle and elastic supporting tissue. This leads to chronic necrotizing infection and inflammation of the airways.
	- **Key Characteristics:**
	    - **a. Dilatation of bronchi:** This is the defining feature of bronchiectasis.
	    - **b. Lung abscess:** Lung abscesses can be a complication of severe or neglected bronchiectasis due to the accumulation of infected secretions and tissue destruction.
	    - **c. Peribronchiolar fibrosis:** Chronic inflammation in the walls of the dilated airways leads to scarring and fibrosis in the surrounding lung tissue (peribronchiolar fibrosis).
	- **Why not large alveoli?**
	    - **d. Large alveoli:** Large alveoli (emphysema) are characteristic of **emphysema**, a component of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD). While bronchiectasis can coexist with COPD in some cases, it is not a primary feature of bronchiectasis itself. The damage in bronchiectasis is centered on the airways, not the alveolar airspaces.
	
	**4. Bronchogenic carcinoma has increased incidence in:**	
		**b. Asbestosis**
			
			**Explanation:**
			
			- **Robbins' Perspective:** Several occupational lung diseases increase the risk of lung cancer (bronchogenic carcinoma).
			- **Strong Association with Asbestos:** **Asbestos exposure significantly increases the risk of lung cancer.** This risk is further multiplied in smokers with a history of asbestos exposure. Asbestos can also cause mesothelioma, a cancer of the pleura.
			- **Increased Risk with Other Pneumoconioses:**
			    - **Silicosis:** Also increases the risk of lung cancer, particularly in the form of adenocarcinoma or squamous cell carcinoma, often in the setting of progressive massive fibrosis.
			    - **Beryllosis:** Chronic berylliosis, characterized by non-caseating granulomas, is associated with an increased risk of lung cancer.
			    - **Coal workers' pneumoconiosis:** While it causes significant lung disease, the increased risk of lung cancer is less pronounced compared to asbestosis and silicosis.
			- **Therefore, asbestosis has a particularly well-established and significant association with an increased incidence of bronchogenic carcinoma.**
		
	**5. A 50-year-old male presents with breathlessness and weight loss. Imaging revealed nodular lesion in the left lung lower lobe. Biopsy shows an adenocarcinoma. Which of the following is the likely mutation associated with this condition?**
	
	**a. EGFR mutation**
		
		**Explanation:**
		
		- **Robbins' Perspective on Lung Adenocarcinoma:** Adenocarcinoma is the most common type of lung cancer in never-smokers and is also seen in smokers. Molecular profiling has revealed important driver mutations that are often targetable with specific therapies.
		- **Key Mutations in Adenocarcinoma:**
		    - **EGFR (Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor) mutations:** These are frequently found in adenocarcinomas, particularly in **never-smokers, women, and individuals of East Asian descent**. They are often associated with a better response to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs).
		    - **ALK (Anaplastic Lymphoma Kinase) rearrangements:** Another important driver mutation in adenocarcinoma, more common in younger, never-smokers.
		    - **KRAS mutations:** Common in smokers with adenocarcinoma and are generally considered a negative prognostic factor and not currently targetable.
		    - **Other less frequent mutations:** BRAF, ROS1, MET, etc.
		- **Why EGFR is the likely mutation:** Given the patient's adenocarcinoma and the fact that EGFR mutations are prevalent in this histological subtype, especially in non-smokers (although the smoking history isn't provided, EGFR is still a strong possibility), it is the most likely mutation among the options.
		- **Why not the other options?**
		    - **PTEN mutation:** While PTEN is a tumor suppressor gene involved in various cancers, it is not as frequently implicated as a primary driver mutation in lung adenocarcinoma compared to EGFR.
		    - **BCL2 mutation:** BCL2 is an anti-apoptotic gene involved in the pathogenesis of some lymphomas and leukemias, but it is not a common driver mutation in lung adenocarcinoma.
		    - **APC mutation:** APC is a tumor suppressor gene classically associated with colorectal cancer (Familial Adenomatous Polyposis). It is not a primary driver mutation in lung adenocarcinoma.
	
	**6. Bronchial carcinoid arises from:**
		
		**c. Neuroendocrine cells**
		
		**Explanation:**
		
		- **Robbins' Perspective on Bronchial Carcinoids:** Bronchial carcinoids are low-grade malignant neuroendocrine tumors of the lung.
		- **Origin:** They originate from **neuroendocrine cells** (also known as Kulchitsky cells or K cells) present in the bronchial epithelium. These cells are part of the diffuse neuroendocrine system and can secrete various hormones.
		- **Histological Features:** Carcinoids typically exhibit a characteristic organoid pattern (nests or ribbons of uniform cells with granular cytoplasm and round nuclei). They are classified as typical or atypical based on mitotic rate and necrosis.
		- **Why not the other options?**
		    - **Columnar epithelial cells:** These are the predominant lining cells of the bronchi but are the origin of most bronchogenic carcinomas (like adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma), not carcinoids.
		    - **Goblet cells:** These are mucus-secreting cells within the bronchial epithelium. While they can undergo hyperplasia in certain lung conditions, they are not the origin of carcinoid tumors.
		    - **Alveolar lining cells (Type I and Type II pneumocytes):** These cells line the alveoli, the gas-exchanging units of the lung. They are the origin of alveolar cell carcinoma (a subtype of adenocarcinoma) but not bronchial carcinoids, which arise in the airways.
