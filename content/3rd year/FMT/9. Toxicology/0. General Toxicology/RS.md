### CLASSIFICATION OF POISONS

Poisons are primarily classified in forensic toxicology based on two major parameters: **(A) The chief clinical symptoms they produce** and **(B) Their morbid anatomic (postmortem) manifestations**. 

---

#### A. CLINICAL CLASSIFICATION (Based on Chief Symptoms)
This is the most widely accepted medicolegal classification. 

**I. CORROSIVES**
These substances fix, destroy, and erode the surface they come into contact with, producing severe coagulation or liquefaction necrosis.
*   **Strong Acids:**
    *   *Inorganic (Mineral):* Sulphuric acid (oil of vitriol), Nitric acid (aqua fortis), Hydrochloric acid.
    *   *Organic:* Carbolic acid (Phenol), Oxalic acid, Acetic acid, Salicylic acid.
*   **Strong Alkalis:** Sodium hydroxide, Potassium hydroxide, Ammonium hydroxide.
*   **Metallic Salts:** Zinc chloride, Ferric chloride, Copper sulphate, Silver nitrate, Potassium cyanide, Chromates, and Bichromates.

**II. IRRITANTS**
These substances cause intense congestion and inflammation of the mucous membranes.
*   **Inorganic:**
    *   *Non-metallic:* Phosphorus, Chlorine, Bromine, Iodine, Carbon tetrachloride.
    *   *Metallic (Heavy Metals):* Arsenic, Antimony, Lead, Copper, Mercury, Silver, Zinc, Thallium, Iron.
    *   *Mechanical:* Powdered glass, Diamond dust, Hair.
*   **Organic:**
    *   *Vegetable:* *Abrus precatorius*, Castor (*Ricinus communis*), Croton, *Calotropis*, Aloes, *Semecarpus anacardium*, *Capsicum*.
    *   *Animal:* Snake venom, Insect venom, Scorpion sting, Cantharides (Spanish fly), Ptomaines.

**III. SYSTEMIC POISONS**
These poisons act primarily on specific organ systems after absorption.
*   **1. Nervous (Neurotoxic):**
    *   *Cerebral Depressants (Inebriants & Sedatives):* Alcohols, General anaesthetics, Opioid analgesics, Barbiturates, Hypnotics.
    *   *Cerebral Stimulants:* Amphetamines, Caffeine, Methylphenidate.
    *   *Cerebral Deliriants:* *Datura*, *Belladonna*, *Hyoscyamus*, Cannabis, Cocaine.
    *   *Psychotropics:* Antidepressants, Neuroleptics, Hallucinogens (e.g., LSD, phencyclidine).
    *   *Spinal:* Strychnine (Stimulant), Gelsemium (Depressant).
    *   *Peripheral:* Curare, Conium (Hemlock).
*   **2. Cardiovascular:** Aconite, Digitalis, Oleanders, Tobacco/Nicotine, Quinine, Cyanide.
*   **3. Asphyxiants:** Carbon monoxide (CO), Carbon dioxide (CO2), Hydrogen sulphide, Cyanide gas.

**IV. MISCELLANEOUS & AGRICULTURAL POISONS**
*   **Food Poisons:** Botulism, bacterial food poisoning.
*   **Agrochemicals:** Organophosphates, Carbamates, Chlorinated hydrocarbons (e.g., Endrin, DDT), Aluminium phosphide.

---

#### B. PATHOLOGICAL CLASSIFICATION (Based on Morbid Anatomy)
This classification is critical for the autopsy pathologist, grouped by the presence or absence of structural tissue changes.

| Pathological Finding | Mechanism & Examples |
| :--- | :--- |
| **1. No Morphologic Changes** | No direct cellular destruction; death is due to shock, terminal anoxia, or functional impairment. Seen in acute CNS depressants (alcohols, barbiturates), chemical asphyxiants (CO, HCN), organophosphates, and most alkaloids. |
| **2. Systemic Lesions ONLY** | No injury at the portal of entry (e.g., normal stomach), but severe systemic damage occurs. Classic for acute haemolytic poisons like Arsine gas and Nitrobenzene. |
| **3. Local Portal Injury ONLY** | Severe destruction at the contact site (mouth/stomach/lungs) with no significant remote systemic tissue injuries. Seen with Corrosives, Chlorine, and Sulphur dioxide. Death is often due to acute vasomotor collapse or pulmonary oedema. |
| **4. Local & Systemic Lesions** | Simultaneous damage at the portal of entry and remote organ systems. Classic hallmark of **Heavy Metals** (e.g., Arsenic, Mercury, Lead). |

### PART 1: FACTORS MODIFYING THE ACTION OF A POISON

The toxic effect of any substance is highly variable and depends on multiple pharmacological, physiological, and physical factors.

**1. Quantity (Dose)**

- **General Rule:** The greater the quantity, the more severe the toxic effects.
- **Exception:** A massive dose of certain poisons (e.g., alcohol or copper sulphate) may act as a direct gastric irritant, causing immediate vomiting that expels the poison and paradoxically reduces toxicity.

**2. Form of the Poison**

- **Physical State:** Gases are absorbed most rapidly, followed by liquids, and lastly solids (whose action heavily depends on their aqueous or lipid solubility).
- **Chemical Combination:** Toxic substances can be rendered completely harmless when combined chemically (e.g., highly toxic silver nitrate combined with hydrochloric acid forms harmless, insoluble silver chloride).
- **Mechanical Combination:** Dilution significantly alters action; for example, a concentrated mineral acid causes severe coagulative necrosis, but the same dose heavily diluted in water is virtually harmless.

**3. Mode of Administration**

- The route of entry dictates the speed of systemic absorption.
- **Order of Rapidity:** Inhalation (gases/vapours) > Intravenous > Intramuscular/Subcutaneous > Application to wounds > Serous surfaces > Respiratory mucosa > Oral (GI tract) > Rectum/Vagina > Intact skin.

**4. Condition of the Body (Physiological Factors)**

- **Age:** Extremes of age (infants and the elderly) are highly susceptible due to immature or failing hepatic drug-metabolizing enzymes and variations in the blood-brain barrier. _(Exception: Children can tolerate proportionately larger doses of belladonna)_.
- **Idiosyncrasy:** An inherent personal hypersensitivity where a normal, safe dose provokes a massive, sometimes fatal, allergic or anaphylactic reaction (e.g., penicillin, aspirin, iodine).
- **Tolerance (Habituation):** Repeated exposure decreases cellular response. Chronic addicts can consume massive doses of drugs (like opium, alcohol, or barbiturates) that would be instantly fatal to a non-user.
- **State of Health:** Underlying diseases increase susceptibility (e.g., carbon monoxide is rapidly fatal in persons with pre-existing coronary artery disease). Conversely, some diseases increase tolerance (e.g., massive doses of opium are tolerated in tetanus, and strychnine in paralysis).
- **Sleep & Intoxication:** The action of a poison is significantly delayed if the person goes to sleep or is already intoxicated soon after ingestion.

**5. Cumulative Action**

- Poisons that are eliminated very slowly (e.g., heavy metals like lead and arsenic) accumulate in the tissues over time, ultimately reaching toxic threshold levels even if individual doses are small.

### PART 2: OPIUM POISONING

Opium is a somniferous (sleep-inducing) poison derived from the poppy plant (_Papaver somniferum_), containing phenanthrene alkaloids like morphine and codeine.

> 💡 **CLINICAL PEARL:** The classic diagnostic triad for opiate toxicity is **Coma, Pinpoint (miotic) pupils, and Respiratory depression**.

#### I. Clinical Manifestations

The clinical progression of acute opium poisoning occurs in three distinct stages:

**1. Stage of Excitement (Early Phase)**

- A brief period of euphoria driven by the limbic system.
- Characterized by an increased sense of well-being, freedom from anxiety, talkativeness, restlessness, flushing of the face, and occasionally hallucinations.

**2. Stage of Stupor**

- The excitement is rapidly replaced by headache, nausea, vomiting, and a heavy feeling in the limbs.
- The patient experiences a strong desire to sleep and passes into a stupor, but remains **arousable** to painful stimuli.
- Pupils contract, the face becomes cyanosed, and an intense itching sensation may be felt all over the skin.

**3. Stage of Coma (Narcosis)**

- The patient sinks into a deep, **unarousable coma** with completely flaccid muscles and abolished reflexes.
- **Ocular signs:** Pupils are strictly contracted to pinpoint size and do not react to light. _(Note: They may dilate terminally due to profound severe hypoxia)_.
- **Vital signs:** Profound respiratory depression occurs, with breathing becoming slow, stertorous, or Cheyne-Stokes in character (dropping to 3-4 breaths per minute). Bradycardia, hypotension, and hypothermia are marked.
- The skin is cold and bathed in sweat, and a characteristic opium odour may be present in the breath.
- Death ultimately results from complete respiratory paralysis.

#### II. Treatment & Management

**1. Resuscitation (Airway & Breathing)**

- Clear the airway of secretions or vomit.
- Immediate mechanical ventilatory support and endotracheal intubation are life-saving against respiratory paralysis.

**2. Specific Antidote Therapy (High-Yield)**

- **Naloxone** is a pure competitive opioid receptor antagonist and is the specific antidote of choice.
- _Dose:_ 0.4 mg to 2.0 mg IV, repeated every 2 to 3 minutes up to a maximum of 10 mg. It rapidly reverses coma and cardiopulmonary depression.
- If the poison is entirely unknown in a comatose patient, administer a "Coma Cocktail" consisting of IV Naloxone, 50% Dextrose, and Thiamine.

**3. Decontamination (Gastric Lavage)**

- Perform thorough gastric lavage using a **1:5000 solution of potassium permanganate (KMnO4)**, which oxidizes the alkaloid.
- 🚨 _Medicolegal / Clinical Pearl:_ Lavage is absolutely indicated _even if_ the opiate was administered parenterally (IV/IM), because morphine is actively re-secreted into the stomach and biliary tract after systemic absorption.

**4. Prevention of Reabsorption**

- Instill activated charcoal into the stomach following lavage to adsorb any remaining drug.
- Administer purgatives (e.g., 30 g of sodium sulphate) or perform enemas to clear the bowel and prevent further absorption.

**5. Contraindications & Supportive Care**

- **Avoid Atropine:** It is contraindicated as it can worsen respiratory centre paralysis.
- Maintain fluid and electrolyte balance via IV fluids. Provide prophylactic antibiotics to prevent aspiration pneumonia or pulmonary infections.

### DIAGNOSIS OF POISONING IN THE LIVING

There is no single symptom or definitive group of symptoms absolutely characteristic of poisoning. Diagnosis relies heavily on a high index of clinical suspicion and detailed history-taking.

**🥇 High-Yield Suspicion Criteria (Exam Pearl):** Poisoning should be strongly suspected if:

- Symptoms appear **suddenly in a previously healthy person**.
- Symptoms appear **immediately or shortly after** consuming food, drink, or medicine.
- Symptoms are **uniform in character** and rapidly increase in severity.
- **Multiple persons are affected simultaneously** with similar symptoms after sharing the same food/drink source.
- Poison is discovered in the food, vomit, or excreta.

**Classic Clinical Presentations (Toxidromes):**

- _Acute Poisoning:_ Sudden onset of abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, and collapse; sudden coma with pinpoint pupils (e.g., opiates, OP poisoning); sudden convulsions; delirium with dilated pupils; lower motor neuron paralysis; or jaundice and hepatocellular failure.
- _Chronic Poisoning:_ Malaise, cachexia, depression, and gradual deterioration; repeated GI attacks; symptoms exaggerate after specific food/medicine and **disappear when the patient is removed from their usual surroundings**.

---

### MATERIALS TO PRESERVE IN THE CASUALTY (LIVING PATIENT)

When treating a suspected poisoning case in the emergency department, it is the doctor's legal duty to collect and preserve evidence to assist the investigating agencies.

**Samples to Collect & Preserve:**

1. **Stomach Wash (Gastric Lavage):** The _entire quantity_ of the first wash should be preserved.
2. **Blood:** 10 ml of blood. Add **100 mg of sodium fluoride** (acts as both preservative and anticoagulant).
3. **Urine:** As much quantity as possible.
4. **Vomitus and Feces:** Collect any vomited matter or soiled materials.
5. **Contaminated Articles:** Leftover food, empty bottles, capsules, paper packets, or liquids found near the patient.
6. **Trace Evidence:** Any recent stains on bedclothes or garments.

---

### DIAGNOSIS OF POISONING IN THE DEAD

Diagnosis at autopsy depends on a combination of circumstantial, external, internal, and toxicological findings.

**1. External Postmortem Appearances:**

- **Odour (Smell):** Around the mouth and nose (e.g., Garlicky in OP or arsenic; Bitter almonds in cyanide; Kerosene in organophosphates).
- **Colour of Postmortem Lividity (Highly Tested):**
    - _Cherry-red:_ Carbon monoxide (CO).
    - _Bright red:_ Cyanide.
    - _Chocolate/Brownish-red:_ Nitrites, chlorates (Methaemoglobinaemia).
    - _Dark brown/Yellow:_ Phosphorus.
    - _Bluish-green:_ Hydrogen sulphide.
- **Skin/Mucosa Changes:** Stains from vomit/feces; corrosion burns (e.g., grey/black in sulphuric acid, yellow in nitric acid); jaundice (phosphorus, hepatic poisons).
- **Injection Marks:** Must be carefully searched for.

**2. Internal Postmortem Appearances:**

- **Cranial Cavity:** Should be opened _first_ to detect volatile odours (cyanide, alcohol, phenol) before they are masked by opening the body cavities.
- **Gastrointestinal Tract:** Congestion, hyperaemia (patchy, deep crimson), softening, ulceration, or perforation of the stomach.
    - _Note:_ A normal GI tract largely rules out corrosive acids/alkalis, heavy metals, and phenols.
- **Liver & Kidneys:** Fatty change, acute yellow atrophy, or centrilobular necrosis (liver); tubular necrosis (kidneys).
- **Heart:** Subendocardial petechial haemorrhages in the left ventricle (classic for arsenic, phosphorus, barium).

---

### VISCERA PRESERVATION DURING AUTOPSY

**A. Routine Viscera (The "Standard 5 Bottles"):** In all fatal cases of suspected poisoning, these routine samples must be preserved:

1. **Stomach & its contents** (If empty, preserve the wall).
2. **Upper part of small intestine** (about 30 cm length) and its contents.
3. **Liver (200 to 500 g) and Kidney (half of each)**.
4. **Blood (10 to 30 ml)** preserved with sodium/potassium fluoride.
5. **Urine (30 ml)**.

- _Control Sample:_ A sample of the preservative used (100 ml) must be sent to rule out pre-existing contamination.

**B. Special Viscera (Tailored to Specific Poisons):**

- **Brain (100 g):** Volatile poisons, alkaloids, opiates, organophosphates, CO, cyanide, strychnine, barbiturates.
- **Heart:** Cardiac poisons (digitalis, aconite, yellow oleander).
- **Spinal Cord:** Strychnine, gelsemium.
- **Lungs (One whole lung in a sealed nylon bag):** Gaseous/volatile poisons (HCN, CO, alcohol, chloroform, solvent abuse). _Plastic bags should be avoided as volatiles diffuse through them_.
- **Skin & Subcutaneous Tissue:** Excise a 10 cm radius of tissue along with the underlying fat/muscle around suspected injection marks (insulin, opiates) or snake bite sites.
- **Bile / Gallbladder:** Opiates (morphine), cocaine, methadone, barbiturates.
- **Bone (10 cm shaft of femur) / Hair with roots / Nails (entire):** Chronic heavy metal poisoning (arsenic, thallium, lead, antimony).
- **Adipose Tissue (Fat - 10 g):** Pesticides and insecticides.
- **Uterus & Appendages:** Suspected criminal abortion.

**🚨 MEDICOLEGAL PRESERVATIVE PEARLS:**

- **Standard Preservative:** Saturated Sodium Chloride (NaCl) solution is used for routine solid viscera.
- **Rectified Spirit** is used alternatively, **EXCEPT** in poisoning by: Alcohol, kerosene, chloroform, ether, chloral hydrate, phenol, phosphorus (diminishes phosphorescence), paraldehyde, and organic acids.
- **Formaldehyde should NEVER be used** for viscera preservation in toxicology, as it makes extraction of non-volatile organic compounds difficult.

### MEDICAL AND LEGAL RESPONSIBILITIES IN SUSPECTED POISONING

**I. MEDICAL RESPONSIBILITIES (PRIORITY)** The primary duty of any doctor in a poisoning case is therapeutic.

- **Life-Saving Measures:** Medical duties stand first in the order of priority; the doctor must make all necessary efforts to save the life of the patient.
- **Protocol:** Follow the general lines of management, including stabilization (A-B-C-D of resuscitation), evaluation, decontamination (gastric lavage, emesis), poison elimination, antidote administration, and nursing care.
- **Documentation:** Note the preliminary particulars of the patient, including age, sex, address, date, time, and identification marks. Maintain detailed written records of the symptoms, signs, and treatments, and keep them under safe custody.

---

**II. LEGAL RESPONSIBILITIES (HIGH-YIELD EXAM FOCUS)**

**1. Duty to Report to Police (Notification)**

- **Government Doctors:** A government medical officer is legally required to report **ALL** cases of suspected poisoning (accidental, suicidal, or homicidal) to the police.
- **Private Practitioners:**
    - **Homicidal:** Bound under **Section 39 Cr.P.C.** to immediately inform the police or Magistrate about any definite or suspected homicidal poisoning. Non-compliance is punishable under **Section 176 I.P.C.**.
    - **Suicidal / Accidental:** A private doctor is _not_ legally bound to inform the police if it is a case of accidental or suicidal poisoning, provided the patient survives.
- **Food Poisoning:** If poisoning results from a common eating place, the doctor is bound to notify the public health authorities.

**2. Management of Death in Poisoning**

- 🚫 **Absolute Rule:** If the patient dies, the doctor **MUST NOT** issue a death certificate.
- The doctor must immediately inform the police and refer the dead body for a medicolegal postmortem examination.

**3. Preservation of Evidence (Corpus Delicti)**

- Every effort must be made to collect and preserve physical evidence, including suspected food, vomitus, feces, stomach washings, empty bottles, and stained clothing.
- These must be properly sealed and dispatched to the Forensic Science Laboratory (FSL) for chemical analysis.
- 🚨 **Medicolegal Pitfall:** Deliberate omission to collect and preserve evidence (or intending to protect the accused) is an offence punishable under **Section 201 I.P.C.**.

**4. Dying Declaration**

- If the patient is conscious but in a serious condition (on the verge of death), the doctor must promptly arrange for a Magistrate to record a **Dying Declaration**.

**5. Police Interrogation & Professional Secrecy**

- When summoned or questioned by the investigating police officer regarding a poisoning case, the doctor is bound to divulge all information (**Section 175 Cr.P.C.**).
- **No Professional Secrecy:** The doctor cannot claim professional secrecy in these matters.
- Concealing information is punishable under **Sections 193 and 202 I.P.C.**, and giving false information is punishable under **Section 177 I.P.C.**.

**6. Special Protocol for Suspected Homicidal Poisoning** Before formally expressing an opinion of homicidal poisoning, the doctor must confirm the suspicion by taking strict ward precautions:

- Obtain a detailed history regarding the route, quantity, and time elapsed since ingestion.
- Consult a senior practitioner in strict confidence.
- Move the patient to a hospital; if refused, engage trusted nurses to personally administer all food and medicine.
- Allow no one to be alone with the patient, and outwit the suspect by randomly altering diet and meal times.
- Suspicion should be heightened if a specific person insists on preparing all the food or hurriedly throwing away leftovers.

### I. LEGAL DUTIES OF A DOCTOR IN A CASE OF SUSPECTED POISONING

The medicolegal responsibilities of a treating physician in poisoning cases are stringent and aimed at assisting law enforcement while ensuring patient care.

- **1. Duty to Report (Information to Police):**
    - _Government Doctors:_ Legally bound to report **all** cases of suspected poisoning (homicidal, suicidal, and accidental) to the police.
    - _Private Practitioners:_ Legally bound under **Sec. 39 Cr.P.C.** to inform the nearest magistrate or police officer of any **homicidal** poisoning. Failure to do so is punishable under **Sec. 176 I.P.C.**. They need not report suicidal or accidental cases unless the patient dies.
    - _Public Health Notification:_ In cases of food poisoning originating from a common eating place, the doctor must notify public health authorities. Failure to report amounts to infamous conduct.
- **2. Preservation of Evidence (Sec. 201 I.P.C.):**
    - The doctor must meticulously collect and preserve all trace evidence: vomitus, gastric lavage (stomach wash), feces, urine, suspected food/drink, and soiled clothing.
    - These must be sealed and dispatched to the Forensic Science Laboratory (FSL). Intentional omission or destruction of this evidence is a punishable offense under **Sec. 201 I.P.C.**.
- **3. Recording a Dying Declaration:**
    - If the patient is conscious but in a critical, deteriorating condition, the doctor must immediately arrange for a magistrate to record a dying declaration. If time does not permit, the doctor should record it in the presence of two witnesses after certifying _compos mentis_.
- **4. In the Event of Death:**
    - The doctor must **not** issue a death certificate.
    - The police must be informed immediately, and the body must be handed over for a medicolegal autopsy.
- **5. Medical Records & Confidentiality:**
    - Detailed, chronological written records of the clinical signs, treatment, and patient/attendant statements must be maintained.
    - When the police demand information regarding the poisoning under **Sec. 175 Cr.P.C.**, the doctor must divulge all facts; professional secrecy does not apply here. Withholding information is punishable under **Sec. 202 I.P.C.**, and giving false information under **Sec. 193 I.P.C.**.

### II. GENERAL PRINCIPLES OF TREATMENT OF ACUTE POISONING

Medical management always takes absolute priority over medicolegal formalities. The treatment follows the **ABCDE** paradigm (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Decontamination, Elimination).

**1. Stabilization & Resuscitation (ABCDs)**

- **Airway:** Clear the airway of secretions, vomit, or foreign bodies. Endotracheal intubation if the airway is compromised.
- **Breathing:** Administer supplemental oxygen. Assisted ventilation is indicated if hypoxia ($PaO_2 < 70$ mmHg) or $CO_2$ retention ($PaCO_2 > 45$ mmHg) occurs.
- **Circulation:** Intravenous fluid administration to maintain intravascular volume and treat shock.
- **CNS Depression:** For comatose patients where the poison is unknown, administer the **"Coma Cocktail"** intravenously: $100$ ml of $50%$ Dextrose (for hypoglycemia), $100$ mg Thiamine (for Wernicke's encephalopathy prevention), and $2$ mg Naloxone (for opioid overdose).

**2. Decontamination (Removal of Unabsorbed Poison)**

- **Inhaled Poisons:** Immediately remove the patient to fresh air; administer oxygen and artificial respiration.
- **Contact Poisons (Skin/Eyes):** Remove contaminated clothes. Wash exposed skin copiously with soap and water for at least $15$ to $30$ minutes. Irrigate eyes with normal saline.
- **Ingested Poisons (GI Decontamination):**
    - _Emesis:_ Indicated for alert patients within 2 hours of ingestion. **Syrup of Ipecac** ($30$ ml for adults, $15$ ml for children) stimulates the vomiting center. _Absolute Contraindications:_ Corrosives, petroleum distillates (hydrocarbons), convulsants, absent gag reflex, or coma.
    - _Gastric Lavage (Stomach Wash):_ Best if done within $2$ to $4$ hours of ingestion (up to 12-18 hrs for drugs slowing GI motility like salicylates/tricyclics). Uses Ewald’s or Boa’s tube. _Contraindications:_ Corrosive acids/alkalis (except carbolic acid) due to perforation risk, and comatose patients unless the airway is secured with a cuffed endotracheal tube.
    - _Activated Charcoal:_ A fine black powder that acts as a physical antidote by adsorbing toxins. Dose: **$1$ g/kg body weight** (usually $50-100$ g in adults) mixed in water as a slurry. Not effective for corrosives, heavy metals, cyanide, or alcohols.
    - _Whole Bowel Irrigation:_ Uses polyethylene glycol to physically expel poisons. Indicated for "body packers" (drug smugglers) and sustained-release or heavy metal overdoses (iron, lithium).

**3. Administration of Antidotes** Antidotes neutralize or counteract specific poisons.

- _Physical/Mechanical:_ Activated charcoal or demulcents (milk, egg-white, mineral oil) which form a protective mucosal coating.
- _Chemical:_ Form harmless/insoluble compounds. E.g., Potassium permanganate ($1:5000$) oxidizes alkaloids (opium, strychnine).
- _Physiological/Pharmacological:_ Produce opposite systemic effects or block receptors.
    - _Naloxone_ (Opioid receptor antagonist).
    - _Atropine_ (Muscarinic receptor blocker for Organophosphates).
    - _Flumazenil_ (Benzodiazepine antagonist).
- _Chelating Agents (For Heavy Metals):_ Form stable, water-soluble complexes excreted in urine. Examples: **BAL (Dimercaprol)** for Arsenic/Mercury/Lead; **EDTA** for Lead; **Desferrioxamine** for Iron; **Penicillamine/DMSA**.

**4. Elimination of Absorbed Poison**

- _Forced Diuresis:_ IV fluids + diuretics. **Alkaline diuresis** (adding sodium bicarbonate to achieve urine pH 7.5-9) specifically promotes the excretion of weak acids like **salicylates and phenobarbitone**.
- _Extracorporeal Techniques:_
    - **Hemodialysis:** Highly effective for low molecular weight, water-soluble, weakly protein-bound toxins (e.g., methanol, ethylene glycol, lithium, salicylates).
    - **Hemoperfusion:** Blood passes over a charcoal/resin cartridge. Highly effective for lipid-soluble and highly protein-bound drugs (e.g., short-acting barbiturates, theophylline, phenytoin).

**5. Symptomatic & Psychiatric Care**

- Treat complications such as seizures (using Diazepam/Phenobarbital), arrhythmias, pulmonary edema, and shock.
- Ensure psychiatric evaluation and support for all suicidal patients before discharge.

### II. PROCEDURE OF STOMACH WASH (GASTRIC LAVAGE)

**OVERVIEW** Gastric lavage is a decontamination procedure used to remove unabsorbed poison from the stomach. It is most effective if performed within **2 hours** of ingestion, though it may be delayed up to 12 to 18 hours for drugs like salicylates, phenothiazines, and tricyclic antidepressants.

**1. CONTRAINDICATIONS (🚨 Danger Zones):**

- **Absolute:** Corrosive acid or alkali poisoning (due to the risk of perforation), EXCEPT carbolic acid. Other absolute contraindications include ingestion of petroleum distillates/hydrocarbons, sharp objects, and convulsant poisons (unless convulsions are medically controlled).
- **Relative:** Comatose patients. _Clinical Pearl:_ In coma, lavage can only be performed after sealing the airway with a cuffed endotracheal tube to prevent aspiration.

**2. EQUIPMENT:**

- **Adults:** Ewald’s or Boa’s stomach tube (a soft, non-collapsible rubber tube of 1 cm diameter and 1.5 meters length, with lateral openings at the rounded tip).
- **Children/Infants:** Ryle's tube or a number 10 to 12 French catheter.

**3. PATIENT POSITIONING (Critical Step):**

- The patient should be placed in the **left lateral decubitus position** or prone, with the head hanging over the edge of the bed and face down supported by an assistant.
- _Anatomical rationale:_ This ensures the mouth is at a lower level than the larynx, so any fluid leaking from the tube trickles out of the mouth rather than being aspirated into the trachea.

**4. PROCEDURE STEPS:**

1. **Preparation:** Remove any dentures and insert a wooden mouth gag to prevent the patient from biting the tube.
2. **Insertion:** Lubricate the tip of the tube (with olive oil, liquid paraffin, or glycerine). Gently pass it through the pharynx and esophagus into the stomach without using force, up to the **50 cm mark** (or a distance equal to the measurement from the bridge of the nose to the tip of the xiphoid process).
3. **Confirmation of Placement (🥇 High-Yield):**
    - Confirm absence of coughing and breath sounds.
    - _Water Test:_ Submerge the free end of the tube just below a water surface. Air from the stomach will bubble out completely in 2 to 3 expirations. If it is mistakenly in the lungs, it will cause continuous bubbling with every expiration.
4. **The Wash (Lavage):**
    - Hold the funnel high above the patient's head and pour in about **250 ml (one-fourth litre)** of warm water or specific neutralizing solution (e.g., 1:5000 KMnO4).
5. **Siphoning:**
    - Before the funnel completely empties, compress the rubber tubing, lower it below the level of the stomach, and release the pressure. The fluid will drain out via **siphon action**.
6. **Repetition & Completion:**
    - Repeat this process using about half a litre of fluid per cycle until the returning fluid is completely **clear, odorless, and free of particulate matter**.
    - Before removing the tube, a small quantity of fluid containing an antidote or activated charcoal (1 gm/kg) with a cathartic is usually left in the stomach to neutralize/adsorb any residual poison.
7. General lines of treatment of a poisoned patient.

### GENERAL LINES OF TREATMENT OF A POISONED PATIENT

**OVERVIEW & PRINCIPLES OF MANAGEMENT** The successful management of acute poisoning relies on a systematic approach. The general lines of treatment are categorized into five major steps: Stabilization, Decontamination, Antidote Administration, Enhanced Elimination, and Symptomatic/Psychiatric Care.

---

#### I. STABILIZATION & RESUSCITATION (The "ABCDs")

Immediate resuscitative measures must be prioritized to stabilize vital functions, especially in comatose patients.

- **A - Airway:** Open and clear the airway of secretions, vomit, or foreign bodies. Secure the airway with endotracheal intubation if necessary.
- **B - Breathing:** Administer supplemental oxygen via a ventimask or endotracheal tube if the patient is hypoxic.
- **C - Circulation:** Maintain cardiovascular stability using intravenous fluid administration.
- **D - Depression of CNS:** Place an unconscious patient in the recovery position (lateral decubitus) to prevent the tongue from blocking the throat and to avoid aspiration of vomit.

> 💡 **EXAM PEARL - The "Coma Cocktail":** If a patient presents in an unexplained coma and the nature of the poison is unknown, administer the "Coma Cocktail" intravenously: **100 ml of 50% Dextrose, 100 mg Thiamine, and 2 mg Naloxone**.

---

#### II. DECONTAMINATION (Removal of Unabsorbed Poison)

The goal is to limit further systemic absorption based on the route of exposure.

- **Inhaled Poisons:** Immediately remove the patient to fresh air, establish a patent airway, and administer artificial respiration and 100% oxygen.
- **Injected Poisons (e.g., snake bites):** Apply a tight ligature above the wound (loosened for 1 minute every 10 minutes to prevent gangrene), excise the wound, and apply suction.
- **Contact Poisons:** Remove contaminated clothing immediately. Wash the exposed skin with soap and water for at least 15 to 30 minutes. If the eyes are involved, irrigate them with normal saline for at least 15 minutes.
- **Ingested Poisons:**
    - **Emesis:** **Syrup of Ipecac** (30 ml for adults, 15 ml for children) is the drug of choice to induce vomiting.
        - _🚨 Absolute Contraindications:_ Corrosive poisons, convulsant poisons, coma, and impaired gag reflex.
    - **Gastric Lavage (Stomach Wash):** Most effective when done within 2 hours of ingestion (up to 6–8 hours in some cases). Uses an Ewald's or Boas tube. The patient should be positioned prone or on the left side with the head dependent to prevent aspiration.
        - _🚨 Absolute Contraindications:_ Corrosive poisoning (except carbolic acid) due to perforation risk.
        - _Relative Contraindications:_ Coma or convulsions (unless the airway is strictly protected with a cuffed endotracheal tube).

---

#### III. ADMINISTRATION OF ANTIDOTES

Antidotes counteract poisons via mechanical, chemical, or physiological mechanisms.

- **1. Mechanical / Physical Antidotes:** Prevent absorption physically.
    - **Activated Charcoal:** A fine, black, tasteless powder with an extremely large surface area that adsorbs poisons. Given as an aqueous slurry (Dose: 1 g/kg body weight; typically 60-100 g in adults).
    - **Demulcents:** Milk, egg-white, or starch water form a protective coating on the gastric mucous membrane.
- **2. Chemical Antidotes:** React directly with the poison to form harmless or insoluble compounds.
    - _Potassium Permanganate (1:5000):_ Oxidizes alkaloids (opium, strychnine).
    - _Tannic Acid:_ Precipitates alkaloids and heavy metals.
- **3. Pharmacological / Physiological Antidotes:** Act on receptors to produce effects opposite to the poison (e.g., Naloxone for opiates; Atropine for organophosphates).
- **4. Chelating Agents (For Heavy Metals):** Bind to heavy metals to form stable, water-soluble, non-toxic complexes that are excreted in urine.
    - _BAL (Dimercaprol):_ Arsenic, mercury, lead.
    - _CaNa2 EDTA:_ Lead poisoning.
    - _Penicillamine:_ Copper, lead, mercury.
    - _Desferrioxamine:_ Acute iron poisoning.

---

#### IV. ENHANCED ELIMINATION PROCEDURES

Utilized when the poison is already absorbed into the systemic circulation and causing severe toxicity.

- **Renal Excretion (Forced Diuresis):** Forced **alkaline diuresis** (using sodium bicarbonate) increases the urinary pH to 7.5–9.0, trapping weak acids like salicylates and phenobarbital in the urine and promoting their rapid excretion.
- **Purging / Catharsis:** Sodium sulfate or sorbitol is used to hasten the passage of unabsorbed poison through the feces.
- **Whole-Bowel Irrigation:** Administration of polyethylene glycol via a nasogastric tube until the rectal effluent is clear. Highly useful for sustained-release drugs, iron, lithium, and illicit drug "body-packers".
- **Extracorporeal Techniques:** **Haemodialysis, Peritoneal dialysis, and Haemoperfusion** (passing blood over activated charcoal or resins) are used for severe poisoning with agents like alcohols, barbiturates, lithium, and salicylates, particularly when complicated by acute renal failure.

---

#### V. SYMPTOMATIC & PSYCHIATRIC CARE

- **Symptomatic:** Maintain fluid and electrolyte balance, administer analgesics for pain, control seizures, and treat shock and pulmonary edema on general medical lines.
- **Psychiatric Care:** Once stabilized, any patient presenting with intentional self-harm or suicidal tendencies must receive prompt psychological assessment, psychotherapy, and support.
1. Classification of poisons.
### CLASSIFICATION OF POISONS

Poisons are primarily classified in forensic toxicology based on two major parameters: **(A) The chief clinical symptoms they produce** and **(B) Their morbid anatomic (postmortem) manifestations**.

---

#### A. CLINICAL CLASSIFICATION (Based on Chief Symptoms)

This is the most widely accepted medicolegal classification.

**I. CORROSIVES** These substances fix, destroy, and erode the surface they come into contact with, producing severe coagulation or liquefaction necrosis.

- **Strong Acids:**
    - _Inorganic (Mineral):_ Sulphuric acid (oil of vitriol), Nitric acid (aqua fortis), Hydrochloric acid.
    - _Organic:_ Carbolic acid (Phenol), Oxalic acid, Acetic acid, Salicylic acid.
- **Strong Alkalis:** Sodium hydroxide, Potassium hydroxide, Ammonium hydroxide.
- **Metallic Salts:** Zinc chloride, Ferric chloride, Copper sulphate, Silver nitrate, Potassium cyanide, Chromates, and Bichromates.

**II. IRRITANTS** These substances cause intense congestion and inflammation of the mucous membranes.

- **Inorganic:**
    - _Non-metallic:_ Phosphorus, Chlorine, Bromine, Iodine, Carbon tetrachloride.
    - _Metallic (Heavy Metals):_ Arsenic, Antimony, Lead, Copper, Mercury, Silver, Zinc, Thallium, Iron.
    - _Mechanical:_ Powdered glass, Diamond dust, Hair.
- **Organic:**
    - _Vegetable:_ _Abrus precatorius_, Castor (_Ricinus communis_), Croton, _Calotropis_, Aloes, _Semecarpus anacardium_, _Capsicum_.
    - _Animal:_ Snake venom, Insect venom, Scorpion sting, Cantharides (Spanish fly), Ptomaines.

**III. SYSTEMIC POISONS** These poisons act primarily on specific organ systems after absorption.

- **1. Nervous (Neurotoxic):**
    - _Cerebral Depressants (Inebriants & Sedatives):_ Alcohols, General anaesthetics, Opioid analgesics, Barbiturates, Hypnotics.
    - _Cerebral Stimulants:_ Amphetamines, Caffeine, Methylphenidate.
    - _Cerebral Deliriants:_ _Datura_, _Belladonna_, _Hyoscyamus_, Cannabis, Cocaine.
    - _Psychotropics:_ Antidepressants, Neuroleptics, Hallucinogens (e.g., LSD, phencyclidine).
    - _Spinal:_ Strychnine (Stimulant), Gelsemium (Depressant).
    - _Peripheral:_ Curare, Conium (Hemlock).
- **2. Cardiovascular:** Aconite, Digitalis, Oleanders, Tobacco/Nicotine, Quinine, Cyanide.
- **3. Asphyxiants:** Carbon monoxide (CO), Carbon dioxide (CO2), Hydrogen sulphide, Cyanide gas.

**IV. MISCELLANEOUS & AGRICULTURAL POISONS**

- **Food Poisons:** Botulism, bacterial food poisoning.
- **Agrochemicals:** Organophosphates, Carbamates, Chlorinated hydrocarbons (e.g., Endrin, DDT), Aluminium phosphide.

---

#### B. PATHOLOGICAL CLASSIFICATION (Based on Morbid Anatomy)

This classification is critical for the autopsy pathologist, grouped by the presence or absence of structural tissue changes.

| Pathological Finding            | Mechanism & Examples                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| :------------------------------ | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1. No Morphologic Changes**   | No direct cellular destruction; death is due to shock, terminal anoxia, or functional impairment. Seen in acute CNS depressants (alcohols, barbiturates), chemical asphyxiants (CO, HCN), organophosphates, and most alkaloids.            |
| **2. Systemic Lesions ONLY**    | No injury at the portal of entry (e.g., normal stomach), but severe systemic damage occurs. Classic for acute haemolytic poisons like Arsine gas and Nitrobenzene.                                                                         |
| **3. Local Portal Injury ONLY** | Severe destruction at the contact site (mouth/stomach/lungs) with no significant remote systemic tissue injuries. Seen with Corrosives, Chlorine, and Sulphur dioxide. Death is often due to acute vasomotor collapse or pulmonary oedema. |
| **4. Local & Systemic Lesions** | Simultaneous damage at the portal of entry and remote organ systems. Classic hallmark of **Heavy Metals** (e.g., Arsenic, Mercury, Lead).                                                                                                  |

### FOUR HOUSEHOLD POISONS

Household poisons include common domestic products, cleaning agents, and pesticides that are frequently involved in accidental or suicidal poisonings. Four high-yield examples of household poisons include:

1. **Naphthalene / Camphor:** Found in mothballs.
2. **Sodium Hydroxide (Caustic Soda):** Found in drain cleaners and oven cleaners.
3. **Phenol / Calcium Hypochlorite (Bleaching Powder):** Found in sanitary disinfectants and toilet cleaners.
4. **Aluminium Phosphide / Zinc Phosphide:** Found in rat poisons and rodenticides. _(Other common examples: Acetone in nail polish removers, Carbon tetrachloride in fire extinguishers, and Turpentine in furniture polish)._

---

### VISCERA PRESERVATION IN SUSPECTED POISONING

In all fatal cases of suspected poisoning, specific viscera must be routinely collected, preserved, sealed, and dispatched to the Forensic Science Laboratory (FSL) for chemical analysis.

**I. ROUTINE VISCERA (Collected in 5 separate bottles):**

- **Bottle 1: Stomach and its contents.**
    - _Amount:_ Entire stomach and contents; if empty, the wall must be preserved.
    - 🥇 _Clinical Inference:_ Helps identify recently ingested substances and estimates the quantity ingested.
- **Bottle 2: Upper part of the small intestine and its contents.**
    - _Amount:_ Upper 30 cm of the duodenum.
    - 🥇 _Clinical Inference:_ Proves that the poison has moved past the pylorus due to active peristalsis (i.e., the person was alive when it was administered).
- **Bottle 3: Liver and Kidneys (Solid Viscera).**
    - _Amount:_ 200 to 500 grams of liver and one-half of each kidney.
    - 🥇 _Clinical Inference:_ Liver is the major detoxifying organ and concentrates many poisons; kidneys are the major organs of excretion. Their preservation proves the poison was absorbed into the system.
- **Bottle 4: Blood.**
    - _Amount:_ 30 to 100 ml collected from a peripheral vessel (e.g., femoral vein).
    - _Preservative:_ Sodium fluoride (10 mg/ml) to prevent glycolysis and bacterial growth, along with potassium oxalate as an anticoagulant.
    - 🥇 _Clinical Inference:_ Gives the best indication of the quantity of poison exerting an effect on the person at the exact time of death.
- **Bottle 5: Urine.**
    - _Amount:_ Minimum 30 ml.
    - 🥇 _Clinical Inference:_ Concentrates the drug; presence of poison in the urine is definitive proof of systemic absorption and excretion.

**II. ADDITIONAL VISCERA FOR SPECIFIC HOUSEHOLD POISONS:** Depending on the specific household poison suspected, the autopsy surgeon must preserve additional tissues:

- **Brain (100 g):** Preserved for volatile organic poisons (e.g., carbon tetrachloride, acetone, alcohols) and barbiturates.
- **Lungs (One entire lung):** Preserved in a heat-sealed nylon bag (not plastic) for inhaled gaseous or volatile poisons (e.g., solvent abuse, sniffing glues, carbon monoxide).
- **Adipose (Fat) Tissue (10 g):** Taken from the abdominal wall or perinephric region specifically for lipid-soluble pesticides and insecticides (e.g., organochlorines like DDT).
- **Skin/Subcutaneous Tissue:** Excised (with a 10 cm radius) in cases of corrosive poison spills or injected drugs.

> ⚠️ **MEDICOLEGAL PEARL:**
> 
> - Viscera should be preserved in **saturated sodium chloride solution** or **rectified spirit**.
> - _Exceptions:_ Rectified spirit is **strictly contraindicated** if the suspected poison is alcohol, kerosene, phenol, phosphorus, or volatile poisons like formaldehyde/acetic acid. Formaldehyde must **never** be used as it makes the extraction of non-volatile organic compounds very difficult. A sample of the exact preservative used (100 ml) must always be sent as a control to rule out contamination.

### EXCRETION OF POISONS AND ENHANCED ELIMINATION

**I. ROUTES OF EXCRETION OF POISONS** The body eliminates poisons through various physiological routes, depending on the chemical nature and absorption status of the toxin.

- **Renal (Major Route):** The absorbed portion of most poisons is primarily excreted by the kidneys into the urine.
- **Gastrointestinal (Unabsorbed):** Unabsorbed portions of ingested poisons are eliminated through vomit and faeces.
- **Other Secretory Routes:** Poisons can also be excreted via bile, breast milk, saliva, sweat (skin), and mucous or serous secretions.
- **Pulmonary:** Volatile poisons and gases (e.g., alcohol, carbon monoxide, hydrogen cyanide, paraldehyde) are eliminated through the lungs in the breath.

---

**II. INDICATIONS FOR ENHANCED ELIMINATION** Procedures to actively enhance the excretion of poisons are indicated in specific clinical scenarios:

1. Severe poisoning with progressive clinical deterioration despite optimal supportive care.
2. High risk of serious morbidity or mortality.
3. Impairment of the patient's normal routes of excretion (e.g., renal or hepatic failure).
4. Poisons known to produce delayed but serious toxic effects.

---

**III. HIGH-YIELD METHODS TO ENHANCE EXCRETION**

**1. Renal Excretion & Forced Diuresis**

- **Mechanism:** Administering large amounts of oral fluids (water, tea) or intravenous fluids to increase urine output.
- 🥇 **Forced Alkaline Diuresis (Exam Pearl):** Highly effective for excreting drugs that are **weak acids** (e.g., salicylates, phenobarbitone, methotrexate). It is achieved by administering IV Sodium Bicarbonate to raise the urinary pH to 7.5–9.0, which ionizes the drug in the renal tubules, preventing its reabsorption.
- _⚠️ Clinical Warning:_ Forced diuresis carries a risk of precipitating pulmonary or cerebral oedema. Urinary acidification is generally not recommended.

**2. Purging / Catharsis**

- **Mechanism:** Accelerates gastrointestinal transit to eliminate unabsorbed poison in the stool.
- **Agents Used:** Sodium sulphate (30 g) with large amounts of water, or Sorbitol (70% solution). Liquid petroleum is useful for removing fat-soluble agents.
- _⚠️ Clinical Warning:_ Avoid Magnesium sulphate in patients with renal failure, as absorbed magnesium can cause CNS depression.

**3. Whole-Bowel Irrigation (WBI)**

- **Mechanism:** Continuous administration of a non-absorbable, osmotically active polyethylene glycol-electrolyte lavage solution via a nasogastric tube (2 Litres/hour for adults) until the rectal effluent is clear.
- 🥇 **Clinical Indications:** Drug of choice for heavy metals (Iron, Lithium), sustained-release drug preparations, and **"Body Packers"** (drug smugglers who swallow wrapped packets of cocaine or heroin).

**4. Haemodialysis**

- **Mechanism:** Extracorporeal removal of toxins across a semi-permeable membrane.
- **Target Toxins:** Highly efficacious for toxins with low molecular weight, low protein binding, and high water solubility.
- _Classic Indications:_ Methanol, ethylene glycol, ethanol, lithium, salicylates, phenobarbital, and severe heavy metal poisoning.

**5. Haemoperfusion**

- **Mechanism:** Patient's blood is passed extracorporeally over an adsorbent material (e.g., activated charcoal or acrylic hydrogel resins).
- 🥇 **Clinical Pearl:** It is superior to haemodialysis for highly protein-bound, lipid-soluble substances with a large volume of distribution. Highly effective for barbiturates, paraquat, digitalis, carbamazepine, theophylline, and paracetamol.

**6. Peritoneal Dialysis**

- **Utility:** Can remove alcohols, chloral hydrate, lithium, and salicylates, but it is only **10% to 25% as effective as haemodialysis**.

**7. Diaphoretics**

- **Mechanism:** Using heat application, hot beverages, or drugs (e.g., pilocarpine nitrate 5 mg SQ) to induce profuse sweating.
- _Note:_ The clinical efficacy of this method in significantly speeding up toxin excretion is doubtful.
1. Diagnosis of poisoning in living.
### DIAGNOSIS OF POISONING IN THE LIVING

There is no single symptom or definitive group of symptoms that are absolutely characteristic of poisoning. Therefore, diagnosis relies heavily on a high index of clinical suspicion, pattern recognition (toxidromes), and a detailed clinical history.

---

**I. 🥇 HIGH-YIELD CRITERIA FOR SUSPECTING POISONING** Poisoning should be strongly suspected in a living patient if the following classic features are present:

1. **Sudden Onset:** Symptoms appear suddenly in a previously healthy person.
2. **Temporal Association:** Symptoms appear immediately or within a short period after consuming food, drink, or medicine.
3. **Symptom Progression:** The symptoms are uniform in character and rapidly increase in severity.
4. **Mass Casualties:** Several persons eating or drinking from the same source suffer from similar symptoms at or about the same time.
5. **Physical Evidence:** The actual discovery of poison in the food taken, in the vomit, or in the excreta.

---

**II. CLINICAL PRESENTATIONS: ACUTE VS. CHRONIC**

**A. Acute Poisoning (Classic Presentations)** Acute toxicity often presents with sudden onset of specific symptom clusters:

- **Gastrointestinal:** Sudden abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, and collapse.
- **Neurological:** Sudden coma with constricted (pinpoint) pupils, sudden convulsions, or delirium with dilated pupils. Lower motor neurone type paralysis may also occur.
- **Hepatorenal:** Jaundice and hepatocellular failure, or oliguria with proteinuria and haematuria.
- **Respiratory/Circulatory:** Persistent cyanosis.
- **Occupational:** Rapid onset of GI or neurological illness in persons with known chemical exposure.

**B. Chronic Poisoning** Chronic poisoning can be insidious. Key diagnostic clues include:

- Symptoms are consistently exaggerated after the administration of suspected food, fluid, or medicine.
- General malaise, cachexia, depression, and gradual deterioration.
- Repeated unexplained attacks of diarrhoea or vomiting.
- 🚨 **Diagnostic Pearl:** Symptoms **disappear** when the patient is removed from their usual surroundings.
- Traces of poison detected in the urine, stool, or vomit.

---

**III. TOXIDROMES (CLINICAL RECOGNITION)** A toxidrome is a constellation of clinical findings resulting from a specific poison, serving as a crucial diagnostic tool.

|Toxidrome|Implicated Poisons|Classic Clinical Findings|
|:--|:--|:--|
|**Anticholinergic**|Datura, atropine, antihistamines, cyclic antidepressants.|Hyperthermia, dry skin, dilated pupils (mydriasis), flushed skin, delirium, tachycardia, urinary retention.|
|**Cholinergic**|Organophosphates (OPC), carbamates, some mushrooms.|**SLUDGE:** Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defecation, GI distress, Emesis. Also miosis, sweating, fasciculations, pulmonary oedema.|
|**Opioid**|Morphine, heroin, codeine, methadone, pethidine.|**Classic Triad:** Coma, pinpoint pupils (miosis), respiratory depression. Also hypotension, bradycardia, hypothermia.|
|**Sympathomimetic**|Cocaine, amphetamines, caffeine.|Paranoia, tachycardia, hypertension, hyperpyrexia, sweating, mydriasis, seizures, arrhythmias.|
|**Sedative / Hypnotic**|Barbiturates, benzodiazepines, ethanol.|CNS depression, coma, hyporeflexia, hypotension, bradycardia, hypothermia.|

---

**IV. MEDICOLEGAL PRESERVATION OF EVIDENCE** In the casualty/emergency department, it is the doctor's duty to collect and preserve specific materials to confirm the diagnosis analytically:

1. **Stomach Wash (Gastric Lavage):** The _entire quantity_ of the first wash should be preserved.
2. **Blood:** Collect 10 ml of blood. Add **100 mg of sodium fluoride** (which acts as both a preservative and an anticoagulant).
3. **Urine:** Collect as much quantity as possible.
4. Duties of a doctor in a suspected case of poisoning.
### DUTIES OF A DOCTOR IN A SUSPECTED CASE OF POISONING

When managing a case of suspected poisoning, a medical practitioner has two paramount responsibilities: **Medical (Resuscitative)** and **Legal (Medicolegal)**. 

> 💡 **CLINICAL PEARL:** Medical duties stand first in the order of priority. Saving the life of the patient supersedes all legal formalities.

---

#### I. MEDICAL DUTIES (Patient Care)
The primary objective is the rapid stabilization and treatment of the patient.
*   **Resuscitation (ABCD):** Establish Airway, Breathing, Circulation, and correct CNS Depression.
*   **Decontamination:** Remove unabsorbed poison via gastric lavage, emesis, activated charcoal, or whole bowel irrigation.
*   **Antidotes & Elimination:** Administer specific antidotes (e.g., Naloxone, Atropine, Chelating agents) and enhance elimination of absorbed poison (e.g., forced diuresis, haemodialysis).
*   **Supportive Care:** Treat symptoms on general lines and provide appropriate nursing care.
*   **Psychiatric Evaluation:** Arrange for psychiatric counseling and follow-up in all suicidal cases once the patient is stabilized.

---

#### II. LEGAL DUTIES (High-Yield for Exams)
The doctor must assist investigating agencies in determining the manner of death or injury while adhering strictly to statutory laws.

**1. Documentation & Record Keeping**
*   Note preliminary particulars: Age, sex, address, date, time of arrival, and at least two identification marks.
*   Maintain detailed, chronological written records of the patient's condition, symptoms, and treatment given. Keep these records under safe custody.

**2. Police Intimation (Notification)**
*   **Government Doctors:** Are legally bound to report **ALL** cases of suspected poisoning (accidental, suicidal, or homicidal) to the police.
*   **Private Practitioners:**
    *   *Homicidal:* Legally bound under **Sec. 39 Cr.P.C.** to report definite or suspected homicidal poisoning to the police or Magistrate. Failure to report is punishable under **Sec. 176 I.P.C.**.
    *   *Suicidal/Accidental:* Not legally bound to inform the police if the patient survives. However, if the patient dies, they must immediately inform the police.

**3. Preservation of Evidence (Corpus Delicti)**
*   The doctor must meticulously collect and preserve suspected articles of food, vomitus, feces, stomach washings, empty bottles, packets, and stained clothing.
*   These must be sealed, labeled, and dispatched to the Forensic Science Laboratory (FSL).
*   🚨 *Medicolegal Pitfall:* Deliberate omission to collect and preserve evidence to protect the accused is punishable under **Sec. 201 I.P.C.**.

**4. Dying Declaration**
*   If the patient is conscious but their condition is deteriorating (on the verge of death), the doctor must immediately arrange for a Magistrate to record a dying declaration.

**5. In the Event of Death**
*   🚫 **Absolute Rule:** The doctor must **NEVER** issue a death certificate in a suspected poisoning case.
*   The police must be informed immediately, and the body must be handed over for a medicolegal autopsy.

**6. Police Interrogation & Professional Secrecy**
*   Under **Sec. 175 Cr.P.C.**, when summoned by an investigating police officer, the doctor must divulge all information regarding the poisoning.
*   **No Professional Secrecy:** The doctor cannot claim professional secrecy in these matters. Concealing information is punishable under **Sec. 202 and 193 I.P.C.**, and giving false information under **Sec. 177 I.P.C.**.

**7. Special Protocol for Suspected Homicidal Poisoning**
Before formally expressing an opinion of homicidal poisoning, the doctor must confirm the suspicion by taking strict ward precautions:
*   Obtain a detailed history regarding the route, quantity, and time elapsed since ingestion.
*   Consult a senior colleague in strict confidence.
*   Move the patient to a hospital. Employ trusted nurses to personally administer all food and medicine.
*   Outwit the suspect by randomly altering diet and meal times, and allow no one to be alone with the patient.

**8. Food Poisoning Outbreaks**
*   If poisoning originates from a common eating place or commercial food source, the doctor is bound to notify public health authorities; failure to do so amounts to infamous conduct.
1. Legal duties of doctor in poisoning cases.
### LEGAL DUTIES OF A DOCTOR IN A CASE OF SUSPECTED POISONING

While the primary and foremost duty of a doctor in any poisoning case is medical (saving the life of the patient via resuscitation, decontamination, and antidotes), the physician also bears strict medicolegal responsibilities to assist law enforcement.

**🥇 HIGH-YIELD LEGAL DUTIES (EXAM FOCUS)**

**1. Duty to Report (Information to Police)**

- **Government Doctors:** Legally bound to report **ALL** cases of suspected poisoning (accidental, suicidal, or homicidal) treated in the hospital to the police without exception.
- **Private Practitioners:**
    - _Homicidal Poisoning:_ Bound under **Sec. 39 Cr.P.C.** to immediately inform the nearest Magistrate or police officer about any definite or suspected homicidal poisoning. Failure to do so makes the doctor liable for punishment under **Sec. 176 I.P.C.**.
    - _Suicidal/Accidental Poisoning:_ A private doctor is _not_ legally bound to inform the police if they are certain the case is accidental or suicidal, provided the patient survives.

**2. Preservation of Evidence (Corpus Delicti)**

- The doctor must meticulously collect and preserve all physical trace evidence: vomitus, feces, stomach washings (gastric lavage), contaminated food, empty bottles, packets, and stained bedclothes or clothing.
- These must be properly sealed, labeled, kept in safe custody, and dispatched to the Forensic Science Laboratory (FSL) for chemical analysis.
- 🚨 _Medicolegal Pitfall:_ Deliberate omission to collect and preserve evidence, if done with the intention of screening or protecting the offender, is a punishable offence under **Sec. 201 I.P.C.**.

**3. In the Event of Death**

- 🚫 **Absolute Rule:** If the patient dies, the doctor **MUST NOT** issue a death certificate.
- The doctor must immediately inform the police and refer the dead body for a medicolegal postmortem examination.

**4. Recording a Dying Declaration**

- If the patient is conscious but in a critical, deteriorating condition (on the verge of death), the doctor must promptly arrange for a Magistrate to record a **Dying Declaration**.

**5. Police Interrogation & Professional Secrecy**

- When summoned or questioned by the investigating police officer regarding a poisoning case, the doctor is bound to divulge all information under **Sec. 175 Cr.P.C.**.
- **No Professional Secrecy:** The doctor cannot claim professional secrecy or confidentiality in these matters.
- _Penalties:_ Intentional concealment of information is punishable under **Sec. 202 and 193 I.P.C.**, while giving false information attracts punishment under **Sec. 177 I.P.C.**.

**6. Medical Documentation**

- Note preliminary particulars meticulously: age, sex, address, date, time, and precise identification marks.
- Maintain a detailed, chronological written record of the number of visits, clinical signs, symptoms, and treatments administered, which must be kept under safe custody.

**7. Public Health Notification**

- If a case of food poisoning originates from a common or public eating place, the doctor is bound to notify the public health authorities. Failure to report this amounts to infamous conduct.

---

**⚠️ SPECIAL PROTOCOL FOR SUSPECTED HOMICIDAL POISONING** Before formally expressing an opinion of homicidal poisoning, the doctor must confirm the suspicion by taking strict ward precautions:

- Obtain a detailed history regarding the route of exposure, quantity consumed, and time elapsed.
- Consult a senior medical practitioner in strict confidence and keep them informed.
- Move the patient to a hospital; if the patient refuses, engage trusted nurses to personally administer all food and medicine.
- Allow no one to be alone with the patient.
- _Diagnostic Clues:_ Suspicion should be heightened if a specific person insists on preparing all the food, serving the patient personally, or hurriedly throwing away the leftovers. Outwit the suspect by randomly altering diet and meal times.

### GASTRIC LAVAGE (STOMACH WASH)

**I. DEFINITION & OBJECTIVE** Gastric lavage is a primary gastrointestinal decontamination procedure aimed at removing unabsorbed poisonous substances from the stomach before they can be systemically absorbed.

**II. INDICATIONS & THE "GOLDEN WINDOW"**

- **Ideal Timeframe:** Most effective when performed within **2 to 4 hours** (maximum 6 to 8 hours) of poison ingestion.
- **Delayed Lavage (🥇 High-Yield):** Lavage is highly indicated even after 12 to 18 hours in specific scenarios:
    - Ingestion of drugs that severely slow gastric motility (e.g., salicylates, phenothiazines, tricyclic antidepressants, antihistamines).
    - Ingestion of massive doses of **Aspirin**, which forms a tightly packed, insoluble bolus (pharmacobezoar) in the stomach.
    - Poisoning by **Opium/Morphine**, because the alkaloid is actively re-secreted into the stomach and biliary tract after systemic absorption, even if administered parenterally (IV/IM).

**III. CONTRAINDICATIONS (🚨 Danger Zones for Exams)**

- **Absolute Contraindications:**
    1. **Corrosive Acids and Alkalis:** Due to the extreme risk of esophageal or gastric perforation.
        - _🥇 Exam Exception:_ **Carbolic acid (Phenol)** is the _only_ corrosive where gastric lavage is indicated. Phenol causes local coagulation necrosis that turns the mucosa into a thick, hard, leathery eschar, which resists perforation.
    2. **Petroleum Distillates / Hydrocarbons:** (e.g., kerosene, gasoline) due to the high risk of aspiration pneumonitis and shock lung.
    3. **Convulsant Poisons (e.g., Strychnine):** Passing the tube can precipitate violent, fatal convulsions. Lavage must only be done _after_ convulsions are medically controlled.
    4. **Ingestion of Sharp Objects:** (e.g., powdered glass).
- **Relative Contraindications:**
    1. **Comatose Patients:** Lavage is contraindicated due to the loss of the gag reflex and high risk of aspiration, **UNLESS** the airway is strictly secured first with a cuffed endotracheal tube.
    2. Extremes of age (very young children) and advanced pregnancy.
    3. Upper alimentary diseases (e.g., esophageal varices).
    4. Severe hypothermia and hemorrhagic diathesis.

**IV. EQUIPMENT REQUIRED**

- **For Adults:** **Boas or Ewald’s stomach tube**. This is a soft, non-collapsible rubber tube, 1 cm in diameter and 1.5 meters in length. It features a funnel at one end, a suction bulb in the middle, and a rounded tip with lateral openings to prevent mucosal injury. It is marked at **50 cm**.
- **For Infants/Children:** A Ryle’s tube or a No. 10 to 12 French catheter (about 25 cm is usually needed to reach the stomach).
- **Accessories:** A wooden mouth gag with a central hole to prevent the patient from biting and occluding the tube.

**V. PATIENT POSITIONING (Critical Step)**

- The patient must be placed in the **left lateral decubitus** or prone position, with the head hanging over the edge of the bed and the face pointing downward.
- **Anatomical Rationale:** This ensures the mouth is at a lower level than the larynx. If fluid leaks around the tube, it will trickle out of the mouth via gravity rather than being aspirated into the trachea.

**VI. PROCEDURE & TECHNIQUE**

1. **Preparation & Insertion:** Remove any dentures and insert the mouth gag. Lubricate the tip of the tube with olive oil, liquid paraffin, or glycerine. Gently pass the tube through the pharynx and esophagus without force until the 50 cm mark is at the lips (or a distance equal to the bridge of the nose to the xiphisternum).
2. **Confirmation of Placement (🥇 Clinical Pearl):**
    - Ensure there is an absence of coughing and breath sounds in the funnel.
    - _The Water Test:_ Submerge the free end of the tube just below a water surface. Air from the stomach will bubble out completely in 2 to 3 expirations. If the tube is mistakenly in the trachea/lungs, it will cause continuous bubbling with every expiration.
3. **The Lavage Cycle:** Hold the funnel high above the patient's head and pour in about **250 ml (1/4 liter)** of warm water (35°C) or specific neutralizing solution.
4. **Siphoning:** Before the funnel completely empties, compress the rubber tube, lower it below the level of the patient's stomach, and release the pressure. The fluid will drain out via siphon action.
5. **Completion:** Repeat this process (using about half a liter per cycle) until the returning fluid is completely clear, odorless, and free of particulate matter.
6. **Post-Lavage:** Before removing the tube, a slurry of **Activated Charcoal** (1 g/kg body weight) and a cathartic (like magnesium sulfate) is instilled and left in the stomach to adsorb and expedite the excretion of any residual poison.

**VII. SPECIFIC LAVAGE SOLUTIONS (High-Yield Toxicology Pairings)** Using the correct neutralizing fluid chemically inactivates the specific poison:

- **Universal/Unknown Poisons:** Warm water or normal saline.
- **Alkaloids (Opium, Strychnine, Datura) & Salicylates:** **1:5000 Potassium Permanganate (KMnO4)**. It acts as a chemical antidote by oxidizing the poison. Lavage is continued until the returning fluid retains its pink color.
- **Cyanides:** 25% Sodium thiosulfate, or a mixture of 5-10% sodium thiosulfate / 1:5000 KMnO4.
- **Carbolic Acid (Phenol):** Castor oil, olive oil, or warm water with 10% glycerine / magnesium sulfate (to dissolve and neutralize phenol).
- **Oxalic Acid (Oxalates):** **Calcium gluconate** or calcium lactate (forms harmless, insoluble calcium oxalate).
- **Iron:** 5% Sodium bicarbonate, or **Desferrioxamine** (2 g in 1 liter of water).
- **Copper:** 1% Potassium ferrocyanide.
- **Formaldehyde:** 0.1% solution of ammonia (forms harmless methenamine).

**VIII. COMPLICATIONS OF GASTRIC LAVAGE** If performed improperly, complications include:

1. Aspiration pneumonitis (most common and dangerous).
2. Perforation of the stomach or esophagus.
3. Laryngeal spasm.
4. Cardiac arrhythmias.
5. Vagal inhibition (sudden reflex cardiac arrest).

### ANTIDOTES

**I. DEFINITION & CORE CONCEPT**

- **Definition:** Antidotes are specific substances administered to directly counteract, neutralize, or antagonize the toxic effects of poisons.
- **Mechanism of Action (🥇 High-Yield Mechanisms):** Antidotes work through various mechanisms:
    - _Inert complex formation:_ Chelating agents binding heavy metals.
    - _Accelerated detoxification:_ Sodium thiosulfate converting cyanide to thiocyanate.
    - _Reduced toxic conversion:_ Ethanol inhibiting the metabolism of methanol.
    - _Receptor site blockade / competition:_ Naloxone for opiates; Atropine for organophosphates.
    - _Toxic effect bypass:_ 100% oxygen bypassing the cellular block in cyanide poisoning.

---

**II. CLASSIFICATION OF ANTIDOTES** Antidotes are broadly classified into four major categories based on their mode of action:

#### 1. MECHANICAL OR PHYSICAL ANTIDOTES

These agents neutralize poisons by mechanical action or physically prevent their systemic absorption from the gastrointestinal tract.

- **Activated Charcoal:** A fine, black, odorless powder with an extremely large surface area (1,000 square meters per gram) that acts by physically adsorbing organic and some mineral poisons, trapping them in its pores.
    - _Examples:_ Highly effective for barbiturates, atropine, quinine, strychnine, digitalis, and phenothiazines.
- **Demulcents:** Substances that form a protective coating over the gastric mucous membrane, preventing corrosive or irritant damage.
    - _Examples:_ Milk, egg-white, starch, and mineral oil.
- **Bulky Food:** Acts mechanically by imprisoning sharp particles.
    - _Example:_ Used in powdered glass ingestion to safely ensnare the sharp glass particles and prevent mucosal damage.

#### 2. CHEMICAL ANTIDOTES

These counteract poisons by reacting with them directly in the stomach to form harmless, insoluble, or oxidized compounds before they can be absorbed.

- **Potassium Permanganate (1:5000 solution):** A powerful oxidizing agent used in gastric lavage.
    - _Examples:_ Oxidizes alkaloids like opium, strychnine, and barbiturates.
- **Tannic Acid / Strong Tea:** Precipitates toxic compounds.
    - _Examples:_ Precipitates alkaloids (apomorphine, strychnine, cocaine) and heavy metals (lead, silver, mercury).
- **Copper Sulfate (0.2% solution):** Forms an insoluble coating over the poison.
    - _Example:_ Coats white phosphorus particles with a film of relatively harmless copper phosphide.
- **Weak Alkalis / Acids:** Neutralize their opposites by direct chemical action (e.g., magnesium hydroxide for acids; vinegar for alkalis).

#### 3. PHYSIOLOGICAL OR PHARMACOLOGICAL ANTIDOTES

These agents act systematically on the tissues/receptors to produce physiological effects that are exactly opposite to those caused by the absorbed poison.

- **Receptor Antagonists:**
    - _Naloxone:_ Specific competitive antagonist at opioid receptors (antidote for morphine, heroin, codeine).
    - _Flumazenil:_ Competitive antagonist at the benzodiazepine receptor.
    - _Atropine:_ Blocks muscarinic receptor effects in organophosphate (OPC) or carbamate poisoning.
- **Physiological Antagonists:**
    - _Amyl Nitrite / Sodium Nitrite:_ Form methaemoglobin, which competes with cytochrome oxidase to pull away the cyanide ion.
    - _Barbiturates:_ Act as CNS depressants to counteract the severe excitatory/convulsant action of strychnine.

#### 4. CHELATING AGENTS (For Heavy Metals)

Chelating agents form stable, water-soluble, non-toxic, un-ionized ring complexes (chelates) with heavy metals, detaching them from tissue enzymes and promoting their rapid renal excretion.

- **B.A.L. (Dimercaprol / British Anti-Lewisite):** Possesses sulfhydryl groups that bind heavy metals.
    - _Examples:_ Drug of choice for Arsenic, Mercury, and Lead poisoning.
- **CaNa2 EDTA (Calcium Disodium Versenate):** Exchanges calcium for heavy metal ions.
    - _Example:_ Primarily used for Lead poisoning.
- **Penicillamine (Cuprimine):** A stable oral chelator.
    - _Examples:_ Highly effective for Copper (Wilson's disease), Lead, and Mercury.
- **DMSA (Succimer) / DMPS:** Water-soluble analogues of BAL that are orally active and less toxic.
    - _Examples:_ Lead, mercury, and arsenic poisoning.
- **Desferrioxamine:** Specific chelator for Acute Iron poisoning.

---

**🚨 CLINICAL & EXAM PEARL: THE "UNIVERSAL ANTIDOTE"** Historically, a "Universal Antidote" was utilized when the exact nature of the ingested poison was unknown or a mixture was suspected.

- **Composition:**
    1. **Activated Charcoal or burnt toast** (2 parts) - physical adsorption.
    2. **Magnesium Oxide** (1 part) - neutralizes acids without forming gas.
    3. **Tannic Acid or strong tea** (1 part) - precipitates alkaloids and metals.
- _Note:_ It is currently considered obsolete and is **not** recommended in modern clinical toxicology.

### ANTIDOTES: DEFINITION AND CLASSIFICATION

**I. DEFINITION**

- **Antidotes** are substances that counteract, neutralize, or prevent the toxic effects of poisons.

---

**II. CLASSIFICATION, MODE OF ACTION, AND EXAMPLES**

Antidotes are broadly classified into four major groups based on their mechanism of action:

#### 1. MECHANICAL OR PHYSICAL ANTIDOTES

- **Mode of Action:** They neutralize poisons by mechanical action or physically prevent their absorption from the gastrointestinal tract.
- **Examples:**
    - **Activated Charcoal:** A fine, black powder with an extremely large surface area (1000 square meters/gram). It acts by adsorbing and retaining organic and some mineral poisons within its pores, delaying their absorption. _Clinical Pearl: It is ineffective for corrosives, heavy metals, cyanide, and alcohols_.
    - **Demulcents (Milk, Starch, Egg-white, Mineral oil):** Form a protective physical coating on the gastric mucous membrane to prevent mucosal damage.
    - **Bulky Food:** Acts as a mechanical antidote to swallowed powdered glass by imprisoning the sharp particles within its meshes to prevent mucosal lacerations.

#### 2. CHEMICAL ANTIDOTES

- **Mode of Action:** They counteract toxicity by directly reacting with the poison to form harmless, insoluble, or oxidized compounds.
- **Examples:**
    - **Potassium Permanganate (1:5000 solution):** Acts via oxidation. It oxidizes alkaloids (like opium, strychnine), phosphorus, and cyanides into harmless products.
    - **Tannic Acid (or strong tea):** Precipitates alkaloids and heavy metals (e.g., lead, silver, cobalt, copper, zinc).
    - **Common Salt (NaCl):** Decomposes toxic silver nitrate by direct chemical action into insoluble, harmless silver chloride.
    - **Weak Alkalis / Acids:** Weak alkalis (like magnesia) neutralize acids, and weak acids (like vinegar/lemon juice) neutralize alkalis via direct chemical interaction.

#### 3. PHYSIOLOGICAL OR PHARMACOLOGICAL ANTIDOTES

- **Mode of Action:** They act systemically on the tissues/receptors of the body to produce physiological effects that are exactly opposite to those caused by the poison. They work on the principle of antagonism at the enzyme, cellular, or receptor level.
- **Examples:**
    - **Naloxone:** Acts via receptor site competition. It competes with opioids at the mu/kappa/delta receptors.
    - **Atropine:** Acts via receptor site blockade. It blocks muscarinic receptor sites in organophosphate poisoning.
    - **Amyl Nitrite / Sodium Nitrite:** Induces the formation of methaemoglobin, which binds toxic cyanide ions to form less toxic cyanmethaemoglobin.

#### 4. CHELATING AGENTS (Heavy Metal Antidotes)

- **Mode of Action:** Chelating agents are organic compounds that have a greater affinity for heavy metals than the body's endogenous enzymes. They act by _inert complex formation_. They firmly bind to the heavy metal to form a stable, non-toxic, and highly water-soluble ring complex (a "chelate") that is readily excreted by the kidneys into the urine.
- **Examples:**
    - **B.A.L. (Dimercaprol / British Anti-Lewisite):** Contains two unsaturated sulphydryl (-SH) groups that combine with metals, preventing the metal from binding to the -SH groups of cellular respiratory enzymes. Used primarily for **Arsenic, Mercury, and Lead**.
    - **E.D.T.A. (Calcium Disodium Versenate):** Forms water-soluble chelates with **Lead**. It acts as an ion exchanger where calcium is exchanged for the heavy metal ion.
    - **Penicillamine (Cuprimine):** A hydrolysis product of penicillin with a stable -SH group. It is highly efficient for **Copper, Lead, and Mercury**.
    - **DMSA (Succimer) & DMPS:** Water-soluble analogues of dimercaprol. Superior to EDTA for **Lead** poisoning (as they don't redistribute lead to the brain) and also effective for **Arsenic and Mercury**.
    - **Desferrioxamine:** Specifically binds circulating **Iron** to form a non-toxic complex.

1. Physiological antidotes.
### PHYSIOLOGICAL (PHARMACOLOGICAL) ANTIDOTES

**I. DEFINITION & CORE MECHANISM**

- **Definition:** Physiological (or pharmacological) antidotes are therapeutic agents that act directly on the body's tissues to produce clinical symptoms **exactly opposite** to those caused by the specific poison.
- **Mechanism of Action:** They operate on the principle of physiological antagonism by interfering with the poison's action on enzymes, tissue cells, specific receptor sites, or opposing nervous systems.
- **Timing of Administration:** Unlike mechanical or chemical antidotes which act in the GI tract, physiological antidotes are specifically indicated **after** the poison has already been absorbed into the systemic circulation.

**II. CLINICAL LIMITATIONS (🚨 Danger Zones)**

- Their clinical application is somewhat limited and is **not without danger**, as the antidotes themselves often have potent systemic effects.
- The antagonistic action of most known physiological antidotes is only **partial**, meaning they may reverse some, but not all, toxic effects of a poison.

**III. 🥇 HIGH-YIELD EXAM EXAMPLES & CLINICAL RELEVANCE**

**1. The "True" Physiological Antidotes:**

- **Atropine and Physostigmine** are considered the two _real_ physiological antidotes.
- Both affect nerve endings to produce directly opposing physiological effects on the state of the pupils, heart rate, and glandular secretory activity.

**2. Receptor Specific Antagonism:**

- **Atropine:** Acts via receptor site blockade at muscarinic receptors to reverse the SLUDGE effects in **Organophosphate** poisoning.
- **Naloxone:** Acts via receptor site competition to reverse CNS and respiratory depression in **Opiate/Opioid** overdose.

**3. Classic Opposing Agents:**

- **Amyl Nitrite** is utilized physiologically against **Cyanide** poisoning.
- **Short-acting Barbiturates** (CNS depressants) are used to counteract the severe tetanic convulsions caused by **Strychnine** poisoning (a spinal excitant).
- **Picrotoxin or Amphetamines** (CNS stimulants) were classically used to physiologically counteract the severe CNS depression caused by **Barbiturates**. _(Note: Modern clinical guidelines generally advise against using analeptic stimulants for barbiturate overdose unless there is a clear and compelling need, prioritizing respiratory support and forced diuresis instead)._

### CHELATING AGENTS

**I. OVERVIEW & MECHANISM OF ACTION**
*   **Definition:** Chelating agents are specific chemical antidotes (metal complexing agents) primarily used in the management of heavy metal poisoning.
*   **Pathophysiology of Heavy Metals:** Heavy metals (like arsenic, lead, mercury) exert their toxic effects by combining with the sulfhydryl (-SH) groups of mitochondrial enzyme systems, thereby severely interfering with cellular respiration. 
*   **Mechanism of Chelation:** Chelating agents possess a significantly greater affinity for these heavy metals than the body's endogenous enzymes. They bind strongly to the heavy metals, forming inert, stable, water-soluble, non-toxic, and un-ionized complexes (chelates) which are then safely excreted from the body, primarily via the urine.

---

**II. HIGH-YIELD CHELATING AGENTS**

**1. B.A.L. (British Anti-Lewisite / Dimercaprol / Dimercaptopropanol)**
*   **Indications:** Primarily used for **Arsenic, Mercury, and Lead** poisoning. Also used for Antimony, Bismuth, Copper, Gold, and Thallium.
*   **Action:** Contains two unsaturated sulfhydryl (SH) groups which readily combine with the metal, preventing the heavy metal from binding to the SH group of the respiratory enzymes. Two molecules of BAL combine with one atom of lead to form a complex excreted in the bile and urine.
*   **Administration:** Given as a deep intramuscular (IM) injection (usually 10% BAL in arachis oil).
*   🚨 **Contraindications/Side Effects:** 
    *   Contraindicated if the liver is damaged.
    *   May induce severe hemolysis in patients with **G6PD deficiency**.

**2. E.D.T.A. (Calcium Disodium Versenate / Edetic Acid)**
*   **Indications:** Currently used almost exclusively for **Lead** poisoning. 
*   **Action:** Acts as an ion exchanger where calcium is exchanged for the heavy metal ion, forming a highly soluble and stable lead chelate. This increases the renal excretion of lead up to fiftyfold above the normal rate.
*   **Administration:** Administered via slow IV infusion in 5% glucose or normal saline (IM administration is very painful).
*   🥇 **Clinical Pearl (Combination Therapy):** In severe lead poisoning with encephalopathy, **BAL must be given at least 4 hours before EDTA**. This is because EDTA alone can mobilize lead from tissue stores into the blood, potentially aggravating encephalopathy symptoms. 
*   🚨 **Contraindication:** Should *not* be used for mercury poisoning, as the EDTA-mercury complex is highly nephrotoxic.

**3. DMSA / Succimer (Meso-2, 3-dimercaptosuccinic acid)**
*   **Indications:** Used in Lead, Mercury, and Arsenic poisoning.
*   **Advantages over EDTA/BAL (Exam Favorite):** 
    *   It is **superior to EDTA** for lead poisoning because it does *not* lead to the redistribution of lead to the brain.
    *   It is significantly less toxic to the kidneys.
    *   Unlike BAL, it is **safe to use in G6PD deficient patients**.
    *   Administered orally.

**4. Penicillamine (Cuprimine / Dimethyl Cystine)**
*   **Indications:** The chelating agent of maximum efficiency for **Copper** (e.g., Wilson's disease or acute copper toxicity), but also used for lead and mercury.
*   **Action:** It is a hydrolysis product of penicillin that contains a highly stable SH group. 
*   **Administration:** Can be administered as an oral chelating agent or via slow IV drip. 
*   🚨 **Contraindication:** Should *not* be used in combination with BAL, as they may form a highly toxic compound.

**5. DMPS (2,3-dimercaptopropane 1-sulfonate)**
*   **Indications:** Highly effective for Mercury, Lead, and Arsenic poisoning.
*   **Action:** Possesses the same dithiol (sulfhydryl) chelating grouping as BAL but is much more hydrophilic, granting it a superior therapeutic index and lower toxicity. 

**6. Desferrioxamine**
*   **Indications:** The specific antidote of choice for **Acute Iron Poisoning**.
*   **Action:** Contains trivalent iron as a chelate, tightly binding free absorbed iron. 
*   **Administration:** Can be given orally (to bind unabsorbed iron in the stomach) or via IV infusion (to bind systemically absorbed iron).

*   **Arsenic (As):** BAL, DMSA, DMPS.
*   **Mercury (Hg):** Penicillamine, BAL, DMSA. *(Avoid EDTA)*.
*   **Copper (Cu):** Penicillamine.
*   **Iron (Fe):** Desferrioxamine.

**1. DATURA (and other Anticholinergics like Atropa belladonna)**

### POISONS MIMICKING NATURAL DISEASES

**OVERVIEW**
Certain poisons produce clinical toxidromes that closely resemble naturally occurring diseases. This is a highly tested medicolegal concept, as these poisons can be used for homicide with the intent of disguising the death as a natural event. 

The closest resemblance to natural disease is generally produced by **Thallium** poisoning. 

**🥇 HIGH-YIELD POISON-DISEASE PAIRINGS**

**1. ARSENIC**
*   **Acute Poisoning:** Perfectly mimics **Cholera**. It presents with sudden onset of severe nausea, projectile vomiting, and profuse "rice-water" stools. 
*   **Chronic Poisoning:** Mimics **Addison's disease** (due to "raindrop" hyperpigmentation resembling the melanosis of Addison's) and **Guillain-Barré syndrome** (due to symmetrical sensorimotor polyneuropathy).

**2. STRYCHNINE**
*   Mimics **Tetanus**. 
*   *Clinical Presentation:* Widespread excitation of the spinal cord produces severe tetanic convulsions, opisthotonus (hyperextension of the body), and *risus sardonicus* (sustained spasm of facial muscles). Homicidal deaths may go completely undetected because of this perfect mimicry.

**3. ACONITE**
*   Mimics **Sudden Cardiac Arrest / Acute Heart Disease**.
*   *Clinical Presentation:* Initial tachycardia followed by severe bradycardia, AV block, hypotension, and fatal cardiovascular collapse. It is considered an "ideal homicidal poison" because its symptoms perfectly resemble natural cardiac arrest and it is difficult to detect chemically.

**4. THALLIUM**
*   Mimics **Guillain-Barré syndrome**, **Acute Porphyria**, **Psychosis**, and **Thiamine deficiency**.
*   *Clinical Presentation:* The classic "Thallium Triad" consists of alopecia, skin rashes, and painful peripheral neuropathy. The recovery phase is also marked by peripheral neuritis closely resembling Guillain-Barré polyneuritis. 

**5. PHOSPHORUS**
*   Mimics **Acute Liver Disease / Acute Yellow Atrophy**.
*   *Clinical Presentation:* If the patient survives the initial gastrointestinal phase, the 3rd stage of toxicity presents with tender hepatomegaly, profound jaundice, hepatic encephalopathy, and a mousy odor in the breath (*fetor hepaticus*).

**6. CARBON MONOXIDE (CO)**
*   Mimics **Influenza (Flu)** or **Bacterial Food Poisoning**.
*   *Clinical Presentation:* The initial non-specific symptoms of mild to moderate CO poisoning—such as headache, nausea, vomiting, dizziness, weakness, and confusion—are frequently misdiagnosed clinically as a viral illness or foodborne gastroenteritis.
1. Ideal homicidal poison.
### IDEAL HOMICIDAL POISON

**I. DEFINITION & CHARACTERISTICS**
An ideal homicidal poison is a theoretical toxic substance that possesses specific properties making it a perfect weapon for murder, ensuring the victim's death while allowing the perpetrator to escape detection. 

🥇 **High-Yield Exam Criteria:**
For a poison to be considered "ideal" for homicide, it should possess the following characteristics:
1.  **Cheap and easily available**.
2.  **Colourless, odourless, and tasteless** so it can be administered secretly.
3.  **Easily administered** in food, drink, or medicine without causing any obvious physical or colour change that would arouse suspicion.
4.  **Highly toxic** (requires only a small dose).
5.  **Mimics natural disease** in its signs and symptoms, or its ill-effects are sufficiently delayed for the accused to escape.
6.  **No specific antidote** exists.
7.  **No postmortem changes** (leaves no characteristic autopsy findings).
8.  **Undetectable** by routine chemical analysis or other toxicological tests.
9.  **Rapidly destroyed** or made undetectable within the body after ingestion.

---

**II. CLASSIC EXAMPLES (🚨 Highly Tested Pairings)**
While no single poison fits *all* the criteria perfectly, several come close and are frequently tested in medicolegal exams:

*   **1. Thallium & Organic Fluorine Compounds (Rodenticides):** 
    These substances satisfy many of the modern criteria for an ideal homicidal poison. Thallium, a soft pliable metal, has acquired a notorious reputation as a modern homicidal poison because it is colourless, tasteless, and its delayed neurological/gastrointestinal symptoms mimic diseases like Guillain-Barré syndrome or acute porphyria. 
*   **2. Aconite (Monk's Hood):** 
    Considered by many toxicologists as the most ideal homicidal plant poison. 
    *   *Why it's chosen:* It is extremely difficult to detect by chemical analysis, and the sudden death it causes perfectly resembles a natural sudden disease (cardiac arrest). 
*   **3. Arsenic (The "Classic" Poison):**
    Historically the most popular homicidal poison.
    *   *Pros:* It is cheap, easily obtained, completely colourless/odourless/tasteless, requires a small dose, and its acute symptoms perfectly mimic **cholera** (a natural disease).
    *   *Cons (Exam Pitfall):* Arsenic is actually a *flawed* homicidal poison in modern times because it severely delays putrefaction, resists decomposition and fire (can be found in charred bones/ashes), and is permanently deposited in keratinized tissues (bones, hair, nails) where it can be detected years or centuries later.

### ARROW POISONS

**I. DEFINITION & MEDICOLEGAL RELEVANCE** Arrow poisons are highly toxic substances traditionally smeared on the tips of arrows, spears, or blow-darts. They are designed to enter the systemic circulation directly through penetrating trauma, thereby bypassing the gastrointestinal tract and exerting rapid, lethal effects (e.g., paralysis, cardiac arrest, or shock).

**II. 🥇 HIGH-YIELD CLASSIFICATION OF ARROW POISONS (EXAM FOCUS)** For maximum marks, classify the common arrow poisons based on their toxicological mechanisms:

**1. Peripheral Nerve Poisons**

- **Curare:** A classic arrow poison obtained from various species of _Strychnos_.
    - _Mechanism:_ It acts entirely peripherally at the myoneural junction, competitively blocking post-synaptic nicotinic acetylcholine receptors.
    - _Clinical Effect:_ Produces a progressive flaccid paralysis of the skeletal muscles, ultimately causing death by asphyxia due to paralysis of the respiratory muscles.

**2. Spinal Poisons**

- **Strychnine (_Strychnos nux vomica_):**
    - _Mechanism:_ Competitively blocks inhibitory post-synaptic receptor sites (glycine) in the spinal cord and brainstem.
    - _Clinical Effect:_ Causes widespread reflex excitation, leading to severe tetanic convulsions (opisthotonus, _risus sardonicus_) mimicking tetanus. Death results from medullary paralysis and asphyxia caused by sustained contraction of the diaphragm and thoracic muscles.

**3. Cardiac Poisons**

- **Aconite (Monk's Hood / _Aconitum ferox_):**
    - _Mechanism:_ Contains the potent alkaloid aconitine, which first stimulates and then depresses the central nervous system, whilst paralyzing the motor ganglia of the heart.
    - _Clinical Effect:_ Causes tingling, numbness, hypotension, and fatal cardiac arrhythmias (AV block, ventricular fibrillation).

**4. Organic Irritants & Toxalbumins**

- **_Abrus precatorius_ (Jequirity / Ratti):**
    - _Mechanism:_ The seeds contain **abrin**, a highly potent toxalbumin (phytotoxin) that inhibits cellular protein synthesis.
    - _Medicolegal Link:_ Aside from arrow poisons, it is famously fashioned into small, sharp spikes called **"suis"** used for homicidal cattle poisoning.
- **Croton Oil (_Croton tiglium_):**
    - _Mechanism:_ The oil extracted from croton seeds contains a powerful vesicating resin.
    - _Clinical Effect:_ Produces extreme burning, purging, prostration, and rapid collapse.
- **_Calotropis_ (Madar / Akdo):**
    - _Mechanism:_ Contains toxic active principles including calotropin, uscharin, and calotoxin.
    - _Clinical Effect:_ Causes severe gastrointestinal irritation, dilated pupils, tetanic convulsions, and collapse.

**5. Animal Toxins**

- **Snake Venom:** Smearing arrow tips with venom from snakes (especially neurotoxic elapids or hemotoxic vipers) is a highly effective method to ensure rapid immobilization or death through systemic absorption.

1. Sites of action of poisons.
### SITES OF ACTION OF POISONS

Poisons produce their toxicological effects through three primary modes of action based on their interaction with the body. Understanding these sites of action is critical for recognizing toxidromes and determining the correct management strategy.

**I. LOCAL ACTION** This occurs strictly at the site where the poison first comes into direct contact with the body (e.g., skin, gastrointestinal mucosa, respiratory tract).

- **🥇 Mechanisms & Classic Examples:**
    - **Chemical Destruction (Corrosion):** Strong mineral acids (e.g., sulfuric acid) and caustic alkalis directly fix, destroy, and erode tissues, causing coagulation or liquefaction necrosis.
    - **Congestion & Inflammation (Irritation):** Irritant poisons, such as heavy metals on the GI tract or Cantharides (Spanish fly) on the skin, produce localized redness, swelling, and vesication.
    - **Local Nerve Effects:** Direct action on superficial motor and sensory nerve endings.
        - _Clinical Pearl:_ **Aconite** causes localized tingling and numbness of the tongue/skin. **Datura** or **Belladonna** causes local pupillary dilation (mydriasis) upon direct contact with the eyes.

**II. REMOTE (SYSTEMIC) ACTION** This occurs when the poison exerts its effects on distant organs or systems, rather than at the initial site of contact.

- **🥇 Mechanisms & Classic Examples:**
    - **Reflex Shock:** A remote response triggered without systemic absorption. For instance, the excruciating pain and massive tissue destruction caused by a swallowed corrosive can reflexively cause profound, rapid neurogenic shock and circulatory collapse.
    - **Target Organ Toxicity (Post-Absorption):** The poison is absorbed into the bloodstream and selectively attacks specific remote organs or tissues.
        - _Spinal Cord:_ **Strychnine** (Nux vomica) acts on the anterior horn cells to cause massive tetanic convulsions.
        - _Kidneys:_ **Cantharides** targets the kidneys, producing severe nephritis and hematuria.
        - _Liver:_ **Paracetamol** or **Phosphorus** targets the liver, causing massive centrilobular or acute hepatic necrosis.
        - _CNS:_ **Opioids**, **Barbiturates**, and **Alcohols** act remotely on the brain to cause profound central nervous system depression and coma.

**III. COMBINED ACTION** These are poisons that possess a dual mechanism: they cause severe damage locally at the site of contact _and_ exert highly toxic remote effects after systemic absorption.

- **🥇 High-Yield Exam Examples:**
    - **Carbolic Acid (Phenol):** _Local:_ Coagulation necrosis and bleaching of the GI mucosa. _Remote:_ Profound CNS depression, shock, and nephrotoxicity (carboluria).
    - **Oxalic Acid:** _Local:_ Corrosive action on the gastric mucosa. _Remote:_ Systemically binds calcium to produce fatal hypocalcemia, tetany, and renal tubular necrosis.
    - **Phosphorus:** _Local:_ Severe GI irritation and mucosal burns. _Remote:_ Systemic absorption leads to profound hepatic necrosis (resembling acute yellow atrophy) and cardiovascular collapse.
1. What are the signs and symptoms in a patient which arouse suspicion of poisoning?
### I. GENERAL CRITERIA AROUSING SUSPICION OF POISONING
There is no single symptom or definitive group of symptoms that are absolutely characteristic of poisoning. However, a high index of clinical suspicion must be maintained if the following classical medicolegal features are present:
*   **Sudden Onset:** Symptoms appear suddenly in a previously completely healthy person.
*   **Temporal Association:** Symptoms appear immediately or within a very short period after the consumption of food, drink, or medicine.
*   **Symptom Progression:** The symptoms are uniform in character and rapidly increase in clinical severity.
*   **Mass Casualties (Cluster Outbreak):** Several persons eating or drinking from the same source suffer from similar symptoms at or about the exact same time.
*   **Physical Evidence:** The actual discovery of poison in the food taken, in the vomit, or in the excreta is strong proof of poisoning.

---

### II. HIGH-YIELD CLINICAL PRESENTATIONS (ACUTE POISONING)
Acute toxicity often presents with sudden onset of specific symptom clusters. The following presentations should immediately prompt a toxicological differential diagnosis:
*   **Gastrointestinal:** Sudden onset of severe abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, and circulatory collapse.
*   **Neurological (Depressant):** Sudden onset of coma accompanied by constriction of the pupils (miosis).
*   **Neurological (Excitatory):** Sudden onset of unexplained convulsions.
*   **Neurological (Deliriant):** Delirium accompanied by dilated pupils (mydriasis).
*   **Neuromuscular:** Paralysis, especially presenting as a lower motor neurone type.
*   **Hepatorenal Failure:** Unexplained jaundice and hepatocellular failure, or renal compromise presenting as oliguria with proteinuria and haematuria.
*   **Respiratory / Haematological:** Persistent cyanosis.
*   **Occupational Context:** Rapid onset of neurological or gastrointestinal illness in persons who are known to be occupationally exposed to chemicals.

---

### III. RED FLAGS FOR CHRONIC POISONING
Chronic poisoning can be highly insidious and frequently mimics natural diseases. Key diagnostic clues for exams and clinical practice include:
*   **Dose-Response Association:** Symptoms are consistently exaggerated following the administration of suspected food, fluid, or medicine.
*   **Constitutional Decline:** General malaise, cachexia, depression, and a gradual deterioration of the patient's general condition.
*   **Recurrent GI Upsets:** Repeated, unexplained attacks of diarrhoea, vomiting, or other gastrointestinal symptoms.
*   🚨 **Diagnostic Pearl (The "Removal" Test):** The clinical symptoms miraculously disappear when the patient is removed from their usual surroundings.
*   **Toxicological Confirmation:** Traces of the suspected poison are analytically detected in the patient's urine, stool, or vomit.
1. Color changes in cyanide and carbon monoxide poisoning.
### COLOR CHANGES IN CARBON MONOXIDE AND CYANIDE POISONING

**OVERVIEW**
Both Carbon Monoxide (CO) and Cyanide are highly lethal chemical asphyxiants that alter the normal color of blood, tissues, and postmortem lividity (hypostasis) due to specific biochemical interactions with hemoglobin and cellular respiration. Differentiating these classic color changes is a high-yield topic in forensic toxicology.

---

#### 1. CARBON MONOXIDE (CO) POISONING
*   **Classic Color:** **Cherry-red** discoloration.
*   **Mechanism:** Carbon monoxide has a 200 to 300 times greater affinity for hemoglobin than oxygen, displacing oxygen to form **carboxyhemoglobin (COHb)**, which gives the blood its characteristic cherry-red appearance.
*   **Distribution:** Seen in the skin, mucous membranes, conjunctivae, nail-beds, internal organs, tissues, and areas of postmortem hypostasis. 
*   🥇 **Clinical & Medicolegal Pearls:**
    *   In dark-skinned victims or anaemic individuals, the color may be faint or masked on the skin, but it can usually be detected on the inner aspects of the lips, tongue, nail-beds, palms, soles, and inside the eyelids.
    *   The cherry-red color is highly stable; it will **not** be changed by embalming or preservation with formalin.
    *   With the onset of decomposition, the cherry-red discoloration changes to dark-green and eventually to brown.
    *   It is typically associated with a COHb saturation of 30% to 40%.

---

#### 2. CYANIDE (HCN) POISONING
*   **Classic Color:** **Bright red** discoloration (though cheeks and postmortem staining may sometimes appear cherry-red).
*   **Mechanism:** Cyanide produces **histotoxic anoxia** by inhibiting the intracellular cytochrome oxidase enzyme system. Because the tissues cannot utilize or extract oxygen, the venous blood remains highly saturated with **oxyhemoglobin**. The concurrent formation of **cyanmethemoglobin** also contributes to the red colour. 
*   **Distribution:** Postmortem staining and all blood vessels (both arteries and veins) contain bright red, fully oxygenated blood. 
*   🥇 **Clinical & Medicolegal Pearls:**
    *   **Stomach Mucosa Exception:** While the blood is bright red, if the poisoning was caused by the ingestion of cyanide salts (like Potassium or Sodium Cyanide), the stomach mucosa may appear **blackened** or eroded due to their highly alkaline, corrosive action leading to the formation of **alkaline hematin**.
    *   A characteristic **bitter almonds odor** may be noted upon opening the body, particularly in the brain tissues. (Note: The ability to smell this is a sex-linked recessive trait absent in 20-40% of the population).
1. Drug automatism.
### DRUG AUTOMATISM

**I. DEFINITION & CONCEPT**

- **Definition:** Drug automatism is a hypothesis suggesting that a patient develops a state of toxic delirium or impaired consciousness after ingesting one or several initial doses of a drug.
- **Mechanism:** While in this confused, delirious, or automatic state, the individual forgets that they have already taken the medication and takes additional doses without realizing it.

**II. COMMONLY IMPLICATED AGENTS**

- This phenomenon is most frequently associated with **CNS depressant drugs**, **alcohol**, or a combination of both.
- 🥇 **Classic Exam Example:** It is characteristically associated with **Barbiturates** ("barbiturate automatism"). A patient taking barbiturate tablets for insomnia may fail to fall asleep immediately, become confused, and repeatedly consume more tablets to achieve sleep, ultimately resulting in acute toxicity.

**III. MEDICOLEGAL & CLINICAL RELEVANCE (🥇 High-Yield)**

- **"Involuntary Suicide":** Because the lethal consumption of the drug is done automatically and without conscious intent to die, drug automatism is a prominent cause of accidental poisoning, often termed **involuntary suicide**.
- **Investigative Challenge:** In forensic practice, when a fatal overdose is discovered, it is notoriously difficult to definitively prove or disprove whether the death was a true intentional suicide or the result of drug automatism.
1. Contraindications of gastric lavage.
### CONTRAINDICATIONS OF GASTRIC LAVAGE

Gastric lavage must be performed with caution as improper use can lead to severe or fatal complications. For exam purposes, contraindications are strictly classified into absolute and relative categories.

**I. ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATIONS (🚨 Danger Zones)**

- **Corrosive Poisons (Strong Acids and Alkalis):** Absolutely contraindicated due to the high danger of causing gastric or oesophageal perforation.
    - _🥇 High-Yield Exam Exception:_ **Carbolic acid (Phenol)** is the _only_ corrosive poison where gastric lavage is indicated. Phenol coagulates proteins, turning the stomach mucosa into a hard, white, rubbery (leathery) state, which reduces the risk of perforation.
- **Petroleum Products / Volatile Poisons:** Such as kerosene or gasoline. There is an extreme risk that the volatile substances or fluids may be inhaled into the respiratory tract, leading to fatal aspiration pneumonitis.
- **Sharp Ingested Objects:** Such as powdered glass, pins, or needles, due to the risk of further mechanical laceration of the gastrointestinal tract.

**II. RELATIVE CONTRAINDICATIONS**

- **Comatose Patients & Impaired Gag Reflex:** Strongly contraindicated due to the severe risk of aspiration of stomach contents and lavage fluid into the air-passages.
    - _🚨 Clinical Management Pearl:_ Lavage can be safely performed in these patients **only if** the airway is first secured and sealed with a **cuffed endotracheal tube**.
- **Convulsant Poisons (e.g., Strychnine):** The physical stimulation of passing the stomach tube can precipitate severe tetanic convulsions. Lavage should only be attempted _after_ convulsions are medically controlled with appropriate drugs.
- **Upper Alimentary Tract Diseases:** Conditions such as oesophageal varices, due to the mechanical risk of rupturing the varices and inducing severe haemorrhage.
- **Patient-Specific Factors:** Lavage is relatively contraindicated in advanced pregnancy, very young children, patients with marked hypothermia, and those suffering from a haemorrhagic diathesis (bleeding disorders) or severe heart disease.
1. Classification of antidotes with examples.
### CLASSIFICATION OF ANTIDOTES

**I. DEFINITION & MECHANISMS** Antidotes are therapeutic substances administered to directly counteract, neutralize, or antagonize the toxic effects of poisons. 🥇 **High-Yield Mechanisms of Action:**

- _Inert complex formation:_ Chelating agents binding heavy metals.
- _Accelerated detoxification:_ Sodium thiosulfate converting cyanide to non-toxic thiocyanate.
- _Reduced toxic conversion:_ Ethanol inhibiting the metabolism of methanol.
- _Receptor site blockade:_ Atropine blocking muscarinic receptors in organophosphate poisoning.
- _Receptor site competition:_ Naloxone competing for opioid receptors.
- _Toxic effect bypass:_ 100% oxygen bypassing the blockade in cyanide poisoning.

---

**II. CLASSIFICATION WITH EXAMPLES**

Antidotes are broadly classified into four major categories:

#### 1. MECHANICAL OR PHYSICAL ANTIDOTES

**Mode of Action:** Neutralize poisons by direct mechanical action or physically prevent their systemic absorption from the gastrointestinal tract.

- **Activated Charcoal:** A fine, black powder with an extremely large surface area (1000 square meters/gram) that physically adsorbs organic and some mineral poisons, retaining them within its pores.
    - _Examples:_ Highly effective for barbiturates, atropine, quinine, strychnine, digitalis, and phenothiazines.
    - _🚨 Clinical Pearl:_ It is **ineffective** for corrosives, heavy metals, cyanide, hydrocarbons, and alcohols.
- **Demulcents:** Substances that form a protective coating over the gastric mucous membrane, preventing corrosive or irritant damage.
    - _Examples:_ Milk, starch, egg-white, mineral oil, and milk of magnesia.
- **Bulky Food:** Acts mechanically by imprisoning sharp particles.
    - _Example:_ Used in powdered glass ingestion to safely ensnare the sharp glass particles and prevent mucosal damage.

#### 2. CHEMICAL ANTIDOTES

**Mode of Action:** Counteract poisons by reacting with them directly in the body to form harmless, insoluble, or oxidized compounds.

- **Potassium Permanganate (1:5000 solution):** Acts via oxidation in gastric lavage.
    - _Examples:_ Oxidizes alkaloids like opium, strychnine, barbiturates, phosphorus, and cyanides.
- **Tannic Acid (4% or strong tea):** Precipitates toxic compounds.
    - _Examples:_ Precipitates alkaloids (apomorphine, strychnine, cocaine) and heavy metals (lead, silver, copper).
- **Common Salt (NaCl):** Decomposes toxic silver nitrate by direct chemical action into insoluble, harmless silver chloride.
- **Copper Sulfate (0.2%):** Coats particles with a relatively harmless film.
    - _Example:_ Coats phosphorus with a film of copper phosphide.
- **Alkalis / Acids:** Neutralize their opposites (e.g., vinegar/lemon juice for alkalis; weak alkalis like magnesia for acids). _Note: Avoid bicarbonates for acid poisoning due to the risk of gastric rupture from liberated CO2_.

#### 3. PHYSIOLOGICAL OR PHARMACOLOGICAL ANTIDOTES

**Mode of Action:** Act systematically on the tissues to produce physiological effects that are exactly opposite to those caused by the absorbed poison (antagonism).

- **Receptor Antagonists:**
    - _Naloxone:_ Specific competitive antagonist at opioid receptors for morphine/heroin overdose.
    - _Atropine:_ Blocks muscarinic receptor effects in organophosphate poisoning.
- **Physiological Antagonists:**
    - _Amyl Nitrite / Sodium Nitrite:_ Form methaemoglobin, which pulls the cyanide ion away from cytochrome oxidase.
    - _Short-acting Barbiturates (e.g., pentobarbital):_ Act as CNS depressants to counteract the severe tetanic convulsions of strychnine poisoning.
    - _Ethanol:_ Competes for the enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase to prevent the metabolism of methanol.

#### 4. CHELATING AGENTS (For Heavy Metals)

**Mode of Action:** Form stable, water-soluble, non-toxic, un-ionized ring complexes (chelates) with heavy metals, detaching them from tissue enzymes and promoting rapid renal excretion.

- **B.A.L. (Dimercaprol / British Anti-Lewisite):** Possesses sulfhydryl groups that bind heavy metals.
    - _Examples:_ Drug of choice for Arsenic, Mercury, and Lead poisoning.
- **CaNa2 EDTA (Calcium Disodium Versenate):** Exchanges calcium for heavy metal ions.
    - _Example:_ Primarily used for Lead poisoning.
- **Penicillamine (Cuprimine):** A stable oral chelator.
    - _Examples:_ Highly effective for Copper, Lead, and Mercury.
- **DMSA (Succimer):** Water-soluble analogue of BAL.
    - _Examples:_ Lead, mercury, and arsenic poisoning. _🥇 Exam Pearl: It is superior to EDTA for Lead poisoning because it does not redistribute lead to the brain_.
- **Desferrioxamine:** Specific chelator for Acute Iron poisoning.

---

> ⚠️ **HISTORICAL EXAM PEARL: THE "UNIVERSAL ANTIDOTE"** The "Universal Antidote" was historically used when the exact nature of the ingested poison was unknown or a mixture was suspected. It consisted of **Activated Charcoal** (2 parts), **Magnesium Oxide** (1 part), and **Tannic Acid** (1 part). It is currently considered obsolete and is **not recommended** in modern clinical toxicology.

**1. MECHANICAL (OR PHYSICAL) ANTIDOTES**

- **Mechanism:** They neutralize poisons by mechanical action or physically prevent their absorption from the gastrointestinal tract.
- **Example:** **Activated Charcoal**. It acts mechanically by adsorbing and retaining organic and some mineral poisons within its extremely large surface area, delaying systemic absorption. _(Another example: Demulcents like milk or egg-white that form a protective physical coating on the gastric mucous membrane)._

**2. CHEMICAL ANTIDOTES**

- **Mechanism:** They counteract the action of the poison by directly reacting with it in the stomach to form harmless, insoluble, or oxidized compounds before absorption can occur.
- **Example:** **Potassium Permanganate (1:5000 solution)**. It acts as a powerful oxidizing agent used in gastric lavage to oxidize and neutralize poisons like opium, strychnine, cyanides, and barbiturates. _(Another example: Tannic acid, which precipitates alkaloids and heavy metals)._

**3. PHYSIOLOGICAL (OR PHARMACOLOGICAL) ANTIDOTES**

- **Mechanism:** They act systemically on the tissues of the body to produce physiological effects that are exactly opposite to those caused by the absorbed poison, often acting via receptor site blockade or competition.
- **Example:** **Naloxone** (acts via receptor site competition for opiate/opioid overdose) or **Atropine** (acts via receptor site blockade for organophosphate poisoning).

_(Note: A highly tested fourth category is **Chelating Agents**, such as B.A.L. or E.D.T.A., which work by inert complex formation to bind heavy metals into stable, water-soluble complexes for renal excretion)._
1. Universal antidote.
### UNIVERSAL ANTIDOTE

**I. DEFINITION & INDICATION**
*   **Concept:** A specific chemical mixture historically used in emergency toxicology when the exact nature of the ingested poison is **unknown**, or when a **mixture of poisons** is suspected.

**II. COMPOSITION (🥇 High-Yield for Exams)**
The classic formulation consists of three specific ingredients mixed in a defined ratio:
1.  **Activated Charcoal (or burnt toast):** 2 parts.
2.  **Magnesium Oxide:** 1 part.
3.  **Tannic Acid (or strong tea):** 1 part.

**III. CLINICAL RELEVANCE & CURRENT STATUS**
*   🚨 **Clinical Pearl:** The use of the "universal antidote" is currently considered **obsolete** and is **strictly not recommended** in modern clinical toxicology. Modern protocols prioritize targeted gastrointestinal decontamination (like isolated activated charcoal) and specific antidotes rather than this outdated general mixture.
1. Physiological (pharmacological) antidote.
### PHYSIOLOGICAL (PHARMACOLOGICAL) ANTIDOTES

**I. DEFINITION & CORE MECHANISM**
*   **Definition:** Physiological (or pharmacological) antidotes are therapeutic agents that act directly on the tissues of the body to produce clinical symptoms **exactly opposite** to those caused by the specific poison.
*   **Mechanism of Action:** They operate on the principle of **antagonism**. They interfere with the poison's action by acting upon enzymes, tissue cells, or opposing nervous systems.

**II. CLINICAL ADMINISTRATION & LIMITATIONS (🚨 Danger Zones)**
*   **Timing:** Unlike mechanical or chemical antidotes which act primarily in the GI tract, physiological antidotes are specifically indicated **after** some of the poison has already been absorbed into the systemic circulation.
*   **Safety Profile:** Their clinical use is somewhat limited and is **not without danger**, as these agents are often potent drugs themselves.
*   **Efficacy:** The antagonistic action of most known physiological antidotes is only **partial**. 

**III. 🥇 HIGH-YIELD EXAM EXAMPLES & CLINICAL PAIRINGS**

**1. The "True" Physiological Antidotes:**
*   **Atropine and Physostigmine** are considered the two *real* physiological antidotes. 
*   Both affect nerve endings to produce directly opposing physiological effects on the state of the pupils, heart rate, and glandular secretory activity.

**2. Classic Toxin-Antidote Pairings:**
*   **Cyanide:** Antagonized physiologically by **Amyl nitrite**.
*   **Strychnine (Spinal Excitant):** Antagonized by **Barbiturates** (CNS depressants).
*   **Barbiturates (CNS Depressants):** Classically antagonized by **Picrotoxin or Amphetamines** (CNS stimulants).
1. Define chelating agents. Give examples of their usage in poisonings.
### CHELATING AGENTS: DEFINITION & MECHANISM

**I. DEFINITION** Chelating agents (metal complexing agents) are specific chemical antidotes primarily utilized in the management of heavy metal poisoning.

**II. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY & MECHANISM OF ACTION (🥇 High-Yield)**

- **Toxin Pathology:** Heavy metals (such as lead, arsenic, and mercury) cause toxicity by firmly binding to the sulfhydryl (-SH) groups of mitochondrial enzyme systems, which severely interferes with normal cellular respiration.
- **Mechanism of Chelation:** Chelating agents counteract this via **inert complex formation**. They possess a much greater chemical affinity for these heavy metals than the body's endogenous enzymes do.
- **Elimination:** The agent binds directly to the heavy metal to form an inert, stable, non-toxic, and highly water-soluble complex known as a "chelate". This water-soluble chelate is then safely and rapidly excreted from the body via the urine.

---

### CLASSIC CHELATING AGENTS & CLINICAL USAGE

For maximum exam marks, match the specific chelating agent with its target poison and clinical caveats:

**1. B.A.L. (British Anti-Lewisite / Dimercaprol)**

- **Usage:** The primary antidote for **Arsenic, Mercury, and Lead** poisoning. It is also utilized for Antimony, Bismuth, Copper, Gold, and Thallium.
- **Action:** Contains two unsaturated sulfhydryl (-SH) groups that combine with the metal, protecting the respiratory enzymes' -SH groups.
- 🚨 **Clinical Pearls:**
    - It is strictly contraindicated in patients with severe liver damage.
    - It can induce severe hemolysis in patients with G6PD deficiency.
    - It should _never_ be combined with Penicillamine, as they may form a highly toxic compound.

**2. E.D.T.A. (Calcium Disodium Versenate)**

- **Usage:** Currently used almost exclusively in the treatment of **Lead** poisoning. (Though it can bind other metals like cobalt, cadmium, and iron).
- **Action:** Acts as an ion exchanger; calcium is exchanged for the heavy metal ion to form a stable, non-toxic, and non-ionized lead chelate, increasing renal excretion fiftyfold.
- 🚨 **Clinical Pearls:**
    - In severe lead poisoning accompanied by encephalopathy, **BAL must be administered at least 4 hours before EDTA**. If given alone first, EDTA can mobilize lead from bone/tissue stores into the bloodstream and aggravate encephalopathy symptoms.
    - It is completely contraindicated in mercury poisoning because the Ca-EDTA-mercury complex is highly nephrotoxic.

**3. DMSA (Succimer) & DMPS**

- **Usage:** Highly effective oral chelating agents for **Lead, Mercury, and Arsenic** poisoning.
- **Action:** These are water-soluble (hydrophilic) analogues of dimercaprol (BAL) with a superior therapeutic index and lower toxicity.
- 🥇 **Exam Advantage:** DMSA is **superior to EDTA** for lead poisoning because it does _not_ lead to the redistribution of lead into the brain. Furthermore, unlike BAL, it is less nephrotoxic and is completely safe to use in G6PD-deficient patients.

**4. Penicillamine (Cuprimine)**

- **Usage:** It is the chelating agent of maximum clinical efficiency for **Copper** poisoning (e.g., Wilson's disease). Also used as an oral chelator for mild to moderate Lead and Mercury toxicity.
- **Action:** A hydrolysis product of penicillin that contains a highly stable -SH group.

**5. Desferrioxamine**

- **Usage:** The specific antidote of choice for **Acute Iron** poisoning.
- **Action:** Contains trivalent iron as a chelate, tightly binding free absorbed iron. It can be given orally to bind iron in the stomach or via IV infusion to bind systemically absorbed iron.
1. Diaphoretics.
### DIAPHORETICS IN TOXICOLOGY

**I. DEFINITION & ROLE**
*   **Concept:** Diaphoretics are pharmacological agents or physical methods used to artificially induce profuse sweating (perspiration). 
*   **Toxicological Aim:** Historically, they were used to speed up the systemic elimination of absorbed toxic agents through the skin.
*   🚨 **Clinical Efficacy Pearl:** In modern toxicology, the clinical utility of this method is highly **doubtful**, as it is questionable whether diaphoresis significantly speeds up the actual excretion of toxic agents. 

**II. METHODS TO INDUCE DIAPHORESIS (🥇 High-Yield for Exams)**

**1. Pharmacological Diaphoretics:**
*   **Pilocarpine Nitrate:** This is the classic pharmacological agent used to induce sweating. Administering a dose of **5 mg subcutaneously (s.c.)** will produce profuse perspiration.
*   **Secondary Agents (Vasodilators):** A less marked diaphoretic effect can be achieved via cutaneous irritation and cutaneous vasodilation produced by drugs such as **alcohol, salicylates, and antipyretics**.

**2. Physical / Thermal Methods:**
*   **External Heat:** Application of heat using blankets or hot water bottles.
*   **Oral Fluids:** Administration of hot beverages such as hot tea, hot milk, or hot lemonade to increase core temperature and trigger sweating.

**III. CLINICAL CONTEXT**
While diaphoretics represent a historical approach to poison elimination, current clinical management protocols for severe poisoning rely on far more effective methods of enhanced elimination, such as forced alkaline diuresis, hemodialysis, and charcoal hemoperfusion.
