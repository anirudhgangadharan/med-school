3. Aconite.  
### ACONITE (Monk's Hood / Blue Rocket)

**I. INTRODUCTION & CHARACTERISTICS**

- **Botanical Source:** Derived from the plants _Aconitum napellus_ and _Aconitum ferox_, which grow widely in the Himalayas.
- **Common Names:** Monk's hood, blue rocket, devil's helmet, _meetha zeher_, _bish_, or _bikh_.
- **Toxic Parts:** All parts of the plant are poisonous, but the **roots and seeds are the most potent**. Toxicity is highest when the plant is in bloom.
- **Root Morphology:** The dried root is conical/tapering (5–10 cm long) with longitudinal wrinkles and scars of broken rootlets. It is dark-brown externally and white internally when freshly cut, but **turns pink on exposure to air**.
    - 🚨 _Exam Pearl:_ It is often accidentally mistaken for **horse-radish root**, which is long, cylindrical, yellowish-white, and has a pungent taste (aconite is sweet, then acrid).

**II. ACTIVE PRINCIPLES & TOXICOKINETICS**

- **Toxins:** Contains **aconitine** (the most potent alkaloid), pseudo-aconitine, indaconitine, bikhaconitine, and several other alkaloids.
- **Mechanism of Action:** It is primarily a **cardiac poison**. It initially stimulates and then depresses the CNS and sensory nerves. It paralyses the motor ganglia of the heart and slows the respiratory centre.

**III. CLINICAL FEATURES (🥇 High-Yield)** Symptoms typically appear immediately or within a few minutes of ingestion.

- **Sensory:** **Tingling and numbness** in the mouth, tongue, and pharynx, which later spreads all over the body. (Even handling the leaves or roots can cause local tingling/numbness).
- **Gastrointestinal:** Sweet taste followed by an acrid/burning sensation from the mouth to the stomach, excessive salivation, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhoea. Later, the patient suffers from severe thirst and an inability to swallow.
- **Ocular (Classic Sign):** **Hippus** – The pupils alternately contract and dilate spasmodically, eventually remaining dilated in the later stages. Vision becomes dim with diplopia.
- **Cardiovascular:** Initial tachycardia is followed by **bradycardia due to AV block**, cardiac arrhythmias, hypotension, and collapse.
- **Neurological:** Profuse sweating, subnormal temperature, muscular weakness (inability to walk or stand), ataxia, and slurred speech. Twitching, cramps, and convulsions may occur.
- _Note:_ The mind usually remains clear until death, although hallucinations may occur. Death is typically due to paralysis of the heart or respiratory centres.

**IV. FATAL DOSE & FATAL PERIOD**

- **Fatal Dose:** 1 gram of root; 2 to 5 mg of aconitine; 250 mg extract; 10 to 30 ml of tincture.
- **Fatal Period:** 2 to 6 hours.

**V. TREATMENT**

- **Decontamination:** Gastric lavage with warm water and a weak solution of iodine in potassium iodide or tannic acid (to precipitate the alkaloid).
- **Adsorption:** Multiple-dose activated charcoal and cathartics.
- **Antidote/Supportive:** **Atropine (0.5 to 1 mg)** is useful to counteract severe bradycardia. Treat symptoms on general lines with cardiac monitoring.

**VI. POSTMORTEM FINDINGS**

- Findings are generally non-specific and characteristic of asphyxia.
- **Gastrointestinal:** The mucosa of the stomach and duodenum show congestion and occasionally ecchymosis.
- **Cardiac:** **Subepicardial petechial-ecchymotic haemorrhages** on the left ventricle and other regions of the heart are a frequent finding.
- 🚨 _Autopsy Pearl:_ Aconitine is extremely unstable and is **rapidly destroyed by putrefaction**, making detection in decomposed bodies very difficult.

**VII. MEDICOLEGAL IMPORTANCE**

- **Homicide:** Aconite is considered an **ideal homicidal poison** because it is difficult to detect by chemical analysis, and the sudden death it causes closely resembles a natural disease (cardiac arrest). It is often given mixed with betel leaf or liquor to conceal its tingling taste.
- **Accidental:** Occurs due to mistakenly eating the roots instead of horseradish, consuming quack/Ayurvedic remedies containing it, or adding it to liquor to enhance intoxication.
- **Other Uses:** Used occasionally for suicide, as an abortifacient, as a cattle poison, and historically as an arrow poison.
3. Hippus. 
### HIPPUS

**I. DEFINITION & CORE CONCEPT**

- **Definition:** Hippus is a pupillary abnormality characterised by the spasmodic, alternating **constriction and dilatation of the pupil**.

**II. CLINICAL & TOXICOLOGICAL RELEVANCE (🥇 High-Yield Exam Pearl)**

- **Classic Association:** In forensic toxicology, hippus is a hallmark clinical sign of poisoning by **Aconite** (also known as Monk's hood or Devil's helmet), which is a highly lethal cardiac poison.
- **Disease Progression:** During acute aconite intoxication, the pupils initially exhibit this alternating rhythmic movement (hippus). However, as the poisoning advances to later stages, the pupils become **persistently dilated**.
- **Associated Ocular Symptoms:** The phenomenon is frequently accompanied by dimness of vision and diplopia (double vision).
