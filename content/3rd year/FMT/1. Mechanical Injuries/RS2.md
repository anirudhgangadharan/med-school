### A. SIX TYPES OF SKULL FRACTURES

1. **Fissured (Linear) Fracture:** Hair’s breadth cracks involving the whole thickness of the bone or inner/outer table. Usually caused by impact over a broad surface (General Deformation).
2. **Depressed Fracture (Fractures a la Signature):** Fractured bone segment is driven inwards. Caused by high kinetic energy over a small area. The inner table fractures more extensively than the outer.
3. **Comminuted Fracture:** Multiple intersecting fracture lines dividing the bone into three or more fragments. Caused by severe force over a broad area (e.g., crushing injury).
4. **Ring (Foramen) Fracture:** Fissured fracture encircling the skull, usually around the **foramen magnum**. Caused by transmitted force (e.g., fall on feet/buttocks or severe blow to the chin).
5. **Pond (Indented) Fracture:** A simple dent of the skull vault. Occurs mostly in **infants** due to pliable bone.
6. **Diastatic (Sutural) Fracture:** Separation along the **sutures**. Seen primarily in young persons before suture fusion is complete.

### B. MECHANISM OF BRAIN INJURIES

Brain injury results from gross or subtle structural changes caused by mechanical forces.

- **Acceleration/Deceleration Forces:** Brain injury is primarily caused by changes in velocity, either linear acceleration (or deceleration) or rotational changes.
- **Shear Strains (Rotational Mechanism):** The brain is incompressible but easily distorted. Damage occurs from rotational movement of the head, causing layers of brain tissue to **glide** over each other. This differential movement (shear strains) tears small vessels and neural connections.
    - This is the primary mechanism for **Contrecoup Lesions** and diffuse axonal injury (DAI).
- **Coup/Contrecoup:**
    - **Coup:** Injury occurs directly beneath the point of impact.
    - **Contrecoup:** Injury occurs opposite the site of impact, often resulting from the momentum of the brain striking the arrested skull following sudden **deceleration**. Contrecoup injuries are often more extensive than coup injuries.
- **Local Penetration:** Injury caused by penetration of the skull by foreign objects (e.g., bullet) or driving bone fragments inwards in a depressed fracture.

### POSTMORTEM OBJECTIVES IN ROAD ACCIDENT VICTIMS

The primary objectives of performing a comprehensive medicolegal autopsy in cases involving traffic accidents are investigative, reconstructive, and evidentiary.

**I. Reconstruction and Causation:**

- **Construct the Accident:** To establish the circumstances and sequence of the crash.
- **Determine Cause and Manner of Death:** To identify whether death was accidental, suicidal, or homicidal, based on injuries and pathology found.
- **Identify Specific Impacts:** To correlate injury patterns with specific parts of the vehicle (e.g., bumper, steering wheel, windshield).
- **Determine Driver/Victim Position:** To reconstruct the position of the occupants/victims prior to the impact.

**II. Identification and Toxicology:**

- **Establish Identity:** To confirm the identity of the deceased, especially in severe vehicular trauma or fragmentation.
- **Toxicological Analysis (Crucial):** To obtain samples (blood, urine) for toxicological screening (alcohol, drugs, carbon monoxide) to determine if impairment contributed to the accident.
- **Identify External Traces:** To collect trace evidence (hair, blood, fibers, grease, paint fragments) from the victim for comparison with the suspect vehicle in hit-and-run cases.

**III. Medico-Legal Interpretation:**

- **Evaluate Pre-existing Disease:** To determine if underlying natural disease (e.g., coronary artery disease, CVA) initiated or hastened the death.
- **Rule Out Homicide/Suicide:** To differentiate injuries from the accident itself versus those inflicted intentionally (e.g., suicide by collision, or homicide disguised as an accident).

### INJURIES SUSTAINED BY A PEDESTRIAN

Pedestrian injuries are broadly classified based on the sequence of impacts delivered by the vehicle and the ground.

#### 1. Primary Impact Injuries (Initial Strike)

- Caused by the vehicle striking the pedestrian first.
- **Bumper Injuries:** Typically involve the **legs (tibia and fibula)**.
    - Fracture Pattern: Often spiral or **wedge-shaped** (the base of the wedge points towards the impact side).
    - Level of Injury: Often at different heights on the two legs, depending on whether the pedestrian was running or walking.
    - Children: In young victims, the **bumper injury may cause femoral fractures** due to their lower center of gravity.
- **Impact Site:** Injuries correspond to the height and shape of the impacting part (e.g., bumper, fender, radiator grille), often leaving **patterned imprints or abrasions**.

#### 2. Secondary Impact Injuries (Vehicle Impact After Initial Strike)

- Injuries sustained when the victim slides onto or impacts other parts of the vehicle after the primary strike.
- **Head Impact:** If the person falls onto the hood, the head may strike the **windscreen, upper windscreen rim, or pillar**.
- **Pelvis/Torso:** Fractures and dislocations of the lumbar/thoracic spine may occur.
- **Whiplash:** A rear impact can cause **fracture-dislocation of the cervical spine** due to hyperflexion-hyperextension.

#### 3. Secondary (Tertiary) Injuries (Striking the Ground/Objects)

- Injuries caused by the victim striking fixed objects or the ground after being thrown clear of the vehicle.
- **Skull Fractures:** Head striking the road often causes **extensive fractures of the skull base**, sometimes dividing the skull into two halves (hinge fractures).
- **Abrasions/Contusions:** Extensive **grazes (brush burns)** are typical due to dragging or scraping on the road surface.
- **Avulsion Injuries:** Shearing/grinding force from a vehicle wheel passing over a limb can cause avulsion (flaying) of skin and soft tissue down to the bone.
- **Internal Organ Damage:** Severe injury to the **lungs, heart, liver, or spleen** often results from striking the ground.

3. Sig**nature fracture**
### DEPRESSED FRACTURES (FRACTURES A LA SIGNATURE)

**Definition & Type**

- "Signature fractures" are synonymous with **Depressed Fractures**.
- They are classified among the types of mechanical fractures of the skull.

**Mechanism/Aetiology**

- Caused by **focal application of force**.
- Typically inflicted by a heavy weapon or object possessing a **small striking surface** (e.g., hammer, stone).

**Key Features (Pathology)**

- The resulting pattern often **resembles the weapon or agent** which caused the trauma ("fractures a la signature").
- The fracture fragments are characteristically **driven inwards** into the cranial cavity.
- They are produced by **local deformation** of the skull.
    - The **inner table** fractures first due to stretching, followed by the outer table.
    - If the force continues, the fragments become loosened and depressed, leading to a **comminuted fracture**.
  

4. Internal injuries caused by blunt force on abdomen 
### INTERNAL INJURIES DUE TO BLUNT FORCE ON ABDOMEN

**KEY CONCEPTS**

- Blunt force to the abdomen is a **closed injury**.
- Severe internal injury may occur **without external signs** (abrasions, contusions) due to the yielding/absorbent nature of the abdominal wall.
- Injuries are caused by **compression, stretching, or disruption (bursting)** forces.
- Solid organs are **more readily lacerated** than hollow organs.

---

#### 1. SOLID ORGANS (Friable/Dense)

|Organ|Injury Type/Mechanism|Key Features & Outcome|
|:--|:--|:--|
|**Liver**|**Most frequently damaged** abdominal organ. Caused by blows, kicks, crushing, or sudden muscle contraction.|Lacerations often **transcapsular** or **subcapsular**. Right lobe is five times more commonly affected. Transcapsular tears cause rapid hemorrhage/shock; subcapsular hematoma may cause fatal **delayed intraperitoneal hemorrhage** upon rupture days later.|
|**Spleen**|Second only to brain in overall visceral susceptibility. Caused by compression or traction forces. Rupture can be caused by mild trauma if spleen is enlarged (e.g., malaria).|Lacerations are often multiple (**Y, H, or L shapes**) on the concave surface. Bleeding is profuse, often causing **rapid death**.|
|**Kidneys**|Result from blunt force to the posterior or lateral aspect (loins).|Kidney has high resistance; severe force causes **bursting injuries** and fragmentation (**Pascal’s law**). Lacerations can be transcapsular, subcapsular, or transrenal (extending to renal pelvis). Can lead to fatal hemorrhage or post-injury sepsis.|

---

#### 2. HOLLOW ORGANS & ATTACHMENTS

|Structure|Injury Type/Mechanism|Key Features|
|:--|:--|:--|
|**Intestines (Small)**|Compression or crushing forces (more common than in stomach/large intestine).|Most common site of rupture is the jejunum, usually along the **antimesenteric border**. Ruptures are often multiple.|
|**Stomach/Duodenum**|Crush injury when compressed against the vertebral column (L2).|**Rupture is rare** unless stomach is distended or diseased (ulcer). Duodenum may be crushed or severed (**transected**).|
|**Pancreas**|Rare injury, typically crushed against the vertebral column when the stomach is empty.|Rupture leads to escape of pancreatic juice, causing **fat necrosis** and potentially **chemical peritonitis**.|
|**Mesentery/Omentum**|Torn by traction forces due to intestinal displacement.|Injury to large mesenteric artery can cause **fatal hemorrhage**. Contusions and bruising of mesentery and omentum are common.|
|**Bladder**|Ruptures only if **distended**. Often associated with **pelvic fracture**.|Rupture may be **intraperitoneal** (if distended) or **extraperitoneal** (causing urine extravasation into surrounding tissues).|
|**Diaphragm**|Penetrating or severe blunt trauma; sudden increase in intra-abdominal pressure.|Often lacerated on the left side. Herniation of abdominal contents (spleen, stomach) can occur, potentially leading to **delayed death**.|

---

#### 3. COMPLICATIONS & OUTCOME

- **Hemorrhage:** Laceration of solid organs (liver, spleen) causes profuse internal hemorrhage. Rupture of large mesenteric arteries is fatal.
- **Peritonitis:** Leakage of contents from hollow organs (stomach, intestines) or pancreatic juice causes chemical/septic peritonitis.
- **Ileus/Obstruction:** Contusions of intestines can cause paralytic ileus.
- **Shock:** Common cause of death due to hemorrhage and fluid loss.
- **Absence of External Marks:** The force may be entirely absorbed by the yielding abdominal wall, meaning severe visceral injury can occur without overlying contusions.

5. Railway spine


**Definition/Synonymy**

- "Railway spine" is synonymous with **Concussion of the Spinal Cord**.
- It is a syndrome resulting from trauma, often seen following **railway or motor car collision**.

**Mechanism/Aetiology (Traumatic Origin)**

- Caused by a **momentary collision** of the spinal cord against the wall of the spinal canal.
- The injury results from transient **acceleration/deceleration** or rotational strains.
- It may result from:
    - Severe **blows to the back**.
    - Compression or fracture/dislocation of the vertebrae.
    - Falls from a height or bullet injury.

**Pathophysiology & Pathology**

- The mechanism involves a **transient deformity** in the profile of the spinal canal.
- It causes functional disturbance (concussion) of the cord due to sudden acceleration/deceleration forces.
- The spinal cord injury may occur **without associated fracture or dislocation** of the vertebrae.
- Pathologically, the trauma may lead to:
    - Oedema, venous thrombosis, and **softening of the cord**.
    - Haemorrhagic discolouration on the surface (Haematorrhachis) or in the substance of the spinal cord (**Haematomyelia**).

**Clinical Features (Key Symptoms)**

- The onset of symptoms may be immediate or delayed for some hours.
- The condition causes **temporary paralysis** (usually resolving) affecting:
    - Arms and hands, or lower extremities (paralysis/weakness).
    - Bladder and rectum (dysfunction).
- Associated neurological and systemic symptoms include:
    - Headache, giddiness, and **neurasthenia** (nervous exhaustion).
    - Weakness in the limbs, loss of sexual power, and amnesia.
- **Prognosis:** The paralysis is typically temporary, and **recovery occurs in about 48 hours**.
