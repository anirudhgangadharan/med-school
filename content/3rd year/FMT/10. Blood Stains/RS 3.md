1. Microchemical tests for blood.
### MICROCHEMICAL TESTS FOR BLOOD

**I. CORE PRINCIPLE** Microchemical tests confirm the presence of blood by utilizing the property of the **haeme (iron)** portion of haemoglobin to form characteristic, coloured crystals when reacted with specific chemical reagents.

**II. 🥇 HIGH-YIELD TESTS**

**1. HAEMIN CRYSTAL TEST (TEICHMANN'S TEST)**

- **Reagents:** A small crystal of **sodium chloride (NaCl)** + 2 to 3 drops of **glacial acetic acid**.
- **Procedure:** Placed on a glass slide with the suspected stain, covered with a coverslip, and gently heated over a small flame until the acid evaporates. It is then cooled and examined microscopically.
- **Positive Result:** Formation of faint **yellowish-red to brownish-black rhombic crystals** of haemin (haematin chloride), arranged singly or in clusters.
- _Confirmatory Step:_ Addition of a drop of hydrogen peroxide ($H_2O_2$) to these crystals produces bubbles of gas.
- 🚨 **Exam Pitfall (Causes of False Negatives):** The reaction will fail if the stain is very old, washed, chemically treated, if there is excess salt, moisture in the acid, or if the slide is overheated during preparation.

**2. HAEMOCHROMOGEN CRYSTAL TEST (TAKAYAMA TEST)**

- **Reagents (Takayama Reagent):** A mixture of **sodium hydroxide, pyridine, and glucose**.
- **Procedure:** 2 to 3 drops are added to the stain on a slide and covered. Crystals typically appear in 1 to 6 minutes (slight warming hastens this). The test is only considered negative if no crystals form after 30 minutes.
- **Positive Result:** Formation of **pink, feathery crystals** of haemochromogen (reduced alkaline haematin), characteristically arranged in clusters or sheaves.
- 🥇 **Clinical/Medicolegal Pearl:** The Takayama test is considered **more delicate and reliable** than Teichmann's test and is especially useful because it gives **good results even with old blood stains**.
1. Procedure and applications of precipitin test.
### PRECIPITIN TEST

**I. PRINCIPLE & DEFINITION**

- **Concept:** It is a highly specific immunological (serological) test used to determine the **species of origin** (human vs. animal) of a biological stain or tissue.
- **Mechanism:** Based on the antigen-antibody reaction. When human serum (containing proteins in colloidal suspension) is injected into an animal (e.g., rabbits or horses), the animal becomes immunized and develops specific antibodies against these human proteins.
- These antibodies are known as **precipitins**, and the extracted animal serum is called **antihuman precipitin serum**.

**II. PROCEDURE (TECHNIQUE)**

- **Preparation of Extract:** A small piece of the stained material is soaked in **normal saline** to prepare an extract. _🚨 Exam Pearl: No chemicals should be added during extraction_.
- **Clarification:** The extract must be clear; it is filtered or centrifuged if necessary and diluted to a **1:100 concentration** with normal saline.
- **The Reaction:**
    - In a small tapering test tube held in a slanting position, **0.75 ml of the diluted stain extract** is taken.
    - **2 drops of undiluted antiserum** are gently added.
    - The heavier antiserum slowly settles to the bottom of the tube.
- **🥇 Positive Result:** A **white ring with well-defined borders** appears at the junction of the two fluids (situated mostly in the antiserum).
- **Timing:** A positive reaction should begin within **10 minutes** and is typically read within **half an hour**. For medicolegal purposes, all doubtful reactions are strictly read as negative.

**III. MEDICOLEGAL APPLICATIONS (CLINICAL RELEVANCE)**

- **Blood Stains:** Acts as a confirmatory test to prove that a suspected blood stain is of human origin.
- **Body Secretions:** Detects the human origin of albuminous substances in secretions such as **semen, saliva, and milk**.
- **Soft Tissues:** Determines the origin of recovered skin or flesh fragments at a crime scene.
- **Skeletal Remains:** Used to differentiate between **human and animal bones**.
    - _🚨 Exam Pearl:_ It is only useful if the bone is fairly fresh and still contains extractable plasma proteins/blood constituents. The precipitin test typically becomes **negative after about 10 years** since death.
1. Blood groups and medicolegal importance.
### BLOOD GROUPS AND THEIR MEDICOLEGAL IMPORTANCE

**I. OVERVIEW & BASIC PRINCIPLES**

- **Discovery:** The ABO blood group system was discovered by Landsteiner in 1930.
- **Components:** Blood groups depend on the presence of specific **antigens** (agglutinogens) on the red blood cell membrane and **antibodies** (agglutinins) in the blood serum.
- **Genetics:** Blood group characteristics are inherited according to simple Mendelian principles, remaining constant and peculiar to an individual throughout their entire life.

**II. 🥇 HIGH-YIELD BLOOD GROUP SYSTEMS**

- **1. ABO System:** Consists of four phenotypes (A, B, AB, O) derived from six genotypes (AA, AO, BB, BO, AB, OO).
    - _Exam Pearl (Bombay Phenotype):_ Rare individuals possess the "hh" genotype; they completely lack A, B, and H antigens on their erythrocytes and in their secretions.
- **2. Rh System:** Based on the Rhesus factor, involving six main antigens (Cc, Dd, Ee). Unlike the ABO system, Rh antibodies do not occur naturally and are only produced following exposure (e.g., mismatched transfusion or pregnancy).
- **3. HLA System (White Cell Antigens):** Controlled by the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) on chromosome 6. Because it is highly polymorphic with over 125 alleles, it provides an extremely high discriminating capacity for parentage testing.
- **4. Additional Systems:** MNSs system, Kell, Duffy, Kidd, serum proteins (Haptoglobins, Gm types), and red cell enzymes (PGM, AK) are also utilized in forensic serology.

**III. THE "SECRETOR" STATUS (🚨 Highly Tested Concept)**

- **Definition:** In about 80% of individuals, ABO blood group antigens are present in a water-soluble form and are secreted into body fluids (e.g., semen, saliva, sweat, vaginal secretions, urine).
- **Genetics:** Secreting capacity is controlled by dominant genes (_SeSe_ or _Sese_). Non-secretors (_sese_) only possess antigens in a lipoidal form confined to tissues and red cells.
- **Forensic Relevance:** Allows forensic experts to determine the blood group of an assailant from seminal stains in rape cases, or salivary stains on cigarette butts and bite marks.

---

### MEDICOLEGAL IMPORTANCE OF BLOOD GROUPS

**1. Disputed Paternity and Maternity (Affiliation Cases)**

- **Rule of Exclusion:** Blood grouping can **never absolutely prove** paternity; it can only indicate possibilities or **definitively exclude** a man from being the father.
- **Principles of Exclusion:**
    - An antigen cannot appear in the child's blood unless it is present in at least one of the parents. (e.g., Two Group 'O' parents cannot have a Group 'A' or 'B' child).
    - A homozygous parent must pass their gene to all their children.
- _Clinical Pearl:_ While traditional blood grouping can exclude paternity in about 82-94% of falsely accused men (when combined with enzymes and serum proteins), **DNA fingerprinting** is now the gold standard as it can positively fix or exclude paternity with 100% certainty.

**2. Criminal Investigation & Trace Evidence**

- **Linking Suspect to Crime Scene:** Blood stains found on a suspect's clothes, weapons, or under their fingernails can be matched to the victim's blood group.
- **Hit-and-Run Accidents:** Blood or tissue found on a suspected vehicle can be matched to the pedestrian victim, establishing a direct connection.
- **Sexual Offences:** Seminal fluid or saliva recovered from the victim or crime scene can be typed (if the assailant is a secretor) and compared against the suspect's blood group.

**3. Identification of Unknown/Mutilated Bodies**

- Blood grouping can be performed on putrefied or skeletonized remains. ABO antigens are highly stable and can be detected in dental pulp, bone marrow, and hair (using the absorption-elution technique) long after the soft tissues have decomposed.
- Possessing a very rare blood group combination can strongly corroborate an individual's absolute identity.

**4. Medical Negligence & Transfusion Hazards**

- Administering mismatched or incompatible blood due to clerical errors or improper cross-matching can lead to fatal immunological reactions (intravascular hemolysis, anaphylaxis, renal failure).
- These cases often result in civil or criminal medical negligence lawsuits, requiring forensic autopsies to demonstrate hemoglobinuric nephrosis and mismatched antigens.
1. Disputed paternity.
### DISPUTED PATERNITY

**I. DEFINITION & MEDICOLEGAL INDICATIONS** Disputed paternity is a civil or criminal medicolegal issue that arises when the biological fatherhood of a child is contested. The question typically arises in the following clinical-legal scenarios:

- **Lawful Wedlock:** A child is born during a lawful marriage, but the husband denies being the biological father.
- **Affiliation Cases:** An unmarried mother accuses a specific man of being the father to claim financial maintenance for the child (under Sec. 125 Cr.P.C.), and the man denies the accusation.
- **Supposititious (Fictitious) Children:** A woman falsely pretends pregnancy and delivery, producing a child as her own to illegally claim a share in her husband's property.
- **Divorce & Nullity:** Suits for nullity of marriage or divorce proceedings based on alleged infidelity.

---

**II. DIAGNOSTIC MODALITIES (🥇 HIGH-YIELD FOR EXAMS)**

**1. DNA Fingerprinting / Profiling (The Gold Standard)**

- **Principle:** A child inherits exactly 50% of its DNA from the mother and 50% from the biological father.
- **Methodology:** The DNA bar codes (bands) of the child are first matched with the mother's DNA pattern. All remaining unmatched bands in the child's profile _must_ match the patterns of the putative (alleged) father.
- **🚨 Medicolegal Pearl:** DNA analysis is the _only_ method that can absolutely **fix (establish)** or **exclude** paternity with 100% certainty.

**2. Blood Grouping (Serological Tests)**

- **Principle:** Blood groups (ABO, MNS, Rh, Kell, Duffy, Kidd) are inherited according to strict Mendelian principles. A specific blood group antigen cannot appear in a child unless it was present in one or both of the parents.
- **Rules of Exclusion:**
    - _First-Order Exclusion:_ The child has a blood group gene that is absent in both the mother and the putative father. (e.g., Mother is O, putative father is O, but the child is A).
    - _Second-Order Exclusion:_ The putative father is homozygous for a blood group gene, but that gene is entirely absent in the child. (e.g., Putative father is AB, meaning he must pass either A or B, but the child is O).
- **🚨 Medicolegal Pearl (Limitations):** Blood grouping is an **exclusionary test**. It can definitively prove that a man is _not_ the father, but it **cannot absolutely prove** that he _is_ the father (it only indicates possibility/probability).

**3. HLA (Human Leukocyte Antigen) Typing**

- **Principle:** The HLA system possesses a very high degree of polymorphism, providing excellent discriminating capacity.
- **Efficacy:** Testing a number of HLA systems can resolve paternal disputes with up to 95% to 98.5% accuracy.

**4. Physical and Morphological Traits (Corroborative Only)**

- **Parental Likeness & Atavism:** Observing physical resemblance to the alleged father or grandparents (atavism due to recessive gene mutation).
- **Developmental Defects:** Tracing the transmission of specific congenital deformities or hereditary diseases.
- **Clinical Value:** Highly unreliable as primary evidence; these are considered only as weak corroborative evidence in court.
1. Write a note on DNA fingerprinting.
### DNA FINGERPRINTING (DNA TYPING / PROFILING)

**I. OVERVIEW & DISCOVERY**

- **Definition:** A highly specific technique involving the chemical division of DNA into fragments to form a unique "identity profile" that can be matched with a suspect or relative.
- **Discovery:** Developed by **Dr. Alec Jeffreys in 1985**.
- **Uniqueness:** The chance of two people having the same DNA sequence is approximately 1 in a million billion.

**II. SCIENTIFIC PRINCIPLE (🥇 High-Yield)**

- The human genome contains about 6 x 10^9 DNA molecules. Only about 5% of this DNA consists of active genes that code for proteins.
- The remaining 95% is **"junk DNA"** which contains short, repetitive sequences of bases (e.g., GCTA, GCTA).
- These repetitive sequences demonstrate extreme individual hypervariability and are known as **Variable Number Tandem Repeats (VNTRs)** or **minisatellites**.
- **Key DNA Markers Used:** RFLPs (Restriction Fragment Length Polymorphisms), VNTRs, STRs (Short Tandem Repeats), and SNPs (Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms).

**III. HIGH-YIELD LABORATORY TECHNIQUES**

- **1. RFLP (Restriction Fragment Length Polymorphism):**
    - DNA is extracted and cut by **restriction endonucleases** (e.g., PsT-1).
    - Fragments are separated by **agarose gel electrophoresis** and transferred to a nylon membrane via Southern blotting.
    - The membrane is hybridized with radioactive probes (e.g., **BKm probe** in India, isolated from the banded krait) and visualized on an autoradiograph.
    - _Drawback:_ Requires a large amount of sample in good condition.
- **2. PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction):**
    - Amplifies minute amounts of DNA over a million-fold using thermal **TAQ polymerase**.
    - _Advantage:_ Ideal for very small or partially degraded biological samples.
    - _Drawback:_ Highly susceptible to contamination.
- **3. STR (Short Tandem Repeats):**
    - The **most prevalent** technique for forensic purposes.
    - Evaluates specific regions (at least 13 core loci); the unique combination of these polymorphisms makes it a highly discriminating tool.
- **4. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) Analysis:**
    - **Maternally inherited** (all daughters/sons have the same mtDNA as their mother).
    - _Clinical Pearl:_ Crucial for analyzing highly degraded samples that lack nucleated cells, such as old bones, teeth, or **hair shafts without roots**.
- **5. Y-Chromosome Analysis:**
    - Passed directly from father to son.
    - Extremely useful for isolating male DNA in mixed biological evidence (e.g., **gang rape** cases).

**IV. SPECIMEN COLLECTION & PRESERVATION (🚨 Exam Pitfalls)**

- **Samples Used:** Blood, semen, vaginal swabs, saliva, hair (must include the root for nuclear DNA), bone marrow, tooth pulp, and fingernail scrapings.
- **Blood Collection:** 2 to 5 ml of IV blood must be collected in sterile tubes containing **EDTA** (preferred because it inhibits enzymes/bacteria that breakdown DNA) or heparin.
- **Preservation Rules:**
    - Liquid samples should be refrigerated or frozen at -70°C.
    - Dried stains (e.g., blood/semen on cloth) should be air-dried in the shade and maintained dry.
    - **NEVER use formaldehyde** as a fixative, as it makes DNA extraction exceptionally difficult.

**V. MEDICOLEGAL APPLICATIONS**

- **Disputed Paternity & Maternity:** Accounts for 50% of DNA typing cases in India. A child receives 50% of their DNA from the mother and 50% from the father.
- **Sexual Offences:** Seminal DNA from vaginal swabs, skin, or clothing is matched with the suspect's blood/semen profile.
- **Child Swapping:** Resolving mixed-up newborns in hospitals.
- **Identification of Unknown Remains:** Identifying mutilated, skeletonized, or burnt bodies in mass disasters (e.g., bomb blasts, plane crashes) by comparing tissue to close blood relatives.
- **Extortion:** Analyzing saliva on envelopes or cigarette butts.

**VI. LIMITATIONS / PROBLEMS**

- Contamination with bacteria can rapidly degrade DNA.
- Gene mutations in a child can occasionally yield false results in paternity testing.
- Routine DNA typing methods **cannot discriminate between monozygotic (identical) twins**; this requires specialized sequence analysis of bone marrow-derived B lymphocytes.
1. Spectroscopy.
### SPECTROSCOPY IN FORENSIC MEDICINE

**I. DEFINITION & PRINCIPLE** Spectroscopic examination is considered the most delicate and reliable test for detecting the presence of blood in both recent and old stains. The core principle relies on the property of a blood solution to absorb specific rays from the light spectrum, producing characteristic dark absorption bands that vary depending on the specific type of blood pigment present.

**II. PROCEDURE FOR BLOOD STAINS**

- A suspected blood stain is dissolved in water, normal saline, or dilute ammonia.
- The extract is placed in a small glass test tube and kept directly between the spectroscope and the light source.
- **Sensitivity:** It is highly sensitive, requiring less than 0.1 mg of blood to yield a positive result.

---

**III. 🥇 HIGH-YIELD SPECTRA OF HAEMOGLOBIN DERIVATIVES (EXAM FOCUS)** Differentiating the absorption bands of various haemoglobin derivatives is a highly tested concept for both biological stain identification and clinical toxicology.

- **Oxyhaemoglobin:** Shows two distinct bands in the yellow region between the Fraunhofer lines D and E. The band nearer to D is about half the breadth of the other and is more defined.
- **Reduced Haemoglobin:** Shows a single broad band lying between D and E.
- **Carboxyhaemoglobin (CO Poisoning):** The spectrum is similar to oxyhaemoglobin, but characteristically remains unchanged after the addition of ammonium sulphide (a reducing agent that would normally alter oxyhaemoglobin). Spectroscopic examination of blood showing these characteristic bands is a primary diagnostic confirmation for Carbon Monoxide (CO) poisoning.
- **Methaemoglobin:** Similar to oxyhaemoglobin but features a third dark band in the red region between C and D, and a fourth indistinct band between E and F.
- **Acid Haematin:** Displays a sharp band between C and D, and a broad, indistinct band between D and F.
- **Alkaline Haematin:** Shows a single band between C and D.
- **Haemochromogen (Reduced Alkaline Haematin):** Displays a dense narrow band midway between D and E, and a pale, broad band over E.
- **Cyanhaemochromogen:** The absorption bands are similar to those of haemochromogen, but are slightly wider.

---

**IV. 🚨 OTHER MEDICOLEGAL APPLICATIONS OF SPECTROSCOPY**

Beyond simple blood stain analysis, specialised forms of spectroscopy are critical in forensic investigations:

**1. Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy (AAS)**

- **Toxicology:** AAS is utilised to detect and quantify heavy metal poisons. For example, it is used to measure the concentration of arsenic deposited in hair, nails, and bones.
- **Forensic Ballistics:** Flameless Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy (FAAS) is highly utilised to detect gunshot primer residues (specifically antimony, barium, and lead) and vaporised copper from the cartridge case or bullet jacket on a suspect's hands. Detecting these residues on the palms and backs of the hands helps confirm whether a person has recently fired a gun.

1. Blood grouping in disputed paternity.
### BLOOD GROUPING IN DISPUTED PATERNITY

**I. MEDICOLEGAL INDICATIONS** Blood grouping is frequently utilised by courts to resolve questions of disputed paternity in the following scenarios:

- A husband denying that he is the father of a child born in lawful marriage.
- A mother accusing a specific man of being the father of her child born out of wedlock (affiliation cases).
- A woman pretending pregnancy and delivery to claim a supposititious child as her own to inherit property.
- In suits for nullity of marriage or divorce.
- To resolve the accidental interchange of newborn babies in hospitals.

**II. CORE PRINCIPLES OF INHERITANCE (🥇 High-Yield)** The application of blood groups to medicolegal problems relies strictly on Mendelian principles of inheritance:

1. A specific blood group antigen (agglutinogen) cannot appear in a child unless it is present in one or both of the parents.
2. If an individual is homozygous for a specific blood group factor, that factor must appear in the blood of all their children.
3. If a child is homozygous for a blood group factor, the gene for that factor must have been inherited from _each_ of its parents.
4. Blood group characteristics are peculiar to the individual and remain completely unchanged throughout life.
5. _ABO Specifics:_ An 'O' parent cannot produce an 'AB' child, and conversely, an 'AB' parent cannot produce an 'O' child.

**III. EXCLUSION OF PATERNITY (🚨 Exam Target)** Blood grouping is primarily an _exclusionary_ tool. Paternity is legally excluded based on two criteria:

- **First-Order Exclusion:** Occurs when the child possesses a blood group gene that is completely absent in both the mother and the putative (alleged) father.
- **Second-Order Exclusion:** Occurs when the putative father is homozygous for a particular blood group gene, but that specific gene is not present in the child in question.

**IV. DIAGNOSTIC LIMITATIONS & PROBABILITY**

- **The Golden Rule:** Blood grouping tests can **only exclude** a certain person as the possible father; they **cannot definitively establish** paternity, but rather only indicate its possibilities.
- If two alleged fathers share the exact same blood group agglutinogen, no positive opinion regarding paternity can be given based on blood groups alone.
- **Exclusion Rates:** Relying on the ABO system alone excludes paternity in only about 18% of cases. Adding the MNSs system raises exclusion to about 60%. Combining red cell antigens, serum proteins, and red cell enzymes yields a 94% exclusion rate, while testing multiple HLA (Human Leucocyte Antigen) systems provides an exclusion rate of 98.5%.

**V. PROCEDURE & SAMPLE COLLECTION**

- **Timing:** Infants should preferably be at least 6 months of age before testing, and strictly not less than 2 months old.
- **Samples:** 5 ml of venous blood is collected in a plain tube from the adults, while 1 ml is collected via heel or earprick from the infant.
- **Crucial Caveat:** Neither party should have received a blood transfusion within the **three months** prior to taking the sample, as this invalidates the results.
- **Protocol:** To ensure legal admissibility and avoid discrepancies, the testing of the mother, child, and putative father must be conducted in the same laboratory, by the exact same person, on the same day, using the same batch of reagents and antisera.

> 💡 **MODERN CLINICAL PEARL:** While classical blood grouping and HLA typing are highly effective for _excluding_ paternity, **DNA fingerprinting (profiling)** has become the modern forensic gold standard, as it can positively fix or completely exclude paternity with **100% certainty**.
1. Hazards of blood transfusion.
 ### HAZARDS OF BLOOD TRANSFUSION

**I. OVERVIEW & INCIDENCE**

- **Incidence:** Adverse transfusion reactions occur in approximately **1% to 2%** of patients receiving blood.
- **Primary Culprits:** The antigens of the **ABO** and **Rh systems** are the most common causes of transfusion reactions, though multiple transfusions can stimulate antibodies against other minor antigens.
- **Cause of Death:** Fatalities typically result from anaphylaxis, acute renal failure, severe electrolyte imbalances, or circulatory overload.

**II. 🥇 HIGH-YIELD CLASSIFICATION OF HAZARDS**

**A. Immunological Reactions**

1. **Intravascular Haemolysis:** Acute, severe destruction of red blood cells within the circulation.
2. **Extravascular Haemolysis:** Delayed destruction of red blood cells in the reticuloendothelial system.
3. **Hypersensitivity:** Allergic or sensitivity reactions targeting donor white blood cells (leucocytes), platelets, and plasma components.

**B. Non-Immunological Reactions**

1. **Transmission of Infections:** Transmission of blood-borne pathogens including HIV (AIDS), Hepatitis, Syphilis, and Toxoplasmosis.
2. **Volume & Metabolic Issues:** Circulatory overload, hyperkalaemia, and citrate toxicity.
3. **Physical Hazards:** Air embolism, hypothermia, and rigors.
4. **Coagulation Defects:** Dilutional coagulopathies.

**III. MEDICOLEGAL PITFALLS (Common Causes of Mismatched Transfusions)** In forensic practice, deaths from mismatched transfusions frequently trace back to human error rather than technical failure. The commonest mistakes include:

1. **Clerical errors**.
2. **Confusing terminology** (e.g., mistakenly writing "group A serum" instead of "anti-B serum").
3. **Failure to verify** the reference on the blood bottle against the actual laboratory compatibility report.
4. **Patient mix-ups** due to similarly named patients in the same ward.

**IV. DIAGNOSTIC INVESTIGATIONS**

- **Haematology:**
    - _Intravascular Haemolysis:_ Serum shows free haemoglobin and methaemalbumin, alongside reduced haptoglobins.
    - _Extravascular Haemolysis:_ Marked by an increase in unconjugated bilirubin.
- **Urine:** Intravascular haemolysis presents with haemoglobinuria, urobilin, urobilinogen, and red cell casts.
- **Serology:** Positive Coomb's test and red cell agglutinates in a 2% saline suspension. In sensitivity reactions to donor plasma/cells, **IgA specific antibodies** and eosinophilia may be found.
- **Bacteriology:** Culture of residual donor blood to confirm the presence of contaminating microorganisms.

**V. 🚨 AUTOPSY FINDINGS**

- **Kidneys:** Acute intravascular haemolysis produces **haemoglobinuric nephrosis**, characterized by acute necrosis of the tubules and haemoglobin casts.
- **Lungs:** Markedly oedematous in cases of death from circulatory overload.
- **Heart:** The presence of air in the right ventricle indicates death due to air embolism.

> 💡 **EXAM PEARL: SAMPLE PRESERVATION PROTOCOL** In suspected transfusion-related deaths, it is absolutely mandatory to preserve:
> 
> 1. The residual sample of the **blood transfused** (donor blood).
> 2. Samples of the **recipient's blood** (both _before_ and _after_ the transfusion).
> 3. At autopsy, the medical officer must collect **blood, urine, and the kidneys** for forensic analysis.
