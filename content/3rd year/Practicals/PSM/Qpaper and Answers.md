### Q1. Epidemiology of Rabies + Management of Dog Bite as per National Rabies Control Program (4+6)

**Epidemiology of Rabies:**

- **Agent:** Rabies virus (Rhabdovirus, genus Lyssavirus), RNA virus
- **Host:** Warm-blooded animals; dogs are responsible for >96% of human cases in India
- **Reservoir:** Dogs (domestic), foxes, bats, jackals
- **Transmission:** Bite/scratch/lick on mucosa or open wound by infected animal
- **Incubation period:** 10 days – 1 year (average 1–3 months)
- **India burden:** ~20,000 deaths/year; highest globally
- **High-risk groups:** Children, veterinarians, dog handlers

**Management of Dog Bite (National Rabies Control Program):**

**Step 1 – Wound Management (immediate):**

- Wash wound with soap and water for 15 minutes
- Apply antiseptic (povidone iodine or alcohol)
- Do NOT suture immediately; if needed, delayed/loose suturing
- Tetanus prophylaxis

**Step 2 – Category Classification:**

|Category|Exposure|Action|
|---|---|---|
|I|Touch/feed animal, licks on intact skin|Wash, no PEP|
|II|Nibbling, minor scratches without bleeding|Wound care + Vaccine|
|III|Transdermal bites, contamination of mucosa, bat exposure|Wound care + Vaccine + RIG|

**Step 3 – Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP):**

- **Vaccine:** Cell culture vaccine (CCV) — Essen regimen: Days 0,3,7,14,28 OR Zagreb: 2-1-1
- **RIG (Rabies Immunoglobulin):** For Category III — 20 IU/kg (Human RIG) or 40 IU/kg (Equine RIG); infiltrate around wound; remainder IM

**Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis:** For high-risk individuals — Days 0, 7, 28

### Q2. Risk Factors + Danger Signals of Cancers in India + Note on Breast Cancer Screening (3+4+3)

**Risk Factors of Cancer in India:**

- Tobacco (oral, lung cancer) — leading cause
- Alcohol consumption
- Infections: HPV (cervical), HBV/HCV (liver), H. pylori (stomach)
- Dietary factors: low fibre, high fat, aflatoxins
- Radiation (UV, ionizing)
- Occupational carcinogens (asbestos, benzene)
- Hormonal factors, obesity
- Genetic predisposition

**Danger Signals of Cancer (7 warning signs – CAUTION):**

1. **C** – Change in bowel/bladder habits
2. **A** – A sore that does not heal
3. **U** – Unusual bleeding or discharge
4. **T** – Thickening or lump in breast or elsewhere
5. **I** – Indigestion or difficulty swallowing
6. **O** – Obvious change in wart or mole
7. **N** – Nagging cough or hoarseness

Additional Indian-specific signals:

- Submucosal fibrosis, leukoplakia (oral precancers)
- Unexplained weight loss, persistent fever

**Breast Cancer Screening:**

- **Breast Self-Examination (BSE):** Monthly, from age 20, 7–10 days after menstruation
- **Clinical Breast Examination (CBE):** By trained health worker, every 1–3 years (age 20–40), annually after 40
- **Mammography:** Gold standard; annually for women 40–74 years
- **USG Breast:** For women <40 or dense breasts
- **National Programme:** Under NPCDCS — CBE offered at health facilities
- Early detection reduces mortality by up to 30%

### Q3. Steps for Investigation of Outbreak of Food Poisoning

1. **Confirm the diagnosis** – clinical features, lab confirmation (stool, vomit, food samples)
2. **Confirm the outbreak** – verify cases exceed expected baseline
3. **Case definition** – define a case (time, place, person)
4. **Case finding** – active search; line listing of all cases
5. **Descriptive epidemiology** – time (epidemic curve), place (spot map), person (attack rates)
6. **Generate hypothesis** – identify suspected food item, source
7. **Analytical study** – cohort study (calculate attack rates); food-specific attack rates
8. **Collect samples** – suspected food, water, stool, vomit, rectal swabs
9. **Control measures:**
    - Remove/destroy suspected food
    - Treat affected persons
    - Improve sanitation, hygiene
10. **Report** – notify health authorities; write outbreak report

### Q4. Epidemiology and Prevention of Hepatitis B

**Epidemiology:**

- **Agent:** HBV — DNA virus, Hepadnavirus family
- **Prevalence:** India — intermediate endemic zone (2–7% HBsAg positive)
- **Transmission:**
    - Parenteral (blood, needles, transfusion)
    - Sexual contact
    - Vertical (mother to child — perinatal)
    - Close household contact
- **Incubation period:** 45–180 days (average 60–90 days)
- **Carriers:** ~40 million in India; major reservoir
- **Complications:** Cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma

**Prevention:**

- **Vaccination (most effective):**
    - 3-dose schedule: 0, 1, 6 months
    - Under UIP: Birth dose + 6, 10, 14 weeks
    - Birth dose within 24 hours crucial to prevent perinatal transmission
- **Blood safety:** Screen all donated blood for HBsAg
- **Safe injection practices:** Use disposable syringes
- **Universal precautions** in healthcare settings
- **HBIG (Hepatitis B Immunoglobulin):** Post-exposure prophylaxis for needle-stick injuries
- **Antenatal screening** of mothers; HBIG + vaccine to neonates of HBsAg+ mothers
- Health education on safe sex, avoid sharing razors/blades

### Q5. Syndromic Management of Urethral Discharge in Males

**Definition:** Syndromic management = treating based on clinical syndrome (symptoms + signs) without waiting for lab results — used in resource-limited settings.

**Syndrome:** Urethral discharge in male

**Common Causes:**

- _Neisseria gonorrhoeae_ (Gonorrhoea)
- _Chlamydia trachomatis_ (Non-gonococcal urethritis)
- Often co-infection present

**Flowchart approach:**

- Patient with urethral discharge/dysuria
- Confirm discharge (milk urethra if needed)
- **Treat for both Gonorrhoea AND Chlamydia simultaneously**

**Treatment (WHO/NACO regimen):**

|Disease|Drug|
|---|---|
|Gonorrhoea|Cefixime 400mg single dose OR Ceftriaxone 250mg IM|
|Chlamydia|Azithromycin 1g single dose OR Doxycycline 100mg BD × 7 days|

**Additional steps:**

- Partner notification and treatment
- Promote condom use
- Counsel on STI/HIV
- Advise abstinence until cured
- Review after 7 days if no improvement

### Q6. Causes of Acute Encephalitic Syndrome (AES) including Japanese Encephalitis + Management in India

**Definition of AES:** Acute onset of fever with change in mental status (confusion, disorientation, coma) or new onset of seizures in a person of any age.

**Causes of AES in India:**

- **Viral:** Japanese Encephalitis (JE), Herpes simplex, Nipah, Chandipura, Dengue, Enteroviruses
- **Bacterial:** TB meningitis, bacterial meningitis
- **Parasitic:** Cerebral malaria, neurocysticercosis
- **Metabolic:** Hypoglycemia, hyponatremia (especially in children — UP belt)
- **Toxic:** Organophosphate poisoning, litchi-associated AES (hypoglycin A)
- **Non-infectious:** ADEM, autoimmune encephalitis

**Japanese Encephalitis specifically:**

- Flavivirus; vector — _Culex tritaeniorhynchus_ mosquito
- Reservoir: Pigs, wading birds
- Seasonal — peak during/after monsoon
- Endemic in UP, Bihar, Assam, West Bengal
- CFR: 20–30%; 30–50% survivors have neurological sequelae

**Management in India:**

**Supportive (mainstay):**

- Maintain airway, breathing, circulation
- Control seizures: IV Diazepam → Phenytoin
- Reduce fever: Paracetamol, tepid sponging
- Treat raised ICP: head elevation, Mannitol
- Correct hypoglycemia (especially in UP AES)
- Nutritional support, prevent bedsores

**Specific:**

- JE — No specific antiviral; supportive only
- HSV encephalitis — IV Acyclovir 10mg/kg 8 hourly × 14–21 days
- Bacterial: Ceftriaxone
- Cerebral malaria: Artesunate IV

**Prevention of JE:**

- **JE Vaccination** — SA-14-14-2 live attenuated vaccine; under UIP in endemic states
- Vector control (larval + adult mosquito)
- Pig husbandry away from human settlements
- Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme (IDSP) reporting

### Q7. Epidemiology and Control of Kyasanur Forest Disease (KFD)

**Epidemiology:**

- **Agent:** KFD virus — Flavivirus (RNA virus)
- **Vector:** Hard tick — _Haemaphysalis spinigera_
- **Reservoir:** Rodents, monkeys (langurs, bonnet macaques)
- **Sentinel event:** Death of monkeys signals human risk
- **Geographic distribution:** Karnataka (Kyasanur Forest, Shivamogga district); spreading to Goa, Kerala, Tamil Nadu
- **Seasonality:** November to June (dry season — tick activity peaks)
- **Transmission:** Tick bite; contact with blood/tissues of infected animals; lab exposure
- **Incubation:** 3–8 days
- **Clinical:** Biphasic fever, hemorrhagic manifestations, neurological involvement in second phase
- **CFR:** 3–5%
- **High-risk:** Forest workers, cattle grazers, farmers

**Control Measures:**

- **Vaccination:** Formalin-inactivated KFD vaccine — given to high-risk population in endemic areas (2 doses + booster)
- **Tick control:** Acaricides in forests; protective clothing
- **Personal protection:** Repellents, boots, full sleeve clothing for forest workers
- **Surveillance:** Monitor monkey deaths; report to health authorities
- **Health education:** Awareness in endemic communities
- **Case management:** Supportive treatment; no specific antiviral
- **Notification:** Under IDSP

### Q8. Preventable Blindness

**Definition:** Blindness that can be prevented or treated — accounts for ~80% of all blindness.

**Magnitude:** ~12 million blind in India; leading cause globally is cataract.

**Causes and Prevention:**

|Cause|Prevention/Control|
|---|---|
|Cataract (major cause ~66%)|Cataract surgery (IOL implantation) — NPCB|
|Refractive errors|Spectacle correction|
|Glaucoma|IOP screening, timely surgery/drugs|
|Diabetic retinopathy|Diabetes control, laser photocoagulation|
|Trachoma|SAFE strategy (Surgery, Antibiotics, Face washing, Environmental improvement)|
|Vitamin A deficiency (children)|Vitamin A supplementation (UIP), diet|
|Corneal blindness|Avoid traditional eye remedies; eye banking|
|Onchocerciasis|Ivermectin mass treatment, vector control|

**National Programme for Control of Blindness (NPCB):**

- Target: Reduce prevalence from 1% to 0.3%
- Free cataract surgeries
- School eye screening
- Low vision services
- Training of ophthalmic assistants

### Q9. Prevention of Road Traffic Accidents (RTAs)

**Magnitude:** India — ~1.5 lakh deaths/year; leading cause of injury death in 15–49 age group.

**Haddon Matrix Approach (Host, Agent, Environment):**

**Pre-crash (Prevention):**

- Seat belts, helmets (mandatory by Motor Vehicles Act)
- Speed limits and enforcement
- Drunk driving laws — BAC limit 0.03%
- Roadworthy vehicles — regular fitness checks
- Road design: proper lighting, signs, dividers
- Driver training and licensing reform
- Ban on mobile phone use while driving

**Crash (Injury reduction):**

- Airbags, crumple zones in vehicles
- Crash barriers on highways
- Speed breakers near schools/hospitals

**Post-crash (Reducing mortality):**

- Golden Hour concept — rapid emergency response
- **108 ambulance services**
- Good Samaritan Law — protects bystanders who help
- Trauma care centers on highways
- Blood banks, trauma ICUs

**Legislation:** Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act 2019 — increased penalties for violations.

### Q10. Risk Factors for Coronary Heart Disease (CHD) with Examples

**Classification:**

**Non-modifiable Risk Factors:**

- Age (men >45, women >55)
- Sex (males more prone; females protected pre-menopause by estrogen)
- Family history/genetics
- Race/ethnicity

**Modifiable Risk Factors:**

|Risk Factor|Example/Detail|
|---|---|
|Hypertension|BP >140/90 mmHg damages arterial walls|
|Dyslipidemia|High LDL, low HDL, high triglycerides|
|Diabetes mellitus|Accelerates atherosclerosis|
|Smoking/tobacco|Nicotine causes vasoconstriction, CO reduces O₂|
|Obesity|BMI >30; abdominal obesity (waist >90cm men, >80cm women)|
|Physical inactivity|Sedentary lifestyle increases CHD risk 2×|
|Unhealthy diet|High saturated fat, salt, low fibre|
|Stress|Increases cortisol, BP, platelet aggregation|
|Alcohol|Excess intake raises BP, triglycerides|

**Emerging Risk Factors:**

- Homocysteine, CRP (inflammation), sleep apnea, air pollution

**Prevention Strategy:** NPCDCS (National Programme for Prevention and Control of Cancer, Diabetes, CVD and Stroke) addresses all modifiable risk factors through population-based screening.

### Q11. Prevention of Tetanus

- **Immunization (most important):**
    - DPT at 6, 10, 14 weeks + booster at 16–24 months, 5 years (DT), 10 years (TT)
    - TT for pregnant women: 2 doses (TT1, TT2) or TT-booster
    - Td vaccine replacing TT in UIP
- **Wound management:** Thorough cleaning, debridement of devitalized tissue; avoid anaerobic conditions
- **Post-exposure:** TT + TIG (Tetanus Immunoglobulin 250–500 IU IM) for unimmunized/wound cases
- **Neonatal tetanus prevention:** Clean delivery, clean cord cutting, cord care

### Q12. Control Measures of Typhoid

- **Water safety:** Chlorination, boiling drinking water, safe water supply
- **Sanitation:** Proper sewage disposal, ODF
- **Food hygiene:** Safe food handling, avoid street food, wash hands
- **Case management:** Antibiotics — Ciprofloxacin, Azithromycin, Ceftriaxone (for MDR strains)
- **Carrier detection and treatment:** Identify chronic carriers (>1 year); treat with Ciprofloxacin 4–6 weeks
- **Vaccination:**
    - Vi polysaccharide vaccine (injectable) — single dose, 2 years+
    - Ty21a oral live vaccine — 3 doses
    - Typhoid Conjugate Vaccine (TCV) — preferred; introduced in India's UIP
- **Health education:** Hand hygiene (5 moments)

### Q13. Emerging and Re-emerging Diseases with Examples

**Emerging Diseases:** Diseases that have newly appeared in a population or have existed but are rapidly increasing in incidence/geographic range.

**Examples:**

- COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2) — emerged 2019
- Nipah virus — Kerala outbreaks
- MERS-CoV
- Zika virus
- Monkeypox (Mpox)

**Re-emerging Diseases:** Previously controlled diseases that are reappearing.

**Examples:**

- Dengue — resurgence in India
- Tuberculosis (especially MDR-TB)
- Cholera
- Plague (Surat, 1994)
- Diphtheria — in unvaccinated pockets

**Reasons for emergence:**

- Microbial adaptation/mutation
- Climate change, deforestation
- International travel
- Antibiotic resistance
- Immunocompromised hosts (HIV)
- Breakdown of public health measures

### Q14. Stroke Control Strategy in India

**Under NPCDCS and National Stroke Programme:**

- **Primordial/Primary Prevention:**
    - Control hypertension (major risk factor)
    - Manage diabetes, dyslipidemia
    - Anti-smoking campaigns
    - Promote physical activity, healthy diet (low salt)
- **Secondary Prevention (after TIA/minor stroke):**
    - Antiplatelet therapy (Aspirin + Clopidogrel)
    - Anticoagulants for AF-related stroke
    - Statin therapy
    - BP control
- **Acute Management:**
    - Thrombolysis with IV rtPA within 4.5 hours (ischemic stroke)
    - Mechanical thrombectomy within 24 hours
    - Stroke units in district hospitals
- **Rehabilitation:**
    - Physiotherapy, speech therapy, occupational therapy
    - Community-based rehabilitation
- **F.A.S.T. campaign:** Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call 108

### Q15. Self-Care in Diabetes

- **Diet:** Low glycemic index foods; reduce refined carbs, sugars; increase fibre; portion control; regular meal times
- **Exercise:** 150 min/week moderate activity; walking 30 min/day; reduces insulin resistance
- **Medication adherence:** Regular oral hypoglycemics/insulin; never skip doses
- **Blood glucose monitoring:** Home glucometer use; maintain target FBS <130, PPBS <180 mg/dL
- **HbA1c monitoring:** Every 3 months; target <7%
- **Foot care:** Daily inspection, proper footwear, avoid barefoot walking, early wound care
- **Eye care:** Annual fundus examination
- **Kidney monitoring:** Annual urine microalbumin, serum creatinine
- **BP and cholesterol control**
- **Regular follow-up** with healthcare provider
- **Quit smoking and alcohol**

### Q16. DASH Diet

**DASH = Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension**

**Key Components:**

- **High in:** Fruits, vegetables, whole grains, low-fat dairy, nuts, legumes, poultry, fish
- **Low in:** Saturated fats, red meat, sweets, sugar-sweetened beverages, sodium

**Targets:**

- Sodium: <2300 mg/day (ideal <1500 mg/day)
- Rich in potassium, calcium, magnesium — all help lower BP

**Benefits:**

- Reduces systolic BP by 8–14 mmHg
- Reduces risk of CVD, stroke, diabetes, kidney disease
- Helps in weight management

**In Indian context:** Emphasize reduction of pickle, papad, excess salt; increase dal, fruits, green vegetables, curd.

### Q17. Tracking of Blood Pressure

- **Regular measurement:** At least once every 5 years for adults; annually if >120/80 mmHg
- **Home BP monitoring (HBPM):** Validated digital BP apparatus; morning and evening readings
- **24-hour Ambulatory BP Monitoring (ABPM):** Gold standard to detect white coat hypertension, masked hypertension, nocturnal dipping
- **Target BP:** <130/80 mmHg (recent guidelines); <140/90 mmHg (standard)
- **WHO HEARTS package:** Simplified protocol for tracking and managing hypertension in primary care
- **SPHYGMO app / digital health tools** for tracking
- **Under NPCDCS:** Community health workers (ASHA/ANM) screen and refer hypertensives
- **30×30 target:** Prevent 30% premature CVD deaths by 2030 — BP tracking is central

### Q18. Nosocomial Infections (Healthcare-Associated Infections) with Examples

**Definition:** Infections acquired in a healthcare facility that were not present or incubating at the time of admission; typically manifest ≥48 hours after admission.

**Examples:**

- **CAUTI** — Catheter-Associated Urinary Tract Infection (_E. coli, Klebsiella_)
- **CLABSI** — Central Line-Associated Blood Stream Infection (_Staph. aureus, MRSA_)
- **VAP** — Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia (_Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter_)
- **SSI** — Surgical Site Infection (_Staph. aureus, Streptococcus_)
- **C. difficile** colitis — after antibiotic use

**Prevention:**

- Hand hygiene (WHO's 5 moments)
- Bundle care for lines and catheters
- Sterilization and disinfection
- Antibiotic stewardship
- Isolation of infected patients
- Surveillance by Infection Control Committee

### Q19. Prevention of Rheumatic Heart Disease (RHD)

**Primary Prevention (most important):**

- Prompt treatment of Group A Streptococcal (GAS) pharyngitis with **Benzathine Penicillin G** (single IM dose: 1.2 MU adults, 0.6 MU children <27kg)
- Alternatives: Oral Amoxicillin 10 days, or Azithromycin (if penicillin allergy)
- Reduces first attack of Acute Rheumatic Fever (ARF)

**Secondary Prevention (critical):**

- Long-term **Benzathine Penicillin G prophylaxis** every 3–4 weeks
- Duration:
    - No carditis: 5 years or till age 18
    - Mild carditis: 10 years or till age 25
    - Severe carditis/valvular disease: lifelong
- Prevents recurrent ARF and progressive valve damage

**Tertiary Prevention:**

- Surgical correction of valvular defects (valve repair/replacement)
- Infective endocarditis prophylaxis before dental procedures

**National Programme:** RHD is targeted under National Programme for Prevention and Control of RF/RHD — school-based throat infection surveillance.

### Q20. National Deworming Day and its Importance

**National Deworming Day (NDD):**

- **Date:** 10th February (twice yearly — February and August in high-burden states)
- **Target:** Children aged 1–19 years (preschool and school-age)
- **Drug:** Single dose **Albendazole** (400 mg for ≥2 years; 200 mg for 1–2 years)
- **Implementing ministry:** Ministry of Health & Family Welfare
- **Platform:** Schools and Anganwadi centres

**Importance:**

- Controls Soil-Transmitted Helminthiasis (STH): Roundworm, Hookworm, Whipworm
- **Reduces anemia** — improves hemoglobin levels
- **Improves nutritional status** — better absorption of nutrients
- **Improves cognitive development** and school performance
- **Reduces school absenteeism**
- Breaks transmission cycle in community
- India's NDD is **world's largest deworming programme** — covers ~24 crore children
