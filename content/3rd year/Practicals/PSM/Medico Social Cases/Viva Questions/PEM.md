**PROTEIN-ENERGY MALNUTRITION (PEM) VIVA: HIGH-YIELD EXAM NOTES**

**I. PUBLIC HEALTH BURDEN & EPIDEMIOLOGY**

- **Magnitude in India:** PEM is a major health and nutrition problem, particularly in the first years of life. The problem exists in all the States of India, and nutritional marasmus is more frequent than kwashiorkor.
- **The "Food Gap":** The chief cause of PEM is an insufficiency of food, commonly referred to as the "food gap".
- **Severity:** The great majority (nearly 80%) of PEM cases are mild and moderate. The incidence of severe cases is 1 to 2% in preschool-age children.
- **Mortality:** PEM is estimated to be an underlying cause in 30% of deaths among children under age 5. Children who are severely wasted are 9 times more likely to die than well-nourished children.

**II. CLINICAL FORMS OF SEVERE PEM** Kwashiorkor and Marasmus are the two different forms of protein and energy malnutrition:

- **Kwashiorkor:** A severe form of undernutrition developing in individuals on diets with a low protein/energy ratio (inadequate protein intake).
    - _Main symptoms:_ Oedema, wasting, liver enlargement, hypoalbuminaemia, steatosis (fatty liver), and possible depigmentation of skin and hair.
- **Marasmus:** Caused by an inadequate intake of _both_ protein and energy.
    - _Main symptoms:_ Severe cachexia with weight loss, severe muscle wasting, minimal subcutaneous fat, little or no oedema, and non-normal serum albumin levels.

**III. ASSESSMENT AND EARLY DETECTION**

- **First Indicator:** The first indicator of PEM is under-weight for age.
- **Growth Charts:** The most practical method to detect this in the field is by maintaining growth charts, which indicate at a glance whether the child is gaining or losing weight.
- **Mid-Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC):** It yields a reliable estimate of muscle mass and can be used between ages 1 and 5 years.
    - > 13.5 cm: Satisfactory nutritional status.
        
    - 12.5 cm to 13.5 cm: Mild-moderate malnutrition.
    - < 12.5 cm: Severe malnutrition.

**IV. SEVERE ACUTE MALNUTRITION (SAM) DIAGNOSIS & MANAGEMENT**

- **Diagnosis of SAM:** Defined by a weight-for-height/length Z-score below -3SD of the WHO child growth standards, a MUAC < 115 mm, or the presence of nutritional oedema.
- **Hospital-Based Management Principles:** The majority of deaths occur within 24 hours of admission; therefore, triage and emergency treatment (ABCD steps: Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Coma, Convulsion, Dehydration) are critical. Management is divided into 3 phases:
    1. **Stabilization Phase (1-2 days):** Intended to stabilize the child. Uses a "Starter diet" to promote recovery of normal metabolic function and electrolyte balance.
    2. **Transition Phase (2-3 days):** Intended to ensure the child is clinically stable and can tolerate an increased energy/protein intake. Uses a "Catch up diet".
    3. **Rehabilitation Phase:** Aimed at promoting rapid weight gain, stimulating emotional/physical development, and preparing the child for normal feeding at home.

**V. PREVENTION AND SOCIAL MEASURES** Based on the WHO Expert Committee on Nutrition, preventive measures include:

- **Health Promotion:** Education directed at pregnant and lactating women, promotion of breast-feeding, development of low-cost weaning foods, family planning and spacing of births, and improvement of the family diet.
- **Specific Protection:** Provision of a diet rich in protein and energy (milk, eggs, fresh fruits), food fortification, and immunization.
- **Early Diagnosis and Treatment:** Periodic nutritional surveillance, early diagnosis of lag in growth, early management of infections and diarrhoea (ORS), and deworming of heavily infested children.
- **Rehabilitation:** Nutritional rehabilitation services (e.g., NRCs) and follow-up care.

**PROBABLE VIVA QUESTIONS & ANSWERS (PSM)**

**Q1: What are the three standard anthropometric indices used to assess undernutrition in children?** _Answer:_ The three indices are Weight-for-Age (underweight), Length/Height-for-Age (stunting), and Weight-for-Length/Height (wasting).

**Q2: How do you differentiate between Wasting and Stunting?** _Answer:_ Stunting (low height-for-age) is an indicator of _chronic_ malnutrition resulting from failure to receive adequate nutrition over a long period. Wasting (low weight-for-height) indicates _acute_ malnutrition representing a recent failure to receive adequate nutrition, often affected by recent episodes of diarrhoea or acute illness.

**Q3: What are the diagnostic criteria for Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM)?** _Answer:_ A child is diagnosed with SAM if they have a weight-for-height/length Z-score below -3 Standard Deviations (-3SD) of the WHO standards, a mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) of less than 115 mm, or the presence of nutritional oedema.

**Q4: Differentiate clinically between Kwashiorkor and Marasmus.** _Answer:_ Kwashiorkor is characterized by oedema, liver enlargement, hypoalbuminaemia, and skin/hair changes due to low protein intake. Marasmus is severe cachexia with severe muscle wasting, minimal subcutaneous fat, and little or no oedema due to inadequate intake of both protein and energy.

**Q5: What are the 3 phases of managing a child with Severe Acute Malnutrition in a Nutritional Rehabilitation Centre (NRC)?** _Answer:_ The principles of management are based on 3 phases:

1. Stabilization phase (treating medical complications and starting feeding).
2. Transition phase (gradually increasing energy and protein intake).
3. Rehabilitative phase (promoting rapid weight gain and development).

**Q6: What is the "food gap" in the context of malnutrition?** _Answer:_ The "food gap" refers to the insufficiency of food intake, which is the chief underlying cause of Protein-Energy Malnutrition (PEM), particularly in the first years of life.

**Q7: Which form of severe PEM is more frequently seen in India?** _Answer:_ Nutritional marasmus is more frequent than kwashiorkor in India.
