1. Write modifiable risk factors for hypertension
	**MODIFIABLE RISK FACTORS FOR HYPERTENSION**
	
	Hypertension is a multifactorial disease. The modifiable risk factors—which are amenable to intervention and lifestyle changes—are classified as follows:
	
	**1. OBESITY**
	
	- **Direct Correlation:** Epidemiological observations strongly identify obesity as a major risk factor for hypertension.
	- **Central Adiposity:** "Central obesity" or abdominal fat distribution, indicated by an increased waist-to-hip ratio, is positively correlated with high blood pressure.
	- **Weight Reduction:** The greater the weight gain, the higher the risk; conversely, losing weight generally decreases blood pressure.
	
	**2. DIETARY FACTORS**
	
	- **Salt (Sodium) Intake:** A high sodium intake (7–8 g per day) proportionately increases blood pressure. Low sodium intake has been proven to lower blood pressure. Essential hypertensives may have a genetic abnormality making salt excretion difficult except at raised arterial pressures.
	- **Potassium:** Potassium antagonizes the biological effects of sodium and reduces blood pressure. Low dietary potassium is implicated in the aetiology of hypertension.
	- **Other Cations:** Adequate intake of calcium and magnesium is also suggested to be important in reducing blood pressure levels.
	- **Saturated Fats:** High intake of saturated fats raises blood pressure as well as serum cholesterol.
	- **Dietary Fibre:** The risk of hypertension is inversely related to the consumption of dietary fibre.
	
	**3. ALCOHOL CONSUMPTION**
	
	- High alcohol intake is associated with an increased risk of high blood pressure.
	- Alcohol consumption raises systolic pressure more than diastolic pressure.
	- Elevations may not be fixed; blood pressure often returns to normal with abstinence.
	
	**4. PHYSICAL INACTIVITY**
	
	- A sedentary lifestyle promotes weight gain, which has an indirect but significant effect on elevating blood pressure.
	
	**5. ENVIRONMENTAL STRESS**
	
	- Psychosocial factors and emotional stress operate through mental processes to produce hypertension.
	- Stress triggers sympathetic nervous system over-activity (reflected by significantly higher noradrenaline levels in young hypertensives), playing an important role in the pathogenesis of the disease.
	
	**6. SOCIO-ECONOMIC STATUS (SES)**
	
	- **Post-transitional societies (Developed):** Consistently higher levels of blood pressure are noted in lower socio-economic groups, showing an inverse relation with education and income.
	- **Transitional societies (Developing):** Higher prevalence of hypertension is noted in upper socio-economic groups, representing the initial stages of the cardiovascular disease epidemic.
	
	**7. HEART RATE**
	
	- Untreated hypertensive subjects invariably exhibit a higher resting heart rate compared to normotensives. This may reflect a resetting of sympathetic activity at a higher level.
	
	**8. OTHER FACTORS**
	
	- **Oral Contraceptives:** The oestrogen component in combined oral contraceptives is the commonest present cause of secondary hypertension.
	- **Environmental hazards:** Factors such as continuous exposure to noise, vibration, temperature, and humidity are recognized as contributing variables requiring further investigation.

1. What is tracking of blood pressure?
**TRACKING OF BLOOD PRESSURE**

**1. DEFINITION AND CONCEPT**

- Tracking of blood pressure refers to the phenomenon of persistence of rank order of blood pressure over time.
- If the blood pressure levels of individuals are followed over a period of years from early childhood into adult life, those individuals whose pressures were initially high in the distribution will probably continue to be in the same high rank as adults.
- In simpler terms, low blood pressure levels tend to remain low, and high blood pressure levels tend to become higher as individuals grow older.

**2. PUBLIC HEALTH SIGNIFICANCE (APPLICATION)**

- **Identifying High-Risk Groups:** This knowledge is practically applied in identifying children and adolescents who are "at risk" of developing hypertension at a future date.
- **High-Risk Strategy:** In the primary prevention of hypertension, the "tracking" of blood pressure from childhood, along with a family history of hypertension, is actively used to screen and identify susceptible individuals for early preventive care.

3. **MODIFIABLE RISK FACTORS FOR HYPERTENSION**
	
	Essential hypertension is a multifactorial disease. The modifiable risk factors are those amenable to lifestyle and medical interventions. They play a major role in the pathogenesis and progression of the disease.
	
	**1. OBESITY**
	
	- **Direct Correlation:** Epidemiological studies identify obesity as a major risk factor. The greater the weight gain, the greater the risk of high blood pressure.
	- **Central Obesity:** An increased waist-to-hip ratio (indicating abdominal or central adiposity) is positively and strongly correlated with high blood pressure.
	- **Reversibility:** When individuals with high blood pressure lose weight, their blood pressure generally decreases.
	
	**2. SALT (SODIUM) INTAKE**
	
	- **High Intake:** A high sodium intake (7–8 g per day) increases blood pressure proportionately.
	- **Low Intake:** Primitive societies ingesting less than 60 mmol/day have virtually no hypertension, whereas populations with high intake (e.g., >400 mmol/day in Japan) have a very high incidence.
	
	**3. POTASSIUM AND OTHER MINERALS**
	
	- **Potassium:** Low dietary potassium is implicated in the aetiology of hypertension. Potassium antagonizes the biological effects of sodium, and supplements can lower blood pressure in mild to moderate hypertensives.
	- **Other Cations:** Elements such as calcium, magnesium, and cadmium are also suggested to be important in reducing blood pressure levels.
	
	**4. DIETARY FATS AND FIBRE**
	
	- **Saturated Fats:** Diets rich in saturated fats are known to raise blood pressure as well as serum cholesterol levels.
	- **Dietary Fibre:** The consumption of dietary fibre is inversely related to the risk of hypertension and coronary heart disease, as it reduces plasma total and LDL cholesterol.
	
	**5. ALCOHOL CONSUMPTION**
	
	- High alcohol intake increases the risk of high blood pressure.
	- It raises the systolic pressure more significantly than the diastolic pressure.
	- This elevation may not be fixed, as blood pressure often returns to normal with abstinence.
	
	**6. PHYSICAL INACTIVITY**
	
	- A sedentary lifestyle promotes weight gain.
	- Lack of physical activity has an indirect but strong effect on raising blood pressure by contributing to obesity.
	
	**7. ENVIRONMENTAL AND PSYCHOSOCIAL STRESS**
	
	- The term "hypertension" inherently implies a disorder initiated by tension or stress.
	- Psychosocial factors and emotional stress trigger sympathetic nervous system over-activity (indicated by significantly higher noradrenaline levels in hypertensives), which plays a crucial role in pathogenesis.
	
	**8. HEART RATE**
	
	- Hypertensive subjects invariably exhibit a higher heart rate compared to matched normotensive subjects.
	- This reflects a resetting of sympathetic activity at a higher level.
	
	**9. SOCIO-ECONOMIC STATUS (SES)**
	
	- **Transitional/Developing Societies:** Higher prevalence is seen in the upper socio-economic groups, representing the initial stage of the cardiovascular disease epidemic.
	- **Post-transitional/Developed Societies:** Higher levels of blood pressure are consistently noted in the lower socio-economic groups (inversely related to income, occupation, and education).
	
	**10. OTHER FACTORS**
	
	- **Oral Contraceptives:** The oestrogen component in combined oral contraceptive pills is the commonest present cause of secondary hypertension.
	- **Physical Environment:** Factors such as continuous exposure to noise, vibration, temperature, and humidity are also contributing variables that require further investigation.

	1. Secondary prevention of hypertension.
	**SECONDARY PREVENTION OF HYPERTENSION**
	
	**1. GOAL AND RATIONALE**
	
	- **Primary Aim:** To detect and control high blood pressure in affected individuals.
	- **Rationale:** Modern anti-hypertensive drug therapy effectively reduces high blood pressure and thereby averts the excess risk of morbidity and mortality from severe complications like coronary heart disease, cerebrovascular disease (stroke), and kidney disease.
	
	**2. KEY COMPONENTS OF SECONDARY PREVENTION** The control measures are broadly divided into three main pillars:
	
	**A. Early Case Detection**
	
	- **The "Iceberg" Challenge:** High blood pressure is an insidious disease that rarely causes symptoms until target organ damage has already occurred; hence, early detection is a major public health challenge.
	- **Screening:** The only effective method of diagnosing hidden hypertension is by screening the population.
	- **Opportunistic Screening:** Measuring blood pressure at every routine contact with the health service (e.g., at least once every 2 years) is highly effective in detecting those in need of care.
	- **Pre-requisite for Screening:** Screening is a fruitless exercise if it is not linked to sustained follow-up care. It should never be initiated if the health resources for adequate treatment and follow-up are lacking.
	
	**B. Treatment**
	
	- **Concept:** In essential hypertension, the exact cause is unknown, so treatment is aimed at scaling down the high blood pressure to acceptable biological levels.
	- **Target Blood Pressure (Exam Essential):** The strict aim of treatment should be to maintain a blood pressure below **140/90 mm Hg**, and ideally at **120/80 mm Hg**.
	- **Comprehensive Risk Management:** Care of hypertensive patients must not be limited to just lowering blood pressure; it must also involve strict attention to modifying other co-existing risk factors such as smoking and elevated serum cholesterol levels.
	
	**C. Patient Compliance**
	
	- **Definition:** It is defined as the extent to which patient behaviour (in terms of taking prescribed medicines, following recommended diets, or executing other lifestyle changes) strictly coincides with clinical prescription.
	- **The Challenge:** The treatment of high blood pressure is normally a life-long affair, which presents significant problems regarding continuous patient adherence and compliance.
	- **Improvement Strategy:** Compliance rates can be significantly improved through targeted and sustained health education directed at patients, their families, and the broader community.
