1. Rule of halves in HTN
	**"RULE OF HALVES" IN HYPERTENSION**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION & CONCEPT**
	
	- Hypertension is a classic example of an **"iceberg disease"** in the community.
	- The "Rule of Halves" became evident in the early 1970s to describe the massive burden of hidden, untreated, and inadequately managed hypertension in the general population.
	
	**2. THE RULE OF HALVES DEFINED (EXAM ESSENTIAL)** The rule states that in any given population of hypertensive individuals:
	
	- Only **half (50%)** of the hypertensive subjects are **aware** of their condition (the other half remain undiagnosed).
	- Of those who are aware (diagnosed), only **half (50%)** are actually **being treated**.
	- Of those who are receiving treatment, only **half (50%)** are **adequately treated** (i.e., their blood pressure is effectively controlled).
	
	**3. SEQUENTIAL BREAKDOWN IN THE COMMUNITY** Based on this rule and population studies, the community can be systematically divided into the following categories to understand the drop-off in care:
	
	1. The whole community.
	2. Normotensive subjects vs. Hypertensive subjects.
	3. Undiagnosed hypertension vs. Diagnosed hypertension.
	4. Diagnosed but untreated vs. Diagnosed and treated.
	5. Inadequately treated vs. Adequately treated.
	
	**4. PUBLIC HEALTH IMPLICATIONS**
	
	- **Developing Countries:** The original rule was based on data from developed countries with highly advanced medical services. In developing countries, the proportion of hypertensive patients who are diagnosed, treated, and adequately controlled is expected to be _far less_ than half.
	- **Need for Screening:** Because high blood pressure rarely causes symptoms until organ damage occurs, the rule highlights the absolute necessity of routine blood pressure screening to uncover the "hidden" portion of the iceberg.
	- **Need for Sustained Care:** Discovering the cases is only the first step; the sharp drop-off between being treated and being _adequately treated_ emphasizes the need for patient compliance, health education, and sustained follow-up care.

	**RISK FACTORS FOR HYPERTENSION**
	
	Hypertension is a multifactorial disease. The risk factors can be broadly classified into two categories: non-modifiable and modifiable.
	
	**1. NON-MODIFIABLE RISK FACTORS**
	
	- **Age:** Blood pressure rises with age in both sexes, representing an accumulation of environmental influences and genetic senescence,.
	- **Sex:** Men display higher average blood pressure levels in young and middle-aged adulthood. Late in life, the pattern may reverse, often attributed to post-menopausal changes in women.
	- **Genetic Factors:** Inheritance is polygenic. Children of two normotensive parents have a 3% risk of developing hypertension, whereas the risk increases to 45% if both parents are hypertensive. Monozygotic twins show stronger correlation in blood pressure values than dizygotic twins.
	- **Ethnicity:** Black communities consistently reveal higher blood pressure levels compared to other ethnic groups.
	
	**2. MODIFIABLE RISK FACTORS**
	
	- **Obesity:** Central or abdominal obesity (indicated by increased waist-to-hip ratio) is strongly correlated with high blood pressure. Weight gain directly increases the risk.
	- **Salt Intake:** A high sodium intake (7-8 g per day) increases blood pressure. Low dietary potassium, calcium, and magnesium are also implicated in the aetiology of hypertension,.
	- **Saturated Fat & Dietary Fibre:** Saturated fats raise blood pressure and serum cholesterol, while dietary fibre shows an inverse relationship and helps reduce blood pressure.
	- **Alcohol:** High alcohol intake raises systolic pressure more than diastolic. Abstinence helps return blood pressure to normal,.
	- **Physical Inactivity:** Sedentary lifestyles promote weight gain, having a direct and indirect effect on elevating blood pressure.
	- **Environmental Stress:** Psychosocial factors and emotional stress trigger sympathetic over-activity (reflected by higher noradrenaline levels), causing hypertension.
	- **Socio-economic Status:** In post-transitional societies, hypertension is higher in lower socio-economic groups. However, in developing/transitional societies, it is more prevalent in the upper socio-economic groups.
	- **Other Factors:** The oestrogen component in combined oral contraceptives is a common cause of secondary hypertension. Heart rate, noise, and vibration are other contributing factors,.

	**PREVENTIVE MEASURES FOR HYPERTENSION**
	
	The WHO recommends the following approaches for the prevention of hypertension,:
	
	**I. PRIMARY PREVENTION** The goal is to reduce the incidence of hypertension by preventing the onset of risk factors.
	
	**A. Population Strategy** Aimed at shifting the entire community's blood pressure distribution towards "biological normality",. It involves multifactorial non-pharmacological interventions:
	
	- **Nutrition (DASH Diet):** Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) recommends a diet rich in fruits, vegetables, and low-fat dairy. Salt intake must be restricted to under 5 g/day (or <100 mEq/day of sodium),.
	- **Weight Reduction:** Maintaining a normal Body Mass Index (BMI) between 18.5 and 24.9 is crucial,.
	- **Exercise Promotion:** Regular aerobic physical activity, such as brisk walking for at least 30 minutes a day, most days of the week,.
	- **Behavioural Changes & Alcohol Moderation:** Limiting alcohol (s 2 drinks/day for men, s 1 drink/day for women), quitting smoking, stress reduction, and practicing yoga/meditation,.
	- **Health Education & Self-Care:** Educating the community on lifestyle changes and encouraging patients to self-monitor and log their blood pressure,.
	
	**B. High-Risk Strategy**
	
	- This approach targets individuals who are highly susceptible to hypertension.
	- High-risk individuals (e.g., those with a strong family history or childhood "tracking" of high blood pressure) are identified early via clinical screening, to prevent their blood pressure from reaching treatment-requiring levels,,.
	
	**II. SECONDARY PREVENTION** Aimed at detecting and controlling high blood pressure to prevent severe complications like stroke, coronary heart disease, and renal failure,.
	
	- **Early Case Detection:** Routine screening of the population, as hypertension is an asymptomatic "iceberg" disease.
	- **Treatment:** Scaling down blood pressure to below 140/90 mm Hg (ideally 120/80 mm Hg) using modern anti-hypertensive drug therapy,.
	- **Patient Compliance:** Treatment is usually life-long; thus, patient education is paramount to ensure strict adherence to prescribed medical and lifestyle regimens.

3. What are the non-modifiable risk factors for hypertension?
	**NON-MODIFIABLE RISK FACTORS FOR HYPERTENSION**
	
	Essential hypertension is a multifactorial condition with its own set of risk factors. A WHO Scientific Group classifies the non-modifiable (immutable) risk factors as follows:
	
	**1. AGE**
	
	- Blood pressure rises with advancing age in both sexes.
	- The rise is greater in those who already have a higher initial blood pressure.
	- Age represents an accumulation of environmental influences as well as the effects of genetically programmed senescence in the body's systems.
	
	**2. SEX**
	
	- **Early life:** There is little evidence of a difference in blood pressure between the sexes.
	- **Adolescence to Middle Age:** Men display a higher average level. This difference is most evident in young and middle-aged adults.
	- **Late life:** The difference between sexes narrows and the pattern may even be reversed, which is thought to be due to post-menopausal changes in women.
	
	**3. GENETIC FACTORS (HEREDITY)**
	
	- There is considerable evidence that blood pressure levels are determined in part by genetic factors, and the pattern of inheritance is polygenic.
	- **Twin Studies:** The blood pressure values of monozygotic (identical) twins are usually more strongly correlated than those of zygotic twins.
	- **Family Studies (Exam Essential):**
	    - Children of two normotensive parents have only a **3 per cent** possibility of developing hypertension.
	    - Children of two hypertensive parents have a **45 per cent** possibility of developing hypertension.
	- Blood pressure levels among first-degree adult relatives are noted to be statistically significant.
	
	**4. ETHNICITY**
	
	- Population studies have consistently revealed higher blood pressure levels in black communities than in other ethnic groups.
	- Black Americans of African origin have been demonstrated to have higher blood pressure levels than whites.
	- The average difference in blood pressure varies from slightly less than 5 mm Hg during the second decade of life to nearly 20 mm Hg during the sixth decade.
