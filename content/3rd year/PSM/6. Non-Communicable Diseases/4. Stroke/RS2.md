1. Describe the natural history of stroke. What measures can be taken at the community level to control it? 
	**NATURAL HISTORY OF STROKE**
	
	**1. DEFINITION AND ONSET**
	
	- **Definition:** Stroke (or apoplexy) is defined by the WHO as "rapidly developed clinical signs of focal disturbance of cerebral function; lasting more than 24 hours or leading to death, with no apparent cause other than vascular origin".
	- **Precursor State:** Often preceded by Transient Ischaemic Attacks (TIAs), which are episodes of focal, reversible neurological deficits of sudden onset lasting less than 24 hours. TIAs are due to microemboli and serve as a strong warning sign of an impending stroke.
	
	**2. PATHOLOGY AND TYPES** The disturbance of cerebral function is caused by three morphological abnormalities: stenosis, occlusion, or rupture of the arteries. Stroke includes syndromes with differing aetiologies:
	
	- **Ischaemic Stroke:** Includes lacunar infarct, carotid circulation obstruction, and vertebro-basilar obstruction. Cerebral thrombosis is the most frequent form encountered, followed by cerebral embolism.
	- **Haemorrhagic Stroke:** Includes spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage, subarachnoid haemorrhage, intracranial aneurysm, and arteriovenous malformations.
	
	**3. CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS**
	
	- Dysfunction of the brain ("neurological deficit") manifests through various signs related to the extent and site of the involved area.
	- These include coma, hemiplegia (which constitutes the main somatoneurological disorder in about 90 per cent of patients), paraplegia, monoplegia, multiple paralysis, speech disturbances, nerve paresis, and sensory impairment.
	
	**4. PROGRESSION AND OUTCOME (PROGNOSIS)**
	
	- **Acute Phase Mortality:** Stroke patients are at the highest risk of death in the first few weeks after the event. Between 20 to 50 per cent of deaths occur within the first month depending on the type, severity, age, co-morbidity, and treatment.
	- **Recovery Phase:** Survivors may be left with no disability or with mild, moderate, or severe disability. Considerable spontaneous recovery can occur up to about 6 months. By one year, around 60 to 83 per cent of patients achieve independence in self-care.
	- **Recurrence:** Stroke survivors are at a high risk of a subsequent event, estimated at around 10 per cent in the first year and 5 per cent every year thereafter.

	**COMMUNITY LEVEL CONTROL MEASURES FOR STROKE**
	
	The aim of a stroke control programme is to apply effective measures at the community level to prevent stroke, as it is no longer considered an inevitable accompaniment of ageing.
	
	**1. PRIMARY PREVENTION (POPULATION & HIGH-RISK STRATEGY)**
	
	- **Control of Hypertension:** This is given the first priority, as arterial hypertension is the single most important and main cause of stroke.
	- **Lifestyle Modifications:** Promoting healthy dietary habits, regular exercise schedules, yoga, and the avoidance of weight gain.
	- **Substance Control:** Complete elimination of smoking and the consumption of alcohol, as both are established risk factors.
	- **Control of Associated Diseases:** Effective management of diabetes and elevated blood lipids at the population level.
	
	**2. SECONDARY PREVENTION (EARLY DETECTION & TREATMENT)**
	
	- **Management of TIAs:** Since TIAs are one of the earliest manifestations, their early detection and treatment are critically important for the prevention of a full-blown stroke.
	- **Acute Care:** Providing prompt treatment for acute stroke, which largely consists of the control of medical complications.
	
	**3. TERTIARY PREVENTION & SURVEILLANCE**
	
	- **Long-Term Follow-Up:** Establishing adequate facilities for the long-term follow-up and rehabilitation of stroke survivors to manage residual disabilities.
	- **Community Assessment:** Obtaining reliable knowledge of the extent of the problem in the community concerned is an essential prerequisite for planning any control programme.
	
	**4. HEALTH EDUCATION**
	
	- **Training & Awareness:** The education and training of health personnel as well as the general public form an integral part of the stroke control programme. The community must be educated to recognize early warning signs (like sudden numbness, confusion, trouble speaking, or loss of balance) and seek immediate medical care.


	1. Epidemiology of stroke.  
	**EPIDEMIOLOGY OF STROKE (CEREBROVASCULAR ACCIDENT)**
	
	**1. DEFINITION**
	
	- The WHO defines stroke as "rapidly developed clinical signs of focal disturbance of cerebral function; lasting more than 24 hours or leading to death, with no apparent cause other than vascular origin".
	- The 24-hour threshold excludes Transient Ischaemic Attacks (TIA).
	- The dysfunction is caused by three morphological abnormalities: stenosis, occlusion, or rupture of the arteries.
	
	**2. CLASSIFICATION** Stroke includes several syndromes with differing aetiologies:
	
	- **Ischaemic Stroke:** Lacunar infarct, Carotid circulation obstruction, Vertebro-basilar obstruction. Cerebral thrombosis is the most frequent form encountered clinically.
	- **Haemorrhagic Stroke:** Spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage, Subarachnoid haemorrhage, Intracranial aneurysm, Arteriovenous malformations.
	
	**3. MAGNITUDE OF THE PROBLEM (BURDEN OF DISEASE)** **A. Global Scenario:**
	
	- In 2021, cerebrovascular disease accounted for 6.97 million deaths worldwide (10.2% of all deaths), with the majority occurring in developing countries.
	- In demographically developed countries, the average age of occurrence is around 73 years, with the probability of a first stroke being 1.6 per 1000.
	- Patients are at the highest risk of death in the first few weeks, with 20-50% mortality within the first month. Survivors face a 10% risk of a subsequent event in the first year.
	
	**B. Indian Scenario:**
	
	- Stroke is the third leading cause of death and tenth leading cause of DALYs in India.
	- In 2021, 0.756 million people died of stroke in India (0.360 million men and 0.396 million women), with a crude death rate of about 53.5 per 100,000 population.
	- The incidence varies from 116 to 163 per 100,000 population.
	- **"Strokes in the Young":** Unlike developed nations, a significantly higher proportion of strokes (about one-fifth) occur below the age of 40 in India. Major causes for this include rheumatic heart disease, CNS infections (bacterial and tubercular meningitis), and ischaemic strokes in the peripartum period.
	
	**4. EPIDEMIOLOGICAL DETERMINANTS (RISK FACTORS)** Risk factors for stroke do not occur at random and precede the event by several years.
	
	**A. Host Factors (Non-Modifiable):**
	
	- **Age:** Incidence rates rise steeply with age, with 47% of all stroke deaths globally occurring in persons over 70 years.
	- **Sex:** Incidence rates are consistently higher in males than in females across all ages.
	- **Personal History:** Nearly three-quarters of stroke patients have a history of associated diseases, mostly in the cardiovascular system or diabetes, indicating stroke is often an acute incident in a generalized vascular disease.
	- **Genetics:** Genetic predispositions also contribute to the risk.
	
	**B. Modifiable & Medical Risk Factors (High-Yield):**
	
	- **Hypertension:** This is the single most important and main risk factor for both cerebral thrombosis and cerebral haemorrhage.
	- **Transient Ischaemic Attacks (TIA):** Episodes of focal, reversible neurological deficits lasting less than 24 hours (due to microemboli); they are a strong warning sign of an impending stroke.
	- **Cardiac Abnormalities:** Conditions like left ventricular hypertrophy and cardiac dilatation elevate risk.
	- **Other Medical Factors:** Diabetes, elevated blood lipids, and blood clotting/viscosity issues.
	- **Lifestyle Factors:** General obesity, central obesity (high waist-to-hip ratio), cigarette smoking, harmful alcohol consumption, and the use of oral contraceptives.
	
	**5. CLINICAL PRESENTATION**
	
	- The most common somatoneurological disorder is hemiplegia, seen in about 90% of patients.
	- Presenting features include sudden numbness or weakness in the face/arm/leg (typically on one side), sudden confusion, trouble speaking or understanding, trouble seeing, dizziness, loss of balance, and impairment of consciousness.
	
	**6. PREVENTION AND CONTROL STRATEGY**
	
	- **Primary Prevention:** The first priority of any stroke control programme is the control of arterial hypertension. Other measures at the community and individual levels include control of diabetes, elimination of smoking and alcohol consumption, promotion of healthy dietary habits, regular physical exercise, yoga, and avoidance of weight gain.
	- **Early Detection:** Early detection and treatment of TIAs are critically important to avert a full-blown stroke.
	- **Medical Management (Secondary Care):** Most acute stroke patients require urgent hospital admission to distinguish the pathology (haemorrhage vs. ischaemia via CT scan), evaluate and control risk factors, and prevent non-neurological complications like aspiration pneumonia.
	- **Tertiary Care:** Facilities for long-term follow-up and rehabilitation are essential to help survivors achieve independence in daily self-care.
