1. Rising trend coronary heart diseases
	
	**RISING TREND OF CORONARY HEART DISEASE (CHD) / ISCHAEMIC HEART DISEASE (IHD)**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION: THE "MODERN EPIDEMIC"**
	
	- **Definition:** Coronary Heart Disease (CHD) is defined as the impairment of heart function due to inadequate blood flow to the heart compared to its needs, caused by obstructive changes in the coronary circulation.
	- **Global Status:** The WHO has drawn attention to the fact that CHD is our modern "epidemic" — a disease that affects populations and is not merely an unavoidable attribute of ageing. It is currently the number one cause of death globally.
	
	**2. SECULAR TRENDS: GLOBAL PERSPECTIVE**
	
	- **The Rise:** "Epidemics" of CHD began at different times globally. The epidemic began in the early 1920s in the United States, in the 1930s in Britain, and later in several other European countries.
	- **The Decline in Developed Nations:** Countries where the epidemic began earlier are now showing a consistent decline. In the US, a steady decline was evident by 1968, with a 25 per cent fall in mortality by 1980. This decline is largely attributed to the success of primary prevention: changes in life-styles, diet (diet-dependent serum cholesterol), reduced cigarette use, exercise habits, and better control of hypertension.
	- **The Rise in Developing Nations:** The middle and low-income countries (including India) are currently at the mid-point of this emerging epidemic and will face its full impact in the coming years. At least 82 per cent of the world's deaths from CVDs now occur in low and middle-income countries.
	
	**3. THE INDIAN SCENARIO (EPIDEMIOLOGICAL TRANSITION)**
	
	- **Rapid Health Transition:** India is experiencing a rapid health and epidemiological transition, with a rising burden of Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs) causing significant morbidity and mortality.
	- **Morbidity and Prevalence:** There is a considerable increase in the prevalence of CHD in urban areas in India over the last decade. Pooled estimates show the prevalence rate of CHD is 6.4 per cent in urban areas and 2.5 per cent in rural areas. It is reported to be 2-3 times higher in the urban population as compared to the rural population.
	- **Mortality:** An estimated 1,567,700 people died of CHD in India during 2021, representing a crude death rate of 110.9 per 100,000 population.
	- **Economic Impact:** Compared with all other countries, India suffers the highest loss in potentially productive years of life due to CVD deaths in the 35–64 years age group.
	
	**4. FACTORS RESPONSIBLE FOR THE RISING TREND (HIGH-YIELD)** The rising trend of CHD is driven by a complex "web of causation" involving multiple risk factors. Present mortality rates are the consequence of previous exposure to behavioural risk factors (the "lag-time" effect).
	
	- **Modifiable Risk Factors Driving the Epidemic:**
	    - **Cigarette Smoking:** A major risk factor responsible for 25 per cent of CHD deaths under 65 years of age in men in countries where smoking is widespread.
	    - **Hypertension:** Accelerates the atherosclerotic process.
	    - **Elevated Serum Cholesterol:** There is a well-established causal relationship between habitual diet (high saturated fats), blood cholesterol-lipoprotein levels, and CHD.
	    - **Physical Inactivity:** Sedentary lifestyles and lack of exercise are directly associated with a greater risk of early CHD.
	    - **Obesity:** The rising prevalence of overweight and central obesity increases the risk of metabolic syndrome and CHD.
	    - **Diabetes:** The risk of CHD is 2-3 times higher in diabetics than in non-diabetics.
	    - **Other Factors:** Type A personality (competitive drive, stress), high alcohol intake, and use of oral contraceptives.
	- **Shifting Social Class Gradient:** Originally, CHD emerged as a disease of the higher social classes in affluent societies. Fifty years later, the situation is changing, and there is now a strong inverse relationship between social class and CHD in developed countries, shifting the burden to lower socio-economic groups.
	
	**5. PUBLIC HEALTH APPROACH TO REVERSE THE TREND** To halt and reverse this rising trend, WHO recommends three main strategies:
	
	1. **Population Strategy:** A mass approach focusing on controlling underlying causes in the whole population (e.g., dietary changes, smoke-free societies, physical activity promotion).
	2. **Primordial Prevention:** Preventing the emergence and spread of CHD risk factors and life-styles in developing countries where they have not yet become fully endemic (e.g., preserving traditional healthy eating patterns).
	3. **High-Risk Strategy:** Identifying individuals at special risk (e.g., via blood pressure and cholesterol screening) and bringing them under specific preventive care.
1. Explain the epidemiology (risk factors) and methods of prevention and control of coronary heart disease.
	**EPIDEMIOLOGY AND PREVENTION OF CORONARY HEART DISEASE (CHD)**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION & DEFINITION**
	
	- **Definition:** Coronary heart disease (synonym: ischaemic heart disease) is defined as the "impairment of heart function due to inadequate blood flow to the heart compared to its needs, caused by obstructive changes in the coronary circulation to the heart".
	- **Clinical Manifestations:** CHD may present as angina pectoris of effort, myocardial infarction, irregularities of the heart, cardiac failure, or sudden death.
	
	**2. EPIDEMIOLOGICAL DETERMINANTS (RISK FACTORS)** The aetiology of CHD is multifactorial. The presence of any one risk factor places an individual in a high-risk category, and the greater the number of risk factors, the higher the likelihood of developing CHD. Risk factors are broadly classified into two groups:
	
	**A. Non-Modifiable (Immutable) Risk Factors**
	
	- **Age and Sex:** Risk increases with advancing age, and males are at a higher risk compared to pre-menopausal females,.
	- **Genetic Factors / Family History:** A family history of CHD increases the risk of premature death, as genetic factors are important determinants of total cholesterol (TC) and LDL levels,.
	- **Personality:** "Type A" behaviour (characterized by competitive drive, restlessness, hostility, and a sense of urgency) makes individuals more coronary-prone compared to the calmer "Type B" individuals.
	
	**B. Modifiable Risk Factors**
	
	- **Cigarette Smoking:** Identified as a major CHD risk factor. It acts through carbon monoxide-induced atherogenesis, nicotine stimulation raising blood pressure and myocardial oxygen demand, and lowering "protective" high-density lipoproteins (HDL). The risk is synergistic with hypertension and hypercholesterolemia. The risk of death decreases substantially within one year of stopping smoking.
	- **Hypertension:** Blood pressure is the single most useful test for identifying individuals at high risk. High blood pressure accelerates the atherosclerotic process, especially when combined with hyperlipidaemia. Both systolic and diastolic pressures are significant predictors.
	- **Elevated Serum Cholesterol:** There is a direct, triangular relationship between habitual diet, blood cholesterol-lipoprotein levels, and CHD. The risk rises steadily with serum cholesterol concentration, particularly beyond a threshold of 220 mg/dl.
	    - _LDL (Low-Density Lipoprotein):_ Most directly associated with CHD.
	    - _HDL (High-Density Lipoprotein):_ Protective against CHD; the higher the mean level, the lower the incidence of infarction. A total cholesterol/HDL ratio of less than 3.5 is the recommended clinical goal for prevention.
	- **Diabetes:** The risk of CHD is 2 to 3 times higher in diabetics.
	- **Physical Inactivity & Obesity:** A sedentary life-style and obesity increase the risk of early CHD. Regular exercise helps increase HDL concentration and decrease body weight and blood pressure.
	- **Alcohol:** High alcohol intake (defined as 75 g or more per day) is an independent risk factor for CHD.
	- **Oral Contraceptives:** Their use increases the risk of myocardial infarction in women, an effect that is compounded by cigarette smoking.
	
	---
	
	**3. PREVENTION AND CONTROL STRATEGIES** The WHO Expert Committee on the Prevention of CHD recommends three main strategies for intervention:
	
	**A. Population Strategy** CHD is a mass disease; therefore, this strategy focuses on controlling the underlying causes in the entire population to shift the whole risk-factor distribution towards "biological normality".
	
	- **Dietary Changes:** Key recommendations include reducing total fat intake to 20-30% of total energy, limiting saturated fats to less than 10%, reducing dietary cholesterol to below 100 mg per 1000 kcal per day, avoiding alcohol, reducing salt intake to 5 g daily or less, and increasing complex carbohydrates (vegetables, fruits, whole grains),.
	- **Smoking Control:** The ultimate goal is a smoke-free society, achieved through public education, legislative restrictions, and smoking cessation programmes,.
	- **Blood Pressure Control:** A multifactorial approach based on a "prudent diet", regular physical activity, and weight control to reduce the average blood pressure of the entire population,.
	- **Physical Activity:** Encouraging regular physical activity as part of daily life, starting from childhood.
	- **Primordial Prevention:** A crucial approach for developing countries, focusing on preventing the emergence and spread of CHD risk factors by preserving traditional, healthy eating patterns and life-styles.
	
	**B. High-Risk Strategy** This approach targets individuals who are highly susceptible to the disease.
	
	- **Identification:** Detecting individuals at special risk (e.g., smokers, diabetics, obese individuals, those with elevated blood pressure or serum cholesterol, or a strong family history) using optimum clinical methods,.
	- **Specific Advice & Intervention:** Motivating these individuals to take positive action against identified risk factors, such as treating elevated blood pressure, reducing serum cholesterol, and weaning off smoking,.
	
	**C. Secondary Prevention** This aims to prevent the recurrence and progression of CHD after a patient has already suffered an event (e.g., a heart attack).
	
	- **Lifestyle Modification:** Cessation of smoking is the most effective single intervention, reducing the risk of a fatal recurrence or sudden death by 20-50 per cent.
	- **Medical Therapy:** Use of drugs like beta-blockers, which can reduce the risk of CHD mortality in survivors of a myocardial infarct by approximately 25 per cent,.
	- **Surgical Procedures:** Revascularization procedures such as coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) and percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty (PTCA) with stent placement are indicated for patients with unacceptable symptoms despite optimal medical therapy,,.
1. Prevention of coronary heart disease.

	**PREVENTION OF CORONARY HEART DISEASE (CHD)**
	
	The WHO Expert Committee on the Prevention of CHD has recommended three main strategies for the prevention and control of the disease.
	
	### **1. POPULATION STRATEGY**
	
	- **Concept:** CHD is primarily a mass disease, so this strategy focuses on controlling the underlying causes and risk factors in the whole population, rather than merely in individuals.
	- **Aim:** The goal is to shift the whole risk-factor distribution in the direction of "biological normality". This requires the mobilization of the entire community to alter lifestyle practices associated with CHD.
	- **Specific Interventions:**
	    - **Dietary Changes (Prudent Diet):** Dietary modification is the principal preventive strategy. The WHO recommends reducing total dietary fat to 20–30 per cent of total energy intake. Saturated fats must be limited to less than 10 per cent of total energy intake, and the reduction should be made up by substituting mono- and poly-unsaturated fats. Dietary cholesterol should be reduced to below 100 mg per 1000 kcal per day. The diet should include an increased consumption of complex carbohydrates (vegetables, fruits, whole grains, and legumes) and a reduced salt intake to 5 g daily or less. Alcohol consumption should be avoided.
	    - **Smoking Control:** The ultimate goal is to achieve a smoke-free society. This requires a comprehensive health programme including public education, legislative restrictions, fiscal measures, and smoking cessation programmes.
	    - **Blood Pressure Control:** Even a small reduction (e.g., 2 or 3 mm Hg) in the average blood pressure of the total population produces a large reduction in the incidence of cardiovascular complications. This is achieved through a multifactorial approach based on a "prudent diet", regular physical activity, and weight control.
	    - **Physical Activity:** Regular physical activity should be encouraged as a part of normal daily life, starting from childhood.
	
	### **2. PRIMORDIAL PREVENTION**
	
	- **Concept:** This is a novel approach to the primary prevention of CHD that involves preventing the emergence and spread of CHD risk factors and life-styles that have not yet appeared or become endemic.
	- **Application:** It is particularly important for developing countries, which should be encouraged to preserve their traditional healthy eating patterns and life-styles that are associated with low levels of CHD risk factors.
	
	### **3. HIGH-RISK STRATEGY**
	
	- **Identification of Risk:** This approach starts by identifying individuals at special risk using simple tests like blood pressure and serum cholesterol measurements. High-risk individuals also include those who smoke, those with a strong family history of CHD, diabetics, the obese, and young women using oral contraceptives.
	- **Specific Advice and Intervention:** Once identified, these individuals are brought under specific preventive care and motivated to take positive action against their risk factors. This includes treating elevated blood pressure, reducing serum cholesterol concentrations, and helping patients to break the smoking habit permanently.
	
	### **4. SECONDARY PREVENTION**
	
	- **Aim:** Secondary prevention aims to prevent the recurrence and progression of CHD after a patient has already suffered an event (e.g., a myocardial infarct).
	- **Life-style Modifications:** The cessation of smoking is the most effective single intervention currently available for patients after a heart attack, reducing the risk of a fatal recurrence or sudden death by 20–50 per cent. Control of hypertension and diabetes, healthy nutrition, and exercise promotion remain equally vital.
	- **Medical Therapy:** The use of drugs like beta-blockers has shown highly promising results, appearing to reduce the risk of CHD mortality in patients who have suffered at least one infarct by approximately 25 per cent.
	- **Surgical Procedures (Revascularization):** Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) and percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty (PTCA) with intracoronary stent placement are indicated for select patients. These include patients with unacceptable symptoms despite optimal medical therapy, those with left main coronary artery stenosis greater than 50 per cent, or those with three-vessel disease combined with left ventricular dysfunction.

1. Risk factors in coronary heart disease
	**RISK FACTORS IN CORONARY HEART DISEASE (CHD)**
	
	The aetiology of Coronary Heart Disease (CHD) is multifactorial. The presence of any one risk factor places an individual in a high-risk category, and the greater the number of risk factors present, the more likely one is to develop CHD.
	
	Epidemiologically, risk factors are broadly classified into two categories: **Non-Modifiable (Immutable)** and **Modifiable**.
	
	---
	
	### **1. NON-MODIFIABLE (IMMUTABLE) RISK FACTORS**
	
	- **Age:** The risk of CHD increases progressively with advancing age.
	- **Sex:** Males are at a higher risk compared to pre-menopausal females. The pronounced difference in mortality rates between sexes suggests a hormonal basis.
	- **Genetic Factors and Family History:** A strong family history of CHD increases the risk of premature death. Genetic factors are probably the most important determinants of an individual's total cholesterol (TC) and Low-Density Lipoprotein (LDL) levels.
	- **Personality:** "Type A" behaviour—characterized by a competitive drive, restlessness, hostility, and a sense of urgency or impatience—makes individuals more coronary-prone compared to the calmer "Type B" individuals.
	
	---
	
	### **2. MODIFIABLE RISK FACTORS**
	
	- **Cigarette Smoking:** Identified as a major CHD risk factor.
	    - _Pathogenesis:_ It acts through carbon monoxide-induced atherogenesis, nicotine stimulation (which raises blood pressure and myocardial oxygen demand), and by lowering "protective" high-density lipoproteins (HDL).
	    - _Synergism:_ Its influence is synergistic with other risk factors like hypertension and elevated serum cholesterol (i.e., the effects are more than simply additive).
	    - _Prevention:_ The risk of death declines substantially within one year of stopping smoking. For those who have already had a myocardial infarction, the risk of a fatal recurrence can be reduced by 50% after giving up smoking.
	- **Hypertension:** Blood pressure is considered the single most useful test for identifying individuals at a high risk of developing CHD. Hypertension accelerates the atherosclerotic process, especially if hyperlipidaemia is also present. Both systolic and diastolic blood pressures are strong predictors.
	- **Elevated Serum Cholesterol:** There is a direct, triangular relationship between habitual diet, blood cholesterol-lipoprotein levels, and CHD. The risk of CHD rises steadily with serum cholesterol concentration, particularly beyond a "threshold level" of 220 mg/dl.
	    - _LDL (Low-Density Lipoprotein):_ Elevated LDL is most directly associated with CHD.
	    - _HDL (High-Density Lipoprotein):_ HDL is protective against the development of CHD.
	    - _Indicator:_ A total cholesterol/HDL ratio of less than 3.5 is the recommended clinical goal for CHD prevention. Recent evidence shows that apolipoproteins (A-I and B) are even better predictors of CHD than HDL or LDL.
	- **Diabetes:** The risk of CHD is 2 to 3 times higher in diabetics than in non-diabetics. It is responsible for 30 to 50 per cent of deaths in diabetics over the age of 40 years in industrialized countries.
	- **Physical Inactivity & Sedentary Habits:** A sedentary life-style is associated with a greater risk of early CHD. Regular physical exercise reduces body weight and blood pressure, and increases the concentration of protective HDL.
	- **Obesity:** Strongly associated as a direct contributor to the development of CHD.
	- **Alcohol:** High alcohol intake (defined as 75 g or more per day) is an independent risk factor for CHD, hypertension, and all cardiovascular diseases.
	- **Oral Contraceptives:** Their use increases the risk of myocardial infarction in women. This risk is heavily compounded by cigarette smoking.
	- **Miscellaneous Factors:** Other debated or contributing risk factors include stress, high sucrose diet, lack of dietary fibre, softness of drinking water, and hyperestrogenemia.
