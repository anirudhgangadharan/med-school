1. Pandemic influenza A—vaccine and treatment
	**PANDEMIC INFLUENZA A (H1N1) 2009: VACCINE AND TREATMENT**
	
	**1. TREATMENT OF PANDEMIC INFLUENZA A (H1N1)** The clinical management of Pandemic Influenza A (H1N1) 2009 includes basic symptomatic care, early administration of antiviral drugs for high-risk populations, antimicrobials for co-infections, and proactive observation for the progression of the illness. Hospital care may require early supplemental oxygen therapy to correct hypoxaemia, careful fluid replacement, and mechanical ventilation for severe respiratory distress.
	
	**Antiviral Susceptibility:**
	
	- **Susceptible to:** Neuraminidase inhibitors (Oseltamivir and Zanamivir).
	- **Resistant to:** M2 inhibitors (Amantadine and Rimantadine).
	
	**A. Oseltamivir (Drug of Choice)**
	
	- **Indications:** Patients with severe or progressive clinical illness, including high-risk groups such as pregnant women and young children <2 years (including neonates).
	- **Timing:** Treatment should be initiated immediately as soon as possible following the onset of illness, without waiting for laboratory confirmation of the diagnosis.
	- **Standard Adult Dose:** 75 mg orally twice daily for 5 days.
	- **Severe Cases:** In patients not responding to normal regimens, higher doses (e.g., 150 mg twice daily in adults) and a longer duration of treatment may be used.
	- **Paediatric Dose (1 to 12 years) for 5 days:**
	    - 15 kg or less: 30 mg twice daily.
	    - 15–23 kg: 45 mg twice daily.
	    - 24–40 kg: 60 mg twice daily.
	    - > 40 kg: 75 mg twice daily.
	        
	- **Infant Dose (< 1 year) for 5 days:**
	    - 0 to 1 month: 2 mg/kg twice daily.
	    - > 1 to 3 months: 2.5 mg/kg twice daily.
	        
	    - > 3 to 12 months: 3 mg/kg twice daily.
	        
	
	**B. Zanamivir (Alternative Therapy)**
	
	- **Indications:** Used when oseltamivir is not available, contraindicated, or if the virus is resistant to oseltamivir.
	- **Dose:** For adults and children from the age of 5 years, the recommended dose is two inhalations (2 x 5 mg) twice daily for 5 days.
	
	**C. Alternative Intravenous Antivirals**
	
	- In persistent severe illness despite oseltamivir treatment, clinicians may consider intravenous administration of alternative drugs like zanamivir, peramivir, and ribavirin under medical supervision. Ribavirin must never be given as monotherapy or to pregnant women.
	
	---
	
	**2. CHEMOPROPHYLAXIS**
	
	- **Drug of Choice:** Oseltamivir is the drug of choice for chemoprophylaxis.
	- **Indications:** Recommended for healthcare personnel and close contacts of a suspected, probable, or confirmed case of pandemic influenza A (H1N1) 2009.
	- **Duration:** It should be given until 10 days after the last exposure.
	- **Adult Dose:** 75 mg Once Daily (OD).
	- **Paediatric Dose:** <15 kg (30 mg OD); 15-23 kg (45 mg OD); 24-40 kg (60 mg OD); >40 kg (75 mg OD).
	
	---
	
	**3. VACCINATION AGAINST INFLUENZA A (H1N1)** Vaccines for influenza A (H1N1) 2009 were officially approved and introduced in 2009. Currently, influenza vaccines are manufactured using three different technologies: egg-based inactivated influenza vaccines (IIVs), cell-cultured vaccines, and recombinant egg-free flu vaccines, alongside the live attenuated influenza vaccine (LAIV).
	
	**Priority Groups for Vaccination (WHO Recommendations):** Due to the unstoppable spread of the virus and limitations in vaccine availability, the WHO provides a strict order of priority for vaccination:
	
	1. **Healthcare Workers:** They are the absolute first priority to protect the essential health infrastructure and prevent the nosocomial spread of the disease to vulnerable patients.
	2. **Pregnant Women**.
	3. **Individuals aged >6 months with chronic medical conditions**.
	4. **Healthy young adults** (between 15–49 years of age).
	5. **Healthy children**.
	6. **Healthy adults** (between 49–65 years of age).
	7. **Healthy elderly adults** (aged >65 years).
	
	**Important Precaution:** A time difference of at least 7 days must be maintained between administering a dose of the COVID-19 vaccine and the influenza vaccine.
1. Influenza A (H1N1)
	**PANDEMIC INFLUENZA A (H1N1) 2009 (SWINE FLU)**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION & EPIDEMIOLOGY**
	
	- **Agent:** The disease is caused by the pandemic Influenza A (H1N1) 2009 virus, which WHO now designates as Influenza A (H1N1)pdm09.
	- **Origin & Pathogenicity:** It emerged in March 2009 in Mexico and was declared a pandemic by the WHO in June 2009. Unlike seasonal influenza, the majority of the human population lacks pre-existing immunity, leading to significant infection rates in children and young adults. The virus also has the unique ability to infect the lower respiratory tract, causing rapidly progressive pneumonia.
	- **Incubation Period:** Ranges from 1 to 4 days, but can extend up to 7 days.
	
	**2. CASE DEFINITIONS**
	
	- **Suspected Case:** An acute febrile respiratory illness (fever >38°C) with onset within 7 days of close contact with a confirmed case, travel to an area reporting confirmed cases, or residence in a community with one or more confirmed cases.
	- **Probable Case:** An acute febrile respiratory illness that tests positive for Influenza A but is unsubtypable by standard RT-PCR reagents, or is positive by a rapid test/IFA and meets the suspected case criteria. It also includes unexplained acute respiratory deaths epidemiologically linked to a probable or confirmed case.
	- **Confirmed Case:** A person with laboratory confirmation by Real-Time PCR, viral culture, or a four-fold rise in specific neutralizing antibodies.
	
	**3. CLINICAL FEATURES** The clinical spectrum ranges from mild, non-febrile upper respiratory tract infections to fatal pneumonia.
	
	- **Uncomplicated Influenza:** Presents with Influenza-Like Illness (ILI) symptoms such as fever, cough, sore throat, rhinorrhoea, headache, muscle pain, and malaise without shortness of breath. Gastrointestinal symptoms like diarrhoea and vomiting are also common, particularly in children.
	- **Complicated/Severe Influenza:** Characterized by clinical or radiological signs of lower respiratory tract disease (e.g., hypoxia, tachypnea, dyspnoea), CNS involvement (e.g., encephalopathy), severe dehydration, renal failure, multi-organ failure, or septic shock. It can also exacerbate underlying chronic diseases like asthma, COPD, and diabetes.
	- **Warning Signs of Progression:** Shortness of breath, cyanosis, hypotension, altered mental status, persistent convulsions, and severe dehydration.
	
	**4. HIGH-RISK GROUPS FOR SEVERE DISEASE** Populations at higher risk of developing severe complications include:
	
	- Infants and young children (<2 years) and elderly individuals aged 65 years and older.
	- Pregnant women.
	- Individuals with chronic conditions (pulmonary, cardiac, renal, hepatic, metabolic, or neurological disorders).
	- Immunosuppressed patients (e.g., due to HIV infection or malignancy).
	- Morbidly obese individuals and children receiving chronic aspirin therapy.
	
	**5. LABORATORY DIAGNOSIS**
	
	- **Gold Standard:** Reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) provides the most timely and sensitive detection.
	- **Clinical Specimens:** Upper respiratory tract samples (nasal swabs, nasopharyngeal swabs, and throat swabs) are advised. In mechanically ventilated patients or those with severe disease, lower respiratory tract samples (tracheal and bronchial aspirates) provide higher diagnostic yields.
	- _Note:_ Rapid diagnostic tests often miss the pandemic (H1N1) 2009 virus, so a negative result cannot be used to definitively rule out the disease.
	
	**6. INFECTION CONTROL**
	
	- **Precautions:** Standard plus droplet precautions must be maintained at all times, including strict hand hygiene and the use of medical face masks.
	- **Aerosol-Generating Procedures:** Healthcare workers performing high-risk procedures (e.g., bronchoscopy, intubation) must use particulate respirators (N95 or FFP2), eye protection, gowns, and gloves.
	- **Isolation Duration:** Hospitalized patients should be isolated for 7 days after the onset of illness or 24 hours after the resolution of fever and respiratory symptoms, whichever is longer.
	
	**7. TREATMENT AND CHEMOPROPHYLAXIS** _(Summary of Medical Management)_
	
	- **Antiviral Susceptibility:** The virus is susceptible to neuraminidase inhibitors (Oseltamivir, Zanamivir) but resistant to M2 inhibitors (Amantadine, Rimantadine).
	- **Drug of Choice:** Oseltamivir is the primary treatment for severe or progressive illness and should be initiated immediately without waiting for lab confirmation. For adults, the standard dose is 75 mg twice daily for 5 days.
	- **Chemoprophylaxis:** Oseltamivir is administered to close contacts and healthcare personnel for 10 days after the last exposure.
	- **Vaccines:** Egg-based inactivated vaccines (IIVs), cell-cultured vaccines, recombinant vaccines, and live attenuated influenza vaccines (LAIV) are available and targeted primarily towards high-priority groups like healthcare workers and pregnant women.
 
3. H1N1 epidemic in India.  
	**PANDEMIC INFLUENZA A (H1N1) 2009 (SWINE FLU) IN INDIA**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION & EPIDEMIOLOGY**
	
	- **Origin & Nomenclature:** The pandemic influenza A (H1N1) 2009 virus emerged in Mexico during the spring of 2009, resulting in the first influenza pandemic of the 21st century. The WHO declared it a pandemic on 11th June 2009. On 26th September 2011, the WHO adapted the new nomenclature **Influenza A (H1N1)pdm09**.
	- **Current Status in India:** The world is now in a post-pandemic period, but the H1N1 virus continues to circulate and cause local outbreaks in India.
	- **Indian Burden (2022 Data):** During the year 2022, India reported 13,202 cases and 410 deaths, representing a case fatality rate (CFR) of 3.105%.
	- **Unique Pathogenic Features:** It differs from seasonal influenza because the majority of the human population lacks pre-existing immunity. Consequently, it does not follow the usual pattern and affects a much wider age range, particularly children and young to middle-aged adults. The virus also has the ability to infect the lower respiratory tract, causing rapidly progressive pneumonia.
	- **Incubation Period:** Typically ranges from 1 to 4 days, but can extend up to 7 days.
	
	**2. CASE DEFINITIONS (For Surveillance & Reporting)**
	
	- **Suspected Case:** An acute febrile respiratory illness (fever >38°C) with onset within 7 days of close contact with a confirmed case, OR within 7 days of travel to areas reporting confirmed cases, OR residing in a community with one or more confirmed cases.
	- **Probable Case:** An acute febrile respiratory illness that is positive for influenza A but unsubtypable by standard RT-PCR reagents, OR positive by a rapid test/IFA meeting suspected case criteria, OR an unexplained acute respiratory death epidemiologically linked to a probable or confirmed case.
	- **Confirmed Case:** An acute febrile respiratory illness with laboratory confirmation by Real-Time PCR, viral culture, or a four-fold rise in specific neutralizing antibodies.
	
	**3. CLINICAL SPECTRUM** The disease presents a wide clinical spectrum ranging from non-febrile mild upper respiratory illness to severe, rapidly progressive pneumonia.
	
	- **Uncomplicated Influenza (ILI):** Presents with fever, chills, cough, sore throat, rhinorrhoea, headache, muscle pain, and malaise without shortness of breath. Gastrointestinal symptoms (diarrhoea/vomiting) without dehydration are especially common in children.
	- **Complicated/Severe Influenza:** Characterized by signs of lower respiratory tract disease (shortness of breath, hypoxia, tachypnea), CNS complications (altered mental status, unconsciousness, persistent convulsions), severe dehydration, and exacerbation of underlying chronic diseases (e.g., asthma, COPD, diabetes). Progression can be rapid, occurring within 24 hours.
	
	**4. HIGH-RISK GROUPS FOR SEVERE DISEASE** Populations at significantly higher risk for severe complications and mortality include:
	
	- Infants and young children (<2 years) and elderly persons (≥65 years).
	- Pregnant women.
	- Individuals with chronic medical conditions (pulmonary, cardiac, renal, hepatic, metabolic, or neurological).
	- Immunosuppressed patients (e.g., HIV infection, malignancy, or on immunosuppressive medication).
	- Morbidly obese individuals.
	- Children receiving chronic aspirin therapy.
	
	**5. LABORATORY DIAGNOSIS**
	
	- **Gold Standard:** Reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) provides the most timely and sensitive detection.
	- **Rapid Diagnostic Tests (RDTs):** While commercially available, RDTs miss many infections with the pandemic (H1N1) 2009 virus. A negative RDT cannot rule out the disease and should not be used to withhold therapy.
	
	**6. MANAGEMENT AND TREATMENT**
	
	- **General Care:** Basic symptomatic care, early supplemental oxygen therapy for hypoxaemia, careful fluid replacement, and mechanical ventilation for severe respiratory distress.
	- **Antiviral Therapy:** The virus is susceptible to neuraminidase inhibitors (**Oseltamivir and Zanamivir**) but resistant to M2 inhibitors. Treatment should be initiated early, especially for high-risk populations, without waiting for laboratory confirmation if the disease is suspected clinically and epidemiologically.
	- **Antibiotics:** Required for treating secondary bacterial co-infections.
	
	**7. PREVENTION & INFECTION CONTROL**
	
	- **Infection Control:** Adherence to standard plus droplet precautions is essential. This includes strict hand hygiene, covering the mouth and nose when coughing/sneezing, and the use of medical face masks.
	- **Vaccination:** Vaccines against pandemic influenza A (H1N1) are available and recommended. Due to supply limitations during outbreaks, the WHO prioritizes vaccination in the following order: healthcare workers (to protect health infrastructure), pregnant women, individuals >6 months with chronic conditions, healthy young adults, children, and finally, healthy older adults.
