1. Carriers’ in cholera 
	**CARRIERS IN CHOLERA**
	
	**1. Definition & Epidemiological Significance**
	
	- A cholera carrier is defined as an apparently healthy person who is excreting _V. cholerae_ O1 (classical or El Tor) in their stools.
	- The human being is the only known reservoir of cholera infection, presenting either as a clinical case or a carrier.
	- Carriers excrete fewer vibrios ($10^2$ to $10^5$ vibrios per gram of stool) compared to clinical cases, but they still make an important contribution to the reservoir of infection,.
	
	**2. Types of Cholera Carriers** Four distinct types of cholera carriers have been described:
	
	- **A. Preclinical or Incubatory Carriers:**
	    - Carriage is of very short duration because the incubation period of cholera is short (ranging from 1 to 5 days).
	    - These individuals are essentially potential patients.
	- **B. Convalescent Carriers:**
	    - These are patients who have recovered from an attack of cholera but continue to excrete vibrios during their convalescence for 2 to 3 weeks.
	    - This state typically occurs in patients who have not received effective antibiotic treatment.
	- **C. Contact or Healthy Carriers:**
	    - This state is the result of a subclinical infection contracted through close association with a source of infection, such as a case or an infected environment.
	    - The duration of the contact carrier state is short, usually less than 10 days,.
	    - Despite the short duration, contact carriers probably play an important role in the spread of cholera.
	- **D. Chronic Carriers:**
	    - A chronic carrier state occurs infrequently, although epidemiologically, the El Tor biotype is more associated with the occurrence of chronic carriers than the classical biotype,.
	    - Studies indicate that the gall bladder is the primary site of infection in chronic carriers.
	    - The duration can be extensive; the longest recorded chronic carrier state was found to be over 10 years.
	
	**3. Detection and Diagnosis of Carriers**
	
	- **Stool Examination:** Carriers are best detected by bacteriological examination of a purged stool, which is induced by the oral administration of 30 to 60 grams of magnesium sulphate in 100 ml of water.
	- **Culture:** Because carriers excrete fewer vibrios than active cases, the use of selective media and proper enrichment techniques are important for a successful diagnosis.
	- **Serology:** The antibody titre against _V. cholerae_ O1 rises and remains positive for as long as the person harbours the organism. This serological method can be used alongside stool cultures to successfully detect long-term chronic carriers.
	

2. Differential diagnosis of cholera and food poisoning  

**DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS OF CHOLERA AND FOOD POISONING**

The clinical presentation of food poisoning and cholera can sometimes overlap, but they have distinct epidemiological and clinical characteristics. Below is the high-yield differentiation between the two for quick exam revision:

|Feature|Cholera|Food Poisoning|
|:--|:--|:--|
|**1. Epidemiology**|Occurs often in epidemic form associated with other cases in the neighbourhood. Secondary cases occur.|Often a single group of persons who shared a common meal. No secondary cases.|
|**2. Incubation Period**|From a few hours up to 5 days.|1 to 24 hours.|
|**3. Onset**|Starts with purging.|Starts with vomiting.|
|**4. Nausea and Retching**|None.|Present.|
|**5. Vomiting**|Projectile, effortless, watery, and continuous.|Often single, severe vomit; may be mucus and blood-streaked.|
|**6. Stools**|Copious, rice-watery, and inoffensive.|Frequent; may contain mucus and blood, offensive.|
|**7. Tenesmus**|None.|Yes.|
|**8. Abdominal Tenderness**|None.|Distinct.|
|**9. Dehydration**|Very marked.|Less constant.|
|**10. Muscular Cramps**|Constant and severe.|Often seldom.|
|**11. Surface Temperature**|Subnormal.|Often raised, up to 100-102°F.|
|**12. Urine Output**|Suppressed.|Often seldom suppressed.|
|**13. Blood Picture**|Leucocytosis.|Normal.|

_(Note: Food poisoning may also be mistaken for acute bacillary dysentery and chemical/arsenic poisoning, and differentiating these clinical features accurately is critical for epidemiological investigation and proper management.)_
