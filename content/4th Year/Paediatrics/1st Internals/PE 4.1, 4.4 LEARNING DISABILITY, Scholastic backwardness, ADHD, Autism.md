### **1. SCHOLASTIC BACKWARDNESS (OVERVIEW)**

Scholastic backwardness refers to a child's consistent failure to perform academically according to their age and intellectual potential. When a child presents with poor scholastic performance, the predominant clinical conditions that must be considered and differentiated include:

- **Specific Learning Disability (e.g., Dyslexia, Dysgraphia, Dyscalculia)**
- **Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)**
- **Intellectual Disability (Mental Retardation)**
- Unrecognized sensory impairments (e.g., hearing or visual deficits).

### **2. SPECIFIC LEARNING DISABILITY (SLD)**

**a. Definition** Specific learning disability is defined as a persistent impairment in reading (dyslexia), writing (dysgraphia), and/or arithmetic (dyscalculia) skills in an individual who has preserved cognition (normal IQ), normal vision and hearing, and adequate educational opportunities. Dyslexia accounts for 80% of all specific learning disabilities.

**b. Etiopathogenesis**

- **Neurological:** These disorders are probably caused by functionally disrupted networks in the cerebral cortex with intact gross anatomy.
- **Risk Factors:** Genetic predisposition appears to play an important role, with a higher risk in first-degree relatives. Other factors include prenatal cigarette exposure, lead exposure, and protein-energy malnutrition (PEM).
- There is no gross organic brain lesion, and it is not primarily caused by socioeconomical, cultural, or environmental deprivation.

**c. Clinical Features** The hallmark is a discrepancy between the child's true potential (IQ) and their actual academic achievement.

- **Dyslexia:** Reading slowly and incorrectly, skipping lines while reading aloud, letter or word reversal, and directional confusion.
- **Dysgraphia:** Untidy or illegible handwriting with poor sequencing and repeated spelling mistakes.
- **Dyscalculia:** Inability to perform even simple mathematical calculations and poor memorization of number facts.

**d. Diagnosis**

- Diagnosis requires the fulfillment of predefined criteria (such as DSM-5) in reading, writing, and arithmetic skills, and these impairments should persist for at least 6 months despite interventions targeting the disability.
- Physical examination should exclude neurological deficits, hearing loss, and visual impairments.

**e. Management** Management revolves around a multidisciplinary approach involving the pediatrician, remedial teacher, parents, and psychologist.

- **Remedial Education:** The cornerstone of management, requiring active participation from both school and parents.
- **Specific Strategies:** Teaching the child to break spoken words into smaller sound units (phoneme awareness), using tape-recorders, employing computers with spelling checkers, and using recorded books.
- **Evaluation Alterations:** Conducting oral examinations rather than written ones to accurately assess the child's knowledge.

### **3. ATTENTION DEFICIT HYPERACTIVITY DISORDER (ADHD)**

**a. Definition and Epidemiology** ADHD is the most common neurobehavioral disorder of childhood, characterized by hyperactivity, impulsiveness, and inattentiveness that are inappropriate for the child's age. Its prevalence in India is estimated at 1.3 per 1000. Boys suffer thrice as often as girls.

**b. Etiopathogenesis** The etiology is multifactorial, involving an interaction between biological (genetic endowment) and psychosocial factors. Predisposing factors include prematurity, low birth weight, and subtle brain damage.

**c. Clinical Features and Diagnostic Criteria** According to DSM-5 criteria, the diagnosis requires the fulfillment of specific criteria in inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity.

- **Onset and Setting:** Symptoms must onset up to 12 years of age, be present in at least two different social settings (e.g., home and school), and significantly interfere with social, academic, and occupational functioning.
- **Inattention:** Difficulty sustaining attention, prone to careless mistakes, easily distracted, frequently loses items, and forgetful in daily activities.
- **Hyperactivity/Impulsivity:** Often on the go ("driven by a motor"), fidgety, runs or climbs inappropriately, inability to wait for their turn, and frequently interrupts others.
- These symptoms must be present for a minimum of 6 months.

**d. Management**

- **Psychotherapy & Behavioral Therapy:** The cornerstone of management is psychotherapy and behavioral modification tailored for both the individual and the family, involving coordination among parents, teachers, and psychologists.
- **Pharmacotherapy:** Indicated in patients with inadequate response to psychological interventions.
    - _Stimulants:_ Methylphenidate and dextroamphetamine are standard therapies.
    - _Non-stimulants:_ Atomoxetine is recommended as a preferred drug for adolescent ADHD and cases with comorbidities or contraindications to stimulants.
    - _Alpha-2 agonists:_ Clonidine or atomoxetine for hyperactivity.
    - _Melatonin:_ Used for associated sleep-related problems.

### **4. AUTISM SPECTRUM DISORDER (ASD)**

**a. Definition** Autism spectrum disorder is a pervasive developmental disorder characterized by a triad of qualitative impairments: impaired social behavior, impaired communication (verbal and non-verbal), and associated stereotypic and restrictive behavioral patterns. The onset is typically before 3 years of age. The estimated global prevalence is 1–2%.

**b. Etiopathogenesis** The exact pathogenesis is unclear, but several factors are implicated:

- **Neurological:** Abnormalities in neural connectivity and migration, dendritic and synaptic morphology, and functioning of mirror neurons.
- **Genetic/Metabolic:** Genetic causes (e.g., tuberous sclerosis, Angelman syndrome, Fragile X syndrome) and metabolic diseases (e.g., phenylketonuria, hypothyroidism) account for roughly 10% of cases.

**c. Clinical Features**

- **Social Interaction Deficits:** Extreme aloofness, withdrawal, lack of eye contact, lack of facial expressions/gestures, and failure to develop normal reciprocal relationships (including with the mother).
- **Communication Deficits:** Speech is poorly developed, severely delayed, or entirely absent. About 60% of patients develop highly individualized or repetitive language (echolalia).
- **Behavioral Patterns:** Negativism, obsessive desire for sameness in routine, stereotyped movements (body rocking, hand flapping), and a peculiar attraction to spinning or rotating objects.

**d. Comorbidities and Diagnosis**

- Common comorbidities include mental retardation, seizures, feeding problems, sleep disturbances, and aggression/hyperactivity.
- Diagnosis is primarily clinical, utilizing DSM-5 guidelines which combine social interaction and communication domains into one, plus the presence of restricted/repetitive behaviors.

**e. Management** There is no curative medication for autism itself; management is supportive and multidisciplinary.

- **Behavioral Intervention:** The essential component is early, structured behavioral intervention (play and language therapy) to establish contact, improve communication, and prevent utter isolation.
- **Family Support & Education:** Parental counseling, educational services, and management of abnormal behaviors.
- **Pharmacotherapy:** Has a limited role, primarily for managing specific comorbidities. Antipsychotics (like risperidone or olanzapine) are used for anxiety, aggression, and repetitive behavior; methylphenidate for hyperactivity; and melatonin for sleep-related problems.
