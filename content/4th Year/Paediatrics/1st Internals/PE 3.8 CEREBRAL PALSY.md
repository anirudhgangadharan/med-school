### **1. DEFINITION**

Cerebral palsy (CP) refers to a permanent, nonprogressive, and occasionally evolving disorder of tone, movement, or posture. It is caused by an insult or damage to the developing brain before birth, during birth, or in the postnatal period. Though it is nonprogressive and nonfatal, it is currently noncurable.

### **2. EPIDEMIOLOGY**

It is the most common chronic motor disability in childhood. The prevalence rate is estimated to be around 2 to 3 affected infants per 1000 live births. In India, spastic quadriplegia is the most commonly encountered type.

### **3. ETIOLOGY AND RISK FACTORS**

While perinatal birth asphyxia was historically considered the leading cause, it actually accounts for less than 10% of cases. Current thought suggests that the roots of pathogenesis often lie in developmental biology. The causes are broadly divided according to the time of insult:

- **Genetic or Prenatal Factors:**
    - Genetic disorders and chromosomal defects.
    - Inborn errors of metabolism.
    - Structural malformations of the central nervous system.
    - Congenital or intrauterine infections (TORCH/STORCH complex).
    - Teratogen exposure and maternal/obstetric complications.
- **Perinatal Causes:**
    - Birth asphyxia and birth trauma.
    - Prematurity and low birth weight.
    - Neonatal infections (meningitis, septicemia) and neonatal seizures.
    - Neonatal hypoglycemia and intracranial hemorrhage.
    - Neonatal hyperbilirubinemia resulting in kernicterus (bilirubin encephalopathy).
- **Postnatal Causes:**
    - Central nervous system infections (meningitis, encephalitis).
    - Hypoxia and trauma (head injury).
    - Drugs, toxins, and metabolic disorders.

### **4. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY**

- **Mild CP:** The brain may appear grossly normal but is underweight, possessing sparse subcortical white matter and a sparse number of nerve fibers.
- **Severe CP:** Findings include widespread cerebral atrophy, porencephalitis, atrophy of the basal ganglia, and cavity formation in the subcortical white matter (cystic encephalomalacia).

### **5. CLINICAL FEATURES**

- **Developmental Delay:** The most common initial presentation is a delay in attaining motor and developmental milestones.
- **Abnormal Muscle Tone and Posture:** Patients exhibit increased tone (hypertonia) or occasionally severe hypotonia (atonic CP).
- **Reflexes:** The deep tendon reflexes are notably brisk (hyperreflexia), and plantar responses may be extensor. A critical finding is the persistence of primitive neonatal reflexes (such as the Moro grasp and tonic neck reflex) beyond 3 to 6 months of age.
- **Specific Motor Signs:** Fisting with a cortical thumb, toe-walking, scissoring of legs (adductor spasm elicited upon sudden lifting of the child), and abnormal gait.
- **Contractures:** Affected children often develop joint contractures that are initially dynamic but later become fixed.

### **6. CLASSIFICATION**

**a. Classification Based on Motor Deficit (Physiologic and Topographic)**

- **Spastic CP (Pyramidal):** The most frequent type, characterized by spasticity and brisk reflexes.
    - _Spastic Quadriplegia:_ Involves all four limbs. Often caused by perinatal asphyxia. Strongly linked to intellectual disability, microcephaly, seizures, and pseudobulbar palsy. Radiology shows cystic encephalomalacia.
    - _Spastic Diplegia:_ Lower limbs are more severely affected than upper limbs. Highly linked to prematurity. Intellect is often preserved. Radiology typically shows periventricular leukomalacia.
    - _Spastic Hemiplegia/Monoplegia:_ Usually results from a vascular insult (perinatal stroke). Characterized by early hand preference in infancy. Intellect may be preserved. Radiology often reveals porencephaly.
- **Dyskinetic or Extrapyramidal CP:** Results from asphyxia or kernicterus. Intellect is usually preserved. It manifests with rigidity, severe drooling, and abnormal movements like choreoathetosis (slow, writhing movements) or dystonia. Radiology may indicate basal ganglia abnormalities.
- **Ataxic CP:** Caused by cerebellar malformations, presenting with cerebellar signs (ataxia, intention tremors, nystagmus) and hypotonia.
- **Mixed CP:** Features a combination of both spastic and extrapyramidal/dystonic signs.

**b. Classification Based on Functional/Therapeutic Status**

- _Class I:_ No practical limitation of activity; no treatment required.
- _Class II:_ Slight to moderate limitation; requires minimal bracing and therapy.
- _Class III:_ Moderate to gross limitation; requires bracing and intervention by a specialized CP team.
- _Class IV:_ Total inability to carry on useful physical activity; requires long-term institutionalization and treatment.

### **7. COMORBIDITIES AND ASSOCIATED HANDICAPS**

Children with CP suffer from numerous associated handicaps.

- **CNS:** Mental retardation (seen in 25–50% of cases), behavioral problems, and seizures/epilepsy.
- **Vision:** Squint (strabismus), cataracts, refractive errors, coloboma, retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), and partial/complete blindness.
- **Hearing:** Partial or complete deafness (particularly in athetotic CP) and receptive auditory aphasia.
- **Speech:** Dysarthria, dyslalia, and aphasia.
- **Gastrointestinal/Dental:** Severe feeding difficulties, recurrent drooling, constipation, incontinence, dental caries, and malocclusion.

### **8. EVALUATION AND DIAGNOSIS**

- **Clinical Assessment:** A highly detailed history (detecting antecedent events like prematurity or asphyxia) and meticulous physical/neurologic examinations are required. Evaluate developmental status, language, vision, hearing, and look for dysmorphism or neurocutaneous markers.
- **Standardized Tools:** Spasticity and motor function are classified utilizing tools like the Gross Motor Function Classification System and the Modified Ashworth Scale.
- **Investigations:**
    - _Neuroimaging:_ CT scan, MRI, or cranial ultrasound are vital to detect structural malformations, porencephalic cysts, atrophy, periventricular leukomalacia, or basal ganglia abnormalities.
    - _EEG:_ Indicated for the evaluation and localization of associated seizure disorders.

### **9. MANAGEMENT**

CP cannot be cured, so the ultimate goal of management is to achieve the maximum possible functional ability, mobility, and independence. This requires a coordinated, multidisciplinary team comprising a pediatrician, neurologist, physiotherapist, occupational therapist, clinical psychologist, orthopedic surgeon, speech therapist, ophthalmologist, ENT specialist, and special educator.

- **Physiotherapy and Occupational Therapy:** The cornerstone of CP management. Involves active/passive exercises, training in rhythmic contractions, and relaxation of spastic muscles to prevent contractures. Occupational therapy helps build functional life skills.
- **Orthotics:** Use of walking calipers, splints, night splints, wedges, and special chairs to maintain posture and prevent deformities.
- **Medical/Pharmacological Therapy:**
    - _Generalized Spasticity:_ Diazepam, baclofen, tizanidine, or dantrolene. An implantable intrathecal baclofen pump can deliver muscle relaxants directly into the spinal fluid.
    - _Localized Spasticity:_ Botulinum toxin A injections into specific muscles.
    - _Dystonia/Athetosis:_ Managed with trihexyphenidyl, levodopa, or chlordiazepoxide.
    - _Associated Seizures:_ Managed with appropriate anticonvulsants.
- **Surgical Management:**
    - _Orthopedic Surgery:_ Indicated for correcting fixed deformities, stabilizing joints, and performing tendon releases or lengthenings to compensate for muscle imbalance.
    - _Selective Posterior Rhizotomy:_ A highly specialized neurosurgical procedure for children with severe lower limb spasticity. It involves electrical stimulation and selective sectioning of abnormal posterior rootlets of the cauda equina, drastically reducing spasticity and often allowing the child to achieve walking milestones.
