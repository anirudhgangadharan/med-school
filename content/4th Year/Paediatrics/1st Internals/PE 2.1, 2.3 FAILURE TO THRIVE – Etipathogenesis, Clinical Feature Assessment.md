### **1. DEFINITION OF FAILURE TO THRIVE (FTT)**

Failure to thrive (FTT) is a descriptive term rather than a specific diagnosis, primarily applied to infants and young children (usually up to 2 to 5 years of age) who show a failure of expected weight gain and a striking lack of physical well-being.

- It usually refers to a child whose weight is below the 3rd or 5th centile for their age and sex, or a child who exhibits a significant change in the rate of growth (e.g., weight for age or weight for length/height crosses two major centiles downward over a period of time).
- Some authorities consider it synonymous with "psychosomatic growth failure" or "maternal deprivation syndrome".

### **2. ETIOPATHOGENESIS**

Traditionally, FTT is classified into non-organic (psychosocial) and organic causes, though both factors may coexist. Poor nutritional intake and feeding problems are by far the most common overarching causes.

**a. Non-organic (Psychosocial / Extrinsic) Causes** Non-organic factors account for up to 80% of FTT cases.

- **Poor Dietary Intake:** Inadequate calorie provision, highly diluted formula (often due to ignorance or economic constraints), delayed introduction of semisolids, and erratic feeding practices.
- **Social and Emotional Deprivation:** Child Abuse and Neglect (CAN), inadequate infant-mother bonding, parental disharmony, unwanted child, emotional trauma from an unstable family, or a psychopath/inexperienced mother.
- **Socioeconomic Factors:** Poverty, bad economy, and general ignorance regarding child nutrition.

**b. Organic (Intrinsic) Causes**

- **Gastrointestinal Disorders:** Malabsorption syndromes (celiac disease, cystic fibrosis, tropical sprue), lactose intolerance, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), pyloric stenosis, inflammatory bowel disease, and Hirschsprung disease.
- **Infections:** Chronic intestinal parasitosis (e.g., giardiasis, ascariasis, threadworm) is a leading cause in developing countries. Other chronic infections include tuberculosis, HIV, and chronic urinary tract infections.
- **Cardiopulmonary Diseases:** Congenital heart disease, cystic fibrosis, and bronchial asthma.
- **Neurological Disorders:** Mental retardation and cerebral palsy.
- **Endocrine and Metabolic Disorders:** Hypothyroidism, diabetes mellitus, galactosemia, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, and inborn errors of metabolism.
- **Renal Disorders:** Renal tubular acidosis and chronic kidney disease.
- **Miscellaneous:** Lead poisoning, multiple congenital anomalies, and malignancies.

### **3. CLINICAL FEATURES**

- **Growth Retardation:** The infant is markedly underweight for their age (<3rd percentile) and may show stunting in height/length. Growth charts typically display one of three patterns: fall-off in weight gain, static weight, or overt weight loss.
- **General Appearance:** The child often appears thin, small, and inactive with expressionless facies.
- **Behavioral Signs:** The infant may exhibit poor gross motor activity, delayed vocalization, poor responsiveness to the environment, and a tendency to remain absorbed in thumb-sucking.
- **Signs of Neglect (in Non-organic FTT):** The child may present with hygienic neglect, such as severe diaper rash, impetigo, scabies, unwashed skin, uncut nails, and dirty clothing.
- **Cognitive Deficits:** FTT is often associated with poor development, learning difficulties, and suboptimal cognitive functioning.

### **4. ASSESSMENT AND DIAGNOSIS**

The assessment of FTT requires a step-by-step approach to differentiate between inadequate intake, malabsorption, and systemic disease.

**a. History Taking**

- **Dietary History:** Elicit detailed information on the quantity and quality of breast milk, artificial formula preparation (e.g., over-dilution), and the introduction of weaning foods. Calculate the actual calorie and protein intake.
- **Feeding Behavior:** Assess the infant's appetite (e.g., voracious appetite in cystic fibrosis vs. poor appetite in celiac disease) and check for persistent vomiting or chronic diarrhea.
- **Psychosocial History:** Look for adverse family environments, economic stress, maternal depression, and signs of emotional deprivation or neglect.
- **Medical History:** Ask about a history of frequent infections, recurrent chest issues (TB, cystic fibrosis), or chronic diseases in the family.

**b. Physical Examination and Anthropometry**

- Record weight, length/height, and head circumference accurately and plot them on standardized growth charts (e.g., WHO or CDC charts) to establish the exact pattern of growth failure.
- Conduct a thorough systemic examination to rule out underlying organic diseases like congenital heart defects, respiratory conditions, or abdominal masses.

**c. Observation of Parent-Child Interaction**

- Observing the actual act of feeding and the parent-child interaction is the gold standard for recognizing non-organic feeding problems.
- Look for inappropriate feeding techniques, forced feeding, lack of eye contact, or a highly strung mother.

**d. Investigations** Extensive laboratory testing is discouraged unless specifically indicated by the history and physical examination. If an organic cause is suspected, an initial screening panel should include:

- Complete Blood Count (CBC) with ESR.
- Urine microscopy and culture (to rule out UTI).
- Stool microscopy (meticulous examination on at least 3 successive days to rule out ova and cysts like _Giardia_ or _Ascaris_) and stool culture.
- Kidney function tests and liver function tests.
- Serum electrolytes.

**e. Therapeutic Trial (Diagnostic and Therapeutic)**

- A trial of adequate calorie feeding, preferably after admitting the child to the hospital, is the definitive test for non-organic FTT.
- If the child exhibits dramatic and impressive weight gain and improved behavior over a minimum of 1 to 2 weeks with proper feeding, environmental manipulation, and emotional warmth, the diagnosis of non-organic (psychosocial) FTT is confirmed.
- During this period, any detected intestinal parasites must be actively treated.
