1. Hyperkalemia—management.
**MANAGEMENT OF HYPERKALEMIA**

**DEFINITION AND CLINICAL CONTEXT** Hyperkalemia is a serious and potentially life-threatening electrolyte disturbance defined as a serum potassium level exceeding 5.5 mEq/L. It is considered a medical emergency because sudden and rapid onset can lead to severe cardiac arrhythmias, marked muscular weakness with flaccid paralysis, bradycardia, shock, and even cardiac arrest. Treatment must be individualized based on the patient's age, clinical presentation, absolute potassium level, and progressive ECG changes (e.g., tall peaked T waves, prolonged PR interval, wide QRS complex, or sine waves).

**TREATMENT PROTOCOL** The management of hyperkalemia is structured into distinct steps aimed at immediate cardioprotection, shifting potassium intracellularly, and eliminating excess potassium from the body.

**1. General Measures and Immediate Monitoring**

- **Discontinuation of Potassium Sources:** Promptly discontinue all intravenous fluids containing potassium, oral potassium supplements, and packed red blood cell transfusions.
- **Withdrawal of Offending Drugs:** Stop medications that impair potassium excretion or shift potassium extracellularly, such as potassium-sparing diuretics, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, NSAIDs, and heparin.
- **Continuous Monitoring:** Initiate continuous ECG monitoring to observe for toxic cardiac effects and response to therapy.

**2. Stabilization of Myocardial Cell Membrane (Cardioprotection)** To prevent or reverse cardiac arrhythmias, the myocardial cell membrane must be stabilized immediately.

- **Intravenous Calcium:** Administer 10% Calcium Gluconate at a dose of 0.5 to 1 mL/kg (or 0.3 to 0.5 mL/kg) very slowly over 5 to 10 minutes.
- **Precautions:** This must be given under strict cardiac monitoring, and the infusion should be discontinued immediately if bradycardia develops.

**3. Enhancing Cellular Uptake of Potassium (Intracellular Shift)** These therapies temporarily drive extracellular potassium into the intracellular compartment, lowering serum levels rapidly but transiently.

- **Sodium Bicarbonate:** Rapid IV administration of 1 to 3 mEq/kg (or 1 to 2 mEq/kg of body weight given over 20 to 30 minutes) corrects coexisting acidosis and shifts potassium into the cells.
- **Glucose and Insulin Infusion:** This combination safely drives potassium into the cells. The recommended dosage is 0.5 to 1 g/kg of 10% to 20% glucose along with 1 unit of crystalline (regular) insulin for every 3 g of glucose. Alternatively, 0.3 units of regular insulin per gram of glucose can be infused over 2 hours.
- **$\beta$-Adrenergic Agonists:** Nebulized or intravenous salbutamol (e.g., 5 to 10 mg nebulized) or terbutaline promotes the rapid intracellular shift of potassium.

**4. Total Body Potassium Elimination** Because shifting potassium intracellularly is only a temporary measure, strategies must be employed to attain a negative potassium balance by eliminating it from the body.

- **Ion-Exchange Resins:** Sodium polystyrene sulfonate (Kayexalate) binds potassium in the gastrointestinal tract in exchange for sodium. It is given orally or as a retention enema at a dose of 1 g/kg (maximum 15 g/dose) in 20% to 30% sorbitol.
- **Diuretics:** Loop diuretics (such as frusemide) or thiazide diuretics can be administered to enhance renal potassium excretion, provided that renal function is adequately maintained.
- **Kidney Replacement Therapy (Dialysis):** Hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis is the definitive treatment for severe, symptomatic hyperkalemia (especially when K+ > 7.0 mEq/L with ECG changes) that is refractory to other medical therapies. Dialysis is particularly indicated in patients with impaired kidney function (acute or chronic kidney disease) or severe tumor lysis syndrome.

**5. Treatment of Underlying Etiology** Once acute stabilization is achieved, the primary or precipitating cause must be identified and managed. For example, children with primary or secondary hypoaldosteronism require maintenance therapy with stress-dose steroids and mineralocorticoids (fludrocortisone). Treat conditions like Barter syndrome or Gitelman syndrome specifically, and ensure volume resuscitation with normal saline if hypovolemia is present.

2. Treatment of hyponatremia
**TREATMENT OF HYPONATREMIA**

The management of hyponatremia (serum Na+ < 135 mEq/L) dictates a systematic approach. The first step is to determine whether the hyponatremia is acute (<48 hours) or chronic (>48 hours), symptomatic or asymptomatic, and to carefully evaluate the patient's volume status.

If the distinction between acute and chronic hyponatremia is unclear at the initial evaluation, it must be assumed to be chronic and corrected gradually to prevent severe neurological complications.

**I. ACUTE AND SYMPTOMATIC HYPONATREMIA (MEDICAL EMERGENCY)** Symptomatic hyponatremia (presenting with seizures, lethargy, or coma) requires early recognition and prompt management with intravenous boluses of hypertonic saline, irrespective of the chronicity.

- **Drug of Choice:** 3% Sodium Chloride (Hypertonic Saline).
- **Dosage:**
    - Administer 3–5 mL/kg of 3% NaCl over 1 hour, OR a rapid bolus of 2 mL/kg over 10–20 minutes.
    - _Alternative regimen:_ 10 mL/kg (maximum 12 mL/kg) at a rate of 1 mL/minute intravenously.
- **Initial Goal:** To increase the serum Na+ concentration by approximately 5 mEq/L, which is usually sufficient for the resolution of acute symptoms (patient becoming awake, alert, and responding to commands).
- **Monitoring:** Serum Na+ must be monitored every 1 to 2 hours during hypertonic saline infusion. Once symptoms resolve, the 3% NaCl infusion is stopped.

**II. CHRONIC AND ASYMPTOMATIC HYPONATREMIA** Chronic asymptomatic hyponatremia should be corrected slowly over 48 to 72 hours or longer, depending on the severity.

- **Calculation of Sodium Deficit:**
    - $\text{Na}^+ \text{ deficit (mEq)} = (\text{Desired Serum Na}^+ - \text{Actual Serum Na}^+) \times \text{Body Weight (kg)} \times 0.6$. _(Note: The target desired Na+ for calculation is usually 130 to 135 mEq/L)_.
- **Rate of Correction (High Yield):**
    - The rise in serum Na+ **should not exceed 0.3 to 0.5 mEq/hour**, or **a maximum of 10 mEq/L in the first 24 hours**.
    - For the subsequent days, the correction rate should be **8 to 12 mEq/L every 24 hours** until the serum Na+ reaches safe levels.
- **Complication of Rapid Correction:** Aggressive and rapid correction of chronic hyponatremia leads to the extrusion of intracellular osmolytes, which causes massive osmotic shifts resulting in **Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome (Pontine Myelinosis)**. This presents biphasically with initial improvement followed 2–7 days later by dysarthria, lethargy, spastic quadriparesis, and pseudobulbar palsy.

**III. MANAGEMENT BASED ON VOLUME STATUS (ETIOLOGY)** Treatment must be strictly tailored to the underlying pathophysiological state of the body's fluid volume.

**1. Hypovolemic Hyponatremia (Volume Depleted)**

- **With Hypotension/Shock:** Volume resuscitation using isotonic fluid (Normal Saline) takes absolute precedence over the specific treatment of hyponatremia, even at the risk of a sudden increase in serum Na+.
- **Stable patients:** The calculated Na+ deficit is administered as Normal Saline. WHO ORS is preferable for patients able to tolerate oral rehydration.
- _Note:_ Normal saline should not be used to treat true hyponatremic encephalopathy in euvolemic states, as it is not sufficiently hypertonic to reduce cerebral edema.

**2. Euvolemic / Normovolemic Hyponatremia (e.g., SIADH)**

- **Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuresis (SIAD/SIADH):** The mainstay of therapy is **Fluid Restriction**. Diuretics (like furosemide) and oral salt supplementation may be added if required. V2 receptor antagonists (vaptans) block ADH binding but are not yet recommended for treating hyponatremic encephalopathy in children.
- **Cerebral Salt Wasting (CSW):** Treated aggressively with volume and salt replacement, alongside **Fludrocortisone**.

**3. Hypervolemic Hyponatremia (Volume Expanded)**

- Seen in congestive cardiac failure (CCF), acute/chronic renal failure, and nephrotic syndrome.
- **Management:** Requires strict **Sodium and Fluid restriction** combined with **Diuretics** (e.g., furosemide) to safeguard against pulmonary edema and CCF.
- _Exception:_ In cases of hyponatremia accompanying gross hypoproteinemia, fluid restriction should generally be avoided.
3. Hypocalcemia—clinical features
**HYPOCALCEMIA**

**DEFINITION** Hypocalcemia is defined as a serum calcium level of less than 8 mg/dL or an ionized calcium level below 4 mg/dL. In the neonatal period, it is defined by a total serum calcium of less than 7 mg/dL (ionized calcium < 4 mg/dL) in preterm infants, and less than 8 mg/dL (ionized calcium < 4.8 mg/dL) in term neonates.

**CLINICAL FEATURES** The clinical manifestations of hypocalcemia primarily result from central nervous system irritability and poor muscle contractility. The presentation varies based on the severity, chronicity, and age of the patient.

**1. Neonatal Presentations**

- Neonates with hypocalcemia (especially early-onset) may often remain asymptomatic.
- Symptomatic neonates classically present with lethargy, poor feeding, jitteriness, vomiting, abdominal distention, and seizures.
- Other associated manifestations include stupor, tremors, apneic spells, tachypnea, and cyanosis.

**2. Neurological and Muscular Manifestations (Tetany)**

- **Overt Tetany:** Characterized by muscle cramps, numbness, and tingling. Children may develop overt signs of nerve irritability such as muscular twitching, cramps, and stridor.
- **Carpopedal Spasm:** A classic manifestation characterized by painful flexion of the wrist and metacarpophalangeal joints, extension of the interphalangeal joints, and adduction of the thumb.
- **Laryngospasm:** A severe and potentially life-threatening spasm of the laryngeal muscles that presents with stridor.
- **Seizures:** Generalized or focal seizures are a common presentation, particularly in neonates towards the end of the first week of life.

**3. Signs of Latent Tetany (High-Yield Clinical Signs)** In cases of mild or latent hypocalcemia, specific clinical maneuvers can be used to elicit signs of peripheral nerve irritability:

- **Chvostek Sign:** Twitching of the orbicularis oculi and mouth elicited by tapping the facial nerve anterior to the external auditory meatus. _(Note: This can occasionally be a normal finding in neonates)._
- **Trousseau Sign:** A carpopedal spasm elicited by inflating an arm blood pressure cuff above the systolic pressure for 3 to 5 minutes, which triggers a spasm of the hand muscles due to ischemia-induced nerve excitability.

**4. Cardiovascular Manifestations**

- **ECG Changes:** The hallmark electrocardiographic finding is a prolonged corrected QT interval (QTc > 0.45 seconds).
- **Cardiac Function:** Cardiac function may be significantly impaired due to poor muscle contractility. Severe, chronic hypocalcemia or vitamin D deficiency can occasionally affect cardiac muscle function, resulting in dilated cardiomyopathy and congestive heart failure.

**5. Chronic and Syndromic Manifestations**

- **Skeletal Changes (Calcipenic Rickets):** Prolonged hypocalcemia typically presents with features of rickets or osteomalacia. Clinical examination may reveal frontal bossing, widely open anterior fontanelles, craniotabes, rachitic rosary, thickening of wrists and ankles, and bony deformities such as bow-legs (genu varum) or knock-knees (genu valgum).
- **Dental and Ectodermal Changes:** Dentition is often delayed, and the development of tooth enamel is impaired. Patients with idiopathic hypoparathyroidism may specifically exhibit defective dental enamel, brittle nails, and an increased susceptibility to fungal infections like moniliasis.
- **Ocular Changes:** Long-standing hypocalcemia, such as that seen in idiopathic hypoparathyroidism or pseudohypoparathyroidism, can lead to the development of lenticular cataracts.
