1. Define protein-energy malnutrition. Discuss various classifications of PEM. Define marasmus, kwashiorkor. Discuss the etiology, pathophysiology, clinical features (cardinal features) and (principles of) management of (severe acute) protein-energy malnutrition (kwashiorkor/marasmic kwashiorkor/marasmus).
**PROTEIN-ENERGY MALNUTRITION (PEM)**

**I. DEFINITIONS**

- **Protein-Energy Malnutrition (PEM):** It is a broad class of clinical conditions resulting from varying degrees of protein lack and energy (calorie) inadequacy. The term replaces "protein-calorie malnutrition" as it is recognized that the major limiting factors in a child's diet are both energy and proteins.
- **Nutritional Marasmus:** An extreme degree of adaptation to prolonged inadequacy of calories (energy) and proteins. It results from a gross deficiency of energy.
- **Kwashiorkor:** A state of adaptation failure (dysadaptation) to prolonged protein and energy inadequacy, predominantly caused by a gross deficiency of proteins, often precipitated by a sudden stress like an infection (e.g., measles, diarrhea).
- **Marasmic Kwashiorkor:** A mixed form demonstrating a combination of clinical features of both kwashiorkor and marasmus (e.g., severe wasting of muscle and fat along with edema).

---

**II. CLASSIFICATION OF PEM (HIGH YIELD)** Various classifications are used to grade the severity of malnutrition based on anthropometric indices.

**1. Indian Academy of Pediatrics (IAP) Classification** Based on weight-for-age as a percentage of the expected weight:

- **Grade I:** 71% to 80% of expected weight.
- **Grade II:** 61% to 70% of expected weight.
- **Grade III:** 51% to 60% of expected weight.
- **Grade IV:** $\le$ 50% of expected weight. _(Note: The letter "K" is added if pitting edema is present)._

**2. Wellcome (International) Classification** Based on weight-for-age percentage and the presence or absence of edema:

- **Undernutrition:** 60–80% weight without edema.
- **Kwashiorkor:** 60–80% weight with edema.
- **Nutritional Marasmus:** <60% weight without edema.
- **Marasmic Kwashiorkor:** <60% weight with edema.

**3. Waterlow's Classification** Differentiates acute vs. chronic malnutrition:

- **Wasting (Acute):** Low weight-for-height (reflects recent weight loss).
- **Stunting (Chronic/Nutritional dwarfing):** Low height-for-age (reflects prolonged malnutrition).

**4. WHO Classification of Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM)** In children 6 to 59 months of age, SAM is defined by any of the following three criteria:

1. Weight-for-height (or length) below –3 Standard Deviations (<–3 SD) on the WHO growth charts.
2. Presence of bilateral pitting (bipedal) edema.
3. Mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) strictly below 11.5 cm.

---

**III. ETIOLOGY AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY**

**A. Etiology (Ecology of Malnutrition)**

- **Primary Causes (Dietetic):** Ignorance, faulty feeding habits, inadequate breastfeeding, highly diluted formula feeding, delayed introduction of complementary foods, poverty, and large family size.
- **Secondary Causes:** Recurrent infections (diarrhea, measles, tuberculosis, malaria), parasitic infestations (giardiasis, ascariasis), and malabsorption syndromes (celiac disease, cystic fibrosis).

**B. Pathophysiological Hypotheses**

1. **Gopalan’s Adaptation Hypothesis:** Marasmus represents a successful extreme adaptation by the body to prolonged starvation (low somatomedin, high growth hormone, low insulin limit tissue breakdown and spare the brain). Kwashiorkor occurs when this adaptation mechanism fails (dysadaptation), typically precipitated by an acute stress like an infection.
2. **Golden’s Free Radical Hypothesis:** Kwashiorkor results from an overproduction of toxic free radicals (triggered by infections or toxins like aflatoxin) and a simultaneous breakdown of antioxidant defense mechanisms (due to deficiency of vitamins A, C, E, zinc, copper, selenium).

---

**IV. CLINICAL FEATURES (CARDINAL SIGNS)**

**A. Nutritional Marasmus** Typically affects infants under 1-2 years.

- **Severe Wasting:** Remarkable wasting of both muscles and subcutaneous fat.
- **Facies:** "Monkey facies" or senile, wizened appearance due to loss of the buccal pad of fat.
- **Baggy Pants Appearance:** Loose, hanging folds of skin over the gluteal region.
- **Sensorium:** The child is extremely irritable, crying, and excessively hungry (until very advanced stages).
- **Negative Findings:** NO edema, NO striking hair changes, NO flaky-paint dermatosis, and the liver is usually shrunken (not enlarged).

**B. Kwashiorkor** Typically affects children 1 to 4 years of age.

- **Essential Features (Must be present):**
    1. Growth retardation (low weight and height).
    2. Muscle wasting but with retention of some subcutaneous fat.
    3. Hypoalbuminemic pitting edema (starting at the feet/pretibial region and may progress to anasarca).
    4. Mental apathy (silent, listless, inert, lack of interest).
- **Non-Essential/Variable Features:**
    - _Hair changes:_ Hypochromotrichia (light-colored), sparse, easy pluckability, and "flag sign" (alternate light and dark bands).
    - _Skin changes:_ "Flaky-paint dermatosis" (hyperpigmentation with raw red peeling skin) and "crazy-pavement dermatosis".
    - _Hepatomegaly:_ Soft, smooth, enlarged fatty liver.

---

**V. MANAGEMENT OF SEVERE ACUTE MALNUTRITION (SAM)**

The WHO strictly guidelines protocolize the management of SAM in hospitals in two phases comprising 10 essential steps. Children with uncomplicated SAM (good appetite, no serious medical conditions) can be managed at home. Complicated SAM must be hospitalized.

**Phase I: Stabilization Phase (Days 1 to 7)** Aims to restore homeostasis and treat life-threatening conditions (No iron is given in this phase, and catch-up weight gain is NOT the goal).

- **Step 1: Treat/Prevent Hypoglycemia:** Give 10% dextrose (50 mL or 5 mL/kg) IV or orally, followed by immediate 2-hourly feeds to prevent a drop in blood sugar.
- **Step 2: Treat/Prevent Hypothermia:** Keep the child warmly clothed (including the head), ensure skin-to-skin contact (Kangaroo mother care), and use overhead warmers. Hypothermia (<35.5°C) often coexists with hypoglycemia and infection.
- **Step 3: Treat/Prevent Dehydration:** Do NOT use standard ORS. Use **ReSoMal** (Rehydration Solution for Malnutrition), which has lower sodium (45 mmol/L) and higher potassium (40 mmol/L). Administer slowly to avoid heart failure. If in shock, give half-normal saline with 5% dextrose or Ringer's lactate with 5% dextrose carefully.
- **Step 4: Correct Electrolyte Imbalance:** All SAM children have excess intracellular sodium and deficient potassium/magnesium. Give supplemental Potassium (3–4 mEq/kg/day) and Magnesium (0.4–0.6 mEq/kg/day). STRICTLY restrict sodium.
- **Step 5: Treat/Prevent Infection:** Routine signs of infection (fever) may be absent. Empirically treat all SAM patients with broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g., parenteral Ampicillin and Gentamicin).
- **Step 6: Correct Micronutrient Deficiencies:** Give Vitamin A (large dose on day 1), Folic acid, Zinc, and Copper. **Do NOT give Iron** in the stabilization phase as it can promote bacterial growth and free-radical damage.
- **Step 7: Initiate Cautious Feeding:** Start with **F-75 starter diet** (75 kcal/100 mL and 0.9g protein/100 mL). Feeds must be small, frequent (2-hourly), low in osmolarity, and low in lactose. Total energy should not exceed 100 kcal/kg/day initially.

**Phase II: Rehabilitation Phase (Weeks 2 to 6)** Aims for rapid catch-up growth.

- **Step 8: Achieve Catch-up Growth:** Transition from F-75 to the **F-100 diet** (100 kcal/100 mL) or energy-dense home foods. Increase caloric intake to 150–200 kcal/kg/day and protein to 4–6 g/kg/day. Iron supplementation (3 mg/kg/day) is started now since the child is gaining weight and infections are controlled.
- **Step 9: Provide Sensory Stimulation:** Provide a cheerful, loving environment, structured play therapy, and physical activity to reverse mental apathy and delayed development.
- **Step 10: Prepare for Follow-up:** Discharge the child when weight-for-height/length reaches -2 SD or higher, and MUAC is >12.5 cm. Advise the mother on periodic monitoring, proper weaning, and continuous immunizations.

2. Describe clinical features of marasmus and kwashiorkor. Discuss the complications in a child with grade IV malnutrition
**I. CLINICAL FEATURES OF MARASMUS AND KWASHIORKOR**

Protein-energy malnutrition (PEM) is a continuous spectrum of manifestations, with nutritional marasmus and kwashiorkor representing the two major, severe clinical syndromes.

**A. NUTRITIONAL MARASMUS** Nutritional marasmus predominantly results from an extreme deficiency of energy (calories), although protein deficiency also coexists. It usually occurs in children under 3 years of age, with a peak incidence during the first year of life.

- **Muscle and Fat Wasting (High Yield):** A characteristic feature is the remarkable, severe wasting of both muscles and subcutaneous fat. The child appears very thin, essentially "skin and bones," with no fat.
- **Facies:** The loss of the buccal pad of fat creates a wizened, aged, or shrivelled face, classically referred to as "monkey facies".
- **Gluteal and Axillary Changes:** The loss of subcutaneous fat leads to loose folds of skin hanging down from the buttocks, giving the characteristic "baggy pants" appearance. The axillary pad of fat is also diminished.
- **Edema:** Clinical edema is absolutely absent.
- **Psychomotor Changes:** In the early stages, the child is highly irritable, hungry, and craves food. Some affected children may appear surprisingly alert in spite of their severe condition. However, in advanced stages, the child may become miserable, apathetic, and anorexic.
- **Abdomen:** The liver is rather shrunk, which is in sharp contrast to the enlarged fatty liver seen in kwashiorkor.
- **Skin and Hair:** Classical flaky-paint dermatosis and significant hair changes (like the flag sign) are usually absent.

**B. KWASHIORKOR** Kwashiorkor results from a gross deficiency of proteins along with energy deficiency. It is primarily seen in the preschool age group, usually affecting children between 1 to 4 years of age.

The diagnosis relies on four essential features (minimal diagnostic criteria):

1. **Growth Retardation:** Evidenced by low weight and subnormal height for age.
2. **Muscle Wasting with Fat Retention:** Marked muscle wasting occurs, but importantly, there is retention of some subcutaneous fat.
3. **Psychomotor Changes:** Mental apathy is a hallmark; the child exhibits a silent, listless inertness, a miserable appearance, and a total lack of interest in their surroundings.
4. **Edema:** Hypoalbuminemic pitting edema is a required essential feature. It usually starts in the legs and feet, and in advanced cases spreads to the face, hands, and trunk, resulting in ascites. Due to edema, the child may have a "fat, sugar baby" appearance or moon facies.

**Non-Essential (Variable) Features of Kwashiorkor:**

- **Hair Changes:** Hair becomes light-colored (hypochromotrichia), sparse, coarse, and easily pluckable. Alternate bands of light and dark color along the hair shaft indicate periods of good and poor nutrition, famously termed the **"flag sign"**.
- **Skin Changes (Dermatosis):** Characterized by areas of hyperpigmentation intervened by areas of raw red skin from shed superficial flakes, known as **flaky-paint dermatosis**. **Crazy-pavement dermatosis** and pellagra-like lesions may also be seen.
- **Hepatomegaly:** The liver is invariably enlarged, soft, and smooth due to severe fatty infiltration, often reaching down to the umbilicus.
- **Associated Deficiencies:** Moderate to severe anemia, diarrhea, and severe vitamin A deficiency (leading to keratomalacia) are very common.

---

**II. COMPLICATIONS IN A CHILD WITH GRADE IV (ADVANCED) MALNUTRITION**

A child with Grade IV malnutrition (weight below 50% of the expected for age) or severe acute malnutrition is at a very high risk of life-threatening complications. These must be anticipated and managed as emergencies:

**1. Dehydration and Dyselectrolytemia:**

- Usually complicating an episode of diarrhea (often with lactose intolerance), dehydration can be rapidly fatal.
- Significant electrolyte imbalances are universal, notably profound total body potassium depletion (causing paralytic ileus and hypotonia) and magnesium deficiency.

**2. Superadded Infections:**

- Because malnutrition severely impairs cell-mediated immunity and complement systems, these children have a high incidence of overt and "hidden" infections.
- Common infections include septicemia, bronchopneumonia, diarrhea, pyoderma, urinary tract infections (UTI), scabies, and tuberculosis.

**3. Hypothermia:**

- An unattended rectal temperature of less than 35°C (95°F) is a grave complication.
- It often occurs alongside hypoglycemia and infection as a triad, and can lead to the sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS).

**4. Hypoglycemia:**

- Reflects impaired glucose stores and altered metabolism.
- It contributes to a poor response to nutritional therapy, carries a poor prognosis, and may prove fatal if not managed with prompt glucose administration.

**5. Congestive Cardiac Failure (CCF):**

- The cardiac muscle is atrophied, making the heart small and functionally weak.
- CCF is usually precipitated iatrogenically by an excessive intake of sodium and intravenous fluids, or secondary to severe anemia.

**6. Severe Anemia:**

- Moderate to severe anemia is almost always associated, resulting from combined nutritional deficits (iron, folate, B12) or as a consequence of systemic infections. It can further precipitate cardiac failure.

**7. Bleeding Diathesis:**

- Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) or specific vitamin K deficiency can complicate the clinical picture, presenting as life-threatening bleeds.

**8. Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS):**

- Sudden unexplained death may occur 4 to 7 days after admission, usually with the precise cause remaining unclear despite medical support.

3. State the WHO criteria for severe acute malnutrition. Describe the management in the first week of SAM
**SEVERE ACUTE MALNUTRITION (SAM)**

**I. WHO CRITERIA FOR SAM** Severe acute malnutrition (SAM) in children aged 6 to 59 months is defined by the WHO and UNICEF by the presence of **any one** of the following three criteria:

1. **Weight-for-height/length:** Below -3 standard deviation score (< -3 SDS) on the WHO Growth Standard.
2. **Visible Severe Wasting:** Presence of bipedal edema.
3. **Mid-Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC):** Below 11.5 cm (115 mm). _(Note: MUAC is not used as a criterion in infants below 6 months of age)._

---

**II. MANAGEMENT IN THE FIRST WEEK (STABILIZATION PHASE)**

The initial management of a child with complicated SAM requires hospitalization and focuses on the **Stabilization Phase** (Days 1 to 7). The primary goal during the first week is to restore homeostasis, prevent death, and treat life-threatening medical complications rather than to promote rapid weight gain.

The WHO recommends a standardized 10-step protocol, with the first week specifically focusing on Steps 1 through 7:

**Step 1: Treat/Prevent Hypoglycemia**

- **Definition:** Blood glucose < 54 mg/dL (3 mmol/L). Hypoglycemia is often asymptomatic and usually coexists with hypothermia and infection.
- **Action:** Immediately give 50 mL of 10% glucose or sucrose solution orally or via a nasogastric (NG) tube,.
- **Prevention:** Initiate 2-hourly feeding with starter formula (F-75) immediately, continuing through the day and night,.

**Step 2: Treat/Prevent Hypothermia**

- **Definition:** Rectal temperature < 35.5°C or axillary temperature < 35°C.
- **Action:** Provide warmth using skin-to-skin contact (Kangaroo Mother Care), warm clothing, and a cap to cover the head,. Use overhead warmers or heated rooms if necessary,.
- **Precaution:** Avoid rapid rewarming as it can cause fatal hemodynamic disequilibrium,. Prevent by feeding 2-hourly immediately.

**Step 3: Treat/Prevent Dehydration**

- **Assessment:** Classical signs of dehydration are unreliable in SAM due to loss of subcutaneous fat (marasmus) or presence of edema (kwashiorkor).
- **Action (No Shock):** Standard WHO ORS should **not** be used. Rehydrate using a special low-sodium, high-potassium solution called **ReSoMal** (Rehydration Solution for Malnutrition) given orally or by NG tube at 5–10 mL/kg/hour,.
- **Action (Severe Dehydration with Shock):** Intravenous fluids are strictly reserved for impending shock,. Use Ringer's lactate with 5% dextrose or half-normal saline (N/2) with 5% dextrose at a slow infusion rate of 15 mL/kg over the first hour.

**Step 4: Correct Electrolyte Imbalance**

- **Pathology:** All severely malnourished children have excess total body sodium (even if serum sodium is low) and severe deficiencies of intracellular potassium and magnesium.
- **Action:** Add supplemental Potassium (3–4 mEq/kg/day) and Magnesium (0.3 mL/kg of 50% magnesium sulfate IM once, followed by oral supplements),.
- **Precaution (High Yield):** Strictly restrict dietary sodium. Never treat edema with diuretics.

**Step 5: Treat/Prevent Infection**

- **Concept:** Classical signs of infection (like fever) are frequently absent in SAM due to immune suppression,. Assume severe systemic infection in all admitted cases,.
- **Action:** Initiate broad-spectrum antibiotics routinely.
    - _Uncomplicated/No obvious infection:_ Oral amoxicillin or cotrimoxazole.
    - _Complicated/Severe:_ Intravenous Ampicillin (50 mg/kg 6-hourly) plus Gentamicin (5-7.5 mg/kg once daily) for 7 days.

**Step 6: Correct Micronutrient Deficiencies**

- **Action:** Administer Vitamin A immediately on day 1 (dose based on age: 2 lakh IU for >1 yr, 1 lakh IU for 6-12 mo). Provide daily Folic acid (1 mg/day, but 5 mg on day 1), Zinc (2 mg/kg/day), and Copper.
- **Precaution (High Yield):** **DO NOT GIVE IRON during the first week** (Stabilization Phase),. Iron given early can exacerbate systemic infections and cause oxidative damage,. Iron is only introduced in the rehabilitation phase once the child starts gaining weight.

**Step 7: Initiate Cautious Feeding**

- **Concept:** The fragile gut and impaired metabolism cannot tolerate high calories or high proteins.
- **Action:** Start with a low-protein, low-calorie starter diet: **F-75 formula** (provides 75 kcal and 0.9 g protein per 100 mL),.
- **Method:** Give small, frequent feeds (e.g., 11 mL/kg/feed every 2 hours) day and night. Continue breastfeeding ad libitum,.

_(Note: Steps 8 [Catch-up growth with F-100 diet], 9 [Sensory stimulation], and 10 [Prepare for follow-up] are primarily initiated in the Rehabilitation Phase, which corresponds to weeks 2 to 6 after the initial stabilization,)._

3. Balanced diet for 7–10 years old child.

**BALANCED DIET FOR 7 TO 10 YEARS OLD CHILD**

**I. DEFINITION AND CONCEPT** A balanced diet is defined as a nutritionally adequate and appropriate intake of food items that provide all the essential nutrients in the required amounts and proper proportions. It ensures normal growth, development, and optimum health.

The "Healthy Plate" concept groups foods conventionally into five major categories:

1. Cereals, millets, and roots
2. Pulses, legumes, and nuts
3. Milk, egg, and flesh foods
4. Vegetables and fruits
5. Fats and sugar.

**II. MACRONUTRIENT DISTRIBUTION (HIGH YIELD)** In a normal balanced Indian diet, the recommended total daily energy intake should be apportioned as follows:

- **Carbohydrates:** 55–60% of total calories.
- **Fats:** 25–30% of total calories.
- **Proteins:** 10–12% of total calories.

**III. DAILY NUTRITIONAL REQUIREMENTS (7 to 10 Years)** A child between 7 and 10 years is in the "school-going" (middle-childhood) and prepubescent phases of growth. The nutritional requirements must account for steady somatic growth and basal metabolism.

- **Caloric (Energy) Requirement:**
    - On a per-kilogram basis, the requirement is approximately 80 kcal/kg/day for 7 to 9 years, and 70 kcal/kg/day for 10 to 12 years.
    - According to ICMR-NIN norms, the total energy requirement ranges from approximately 1,635 to 1,896 kcal/day for boys and 1,510 to 1,761 kcal/day for girls between 7 and 10 years of age.
- **Protein Requirement:**
    - The requirement is roughly 2.8 g/kg/day for 7 to 9 years and 2.0 g/kg/day for 10 to 12 years.
    - For a reference Indian child aged 7–9 years (weighing ~25.3 kg), the daily recommended dietary allowance (RDA) of protein is 23.3 g/day.
- **Key Minerals and Vitamins (RDA for 7–9 years):**
    - **Calcium:** 600 mg/day.
    - **Iron:** 15.0 mg/day.
    - **Zinc:** 4.5 mg/day.
    - **Vitamin A:** $630\ \mu\text{g/day}$.
    - **Vitamin C:** 43 mg/day.

**V. DIETARY GUIDELINES AND PREVENTIVE COUNSELING**

- **Protein Quality:** Proteins of animal origin (milk, egg, meat) are "biologically complete" as they provide all essential amino acids. If the child is strictly vegetarian, a combination of cereals and pulses must be given concurrently to make up for the deficiency of specific amino acids (e.g., cereals lack lysine, which is supplemented by legumes).
- **Micronutrient Adequacy:** Dark green leafy vegetables (spinach, amaranth) and yellow fruits (mango, papaya) should be strongly encouraged to meet the high Vitamin A ($\beta$-carotene) and iron requirements.
- **Avoidance of Junk Food:** Foods high in fat, refined carbohydrates, and salt (e.g., pizzas, burgers, french fries, sweetened beverages) must be actively avoided. They are energy-dense but lack vitamins and minerals, predisposing the child to pediatric obesity, dental caries, and future lifestyle diseases.
- **Establishing Healthy Habits:** Breakfast should never be skipped. Proper, structured, and timely meals with an appropriate portion size should be given. Ensure the child engages in at least 60 minutes per day of moderate to vigorous physical activity (e.g., swimming, football, cycling) to maintain a healthy body mass index (BMI).

4. Weaning (weaning foods—low cost).
**WEANING (COMPLEMENTARY FEEDING) AND LOW-COST WEANING FOODS**

**I. TERMINOLOGY AND DEFINITION**

- The term "weaning" traditionally means "to be taken off the breasts" or the "introduction of top feed". However, the preferred and more appropriate terminology is **complementary feeding**.
- It is defined as the introduction of nutritious, energy-dense foods over and above breastfeeding to meet the growing nutritional requirements of the infant.

**II. RECOMMENDED AGE OF INTRODUCTION**

- **Initiation:** The most appropriate time to introduce complementary foods is just after six months of age.
- **Completion:** The process should be gradually completed by 9 months to 1 year of age, by which time the child should be consuming almost the normal family/adult diet.
- **Hazards of improper timing:** Delayed introduction leads to malnutrition and growth retardation, whereas premature introduction (before 6 months) exposes the infant to the risk of infections, weaning diarrhea, decreased breast milk production, and allergies.

**III. ATTRIBUTES OF IDEAL LOW-COST WEANING FOODS** To be practical and effective in developing countries, weaning foods must possess specific characteristics. They should be:

1. Fresh and home-made.
2. Locally available and culturally acceptable.
3. Cost-effective and affordable.
4. Hygienically prepared.
5. Energy-dense with low bulk.

**IV. TYPES OF LOW-COST WEANING FOODS (FIRST-LINE / APPROPRIATE)** These are prepared from the "family pot" and utilize inexpensive household ingredients:

- **Cereal-Pulse Combinations:** Preparations like _khichri_, _dal-rice_, porridge, _suji_, _dalia_, _kheer_, _idli_, _dosa_, and _ragi_. _High-Yield Note:_ Cereals are deficient in the amino acid lysine, while pulses are deficient in methionine. Combining cereals and pulses mutually supplements these deficiencies, thereby optimizing the overall biological value of the dietary protein.
- **Mashed Roots and Fruits:** Mashed ripe banana, sweet potato, and potato are excellent, easily digestible, low-cost sources of calories.
- **Dairy Additions:** Milk added to cereal preparations.
- **Nutrient-Dense Additions:** Sprouted legumes, nuts, and dry fruits can be added depending upon the family's affordability.
- **Low-Cost Protein-Energy Mix Example:** The Hyderabad mix is a classic low-cost formula containing roasted whole wheat (40 g), roasted Bengal gram (16 g), roasted groundnut (10 g), and jaggery (20 g). This 86 g mixture provides 330 kcal and 11.3 g of proteins.

**V. STRATEGIES TO INCREASE ENERGY DENSITY WITHOUT INCREASING BULK** Infants have a low stomach capacity, so they cannot consume bulky, high-water-content gruels in large enough quantities to meet their energy needs. The energy density of low-cost foods must be increased using the following methods:

- **Addition of Oil, Ghee, Sugar, or Jaggery:** Fat is a concentrated source of energy. Adding oil or ghee increases the energy content without increasing the bulk. It is a false belief that young children cannot digest fat; they can easily digest the fat present in breast milk as well as added fats.
- **Use of Amylase-Rich Foods (ARF):** Thick, viscous gruels provide inadequate energy. Amylase-rich foods can dramatically reduce the viscosity of high-dietary porridges. ARF is produced by germinating or malting whole grain cereals or pulses, drying them, and then grinding them. This germination process yields amylase, which digests the complex carbohydrates and thins the gruel, allowing the child to eat larger quantities of energy-dense food.

**VI. REGIMEN FOR INTRODUCING COMPLEMENTARY FOODS**

- **Initial Phase:** Begin with small amounts (2 to 3 tablespoonfuls) of a single soft porridge, mashed banana, or mashed potato.
- **Progression:** Gradually increase the quantity as the child adapts. The frequency should be increased from twice a day initially to 3 or 4 times a day by the end of the first year.
- **Introduction of New Foods:** A new food item should be added every 1 or 2 weeks.
- **Animal Proteins:** Foods like fish, meat, and egg should be introduced later due to the possible risk of protein allergy. When introducing egg, it is advised to begin with the yolk.

**VII. FOODS TO STRICTLY AVOID (THIRD-LINE / AVOIDABLE)**

- Commercially available artificial or tinned foods, which are expensive and unnecessary.
- Junk food, fast food, and repeatedly fried foods containing trans fatty acids, as they predispose to obesity and future atherosclerosis.
- Low-calorie fruit juices and soups that fill the stomach without providing adequate energy.
5. Complementary feeding—principles
**COMPLEMENTARY FEEDING (WEANING)**

**I. DEFINITION** Complementary feeding refers to the introduction of nutritious, energy-dense foods over and above breast milk. It ensures that the growing child continues to receive enough energy, protein, and other essential nutrients to grow normally when breast milk alone is no longer sufficient.

**II. RECOMMENDED TIMING (HIGH YIELD)**

- **Initiation:** It must be started exactly at **6 months of age**, alongside continued breastfeeding.
- **Continuation:** Breastfeeding should be actively encouraged on demand up to two years of age or beyond.
- **Significance of Timing:** Delayed introduction leads to growth faltering and malnutrition, whereas premature introduction exposes the immature gut to infections (weaning diarrhea) and allergies.
- **Target:** The transition process should be completed by 9 to 12 months of age, by which time the child should be taking almost the normal adult (family) diet.

**III. PREREQUISITES & ATTRIBUTES OF IDEAL COMPLEMENTARY FOODS** An ideal complementary food must adhere to the following core principles:

1. **Home-made & Locally Available:** Foods should be fresh, economical, and easily available within the household.
2. **Culturally Acceptable:** It is preferable to modify and utilize foods normally consumed by the family.
3. **High Energy & Nutrient Density:** Since a child's stomach capacity is small and normal gruels are bulky, the energy density must be increased without increasing the bulk. This is achieved by:
    - Adding concentrated energy sources like **oil, ghee, sugar, or jaggery**.
    - Adding **Amylase-Rich Foods (ARF)** (e.g., germinated or malted cereal/pulse flours) which dramatically reduce the viscosity of thick porridges, making them easier to swallow while retaining high calories.
4. **Hygienic:** Meticulous handwashing and hygienic preparation are paramount to prevent gastrointestinal infections.
5. **Palatable:** The food must be soft, easy to eat, and tasty.

**IV. PRINCIPLES OF ADMINISTRATION (THE REGIMEN)**

- **Consistency:** Begin with pureed, mashed, and semisolid foods. The food should be a thick liquid that does not flow out of the spoon readily.
- **Quantity:** Start with very small amounts (1 to 2 teaspoons, or 2 to 3 tablespoonfuls) and gradually build up the quantity as the infant gets older and adapts to the food. By 1 year, the child should eat 1 to $1\frac{1}{2}$ katori per meal.
- **Frequency:**
    - 6 to 8 months: 3 to 4 times a day.
    - 9 to 11 months: 3 to 4 times a day.
    - 12 to 24 months: 4 to 5 times a day (including nutritious snacks).
- **Step-wise Introduction:** Introduce only **one new food at a time**, waiting 1 to 2 weeks before adding another new item. If the child dislikes a food, omit it temporarily.
- **Sequence of Foods:** Start with simple soft foods like mashed ripe banana, mashed potato, suji, or khichri. Highly allergenic foods (like fish, egg white, and meat) should be introduced later. When introducing eggs, always begin with the yolk.

**V. PSYCHOSOCIAL PRINCIPLES OF FEEDING** Feeding must be made a pleasant, interactive experience for the child:

- The feeding time should be structured and the environment free from distractions (e.g., avoid TV/mobile screens).
- The caregiver must be seated comfortably and feed the child with immense patience.
- The caregiver should interact, talk, and maintain eye contact. The principle is to feed playfully and coaxingly (_"pyar se, aaram se, bahlaa ke, manaa ke khilao"_).

**VI. CLASSIFICATION OF COMPLEMENTARY FOODS (THE 3 'A's)**

- **Appropriate (First-line) Foods:** Fresh, home-made, energy-dense foods like khichri (dal-rice), porridge, suji, mashed banana/potato, and sprouted legumes fortified with oil/ghee.
- **Acceptable (Second-line) Foods:** Home-available ready-to-eat foods like breads, biscuits, and cheese.
- **Avoidable (Third-line) Foods:** Commercial artificial/tinned foods, junk fast-foods, low-calorie clear soups/fruit juices, and repeatedly fried foods containing harmful trans-fatty acids.
6. IYCF guidelines.
**INFANT AND YOUNG CHILD FEEDING (IYCF) GUIDELINES**

**I. INTRODUCTION AND RATIONALE** Appropriate feeding practices are fundamental to the optimal growth, development, and survival of infants and young children. After 6 months of age, breast milk alone is not sufficient to meet the child's growing nutritional requirements. The Infant and Young Child Feeding (IYCF) guidelines provide a standardized, scientific approach to exclusive breastfeeding and the timely introduction of complementary foods, which protects children from infections, strengthens the immune system, and prevents malnutrition.

---

**II. AGE-SPECIFIC IYCF RECOMMENDATIONS (HIGH-YIELD)**

**1. Up to 6 Months of Age (Exclusive Breastfeeding)**

- **Exclusive Breastfeeding:** The infant must receive only breast milk. Do not give any other foods or fluids, not even water.
- **Frequency:** Breastfeed on demand, as often as the child wants, day and night. It should be given at least 8 times in 24 hours.
- **Special Circumstances:** Low birth weight babies who are unable to suck directly from the breast optimally should be fed expressed breast milk.
- **During Illness:** Breastfeeding must be actively continued even if the child is sick.

**2. 6 to 12 Months of Age (Initiation of Complementary Feeding)**

- **Breastfeeding:** Continue to breastfeed as often as the child wants.
- **Quantity & Frequency:** Give at least 1 _katori_ serving at a time. The frequency should be 3 to 4 times a day if the child is breastfed, and 5 times a day if not breastfed.
- **Food Choices:**
    - Mashed _roti_, bread, biscuits, or _sattu_ mixed in sweetened undiluted milk.
    - Mashed _roti_, rice, or bread in thick _dal_ with added ghee or oil.
    - _Khichri_ with added oil or ghee.
    - _Halwa, kheer_, or porridge prepared using _sevain, dalia_, or any other cereal in milk.
    - Cooked vegetables must be added to these servings.
- **Snacks:** Offer boiled mashed potato, fried potato, biscuits, or fruits like banana, cheeku, mango, and papaya as snacks between main servings.

**3. 12 Months to 2 Years of Age**

- **Breastfeeding:** Continue breastfeeding as often as the child wants.
- **Quantity & Frequency:** The serving size increases to at least 1½ _katori_ at a time, given 3 to 5 times a day. Offer food directly from the "family pot".
- **Food Choices:** Mashed _roti_, rice, or bread mixed with thick _dal, khichri_, or rice _kanji_ with added ghee or oil. Add cooked vegetables to the servings.

**4. 2 Years and Older**

- **Diet:** Give family food 3 to 4 times a day.
- **Snacks:** Offer a nutritious snack between meals at least twice daily (e.g., any fruit or biscuits).
- Ensure that the child finishes their servings completely.

---

**III. KEY MESSAGES FOR COMPLEMENTARY FEEDING** The IYCF emphasizes four critical messages regarding the nature of complementary foods:

1. **Consistency:** The food should be semisolid and form a thick liquid that does not flow out of the spoon readily.
2. **Quantity (How much each time):** From 6 months to 1 year, the quantity should be ½ to 1 _katori_. After 1 year of age, it increases to 1 to 1½ _katori_.
3. **Frequency (How often):** Provide complementary feeds 3 times a day between 6 months to 1 year, and 5 times a day after 1 year of age.
4. **Method (How to feed):** The approach should be affectionate and patient—summarized as _"Pyar se, aaram se, bahla ke"_ (with love, comfortably, and by coaxing).

---

**IV. STRATEGIES TO INCREASE ENERGY AND NUTRIENT DENSITY** Children possess a low stomach capacity, making it imperative to give them small but highly energy-dense feeds to ensure adequate caloric intake.

- **Adding Fats/Sugars:** The energy content of foods can be increased without increasing the bulk by adding oil, ghee, sugar, or jaggery. It is a false belief that a young child cannot digest fat; up to 45% of energy can safely be provided from fat in malnourished children.
- **Thickening the Gruel:** Thin gruels contain high water content and provide poor energy. A complementary food recipe should be thick and viscous (e.g., _dal ka paani_ is absurd; medium thickness _dal_ with added oil should be given).
- **Amylase-Rich Foods (ARF):** Malted foods (germinated, dried, and ground whole grain cereals or pulses) act as ARF. They reduce the viscosity of foods, digest carbohydrates, and allow the child to eat larger quantities at a time. ARF can be added to items like _khichri_.
- **Nutrient Diversity:** Incorporate a variety of foods from all food groups, starting with small portions as early as 9 months of age, to ensure the adequate intake of macronutrients and micronutrients.

---

**V. MINIMAL ACCEPTABLE DIET CRITERIA** To prevent increasing predisposition to undernutrition, children must meet specific dietary criteria:

- **Breastfed Children:** Should receive foods from 4 or more food groups, along with a minimum meal frequency (solid/semisolid food at least twice a day for infants 6-8 months, and at least three times a day for children 9-23 months).
- **Non-Breastfed Children:** Must be fed with other milk or milk products at least twice a day, and receive solid or semisolid foods from at least four food groups (excluding the milk/milk products group).

---

**VI. HYGIENE AND PSYCHOSOCIAL ASPECTS OF FEEDING**

- **Active Feeding:** The caregiver must keep the child in their lap or sit by their side, actively engaging and interacting with the child while feeding. Feeding should be a structured, lively, and pleasant interactive affair rather than a forced task.
- **Avoid Distractions:** The meal environment should be free from distractions, such as viewing mobile phones or television screens.
- **Hygiene:** Proper food handling is critical to prevent diarrheal infections. The caregiver must wash their hands and the child's hands with soap and water every time before feeding. Foods must be freshly cooked, and the use of feeding bottles must be strictly avoided.



7. Define the WHO classification of undernutrition types. Illustrate the ten steps of management of SAM
**WHO CLASSIFICATION OF UNDERNUTRITION**

The World Health Organization (WHO) classifies undernutrition based on reference standards and standard deviation scores (SDS or Z-scores) into three major subgroups: underweight, stunting, and wasting.

**1. Underweight (Low weight-for-age)** It reflects a low weight for the child's age, which could indicate either stunting, wasting, or both.

- **Underweight:** Weight-for-age < –2 SDS up to –3 SDS.
- **Severe Underweight:** Weight-for-age < –3 SDS.

**2. Stunting (Low height-for-age)** It indicates chronic undernutrition, leading to a child being short for their age.

- **Stunting:** Height-for-age < –2 SDS up to –3 SDS.
- **Severe Stunting:** Height-for-age < –3 SDS.

**3. Wasting (Low weight-for-height)** It indicates acute undernutrition resulting from a recent severe food deficit or an acute illness like diarrhea. The child has a thin appearance.

- **Wasting:** Weight-for-height < –2 SDS up to –3 SDS.
- **Severe Wasting:** Weight-for-height < –3 SDS.

_High-Yield Note on Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM):_ WHO defines SAM in children 6–59 months of age by the presence of any of the following three criteria:

1. Weight-for-height below –3 standard deviation score (<–3 SDS).
2. Presence of bipedal edema.
3. Mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) below 11.5 cm.

---

**TEN STEPS OF MANAGEMENT OF SEVERE ACUTE MALNUTRITION (SAM)**

The general hospital-based management of complicated SAM involves 10 essential steps divided into two phases:

- **Stabilization Phase:** Focuses on restoring homeostasis and treating medical complications (usually takes 1–7 days).
- **Rehabilitation Phase:** Focuses on rebuilding wasted tissues and catch-up growth (weeks 2–6).

**Step 1: Treat/Prevent Hypoglycemia**

- _Action:_ Give 50 mL of 10% glucose or sucrose solution orally or via nasogastric (NG) tube immediately, followed by the first feed.
- _Prevention:_ Initiate 2-hourly feeding immediately and continue day and night.

**Step 2: Treat/Prevent Hypothermia**

- _Action:_ If rectal temperature is < 35.5°C or axillary temperature < 35°C, actively warm the child using skin-to-skin contact (Kangaroo Mother Care), an overhead warmer, or a heated room. Avoid rapid rewarming.
- _Prevention:_ Clothe the child warmly, ensure the head is covered, and avoid draughts.

**Step 3: Treat/Prevent Dehydration**

- _Action:_ Do not use standard ORS. Use special low-osmolarity ORS for SAM (ReSoMal) which contains lower sodium and higher potassium.
- Give ReSoMal at 5–10 mL/kg/hour initially. Intravenous fluids (like half normal saline with 5% dextrose) are strictly reserved for severe dehydration with shock.

**Step 4: Correct Electrolyte Imbalance**

- _Action:_ All severely malnourished children have excess body sodium and severe deficiencies of potassium and magnesium.
- Give supplemental potassium at 3–4 mEq/kg/day.
- Give supplemental magnesium sulfate (0.3 mL/kg up to a maximum of 2 mL IM once), followed by oral magnesium.
- Restrict sodium intake.

**Step 5: Treat/Prevent Infection**

- _Action:_ Routine signs of infection are often masked. Therefore, routinely administer broad-spectrum parenteral antibiotics on admission.
- Common regimen: IV Ampicillin (50 mg/kg/dose 6-hourly) plus IV Gentamicin (5–7 mg/kg/day).

**Step 6: Correct Micronutrient Deficiencies**

- _Action:_ Administer Vitamin A immediately on day 1.
- Provide daily supplements of Folic acid (1 mg/day), Zinc (2 mg/kg/day), and Copper (0.2–0.3 mg/kg/day).
- _High-Yield Warning:_ **Do not give Iron during the stabilization phase**; start iron (3 mg/kg/day) only when the child begins to gain weight in the rehabilitation phase.

**Step 7: Initiate Cautious Feeding**

- _Action:_ Start feeding immediately using a starter diet, usually **F-75** (containing 75 kcal and 0.9 g protein per 100 mL).
- Feeds must be small, frequent (e.g., 2-hourly), and of low osmolarity and low lactose to prevent overloading the fragile intestine, liver, and kidneys.

**Step 8: Achieve Catch-up Growth**

- _Action:_ Once appetite returns (usually in 2–3 days), transition from the starter diet to the catch-up diet, usually **F-100** (containing 100 kcal and 2.9 g protein per 100 mL).
- Increase caloric intake to 150–200 kcal/kg/day and protein to 4–6 g/kg/day to support rapid weight gain.

**Step 9: Provide Sensory Stimulation and Emotional Support**

- _Action:_ Malnutrition delays mental and behavioral development. Provide a cheerful, stimulating environment.
- Ensure tender loving care, structured play therapy for 15–30 mins/day, and maternal involvement.

**Step 10: Prepare for Follow-up**

- _Action:_ A child is ready for discharge when they are eating well, gaining adequate weight, and medical complications have resolved.
- Prepare the parents for home-based care, introduce culturally acceptable energy-dense home foods, complete routine immunizations, and schedule regular follow-up visits to monitor anthropometry.

8. Protein-energy malnutrition (PEM)—ecological causes, classification.
**PROTEIN-ENERGY MALNUTRITION (PEM)**

**DEFINITION** Protein-energy malnutrition (PEM) represents a continuous spectrum of clinical conditions resulting from a varying degree of protein lack and energy (calorie) inadequacy. The term replaces the older designation "protein-calorie malnutrition" because protein deficiency is rarely isolated; it is almost always accompanied by energy deficiency.

---

**I. ECOLOGICAL CAUSES OF MALNUTRITION**

The ecology of malnutrition is complex and multifactorial. It is broadly categorized into **Primary** (due to dietary deficiency) and **Secondary** (due to underlying diseases like tuberculosis or malabsorption) causes. In many cases, both factors overlap. The high-yield ecological factors include:

**1. Bad Economy (Poverty)**

- Poor socioeconomic status severely limits the family's ability to provide a nutritious diet.
- Among the underprivileged, hardly 10% of the food budget is spent on high-quality animal-source foods like milk, eggs, or meat.

**2. Ignorance, Faulty Food Habits, and Feeding Practices**

- **Delayed Complementary Feeding:** Many families delay the introduction of semisolids until a formal ceremony (like _annaparasana_), by which time the infant (often approaching one year of age) is already undernourished and anemic.
- **Artificial Feeding:** Ignorant mothers aping urban trends often shift to artificial bottle feeding, providing highly diluted and contaminated formula, leading to malnutrition and infections.
- **Over-reliance on Milk:** Prolonged, exclusive feeding on milk (even if undiluted) without the addition of solid foods leads to significant caloric and iron deficiency.
- **Withholding Food During Illness:** A widespread detrimental practice is starving the child or withholding foods during illnesses like diarrhea or measles.
- **Food Taboos:** Beliefs that certain foods are "hot" or "cold" lead to the unnecessary restriction of nutritious items.

**3. Medical Reasons (Infections)**

- Common infections such as diarrhea, measles, and malaria contribute significantly to PEM by reducing the child's appetite and increasing catabolic heat/energy loss during febrile episodes.
- Intestinal parasitic infestations deprive the host of nutrients, reduce appetite, and cause diarrhea or absorptive defects.

**4. Family Size and Spacing**

- **Large Families:** Malnutrition incidence is significantly higher in children of birth order fourth and above.
- **Closely-spaced Pregnancies:** Pregnancies occurring every year or every other year lead to a higher incidence of malnutrition. An ideal gap of at least 3 years is recommended.

**5. Maternal Factors**

- **Working Mothers:** Mothers who work as daily laborers find little time for child-rearing and feeding; "mothering" is often left to an elder, inexperienced sibling.
- **Bad Start (IUGR/LBW):** Malnourished mothers give birth to Low Birth Weight (LBW) or Intrauterine Growth Retarded (IUGR) infants who start life with a handicap, are difficult to feed, and are highly vulnerable to infections.

**6. Secondary Malnutrition**

- Underlying organic diseases can precipitate PEM. Important causes include malabsorption states (celiac disease, cystic fibrosis, tropical sprue), tuberculosis, diabetes, galactosemia, and iatrogenic mismanagement of diarrhea with starvation therapy.

---

**II. CLASSIFICATION OF PEM**

PEM is classified using various clinical and anthropometric scales to assist in diagnosis and management.

**1. Syndromal Classification** Based on the predominant clinical presentation, PEM is classified into:

- Kwashiorkor
- Nutritional marasmus
- Marasmic kwashiorkor
- Prekwashiorkor
- Nutritional dwarfing (Stunting)

**2. Gomez Classification** Based on weight for age as a percentage of the expected weight (using the Harvard standard):

- **First degree:** Weight between 90% and 75% of expected.
- **Second degree:** Weight between 75% and 60% of expected.
- **Third degree:** Weight below 60% of expected.

**3. Wellcome (International) Classification** Classifies PEM based on a combination of weight for age percentage and the presence or absence of edema:

- **Weight 80-60% of expected:**
    - With edema = Kwashiorkor
    - Without edema = Undernutrition
- **Weight below 60% of expected:**
    - With edema = Marasmic kwashiorkor
    - Without edema = Nutritional marasmus

**4. Indian Academy of Pediatrics (IAP) Classification** Based on weight for age percentage of the expected weight:

- **First degree:** Weight between 80% and 70% of expected.
- **Second degree:** Weight between 70% and 60% of expected.
- **Third degree:** Weight between 60% and 50% of expected.
- **Fourth degree:** Weight below 50% of expected. _(Note: If the child has demonstrable pitting edema, the letter “K” is added in front of the evaluated grade)_.

**5. Waterlow Classification** Categorizes malnutrition based on wasting (weight for height) and stunting (height for age):

- **Acute (Wasted but not stunted):** Weight for height is low, but height for age is normal.
- **Acute/chronic (Wasted and stunted):** Weight for height is low, and height for age is low.
- **Nutritional dwarfing (Stunted but not wasted):** Weight for height is normal, but height for age is low.

**6. WHO Classification** Grades PEM based on multiple anthropometric indices:

- **Moderate PEM:** No symmetrical edema; Weight for height (wasting) is 70-79% of expected; Height for age (stunting) is 85-89% of expected.
- **Severe PEM:** Symmetrical edema is present; Weight for height (severe wasting) is <70% of expected; Height for age (severe stunting) is <85% of expected.

**7. Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM) - WHO/UNICEF Criteria** For children aged 6 to 59 months, SAM is defined by the presence of any of the following three high-yield criteria:
	1. Weight-for-height below -3 standard deviation score (<3 SDS) on the WHO Growth Standard.
	2. Presence of bipedal edema.
	3. Mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) below 11.5 cm.
	

9.  Kwashiorkor—etiology and clinical manifestations (classical features), complications
**KWASHIORKOR**

**I. INTRODUCTION AND DEFINITION** Kwashiorkor is a severe clinical syndrome of protein-energy malnutrition (PEM) resulting from a gross deficiency of proteins, although energy (calorie) deficiency is also present.

- **Age of onset:** It is primarily seen in the preschool age group, predominantly affecting children between 1 to 4 years of age.
- **Terminology:** The term "Kwashiorkor" was introduced by Cicely Williams in 1935, originating from Ghana, meaning "the red boy" (due to pigmentary changes) or "the disease of the deposed child" (affecting the deprived child due to the arrival of the next sibling).

**II. ETIOLOGY AND PATHOGENESIS** The exact etiology involves complex interactions of dietary imbalances, infections, and metabolic failures. Several high-yield hypotheses explain its pathogenesis:

- **Dietetic Hypothesis:** Kwashiorkor results predominantly from a severe lack of dietary proteins, with concurrent but less severe energy lack.
- **Gopalan’s Adaptation Hypothesis:** Malnutrition requires physiological adaptation. Marasmus is a successful extreme adaptation to starvation. Kwashiorkor represents a stage of **adaptation failure (dysadaptation)**. This dysadaptation is typically precipitated by an acute stress factor, such as a fulminant infection (e.g., measles, pertussis, or acute diarrhea).
- **Golden’s Free Radical Hypothesis:** Kwashiorkor is caused by the overproduction of toxic free radicals (due to infections, toxins, or iron) and the simultaneous breakdown of the body's antioxidant protective mechanisms (due to deficiency of vitamins A and E, carotene, zinc, copper, and selenium).
- **Aflatoxin Contamination Hypothesis:** Consumption of food contaminated with aflatoxins is postulated to be an important trigger for Kwashiorkor.
- **Jelliffe’s Hypothesis:** It is a cumulative result of interactions between dietary imbalance, infections, parasitosis, toxins, and emotional trauma from maternal deprivation (abrupt weaning).

**III. CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS** The clinical features are classically divided into essential (minimal diagnostic criteria) and non-essential (variable) features.

**A. Essential Features (Minimal Diagnostic Criteria)** These four features _must_ be present to label a case as Kwashiorkor:

1. **Growth Retardation:** Evidenced by significantly low weight and subnormal height for the child's age.
2. **Muscle Wasting with Fat Retention:** There is marked wasting of muscles, but importantly, there is retention of some subcutaneous fat.
3. **Psychomotor Changes (Mental Apathy):** The child exhibits a silent, listless inertness, a miserable appearance, and a total lack of interest in their surroundings.
4. **Hypoalbuminemic Pitting Edema:** This is the hallmark sign. It begins over the pretibial region (feet and legs) and may progress to the face, hands, trunk, and cause ascites (anasarca). Because of the edema, the child may have a deceptively plump, **"fat, sugar baby"** appearance or moon facies. _(Note: Cardiac, hepatic, and renal causes of edema must be ruled out, and serum albumin is characteristically < 2.5 g/dl)_.

**B. Non-Essential (Variable) Features** These features may or may not be present depending on the severity and regional variations:

1. **Hair Changes:** Hair becomes light-colored (hypochromotrichia), sparse, dry, coarse, and easily pluckable. A classic finding is the **"Flag Sign"**, which represents alternating bands of light and dark color along the hair shaft, signifying alternating periods of inadequate and adequate nutrition.
2. **Skin Changes (Dermatosis):**
    - **Flaky-paint dermatosis:** Areas of hyperpigmentation intervened by areas of raw, red skin caused by the shedding of superficial skin flakes.
    - **Crazy-pavement dermatosis** and pellagra-like lesions over exposed parts.
3. **Hepatomegaly:** The liver is invariably enlarged, soft, and smooth due to severe fatty infiltration. It may reach down to the umbilicus.
4. **Gastrointestinal Symptoms:** Diarrhea, vomiting, and anorexia are common. Diarrhea has a multifactorial origin (superadded infections, villous atrophy, lactase deficiency).
5. **Associated Deficiencies:** Moderate to severe anemia (often iron-deficiency or dimorphic) and severe vitamin A deficiency (leading to keratomalacia and blindness) frequently complicate the picture.

**IV. COMPLICATIONS** Children with advanced PEM (Kwashiorkor) are highly vulnerable to life-threatening complications, which include:

1. **Dehydration and Dyselectrolytemia:** Usually secondary to diarrheal episodes (often with lactose intolerance). There is profound depletion of total body potassium and magnesium.
2. **Superadded Infections (Overt and Hidden):** Due to severe impairment of cell-mediated immunity, these children are prone to septicemia, bronchopneumonia, diarrhea, pyoderma, urinary tract infections (UTI), and tuberculosis.
3. **Hypothermia:** An unattended rectal temperature below 35°C (95°F) is a grave complication that can lead to death.
4. **Hypoglycemia:** Often asymptomatic but carries a very poor prognosis and contributes to treatment failure.
5. **Congestive Cardiac Failure (CCF):** The cardiac muscle is atrophied. CCF is usually iatrogenically precipitated by excessive intravenous fluids/sodium intake or by severe anemia.
6. **Bleeding Diatheses:** Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) may complicate the clinical picture.
7. **Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS):** Sudden, unexplained death may occur 4 to 7 days after admission.
	1. **Complications During Nutritional Rehabilitation:**
	    - _Pseudotumor Cerebri:_ A transient rise in intracranial tension.
	    - _Nutritional Recovery Syndrome:_ Characterized by increasing hepatomegaly, ascites, parotid swelling, and hypertrichosis.
	    - _Encephalitis-like Syndrome:_ Drowsiness, tremors (Kwashi-shakes), or coma, likely due to an excess of dietary proteins during early recovery.


8. WHO criteria for diagnosis of SAM. Describe short-term complications
**SEVERE ACUTE MALNUTRITION (SAM)**

**I. WHO DIAGNOSTIC CRITERIA FOR SAM** According to the World Health Organization (WHO) and UNICEF, Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM) in children aged 6 to 59 months is defined by the presence of **any one** of the following three independent criteria:

1. **Weight-for-height/length:** Below –3 standard deviation score (< –3 SDS) on the WHO Growth Standard charts.
2. **Edema:** Presence of symmetrical bipedal (pitting) edema.
3. **Mid-Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC):** Strictly below 11.5 cm (115 mm).

_(High-Yield Note: In infants below 6 months of age, the MUAC measurement is not used as a diagnostic criterion.)_

---

**II. SHORT-TERM COMPLICATIONS OF SAM (ADVANCED PEM)** Children with severe acute malnutrition are in a state of fragile metabolic adaptation. They have a high risk of imminent death due to acute, life-threatening complications that often present silently without classic clinical signs. The major short-term complications include:

**1. Hypoglycemia**

- It reflects profoundly impaired glucose stores and altered metabolism.
- It contributes significantly to a poor response to nutritional therapy and carries a grave prognosis. It often co-exists as a lethal triad with hypothermia and systemic infection.

**2. Hypothermia**

- Defined as an unattended rectal temperature falling below 35.5°C or 35°C.
- It is a critical emergency that may precipitate Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS).

**3. Dehydration and Dyselectrolytemia**

- Dehydration usually complicates an accompanying episode of diarrhea (often associated with lactose intolerance).
- Profound electrolyte imbalances are universal, specifically severe intracellular potassium and magnesium depletion. Potassium depletion can lead to paralytic ileus, abdominal distention, and hypotonia. Furthermore, despite low serum sodium, total body sodium is paradoxically retained and increased.

**4. Superadded Infections (Overt and Hidden)**

- Severe malnutrition drastically impairs the immune response, making the child highly susceptible to infections such as septicemia, bronchopneumonia, diarrhea, pyoderma, scabies, urinary tract infections (UTI), and tuberculosis.
- _Exam Clue:_ Due to immune anergy, classical signs of infection like fever are frequently absent (hidden infections).

**5. Congestive Cardiac Failure (CCF)**

- The cardiac muscle in SAM is severely atrophied, resulting in a small and functionally weak heart.
- CCF is usually precipitated iatrogenically by the excessive intake of sodium or intravenous fluids, or it may develop secondary to severe anemia.

**6. Severe Anemia**

- Moderate to severe anemia is almost invariably present.
- It results directly from severe nutritional deficits (iron, folate, B12) or secondary to systemic infections and intestinal parasitic infestations. It further exacerbates the risk of CCF.

**7. Bleeding Diathesis**

- Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) can complicate the advanced clinical picture, presenting as life-threatening bleeds.

**8. Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS)**

- Sudden, unexplained death may occur 4 to 7 days after hospital admission.
- The precise cause usually remains unclear despite supportive medical care.


10. Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS) program
**INTEGRATED CHILD DEVELOPMENT SERVICES (ICDS) SCHEME**

**INTRODUCTION** The Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS) scheme is a major national program introduced in 1975 on an experimental basis with 33 projects, which has now expanded to over 5,000 projects. It provides an integrated approach for converging basic services through community-based workers to directly reach out to children below 6 years of age, especially those from vulnerable groups and remote areas. It is viewed as a vital drive against poverty and an instrument to improve the health, nutritional, and educational status of underprivileged sections.

**AIMS AND OBJECTIVES (HIGH YIELD)** The scheme is targeted at the holistic development of children with the following specific objectives:

1. To improve the nutritional and health status of children in the age group of 0 to 6 years.
2. To lay the foundation for proper psychologic, physical, and social development of the child.
3. To reduce the incidence of mortality, morbidity, malnutrition, and school dropout.
4. To achieve effective coordination of policy and implementation amongst the various departments to promote child development.
5. To enhance the capability of the mother to look after the normal health and nutritional needs of the child through nutrition and health education.

**TARGET BENEFICIARIES** The major beneficiaries of the ICDS scheme constitute over 40% of the total population and include:

- Children under 6 years of age.
- Pregnant and lactating (nursing) mothers.
- Women in the age group of 15 to 44 years.

**PACKAGE OF SERVICES** The services are broadly categorized into essential and supplementary components:

**A. Essential Services**

1. Supplementary nutrition
2. Immunization
3. Health check-up
4. Referral services
5. Nutrition and health education
6. Nonformal education

**B. Supplementary Services**

1. Nonformal education of adult women
2. Applied knowledge about increased local production and consumption of nutritious foods
3. Rural drinking water supply

**SERVICES DELIVERED BASED ON BENEFICIARY CATEGORY** The scheme tailors its services according to the specific needs of the beneficiaries:

- **Children under 3 years:** Supplementary nutrition, immunization, health check-up, and referral services.
- **Children 3 to 6 years:** Supplementary nutrition, immunization, health check-up, referral services, and **nonformal preschool education**.
- **Expectant and nursing mothers:** Health check-up, immunization against tetanus (for expectant mothers), supplementary nutrition, and nutrition and health education.
- **Other women (15 to 44 years):** Nutrition and health education.

**NORMS FOR SUPPLEMENTARY NUTRITION** The ICDS program provides specific daily supplementary rations to its beneficiaries:

|Beneficiary Category|Energy Requirement (kcal/day)|Protein Requirement (g/day)|
|:--|:--|:--|
|**Children (6 to 72 months)**|500|12–15|
|**Severely malnourished children (SAM) (6 to 72 months)**|800|20–25|
|**Pregnant women and lactating mothers**|600|18–20|

**DELIVERY OF SERVICES AND ADMINISTRATIVE HIERARCHY**

- **Anganwadi Center:** The services are delivered at a community center called the _anganwadi_ (which translates to a courtyard).
- **Anganwadi Worker (AWW):** She is the backbone of the center. She comes from the local community and receives 4 months of training in child development, nutrition, immunization, personal hygiene, and management of at-risk children. In urban ICDS projects, the AWW must be at least a matriculate. She is usually assisted by a local, uneducated, and unskilled woman.
- **Mukhyasevika:** She supervises the work of the AWW. She is a graduate who undergoes 2 months of special training.
- **Child Development Project Officer (CDPO):** The CDPO is in charge of each ICDS project and supervises the _mukhyasevikas_. The CDPO is preferably a graduate in child development, social work, home science, or nutrition, with 2 months of special training.
- **Administration:** The scheme is jointly operated by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare and the Ministry of Women and Child Development, but the administrative control primarily lies with the Social Welfare Ministry.

**COMMUNITY PARTICIPATION** The program emphasizes community involvement. Local health committees and _Mahila Mandals_ are encouraged to participate in preparing nutritious food mixes using local foods, facilitating immunizations, and supporting vitamin A, iron, and folic acid supplementation.
