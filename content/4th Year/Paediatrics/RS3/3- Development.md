1. Define growth. Discuss principles (laws) of development and milestones achieved until one/two/three years of age.
**I. DEFINITION OF GROWTH**

Growth is a fundamental biological process defined as a measure of physical maturation that signifies an increase in the size of the body and its various organs.

- **Quantitative Assessment:** It can be measured objectively in terms of centimetres (length/height) and kilograms (weight).
- **Physiological Basis:** Growth is primarily driven by the multiplication of cells and a progressive increase in intracellular substance. Tissues characteristically show a progressive increase in their DNA content as growth proceeds.
- **Significance:** Unlike in adulthood, growth is an essential and distinguishing feature of a child's life, starting from conception and continuing until maturity.

---

**II. PRINCIPLES (LAWS) OF DEVELOPMENT**

Development refers to the maturation of functions, acquisition of skills, and myelination of the nervous system. The complex process of human development follows these distinct, high-yield laws and patterns:

**1. Development is a Continuous Process:** Development does not occur abruptly; it is a continuous and orderly process that starts _in utero_ and progresses through various predictable stages before achieving a milestone.

**2. Dependence on Nervous System Maturation:** Development is critically dependent on the functional maturation and myelination of the central nervous system. While practice is necessary for a child to learn a new skill, the child cannot acquire the skill without the prerequisite neural maturation.

**3. Sequence of Attainment is Universal:** The order in which milestones are attained is the same in all children. For example, all normal infants will babble before they speak words, and they will sit before they stand. Variations exist only in the exact time and manner of their attainment.

**4. Cephalocaudal and Proximal-to-Distal Direction:** Development strictly progresses in a cephalocaudal (head-to-toe) direction. For example, head control precedes trunk control (sitting), which in turn precedes the use of lower limbs (standing/walking). Furthermore, control of the limbs happens from proximal to distal (e.g., the child learns to use arms and hands before mastering fine finger movements).

**5. Disappearance of Primitive Reflexes:** The attainment of certain voluntary milestones requires that the relevant primitive neonatal reflexes disappear. For instance, a child can only voluntarily hold an object once the primitive palmar grasp reflex disappears, and can only turn over once the asymmetric tonic neck reflex vanishes.

**6. Replacement of Disorganized Mass Activity:** Specific, purposeful actions eventually replace initial, disorganized mass activity. For example, a 3-month-old infant excitedly moves all limbs upon seeing a bright toy, whereas a 3-year-old child will simply smile and ask for it.

**III. DEVELOPMENTAL MILESTONES (BIRTH TO 3 YEARS)**

Assessment of development is classically divided into four major domains. The following are the normal high-yield milestones achieved up to three years of age:

- **5 months:** Sitting with support.
- **7 to 8 months:** Sitting without support.
- **9 months:** Standing with support.
- **10 months:** Walking with support.
- **11 months:** Crawling or creeping.
- **12 months:** Standing without support.
- **13 months:** Walking without support.
- **18 months:** Running.
- **2 years (24 months):** Climbing upstairs.
- **3 years (36 months):** Riding a tricycle.

**B. Fine Motor and Adaptive Development**

- **4 months:** Grasping a rattle when it is placed in the hand.
- **5 months:** Intentional reaching out for a bright object and grasping it with both hands (bidextrous grasp).
- **7 months:** Holding an object with a crude grasp from the palm (palmar grasp).
- **9 months:** Holding a small object neatly between the index finger and the thumb (pincer grasp).

**C. Personal and Social Development**

- **4 to 8 weeks:** Social smile.
- **3 months:** Recognition of the mother.
- **6 months:** Smiling at a mirror image.
- **9 months:** Waving "bye-bye".
- **1 year (12 months):** Playing a simple ball game.
- **3 years (36 months):** Knowing their own gender.

**D. Language Development**

- **1 month:** Turning the head to a sound, such as a rattle or bell.
- **3 months:** Cooing.
- **6 months:** Speaking monosyllables (e.g., _ma, pa, ba_).
- **9 months:** Speaking bisyllables (e.g., _mama, papa, baba_).
- **1 year (12 months):** Speaking two words with distinct meaning.
- **2 years (24 months):** Speaking in simple sentences.
- **3 years (36 months):** Telling a story.

**II. CAUSES OF MENTAL RETARDATION**

Mental retardation refers to a limitation in performance characterized by significant impairment in measured intelligence (IQ below 68) and adaptive behaviour. The etiological factors are broadly classified into prenatal, perinatal, and postnatal causes according to the time of insult:

**A. Prenatal Causes**

1. **Genetic/Inborn Errors of Metabolism:** Phenylketonuria (PKU), galactosemia, gargoylism (Hurler syndrome), Niemann-Pick disease, Gaucher disease, microcephaly, craniosynostosis, and congenital hydrocephalus.
2. **Chromosomal Anomalies:** Down syndrome (Trisomy 21), Turner syndrome, Klinefelter syndrome, and Fragile X syndrome.
3. **Maternal Infections (TORCH):** Rubella, toxoplasmosis, cytomegalic inclusion disease, syphilis, and chickenpox.
4. **Maternal Diseases and Toxic Exposures:** Toxemias of pregnancy, maternal lead poisoning, exposure to teratogenic agents, and irradiation during pregnancy.

**B. Perinatal Causes**

1. **Birth Asphyxia & Trauma:** Cerebral anoxia, birth trauma, intracranial hemorrhage, and subdural hematoma.
2. **Gestational Issues:** Prematurity and "small-for-dates" (Intrauterine Growth Retardation) infants.

**C. Postnatal Causes**

1. **Infections:** Meningitis and encephalitis.
2. **Trauma:** Severe head injury and subdural hematoma.
3. **Metabolic/Endocrine:** Severe hypoglycemia, hypocalcemia, profound electrolyte imbalance, and hypothyroidism (cretinism).
4. **Encephalopathies & Toxins:** Toxic encephalopathy, whooping cough (pertussis) encephalopathy, and Kernicterus (bilirubin encephalopathy).
5. **Cerebrovascular Episodes:** Thrombosis of the cerebral arteries and veins.
6. **Nutritional:** Gross protein-energy malnutrition (PEM) occurring in early infancy.
7. **Cultural and Psychosocial Factors:** Postnatal experiential disruptions such as poverty, family disorganization, faulty infant-caretaker interaction, parental psychopathology, and parental drug abuse (also known as emotional, cultural, or sensory deprivation).

_High-Yield Note for Differential Diagnosis:_ When evaluating a child for mental retardation, it is important to rule out "pseudo-mental retardation" caused by unrecognized deafness, blindness, severe motor disability (like cerebral palsy), or psychiatric problems such as autism. Preventable causes of mental retardation include cretinism, galactosemia, phenylketonuria, and severe PEM, which must be identified and managed promptly.

3. A 2-year-old child is brought to you with history of delay in attainment of walking. Write how history and a physical examination will help you arrive at a probable etiological diagnosis.

**EVALUATION OF DELAYED WALKING IN A 2-YEAR-OLD CHILD**

**INTRODUCTION** Normally, a child achieves the gross motor milestones of walking with support by 10 months, walking without support by 13 months, and running by 18 months. A 2-year-old presenting with an inability to walk signifies a definitive developmental delay in the gross motor domain,. Arriving at a probable etiological diagnosis requires a systematic approach through history and physical examination.

**1. ROLE OF HISTORY TAKING**

**A. Antenatal, Natal, and Perinatal History:**

- **Maternal Factors:** Enquire about maternal illnesses during pregnancy, toxemias, or intrauterine infections (TORCH/STORCH) which are major risk factors for developmental delay.
- **Birth History:** Elicit a history of obstetric complications, prematurity, low birth weight (LBW), birth asphyxia, hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE), neonatal seizures, intraventricular hemorrhage, or severe jaundice (kernicterus). These are high-yield pointers towards Cerebral Palsy (CP) and mental retardation,.

**B. Developmental History:**

- **Pattern of Delay:** Ascertain if the delay is isolated to motor skills or if other milestones (like social smile, speech, fine motor skills) are also delayed, indicating a global developmental delay,,.
- **Regression:** Determine if there has been a loss or regression of already achieved milestones, which strongly suggests progressive neurodegenerative disorders or infantile tremor syndrome,.

**C. Family and Genetic History:**

- **Consanguinity and Pedigree:** Ask about consanguineous marriage, similar delays in siblings, or delayed puberty in parents,.
- **Muscular Dystrophy:** A family history of maternal uncles or male cousins affected by muscle weakness or delayed walking is a classic clue for X-linked recessive disorders like Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD).

**D. Nutritional and Psychosocial History:**

- **Diet:** A prolonged history of poor dietary intake (inadequate calories and proteins) leads to chronic protein-energy malnutrition (PEM), while lack of vitamin D leads to rickets; both conditions cause delayed motor milestones,.
- **Social:** Poor socioeconomic status, emotional deprivation, or maternal neglect are known contributors to delayed development and failure to thrive,.

---

**2. ROLE OF PHYSICAL EXAMINATION**

**A. General Physical Examination:**

- **Anthropometry:** Measure the head circumference to rule out microcephaly (seen in intrauterine infections, chromosomal defects, or anoxia) or macrocephaly (seen in hydrocephalus, rickets, or achondroplasia),,.
- **Dysmorphism:** Look for characteristic facial features indicating chromosomal abnormalities (like Down syndrome) or congenital hypothyroidism (cretinism), which typically presents with a large protruding tongue, coarse facies, wide open fontanels, and umbilical hernia,,.
- **Cutaneous Markers:** Examine the skin for neurocutaneous markers such as cafe-au-lait spots (neurofibromatosis) or ash leaf macules (tuberous sclerosis).

**B. Neurological Examination (High Yield for Motor Delay):**

- **Muscle Tone Assessment:**
    - _Hypotonia:_ A "floppy baby" lacking resistance to passive movements suggests Werdnig-Hoffmann disease (spinal muscular atrophy), benign congenital hypotonia, Down syndrome, or rickets,,.
    - _Hypertonia/Spasticity:_ Sudden lifting of the child causing adductor spasm and "crossing of the legs" (scissoring) is a hallmark sign of spastic cerebral palsy.
- **Primitive Reflexes:** The persistence of primitive reflexes (such as the Moro reflex or tonic neck reflex) beyond the age of 3 to 6 months strongly points to a central nervous system pathology like cerebral palsy,,.
- **Deep Tendon Reflexes:** Exaggerated or brisk reflexes with ankle clonus point to upper motor neuron lesions like CP. Sluggish or absent reflexes are seen in muscular dystrophies and spinal muscular atrophy,.

**C. Musculoskeletal Examination:**

- **Signs of Muscular Dystrophy:** In a boy with delayed walking, look for pseudohypertrophy of the calf muscles and a waddling (Trendelenburg) gait,,. A positive **Gowers sign**—where the child uses his hands to climb up his own thighs to arise from the floor—is a classic indicator of Duchenne muscular dystrophy,,.
- **Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip (DDH):** A missed congenital dislocation of the hip can be responsible for delayed walking or a limp. Examination will show asymmetry of the thigh and gluteal creases, inability to abduct the hip fully, and apparent shortening of the affected leg. Specific maneuvers like the Ortolani and Barlow tests help confirm hip instability.
- **Signs of Rickets:** Poor muscle tone and relaxation of ligaments in rickets lead to delayed milestones and flat feet. Examine for specific bony deformities such as widening of the wrists and ankles, rachitic rosary (costochondral beading), Harrison's sulcus, frontal bossing, and bow legs (genu varum) or knock knees (genu valgum),.

**ETIOLOGY OF SPECIFIC DEVELOPMENTAL DELAYS**

Developmental dissociation (or deviance) occurs when a child acquires milestones in various domains at differing rates.

**1. Most Likely Causes for Predominant Speech Delay** When a child presents with isolated or predominant speech and language delay, but has normal motor and social development, the primary clinical conditions that must be considered are:

- **Hearing Impairment:** This is a critical factor, as advanced language development heavily predicts normal intelligence and relies on intact hearing.
- **Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD):** Characterized by qualitative impairment in social behavior and communication.

**2. Most Likely Causes for Predominant (Isolated) Motor Delay** When a child presents with a predominant delay in gross motor milestones with relatively preserved language and cognitive skills, the clinical evaluation should focus on:

- **Cerebral Palsy:** A nonprogressive motor disability resulting from damage to the growing brain.
- **Neuromuscular Disorders:** Conditions primarily affecting the motor unit, such as spinal muscular atrophies or myopathies.
- **Rickets:** A metabolic bone disease that causes poor muscle tone and delayed motor milestones.
- **Chronic Systemic Illnesses:** Prolonged or severe organic diseases can cause a generalized lag, often more apparent in the energy-dependent gross motor domain.

**NORMAL MOTOR DEVELOPMENTAL MILESTONES (BIRTH TO 2 YEARS)**

Motor development is a continuous process that depends on the functional maturation and myelination of the central nervous system. It is classically evaluated in two major domains: Gross Motor and Fine Motor (adaptive) development.

**A. GROSS MOTOR MILESTONES** Gross motor development pertains to the child's control over their body and is observed in supine, prone, sitting, and standing postures. It typically progresses in a cephalo-caudal direction (head control precedes trunk control and lower limb control).

- **3 Months:** Head and neck holding.
- **5 Months:** Sitting with support.
- **7 to 8 Months:** Sitting without support.
- **9 Months:** Standing with support.
- **10 Months:** Walking with support.
- **11 Months:** Crawling or creeping.
- **12 Months (1 Year):** Standing without support.
- **13 Months:** Walking without support.
- **18 Months:** Running.
- **24 Months (2 Years):** Climbing upstairs.

**B. FINE MOTOR MILESTONES** Fine motor development pertains to the coordination of eyes, hand-eye coordination, and the acquisition of manipulative skills using the hands. It generally develops from proximal to distal.

- **4 Months:** Grasping a rattle when it is placed in the hand. The infant shows a bidextrous grasp approach to a dangling ring.
- **5 Months:** Intentional reaching out for a bright object and grasping it with both hands (bidextrous grasp).
- **6 Months:** Demonstrates an immature grasp (palmar grasp) from the ulnar side.
- **7 Months:** Holding an object with a crude grasp from the palm (palmar grasp). The child can transfer an object (like a cube) from one hand to the other.
- **8 Months:** The infant compares two objects (e.g., cubes) simultaneously.
- **9 Months:** Holding a small object between the index finger and the thumb (pincer grasp). The infant will also look for a dropped cube, indicating the concept of object permanence.
- **9 to 10 Months:** Pokes at a small pellet using the index finger. Develops a crude radial grasp, beginning to use the radial aspect of the hand.
- **12 Months (1 Year):** Mature radial grasp is established, utilizing the thumb and index finger securely.
- **24 Months (2 Years):** The child is able to scribble (the inability to scribble by 24 months is considered a red flag/warning sign for developmental delay).

**DEVELOPMENTAL MILESTONES AT 9 MONTHS**

Development is a continuous process that signifies the acquisition of qualitative and quantitative skills. The developmental milestones achieved by a normal 9-month-old infant are assessed in four major domains:

**1. Gross Motor Development**

**2. Fine Motor and Adaptive Development**

**4. Language and Hearing Development**

- **Vocalization:** The child coos and gurgles with inflection, and speaks bisyllables such as "ma-ma", "pa-pa", or "ba-ba".

10. Red flag signs in child development.
**RED FLAG SIGNS IN CHILD DEVELOPMENT**

Red flag signs (warning signs) indicate a significant deviation or delay in the normal developmental trajectory. A child who exhibits these warning signs or fails to attain a milestone by its upper age limit requires immediate detailed evaluation by a pediatrician.

**I. AGE-SPECIFIC RED FLAG SIGNS (HIGH YIELD)** The following are the critical warning signs of developmental delay adapted from the Mother and Child Protection (MCP) card, categorized by age:

- **At 3 Months:**
    - No social smile.
    - No eye contact or persistent squint.
    - Does not startle or respond to sound.
    - Abnormal posture: Head pushed back with stiff arms and legs.
    - Persistent fisting of the hands.
- **At 6 Months:**
    - Lacks head control or is unable to raise the head when on the tummy.
    - Cannot sit, even with support/help.
    - Does not grasp objects within reach.
    - Does not vocalize.
    - Head and eyes fail to track objects.
- **At 9 Months:**
    - Cannot roll over or sit.
    - Does not turn towards a sound.
    - Does not utter bisyllables (e.g., pa-pa, ma-a, ba-ba).
    - Consistently tilts the head to one side when looking at objects.
- **At 12 Months:**
    - Does not stretch hands out to be picked up.
    - Does not pick up small objects with the finger and thumb (lack of pincer grasp).
    - Does not respond to their own name.
    - Does not search for half-hidden toys that the child saw being hidden.
    - Does not participate in simple social games (e.g., peek-a-boo).
- **At 18 Months:**
    - Cannot stand.
    - Does not use both hands equally (early handedness).
    - Cannot put small objects into a container.
    - Does not point a finger at an object when it is named.
    - Does not respond to the mother's gestures and appears to be in his/her own world.
    - Does not say single words like "mama" or "dada".
- **At 24 Months:**
    - Does not walk steadily while pulling a toy.
    - Cannot scribble.
    - Does not point to body parts.
    - Does not use two-word phrases (e.g., "give milk", "amma come").
    - Does not respond to social gestures like bye-bye or namaste.
    - Does not seem to understand or follow simple instructions.
- **At 36 Months:**
    - Cannot climb stairs or feed self.
    - Continuous drooling or unclear speech.
    - Does not communicate meaningfully and frequently repeats others' speech (echolalia).
    - Cannot speak simple sentences.

**II. UPPER LIMIT OF AGE FOR MILESTONE ATTAINMENT** Failure to achieve the following milestones by these maximum age limits constitutes an absolute red flag warranting definitive testing:

- **Visual fixation or following:** 2 months
- **Vocalization:** 6 months
- **Sitting without support:** 10 months
- **Standing with assistance:** 12 months
- **Hands and knees crawling:** 14 months
- **Standing alone:** 17 months
- **Walking alone:** 18 months
- **Single words:** 18 months
- **Imaginative play:** 3 years

---

**III. UNIVERSAL RED FLAG (AT ANY AGE)**

- **Developmental Regression:** Any loss of previously acquired skills, such as the loss of comprehension, single words, or phrases at any age, is a severe red flag indicating a potential neurodegenerative or pervasive developmental disorder.
