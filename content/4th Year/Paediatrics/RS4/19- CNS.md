1. Neonatal seizures/ convulsions (in a 3 days old) - causes and management
**NEONATAL SEIZURES**

**Etiology (Causes of Neonatal Seizures)** Neonatal seizures are typically subcortical in origin and are never febrile or idiopathic. The etiology can be classified based on the day of onset, which is highly relevant for a 3-day-old neonate:

- **Third Day of Life:**
    - **Hypoglycemia** is the most characteristic cause of seizures presenting specifically on the third day.
- **First and Second Day of Life (Perinatal Problems):**
    - Hypoxic-Ischemic Encephalopathy (HIE) / Birth Asphyxia (Accounts for 40-50% of all neonatal seizures).
    - Birth trauma and intracranial hemorrhage (especially intraventricular hemorrhage in preterm neonates).
    - Drug (narcotic) withdrawal syndrome.
    - Pyridoxine dependency.
    - Accidental injection of maternal local anaesthesia into the infant's scalp during labour.
    - Inborn errors of metabolism such as phenylketonuria (PKU).
- **Fourth Day and Onwards:**
    - **Infections:** Pyogenic meningitis, septicemia, and intrauterine STORCH infections (Syphilis, Toxoplasmosis, Rubella, Cytomegalovirus, Herpes simplex).
    - **Metabolic:** Hypocalcemia (tetany), hypo- or hypernatremia, and hypomagnesemia.
    - **Toxic:** Severe hyperbilirubinemia leading to kernicterus (bilirubin encephalopathy).
    - **Others:** Tetanus neonatorum (usually manifests around 5-6 days).
    - **Congenital Malformations:** Arteriovenous fistulae, porencephaly, congenital hydrocephalus, lissencephaly, and cerebral dysgenesis.

**Management of Neonatal Seizures** A neonatal seizure is a medical emergency. The primary goals are stabilization, correction of transient metabolic abnormalities, and administration of anticonvulsants.

**1. Initial Stabilization and Assessment**

- Identify and characterize the episode as a true seizure.
- Ensure **TABC** (Temperature, Airway, Breathing, Circulation).
- Secure an intravenous (IV) line and immediately check blood glucose and calcium levels.

**2. Correction of Metabolic Abnormalities**

- **Hypoglycemia:** If blood glucose is < 40 mg/dL, administer a stat bolus of 2 mL/kg of 10% dextrose. This must be followed by a continuous glucose infusion at a rate of 6 mg/kg/min.
- **Hypocalcemia/Hypomagnesemia:** If documented or clinically suspected (tetany), administer 10% calcium gluconate (5–10 mL by slow IV) and/or magnesium sulphate as required.

**3. Sequential Anticonvulsant Therapy (If seizures continue despite glucose > 40 mg/dL)**

- **Step 1: Intravenous Phenobarbitone (Gold Standard)**
    - Administer a loading dose of **20 mg/kg** over a period of 20 minutes.
    - If seizures continue, repeat IV phenobarbitone with an additional dose of 20 mg/kg given in two aliquots (maximum loading dose is 40 mg/kg).
    - _Maintenance dose:_ 3–5 mg/kg/day divided into two doses.
- **Step 2: Second-Line Agents (2 Options)**
    - If seizures persist, administer **IV Phenytoin**: Loading dose of 20 mg/kg over 20 minutes under strict cardiac monitoring. (_Maintenance dose:_ 5 mg/kg/day in three doses).
    - _OR_ Administer **IV Levetiracetam**: Loading dose of 50 mg/kg over 20 minutes. (_Maintenance dose:_ 40 mg/kg/day in two doses).
- **Step 3: Third-Line Agents**
    - If seizures still persist, administer **IV Midazolam**: Loading dose of 0.15 mg/kg, followed by a continuous infusion at 1 µg/kg/minute.
    - Consider increasing respiratory support (e.g., mechanical ventilation) as midazolam and phenobarbitone can cause severe respiratory depression.
- **Step 4: Refractory Seizures**
    - Consider **IV Lidocaine** (4 mg/kg).
    - Administer a therapeutic trial of **IM Pyridoxine** (100 mg) to rule out pyridoxine dependency, which typically shows a dramatic and rapid response.
1. Tubercular meningitis — stages, clinical manifestations in each stage, complications, investigations (CSF findings), treatment.
**TUBERCULOUS MENINGITIS (TBM)**

Tuberculous meningitis is the most common and serious form of central nervous system (CNS) tuberculosis, accounting for 12–20% of children with tuberculosis. The maximum risk of developing TBM is 3 to 6 months after the primary infection, leading to peak incidence in preschool children and infants. It invariably results from hematogenous dissemination from a primary focus, usually in the lungs, followed by the discharge of bacilli into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from a small tuberculoma in the cortex, spinal cord, or vertebrae.

**STAGES AND CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS** The clinical course of TBM typically has an insidious onset (though it can be acute in infancy) and progresses through three distinct stages. Each stage roughly lasts for about a week, with considerable overlap.

**1. First or Prodromal Stage** This stage is characterized by nonspecific and vague manifestations:

- Change in disposition and temperament, such as apathy or irritability.
- Drowsiness, malaise, and mild fever.
- Anorexia, vomiting, constipation, and headache.
- Occasional convulsions.

**2. Second or Transitional Stage** This stage is marked by overt manifestations of raised intracranial pressure (ICP) and meningeal irritation:

- **Meningeal signs:** Positive neck rigidity, Kernig sign, and Brudzinski sign.
- **Sensorium:** Progressive drowsiness, leading to stupor or unconsciousness; aggravation of headache and vomiting.
- **Neurological signs:** Hypertonia, extensor plantar reflexes, and elicitable ankle or patellar clonus. Abdominal reflexes disappear. Seizures are common.
- **Cranial Nerve Palsies:** Involvement of the 3rd, 4th, 6th, and 7th cranial nerves is frequent. This manifests as ocular paralysis, strabismus, nystagmus, contracted pupils, and facial palsy.
- **Ophthalmic signs:** Papilledema and the presence of choroid tubercles along the blood vessels of the choroid plexus (seen in a small proportion of cases).
- **Infants:** A bulging and tense anterior fontanelle is often noted.

**3. Third or Terminal Stage** This is the stage of irreversible damage, characterized by paralysis and deep coma:

- Deep coma with only short periods of wakefulness.
- Meningeal signs are no longer prominent.
- Pupils become fixed and dilated.
- Clonic spasms of the limbs, widespread paralysis, decerebrate or decorticate posturing.
- Autonomic instability: Irregular respiration, slow or rapid irregular pulse, and rising fever.
- Development of frank hydrocephalus if treatment is delayed or inadequate.

**COMPLICATIONS AND SEQUELAE** Without treatment, TBM is almost always fatal, and even with treatment, mortality ranges from 33% to 50%. Survivors, particularly those treated in the third stage, are frequently left with severe neurological sequelae:

- Mental retardation and epilepsy.
- Acquired hydrocephalus.
- Cranial nerve paralysis and visual impairment/blindness.
- Motor deficits: Hemiplegia, spasticity, and cerebellar ataxia.
- Endocrinal disturbances: Obesity, diabetes insipidus, and precocious puberty (due to hypothalamic-pituitary axis involvement).

**INVESTIGATIONS** **1. Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF) Analysis** Lumbar puncture is mandatory to confirm the diagnosis and provides the most dependable information:

- **Physical Appearance:** CSF gushes out under high pressure and is clear or slightly turbid. When allowed to stand in a test tube for 12 hours, a characteristic **"cobweb"** forms.
- **Cell Count:** Increased to 10–500 cells/cumm. The predominant cells are **lymphocytes** (though a relative polymorphonuclear predominance may be seen very early in the disease or in infants).
- **Biochemistry:** CSF proteins are moderately elevated, sugar is significantly reduced, and **chlorides are markedly reduced**.
- **Microbiology:** Absolute confirmation is obtained by demonstrating acid-fast bacilli (AFB) via Ziehl-Neelsen or Auramine-O fluorescent smear, culture (Lowenstein-Jensen or BACTEC/MGIT), or guinea-pig inoculation.
- **Special CSF Tests:** Bromide partition test, adenosine deaminase (ADA) levels, and tuberculostearic acid yield quick diagnostic clues. Molecular diagnosis via Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) or DNA probing is highly specific and sensitive.

**2. Neuroimaging**

- **CT Scan / MRI:** Crucial for detecting basal exudates, hydrocephalus (dilated ventricular system), cerebral edema, infarctions, and associated tuberculomas.

**3. Other Supportive Investigations**

- **Tuberculin (Mantoux) Test & BCG Test:** May be positive, though false negatives are common in advanced TBM due to immunosuppression/anergy.
- **Chest X-ray:** To detect a primary complex, miliary mottling, or progressive pulmonary disease.
- **Fundoscopy:** To visualize choroid tubercles, which strongly suggest hematogenous dissemination.
- **Gastric lavage or Sputum:** For AFB smear and culture.

**TREATMENT** Management requires aggressive multidrug antituberculous therapy (ATT), corticosteroids to reduce inflammation, and supportive neurological care.

**1. Specific Antituberculous Therapy (ATT)** The Indian Academy of Pediatrics (IAP) recommends the **Group 5 regimen (2HRZE / 10HRE)** for neurotuberculosis:

- **Intensive Phase (2 months):** Four drugs—Isoniazid (H), Rifampicin (R), Pyrazinamide (Z), and Ethambutol (E) or Streptomycin (S).
- **Continuation Phase (10 months):** Three drugs—Isoniazid (H), Rifampicin (R), and Ethambutol (E).
- _Dosages:_ Isoniazid (5 mg/kg), Rifampicin (10 mg/kg), Pyrazinamide (25 mg/kg), Ethambutol (20 mg/kg), Streptomycin (20 mg/kg).

**2. Corticosteroid Therapy**

- **Prednisolone** (1–2 mg/kg/day) is administered for **8 to 12 weeks**.
- Corticosteroids are essential as they reduce basilar exudates, prevent spinal block, reduce cerebral edema, and lower the profoundly raised intracranial pressure.

**3. Supportive and Symptomatic Management**

- **Reduction of Intracranial Pressure:** Achieved through hypertonic solutions like intravenous mannitol or careful, repeated withdrawal of CSF.
- **Control of Seizures:** Administer anticonvulsants such as phenobarbital, diazepam, lorazepam, or phenytoin round the clock.
- **General Care:** Maintenance of hydration and nutrition (intravenous fluids or Ryle tube feeding for comatose patients), control of hyperpyrexia via tepid sponging/antipyretics, and airway maintenance (frequent suctioning or assisted ventilation).

**4. Surgical Intervention**

- **Shunt procedures** (e.g., ventriculoperitoneal shunt) are indicated if the child develops obstructive hydrocephalus.
