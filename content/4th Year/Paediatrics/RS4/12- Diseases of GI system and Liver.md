1. Portal hypertension in children — etiology, diagnosis (clinical and investigation approach), management (of complications — hematemesis).

**PORTAL HYPERTENSION IN CHILDREN**

**Definition** Normal portal pressure is between 5 and 10 mm Hg. Portal hypertension is defined as an increase in portal pressure of >12 mm Hg. It occurs due to increased portal resistance, leading to the development of portosystemic venous channels giving rise to esophageal, gastric, or colonic varices.

**ETIOLOGY** The causes of portal hypertension are anatomically classified into three categories:

**1. Prehepatic (Extrahepatic)**

- **Extrahepatic Portal Venous Obstruction (EHPVO):** The commonest cause of portal hypertension in children in India. It is characterized by cavernous transformation of the portal vein. Common underlying risk factors include neonatal umbilical sepsis, umbilical vein catheterization, dehydration, or congenital anomalies leading to splenic/portal vein thrombosis.
- Portal venous thrombosis and isolated splenic vein thrombosis.

**2. Intrahepatic**

- Liver cirrhosis (most common cause in the West, accounts for 25–35% in India). In India, Indian childhood cirrhosis is a notable cause.
- Congenital hepatic fibrosis and noncirrhotic portal fibrosis (NCPF).
- Veno-occlusive disease (VOD), schistosomiasis, and nodular regenerative hyperplasia.

**3. Posthepatic**

- Budd-Chiari syndrome (obstruction of hepatic vein or inferior vena cava).
- Constrictive pericarditis.

**DIAGNOSIS**

**A. Clinical Approach (Clinical Features)** The presentation varies depending on the etiology and age (infancy to teenage).

- **EHPVO (Non-Cirrhotic):** Usually presents with well-tolerated episodes of upper GI bleeding (hematemesis and melena) due to ruptured esophageal or gastric varices. Splenomegaly is the most constant sign, and isolated splenomegaly is seen in 10-20% of cases. History of neonatal umbilical sepsis or catheterization is a strong clue. Stigmata of chronic liver disease and overt hepatic encephalopathy are characteristically absent. Ascites is uncommon, though transient ascites may follow a variceal bleed.
- **Cirrhosis (Intrahepatic):** Presents with jaundice, ascites, and hepatosplenomegaly. Stigmata of chronic liver disease (spider angiomata, palmar erythema) and hepatic encephalopathy are commonly seen.
- **Budd-Chiari Syndrome:** Ascites, hepatomegaly, and tortuous prominent back veins with flow from below upwards.
- **General Signs:** Presence of caput medusae (dilated umbilical portosystemic collaterals) may be seen.

**B. Investigative Approach**

- **Ultrasound and Doppler Study:** The primary screening tool. It defines vascular anatomy, detects blocks in portal, splenic, or hepatic veins, and reveals collaterals, ascites, and splenomegaly. Increased portal vein size suggests intrahepatic disease, while cavernous transformation confirms EHPVO.
- **Upper GI Endoscopy:** Crucial to visualize the presence of esophageal and gastric varices or congestive gastropathy.
- **Liver Function Tests (LFTs):** Grossly deranged in cirrhosis (poor synthetic function, elevated transaminases), but typically well-preserved and normal in non-cirrhotic causes like EHPVO.
- **Hemogram:** May show anemia, leukopenia, and thrombocytopenia, which suggests hypersplenism.
- **Splenic Venoportogram/Selective CT and MR Portovenography:** Splenic venoportography is highly reliable in locating the site of the block and demonstrating high splenic pulp pressure (>12 mm Hg). CT/MR is useful to delineate vascular anatomy in Budd-Chiari syndrome or in planning shunt surgeries for EHPVO.
- **Liver Biopsy:** Essential to differentiate cirrhotic from non-cirrhotic causes if imaging is inconclusive.

**MANAGEMENT OF COMPLICATIONS: HEMATEMESIS (VARICEAL BLEEDING)** Acute upper gastrointestinal bleeding from esophageal or gastric varices is a life-threatening medical emergency.

**1. General Management & Resuscitation**

- **Stabilize:** Maintain airway, support breathing, and ensure circulation.
- **Vascular Access & Fluids:** Insert two large-bore IV cannulas. Rapid volume replacement with crystalloids (normal saline or Ringer's lactate at 10–20 ml/kg).
- **Blood Transfusion:** Send blood for grouping and cross-matching. Transfuse packed cells or whole blood to maintain a target hemoglobin of ~7 g/dL or a hematocrit of 30%.
- **Correct Coagulopathy:** Administer Vitamin K and fresh frozen plasma (FFP).
- **Nasogastric Tube:** Insertion helps decompress the stomach, assess the bleeding, and perform iced-saline lavage.
- **Antibiotic Prophylaxis:** Short-term third-generation cephalosporin for 7 days reduces bacterial infection and mortality in cirrhotic children with variceal bleeds.

**2. Pharmacotherapy**

- Start early intravenous infusion of **Somatostatin** or its analogue, **Octreotide**. This decreases splanchnic and azygous blood flow, effectively reducing portal pressure.
- Infusion must be continued for at least 24–48 hours after bleeding has stopped.

**3. Endoscopic Therapy**

- Must be performed within 12 hours of the bleed.
- **Endoscopic Variceal Ligation (EVL):** The preferred method due to fewer side effects. Bands are deployed around the sucked-in variceal columns.
- **Endoscopic Sclerotherapy (EST):** Injection of 1–3 mL of sclerosant (e.g., 1% ethoxysclerol) into each variceal column.
- **Tissue Adhesive Glue:** Agents like N-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate are injected endoscopically to manage bleeding gastric varices, hardening upon contact with blood for rapid hemostasis.

**4. Balloon Tamponade**

- Required only if pharmacologic and endoscopic measures fail. A Sengstaken-Blakemore tube (triple lumen with esophageal and gastric balloons) is used to compress the bleeding varices.

**5. Shunt Procedures & Surgery**

- **TIPS (Transjugular Intrahepatic Portosystemic Shunt):** An interventional radiology technique placing a stent between the portal and hepatic veins to bypass liver resistance. Indicated for refractory bleeding from ectopic varices.
- **Surgical Management:** Considered when bleeding cannot be controlled endoscopically. Options include portocaval/splenorenal shunts or devascularization with esophageal staple transection.

**6. Secondary Prophylaxis (Prevention of Re-bleeding)**

- **Beta-blockers:** Propranolol is highly effective in preventing recurrent GI bleeds by decreasing cardiac output and portal pressure.
- All patients must receive repeated endoscopic prophylaxis (band ligation or sclerotherapy) until the complete eradication of varices is achieved.

2. List the complications of acute watery diarrhea

**COMPLICATIONS OF ACUTE WATERY DIARRHEA**

The two most important consequences of acute diarrhea in children are dehydration and malnutrition. The complications can be systematically categorized into the following high-yield groups:

**1. Fluid, Electrolyte and Metabolic Complications**

- **Dehydration and Shock:** Rapid fluid loss leads to varying grades of dehydration, which can culminate in hypovolemic shock, cold and clammy skin, and almost impalpable peripheral pulses.
- **Dyselectrolytemia:** Significant electrolyte imbalances frequently occur, including marked hyponatremia, hypocalcemia, and hypomagnesemia.
- **Hypoglycemia:** A common metabolic complication that can worsen the clinical state and contribute to neurological signs.

**2. Renal Complications**

- **Acute Renal Shutdown:** Severe, uncorrected dehydration leads to impaired renal perfusion, resulting in oliguria or anuria and acute renal failure.

**3. Neurological Complications**

- **Seizures:** Can be precipitated by multiple factors during an acute diarrheal episode, including fever, marked hyponatremia, the rapid correction of hypernatremia, hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, encephalitis, or concomitant _Shigella_ infection.
- **Cerebral Thrombosis:** The high viscosity of blood, particularly in hypertonic dehydration, predisposes the child to cerebral thrombosis.
- **Subdural Effusion/Hemorrhage:** Subdural collection of fluid or blood can occur, which may possibly cause mental retardation in later life.

**4. Gastrointestinal and Nutritional Complications**

- **Malnutrition:** Diarrhea and malnutrition form a vicious cycle; acute diarrhea can precipitate severe malnutrition, vitamin deficiencies, and mineral deficiencies.
- **Paralytic Ileus:** Manifested by abdominal distension and absent bowel sounds, often related to hypokalemia.
- **Carbohydrate Intolerance:** Transient damage to the intestinal mucosa and loss of brush-border enzymes can lead to carbohydrate (lactose/monosaccharide) intolerance, predisposing the child to chronic or persistent diarrhea.
- **Toxic Megacolon:** A life-threatening widening of the large intestine.

**5. Hematological and Cardiovascular Complications**

- **Thrombo-embolism:** Increased blood viscosity and dehydration heighten the risk of systemic thrombo-embolic events.
- **Consumptive Coagulopathy:** Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) may complicate severe or systemic cases.
- **Congestive Cardiac Failure (CCF):** Typically an iatrogenic complication resulting from overhydration during intravenous fluid resuscitation.

**6. Endocrinal and Infective Complications**

- **SIADH:** The syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone (SIADH) may complicate the clinical picture.
- **Superadded Infections:** Children with severe diarrhea and malnutrition are at increased risk of severe systemic infections. Iatrogenic infections, such as thrombophlebitis at the site of an intravenous cutdown, are also notable complications.

2. Low osmolar WHO oral rehydration salt - (ORS) - composition
**LOW OSMOLAR WHO ORAL REHYDRATION SALT (ORS)**

**Introduction** Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT) is the cornerstone of management for acute diarrhoeal dehydration. Based on WHO/UNICEF and IAP recommendations, the Government of India has adopted the low osmolarity ORS as the single universal ORS to be used for all ages and all types of diarrhoea. It has a total osmolarity of 245 mOsm/L, replacing the older standard formulation which had an osmolarity of 311 mOsm/L.

**Composition (per Litre of Water)** The contents to be dissolved in 1 Litre of clean drinking water are:

- **Sodium chloride:** 2.6 g
- **Glucose, anhydrous:** 13.5 g
- **Potassium chloride:** 1.5 g
- **Trisodium citrate, dihydrate:** 2.9 g

**Osmolarity (mmol/L)** The individual ionic concentrations yield a total osmolarity of 245 mOsm/L:

- **Sodium:** 75 mmol/L
- **Chloride:** 65 mmol/L
- **Glucose (anhydrous):** 75 mmol/L
- **Potassium:** 20 mmol/L
- **Citrate:** 10 mmol/L
- **Total Osmolarity:** 245 mOsm/L

**Physiological Basis of ORS**

- **Glucose-Coupled Sodium Absorption:** In acute diarrhoea, despite the pathogen-induced dysfunction of several active pumping mechanisms, the glucose-dependent sodium pump remains intact.
- **Mechanism:** One molecule of glucose transports one molecule of sodium and drags water along across the intestinal mucosa, resulting in the repletion of water and sodium losses.
- **Optimal Ratio:** The optimal absorption occurs when glucose and sodium are present in a 1:1 molar ratio, which is the principle behind the WHO ORS.

**Advantages of Low Osmolar ORS (High Yield)**

- **Avoids Hypernatremia:** The reduced osmolarity (from 311 to 245 mOsm/L) minimizes the risk of hypernatremia, which was a concern with the older standard formulation.
- **Reduces Stool Output:** Keeping the luminal osmolality lower than blood (290 mOsm/L) allows for a greater concentration gradient, driving better absorption of fluids and electrolytes (solvent drag). It significantly reduces total stool output.
- **Decreases Vomiting:** It has been shown to reduce the frequency of vomiting.
- **Shortens Duration:** It shortens the overall duration of the diarrhoeal episode.
- **Reduces IV Fluid Requirement:** Due to improved absorption and reduced losses, it significantly decreases the need for unscheduled intravenous fluid therapy.
- **Increased Shelf-life:** The use of trisodium citrate dihydrate (2.9 g) instead of sodium bicarbonate enhances the shelf-life and stability of the ORS packets. Citrate also effectively corrects diarrhoeal acidosis.

3. 5 year old child presents with bloody stools. Mention the causative organisms
**CAUSATIVE ORGANISMS OF BLOODY STOOLS IN A 5-YEAR-OLD**

The presence of grossly visible blood and mucus in the stools is clinically termed dysentery. In a 5-year-old child, the causative organisms for bloody diarrhea or lower gastrointestinal bleeding are broadly classified into bacterial, parasitic, and viral agents.

**1. BACTERIAL PATHOGENS (Invasive Bacteria)** Bacterial infections are the most frequent cause of acute dysentery in children.

- **_Shigella_ species:** This is the most common cause of bacillary dysentery. The species include _S. flexneri_ (the commonest organism reported in developing countries), _S. dysenteriae_ (associated with epidemics and hemolytic uremic syndrome), _S. boydii_, and _S. sonnei_.
- **_Escherichia coli_:** Specific strains cause bloody stools, primarily Enterohemorrhagic _E. coli_ (EHEC), which causes hemorrhagic colitis and can lead to hemolytic uremic syndrome, and Enteroinvasive _E. coli_ (EIEC), which causes a shigellosis-like illness with mucosal invasion and exudation.
- **_Salmonella_ species:** Nontyphoidal _Salmonella_ is frequently isolated in childhood diarrheas and can cause invasive bloody stools.
- **_Campylobacter jejuni_:** An important invasive bacterium recognized as a major differential diagnosis for bloody diarrhea.
- **_Clostridium difficile_:** A gram-positive anaerobic bacillus that produces toxins A and B, causing pseudomembranous colitis and hemorrhagic colitis, usually following broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy.
- **Other Bacteria:** Organisms such as _Yersinia enterocolitica_, _Aeromonas hydrophilia_, _Plesiomonas shigelloides_, and _Mycobacterium tuberculosis_ (causing abdominal/intestinal tuberculosis) can also present with blood in the stools.

**2. PROTOZOAL AND HELMINTHIC PATHOGENS (Parasites)**

- **_Entamoeba histolytica_:** Causes amebic dysentery, accounting for approximately 5% of dysentery cases. It invades the large intestine to cause both luminal and invasive mucosal ulcerations.
- **_Trichuris trichiura_ (Whipworm):** Heavy infestation in the cecum and ascending colon can cause prolonged diarrhea with blood-streaked stools, tenesmus, malnutrition, and rectal prolapse.
- **_Giardia lamblia_:** Although classically associated with watery, fatty, and steatorrheic stools, it can occasionally present with acute dysentery-like manifestations.
- **_Hymenolepis nana_ (Dwarf tapeworm):** May manifest with a sudden onset of mucous or bloody diarrhea accompanied by abdominal pain.
- **_Strongyloides stercoralis_:** An intestinal nematode that can cause severe diarrhea, which may sometimes be bloody.
- **Hookworm:** While typically causing occult blood loss or melena from the upper-to-mid gastrointestinal tract, it is an important differential for blood loss in stools.
- **_Schistosoma_ (Bilharzia):** A pre-eminent cause of bloody diarrhea in certain endemic areas.

**3. VIRAL PATHOGENS**

- **Cytomegalovirus (CMV):** Causes CMV colitis or enterocolitis, particularly seen in immunocompromised children or those receiving chemotherapy.
- **Rotavirus and Adenovirus:** While typically causing acute watery diarrhea, they can occasionally present with blood in the stools (up to 25% have mucus and occasionally blood) and are recognized infectious triggers for intussusception, which classically presents with red currant-jelly stools.

**HIGH-YIELD NON-INFECTIOUS DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES (>2 YEARS OF AGE)** For comprehensive exam purposes, if a 5-year-old child presents with lower gastrointestinal bleeding, the following non-infectious etiologies must also be ruled out:

- **Structural/Surgical:** Anal fissure (painful defecation with bright red blood coating hard stools), Rectal polyp or polyposis syndrome (painless bleeding), Intussusception (episodic pain, vomiting, and red currant-jelly stools), and Meckel's diverticulum (painless, massive dark red bleeding).
- **Inflammatory/Autoimmune:** Inflammatory Bowel Disease (Ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease), and Henoch-Schönlein purpura (IgA vasculitis).
- **Systemic/Hematologic:** Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (HUS), blood dyscrasias (e.g., leukemia), and systemic coagulopathies.
