**1. A 10-year-old child is brought to the emergency with progressive weakness of the lower limbs since 3 days.**
a. What is acute flaccid paralysis?  
b. Describe any three differential diagnoses.

**A. ACUTE FLACCID PARALYSIS (AFP)**

**Definition:** Acute flaccid paralysis is a clinical syndrome characterized by a rapid onset of weakness that progresses to maximum severity within several days to weeks.

**Key Characteristics:**

- The term 'flaccid' denotes that the involved limbs are floppy or limp, indicating the absence of spasticity or any other upper motor neuron signs.
- **WHO Surveillance Definition:** In the Global Polio Eradication Initiative, AFP is defined as any case of flaccid weakness occurring in children <15 years of age, or any paralytic illness at any age when poliomyelitis is suspected.

---

**B. DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES OF AFP**

A 10-year-old child presenting with progressive lower limb weakness of 3 days duration is a classic presentation of Acute Flaccid Paralysis. Three high-yield differential diagnoses include:

**1. Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS)** Also known as Acute Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyradiculoneuropathy, this is a postinfectious polyneuritis.

- **Clinical Presentation:** Characterized by acute, bilaterally symmetrical, ascending flaccid paralysis. The weakness typically begins in the lower limbs and spreads upwards to involve the trunk, upper limbs, and face.
- **Antecedent History:** It frequently occurs 1–6 weeks following a viral upper respiratory or gastrointestinal infection.
- **Neurological Signs:** Deep tendon reflexes are sluggish or completely absent (areflexia). Variable sensory involvement (paresthesias) may be present, which helps differentiate it from polio. Bladder and bowel involvement is usually transient and late due to autonomic dysfunction.
- **Hallmark Investigation:** Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis typically reveals "albumino-cytological dissociation" (markedly high protein concentration with a normal or only slightly raised mononuclear cell count), usually appearing in the second week of illness.

**2. Poliomyelitis** An acute viral infection caused by an RNA enterovirus that specifically targets the anterior horn cells of the spinal cord.

- **Clinical Presentation:** Manifests as an acute onset of patchy and asymmetrical flaccid paralysis. It affects the lower limbs more often than the upper limbs, and large muscles more than small muscles.
- **Associated Features:** The paralysis is usually accompanied by fever, significant muscle pain, and tenderness (spasm).
- **Neurological Signs:** There is hypotonia and loss of reflexes in the affected muscle groups. A pathognomonic feature is that there is absolutely no sensory loss. Bladder and bowel complaints are absent.
- **Hallmark Investigation:** CSF shows lymphocytic pleocytosis with normal or slightly increased proteins. Stool examination for poliovirus isolation is the gold standard for confirmation.

**3. Transverse Myelitis** An acute demyelinating inflammatory condition of the spinal cord.

- **Clinical Presentation:** Characterized by a sudden onset of symmetrical leg weakness (paraplegia). It may be preceded by a prodromal illness.
- **Neurological Signs:** Unlike GBS or polio, there is a distinct sensory loss and impairment of all movements and reflexes below a specific affected segmental level of the spinal cord.
- **Autonomic Signs:** Significant bladder and bowel dysfunction occurs present and early in the course of the disease.
- **Hallmark Investigation:** Contrast-enhanced Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) of the spine demonstrates characteristic changes of demyelination.
