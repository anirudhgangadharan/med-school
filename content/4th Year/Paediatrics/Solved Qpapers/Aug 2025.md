1. 15 yrs old adolescent female is admitted with history of fever for 2 weeks, productive cough and underweight with family history of contact with Koch’s
	
	a) List the clinical types b) Discuss the investigations and treatment of tuberculosis in paediatric age group


**A. CLINICAL TYPES OF TUBERCULOSIS IN PEDIATRIC AGE GROUP**

**I. Intrathoracic Tuberculosis**

- **Primary Pulmonary Complex:** Often a silent primary focus, generally asymptomatic in young children.
- **Hilar Lymphadenitis:** Presents with cough, fever, and weight loss.
- **Progressive Primary Disease (PPD):** Results from uncontained focal progression causing lobar consolidation or bronchopneumonia.
- **Pleural Effusion:** Occurs following the rupture of a subpleural focus into the pleural cavity, mostly seen in children >5 years of age,.
- **Chronic Pulmonary Tuberculosis (Reinfection / Adult Type):** Most commonly seen in adolescent girls, characterized by apical/infraclavicular lesions, extensive fibrosis, and frequent cavitation. _(Note: Highly probable in this 15-year-old female patient)._

**II. Miliary Tuberculosis**

- Characterized by hematogenous dissemination resulting in extensive "snow-storm" miliary mottling of the lungs and multiorgan involvement (liver, spleen, choroid tubercles),.

**III. Extrathoracic Tuberculosis**

- **Central Nervous System (CNS) Tuberculosis:** Includes Tuberculous Meningitis (TBM), Tuberculous Encephalopathy, and Tuberculoma,,,.
- **Superficial Lymphadenitis:** Most common extrathoracic form, frequently involving cervical and axillary glands which may mat and caseate.
- **Abdominal Tuberculosis:** Presents as ascitic (wet) type, plastic/hypertrophic (dry) type, or intestinal ulceration,.
- **Osteoarticular Tuberculosis:** Tuberculous osteomyelitis (short/long bones), tuberculous arthritis (hip, knee), and Pott's spine (tuberculosis of the spine).
- **Skin Tuberculosis:** Erythema nodosum, scrofuloderma, lupus vulgaris, tuberculides, and tuberculosis verrucosa cutis,.
- **Congenital Tuberculosis:** Acquired via transplacental spread or ingestion/inhalation of infected amniotic fluid,.

---

**B. INVESTIGATIONS AND TREATMENT OF TUBERCULOSIS**

**INVESTIGATIONS** **1. Immunological and Screening Tests**

- **Tuberculin (Mantoux) Test:** Intradermal injection of 5 TU of PPD. An induration of >10 mm after 48–72 hours suggests active disease,.
- **BCG Diagnostic Test:** Useful in detecting hypersensitivity in severely malnourished or critically ill children where Mantoux may be falsely negative,.

**2. Radiological Studies**

- **Chest X-ray:** Detects unifocal/homogenous consolidation (Primary Complex), hilar/paratracheal adenopathy, apical cavitation, miliary shadows, or pleural effusion,.
- **CT Scan / MRI:** Highly sensitive for localizing occult hilar/mediastinal nodes, tuberculomas, basal exudates in TBM, or skeletal lesions,,.

**3. Microbiological and Molecular Assays (Gold Standard)**

- **Specimen Collection:** Early morning gastric aspirate (minimum two samples), induced sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), CSF, pleural fluid, or lymph node aspirate,,.
- **Smear Microscopy:** Ziehl-Neelsen (acid-fast) staining or Fluorescent microscopy (auramine-rhodamine) to demonstrate tubercle bacilli,.
- **Culture:** Using Lowenstein-Jensen (LJ) medium (takes 6–12 weeks) or Mycobacterial Growth Indicator Tube (MGIT) for faster isolation,.
- **Cartridge-Based Nucleic Acid Amplification Test (CBNAAT):** Tests like GeneXpert and TrueNAT are rapid, preferred methods that simultaneously confirm _M. tuberculosis_ and test for rifampicin resistance,.

**4. Histopathology**

- Fine Needle Aspiration Cytology (FNAC) or biopsy of lymph nodes, liver, or bone marrow showing classical granulomas with epithelioid cells, Langhans giant cells, and central caseating necrosis,.

**TREATMENT** **1. Principles of Chemotherapy**

- Treatment requires Short-Course Chemotherapy (SCC) using multiple drugs to rapidly kill multiplying bacilli and prevent the emergence of drug-resistant mutants,,.
- Divided into an **Intensive Phase (2 months)** with 3–4 drugs, followed by a **Continuation Phase (4–10 months)** with 2–3 drugs,.

**2. First-Line Antitubercular Drugs & Dosages**,

- **Isoniazid (H):** 5 to 15 mg/kg/day (daily dose usually 5-7 mg/kg; max 300 mg).
- **Rifampicin (R):** 10 to 20 mg/kg/day.
- **Pyrazinamide (Z):** 25 to 35 mg/kg/day.
- **Ethambutol (E):** 15 to 25 mg/kg/day.
- **Streptomycin (S):** 20 to 40 mg/kg/day (intramuscular).

**3. IAP Recommended Treatment Regimens**,,

- **Group 1 (Preventive):** 6 months HR (For asymptomatic Mantoux +ve children <3 yrs, contacts <3 yrs, or <5 yrs with severe malnutrition).
- **Group 2 (Primary complex, isolated lymphadenitis, pleural effusion):** 2 months HRZ + 4 months HR.
- **Group 3 (Progressive pulmonary disease, multiple lymphadenitis):** 2 months HRZE + 4 months HR. _(Note: Patient likely falls into Group 3 or 4 based on symptomatic cavitatory/adult-type disease)._
- **Group 4 (Miliary/disseminated, cavitatory/bronchopneumonia, osteoarticular, abdominal):** 2 months HRZE + 7 months HR.
- **Group 5 (Neurotuberculosis):** 2 months HRZE + 10 months HRE.

**4. Role of Corticosteroids**

- **Prednisolone** (1–2 mg/kg/day for 4–8 weeks; 8-12 weeks in TBM) is indicated to reduce inflammation in: CNS tuberculosis, miliary tuberculosis, endobronchial TB, severe pleural/pericardial effusion, and genitourinary TB,.

**5. Supportive & Surgical Management**

- Assure high caloric intake, protein supplementation, and correction of underlying anemia.
- Surgical intervention is indicated for complications such as fluctuating/caseating lymph node abscesses, relieving airway obstructions, constrictive pericarditis, or spinal decompression in Pott's spine,,.

2.   One year old child is brought to the OPD with complaints of decreased activity and on and off respiratory infections since 3 months with family history of blood transfusion. On examination, child looks very pale, and has hepatosplenomegaly. a) Write the probable diagnosis. b) Describe the investigations necessary for this child c) Elaborate on management of this clinical condition.
**a) Probable Diagnosis**

**Beta Thalassemia Major (Cooley’s Anemia / Mediterranean Anemia)**

_Justification:_ The clinical presentation of progressive severe pallor, hepatosplenomegaly, and recurrent respiratory infections starting in later infancy (around 3 to 6 months of age), combined with a family history requiring blood transfusions, is highly characteristic of homozygous Beta Thalassemia Major.

**b) Investigations Necessary for this Child**

- **Complete Blood Count (CBC) and Red Cell Indices:**
    - Reveals severe anemia with hemoglobin levels typically ranging from 2–8 g/dL (or 4–9 g/dL).
    - Mean corpuscular volume (MCV <80 fl) and mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH <27 pg) are significantly reduced, indicating microcytic, hypochromic anemia.
- **Peripheral Blood Film (PBF):**
    - Demonstrates marked microcytosis and hypochromasia with pronounced anisocytosis and poikilocytosis.
    - Characteristic findings include target cells, nucleated red blood cells (normoblasts), fragmented erythrocytes, polychromatophilic cells, and basophilic stippling.
    - Reticulocyte count is characteristically elevated (5–8%).
- **High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) / Hemoglobin Electrophoresis:**
    - This is the confirmatory diagnostic test and must be sent _prior_ to the first blood transfusion.
    - It reveals an absence or marked reduction of adult hemoglobin (HbA) and a significant elevation of fetal hemoglobin (HbF), which characteristically exceeds 40% of the total hemoglobin.
- **Osmotic Fragility Test:** Reveals reduced osmotic fragility, meaning the red cells show increased resistance to hemolysis in hypotonic solutions.
- **Radiological Studies:**
    - X-ray of the skull may show the classic "hair-on-end" appearance due to vertical striations from the widening of the diploic space and atrophy of the outer table.
    - X-rays of long bones, metacarpals, and metatarsals may show thinning of the cortex, widening of the medulla, and coarsening of trabeculations secondary to marked erythroid marrow hyperplasia.
- **Bone Marrow Examination:** Will show marked erythroid hyperplasia.

**c) Management of this Clinical Condition**

**1. Blood Transfusion Therapy:**

- This is the mainstay of palliative treatment. Regular packed red blood cell transfusions should be initiated at an early age if the hemoglobin persists below 7 g/dL.
- Transfusions are given periodically at 3–5 week intervals with the goal of maintaining a pre-transfusion hemoglobin level of 9–10 g/dL (up to 11 g/dL). This suppresses ineffective erythropoiesis, promotes normal growth, and prevents bony deformities.

**2. Iron Chelation Therapy:**

- Essential to prevent morbidity and mortality from iron overload (hemosiderosis and hemochromatosis) leading to cardiac, hepatic, and endocrine toxicity.
- It is usually initiated after 2–3 years of transfusions (or 15–20 transfusions) or when serum ferritin exceeds 1000 $\mu$g/L.
- **Deferoxamine (DFO):** The gold standard, administered via a prolonged subcutaneous infusion pump at a dose of 40–60 mg/kg/day over 8–12 hours for 5–6 days a week.
- **Oral Chelators:** Deferasirox (binds iron with high affinity, excreted in feces) and Deferiprone (administered at 50–75 mg/kg/day or 75-100 mg/kg/day) are effective alternatives.

**3. Nutritional and Supportive Care:**

- Strict avoidance of iron preparations.
- Supplementation with folic acid, small doses of Vitamin C (100 mg/day, to enhance iron excretion via chelation), and Vitamin E (alpha-tocopherol).
- Drinking tea with meals is recommended to decrease the intestinal absorption of iron.

**4. Curative Therapy (Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation):**

- Allogeneic bone marrow transplant (BMT) or hematopoietic stem cell transplant from an HLA-matched sibling is the only definitive curative treatment.
- Early transplantation, before the onset of iron-overload complications, yields the best outcomes.

**5. Splenectomy:**

- Considered only if the patient develops severe hypersplenism (indicated by excessive destruction of erythrocytes and an increasing transfusion requirement of more than 200–250 mL/kg/year) or massive splenomegaly causing discomfort.
- The procedure should be delayed until the child is at least 5 years of age to minimize the risk of overwhelming post-splenectomy sepsis, and must be preceded by pneumococcal immunization and followed by prophylactic antibiotics.
