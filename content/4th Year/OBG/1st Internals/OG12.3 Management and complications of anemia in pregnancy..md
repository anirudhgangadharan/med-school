### **1. Management of Anemia in Pregnancy**

The management of anemia in pregnancy is divided into prophylactic and curative approaches, based on accurate diagnosis of the cause and severity of the anemia.

**A. Prophylactic Management:**

- **Spacing of Birth:** A minimum interval of at least 2 to 3 years between pregnancies is advised to replenish the iron lost during childbirth and lactation.
- **Dietary Prescription:** A realistic, balanced diet rich in iron, protein, and vitamins is prescribed. Iron-rich foods include liver, meat, egg, green vegetables, beans, and jaggery. Iron utensils can be used for cooking, and tea should be avoided within 1 hour of taking iron tablets.
- **Routine Supplementation:** As only 20% of pregnant women have adequate iron stores, routine supplementation is essential. Daily administration of 200 mg ferrous sulfate (containing 60 mg elemental iron) along with 1 mg folic acid is recommended. For all women of reproductive age, 400 µg of folic acid daily is advised, increased to 5 mg/day in high-demand states (e.g., multiple pregnancy, hemoglobinopathies).
- **Eradication of Root Causes:** Prompt treatment to eradicate hookworm infestation, dysentery, malaria, bleeding piles, and urinary tract infections.
- **Antenatal Screening:** Hemoglobin estimation should be routinely done at the first antenatal visit, at 30 weeks, and at 36 weeks to detect falling levels early.

**B. Curative Management:** The choice of therapy depends on the severity of anemia, duration of pregnancy (time available before delivery), and associated complicating factors.

**1. Oral Iron Therapy:**

- **Indications:** The best and first-line treatment for most cases. Iron is best absorbed in the ferrous form (ferrous sulfate, fumarate, gluconate, or succinate).
- **Dose:** 200 mg of ferrous sulfate (or equivalent) thrice daily after meals, providing about 180 mg of elemental iron daily. It must be continued for at least 100 days postpartum to replenish iron stores.
- **Response:** Evidenced by a sense of well-being, increased appetite, reticulocytosis within 7-10 days, and a rise in Hb by 2.0 g/dL every 3-4 weeks.
- **Side Effects:** Gastric irritation, nausea, vomiting, constipation, diarrhea, epigastric pain, and a metallic taste.

**2. Parenteral Iron Therapy (Intramuscular/Intravenous):**

- **Indications:** Intolerance to oral iron (severe GI side effects), non-compliance, or severe anemia seen for the first time in advanced pregnancy (>32 weeks) where rapid replenishment is needed before labor.
- **Preparations and Administration:** Iron sucrose, sodium ferric gluconate, or iron isomaltose can be used intravenously. It can be given as multiple doses or as a Total Dose Infusion (TDI).
- **TDI Calculation:** Total dose of iron required (mg) = body weight (kg) x [target hemoglobin - actual hemoglobin (g/L)] x 0.24 + 500 mg (to replenish stores).

**3. Blood Transfusion:**

- **Indications:** Severe anemia detected beyond 36 weeks (to urgently improve oxygen-carrying capacity before labor), correction of blood loss (APH/PPH), refractory anemia not responding to hematinics, or associated severe infections.
- **Product:** Packed Red Blood Cells (PRBC) are transfused.
- **Hazards:** Risk of precipitating premature labor, cardiac failure and pulmonary edema due to circulatory overload, and transfusion reactions.

### **2. Management During Labor and Puerperium**

**During Labor:**

- **First Stage:** Nurse the patient in a comfortable, propped-up position in bed. Provide continuous oxygen inhalation and maintain strict asepsis. Monitor pulse, BP, and respiration closely for early signs of cardiac failure, and restrict IV fluids to less than 125 mL/hr.
- **Second Stage:** Prophylactic operative vaginal delivery (forceps or ventouse) may be done to shorten the duration of the second stage and reduce maternal exhaustion.
- **Third Stage:** Active management is mandatory. Injection Oxytocin 10 IU IM is given immediately following the delivery of the baby. Note: Ergometrine is avoided to prevent sudden cardiac overload. Any significant blood loss must be promptly compensated by fresh PRBC transfusion, covered with a prophylactic diuretic (e.g., Furosemide 20 mg IV) to prevent volume overload.

**During Puerperium:**

- Prophylactic antibiotics are given to prevent puerperal sepsis.
- Continue pre-delivery oral hematinic therapy for at least 100 days until clinical and hematological parameters normalize.
- Counseling in the postpartum clinic regarding effective contraception and birth spacing.

### **3. Maternal Complications**

Anemia contributes directly or indirectly to about 20% of maternal deaths in low and middle-income countries.

- **During Pregnancy:**
    
    - Increased incidence of pre-eclampsia (often related to underlying hypoproteinemia).
    - Intercurrent infections (pre-existing diseases flare up, and infections further depress bone marrow erythropoiesis).
    - Congestive heart failure (highest risk around 30-32 weeks) and angina pectoris.
    - Preterm labor and prematurity.
    - Specific deficiencies can cause atrophic glossitis and sideropenic dysphagia (Plummer-Vinson syndrome). Folic acid deficiency specifically increases the risk of abruptio placentae and miscarriage.
- **During Labor:**
    
    - Uterine inertia is uncommon; labor is often short due to multiparity and a small baby.
    - **Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH):** A life-threatening threat, as an anemic mother cannot tolerate even minimal/normal blood loss.
    - **Congestive Cardiac Failure (CCF):** Highly likely during labor or immediately after delivery when blood from the contracting uterus is squeezed into the general circulation, overloading the hypoxic myocardium.
    - Profound shock and lethal hypoxia from even slight hemorrhage.
- **During Puerperium:**
    
    - Puerperal sepsis, poor or delayed wound healing, and subinvolution.
    - Puerperal venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism.
    - Failing or poor lactation, and postpartum depression.
- **High-Risk Periods for Sudden Maternal Death:** (1) At 30-32 weeks of pregnancy, (2) During labor, (3) Immediately following delivery, and (4) Anytime in the puerperium (especially 7-10 days postpartum due to cardiac failure or pulmonary embolism).

### **4. Fetal and Neonatal Complications**

The amount of iron transferred to the fetus is typically unaffected unless the mother has severe iron deficiency. However, the cumulative effects of maternal anemia lead to high perinatal morbidity and mortality:

- Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR).
- Preterm delivery and dysmaturity.
- In severe anemia: Reduced amniotic fluid volume, low fetal oxygenation, and Intrauterine Death (IUD).
- **Neonatal Anemia:** A term baby may not appear anemic at birth but is born with little to no reserve iron, developing anemia in infancy.
- **Fetal Malformations:** Specifically associated with megaloblastic (folic acid deficiency) anemia, increasing the risk of neural tube defects, cleft lip, and cleft palate.
