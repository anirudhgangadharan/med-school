### **1. Hematological Changes in Pregnancy**

**Blood Volume and Plasma Volume**

- During pregnancy, the blood volume is markedly raised, starting to increase from about the 6th week and expanding rapidly to a maximum of 40-50% (about 1500 mL) above the nonpregnant level at 30-34 weeks.
- Plasma volume starts to increase by 6 weeks and plateaus at 30 weeks, reaching a maximum increase of 40-50% (an addition of about 1250 mL).

**Red Blood Cells and Hemoglobin (Physiological Anemia)**

- The RBC mass increases to the extent of 20-30% (about 350 mL) to meet the increased demand of oxygen transport.
- The total hemoglobin mass increases by 18-20% (about 85 g).
- Despite this total increase, the disproportionate expansion in plasma volume compared to the RBC volume produces a state of hemodilution.
- As a result, there is an apparent fall in hemoglobin concentration by about 2 g% at term, creating a state of "physiological anemia".
- The hematocrit drops from a nonpregnant level of 38% down to 32%.

**Leukocytes and Immune System**

- Neutrophilic leukocytosis occurs, with leukocyte counts rising to the extent of 8,000/mm³ and even up to 20,000/mm³ during labor.
- The major change in the immune system is the modulation away from a cell-mediated cytotoxic immune response toward an increased humoral and innate immune response.

**Plasma Proteins**

- Total plasma protein increases from the normal 180 g (nonpregnant) to 230 g at term.
- However, due to marked hemodilution, the plasma protein concentration falls from 7 g% to 6 g%.
- Albumin levels fall by about 30% (from 4.3 to 3 g%), while globulin levels rise slightly.
- Consequently, the normal albumin:globulin ratio of 1.7:1 is diminished to 1:1.

**Blood Coagulation Factors and ESR**

- Pregnancy is a hypercoagulable state.
- Fibrinogen levels rise by 50%, from 200-400 mg/dL in the nonpregnant state to 300-600 mg/dL in pregnancy.
- Plasma levels and activities of clotting factors VII, VIII, IX, X, and I are increased.
- Platelet counts may remain static or show a slight fall (up to 15%).
- Fibrinolytic activity is depressed until 15 minutes after delivery, while the clotting time remains unaffected.
- Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) shows a marked, four-fold increase (from 10 mm/h to 40 mm/h) due to the rise in globulin and fibrinogen.

### **2. Renal (Urinary) System Changes in Pregnancy**

**Kidneys and Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR)**

- The kidneys enlarge in length by 1 cm, and there is dilatation of the renal pelvis and calyces.
- Renal plasma flow is increased by 50-75%, peaking at 16 weeks and maintained until 34 weeks, after which it falls by 25%.
- Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) is increased by 50% throughout pregnancy.
- This increased GFR causes a reduction in maternal plasma levels of creatinine, Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN), and uric acid.
- The renal tubules fail to completely reabsorb glucose, uric acid, amino acids, and water-soluble vitamins, leading to glycosuria in 50% of normal pregnant women.

**Ureters**

- The ureters become atonic due to high progesterone levels.
- Dilatation of the ureter above the pelvic brim with urinary stasis is marked on the right side.
- This dextral predominance is due to dextrorotation of the gravid uterus pressing the right ureter against the pelvic brim, and pressure from the right ovarian vein crossing the ureter.
- There is also elongation, kinking, and outward displacement of the ureters.

**Bladder**

- The bladder experiences marked congestion with hypertrophy of its muscles and elastic tissues.
- Increased frequency of micturition is noticed at 6-8 weeks of pregnancy, which subsides after 12 weeks due to resetting of osmoregulation.
- In late pregnancy, the frequency of micturition reappears due to pressure on the bladder as the presenting part descends.
- Bladder mucosa becomes edematous in late pregnancy due to venous and lymphatic obstruction.
- Stress incontinence may be observed in late pregnancy due to urethral sphincter weakness.

### **3. Gastrointestinal System Changes in Pregnancy**

**Oral Cavity**

- The gums become congested and spongy, and may bleed to touch.
- Rarely, a highly vascular pedunculated lesion (epulis gravidarum) may develop on the gums.
- Ptyalism (increased salivation) is an uncommon complication observed during pregnancy.

**Stomach and Intestines**

- Muscle tone and motility of the entire gastrointestinal tract are diminished due to high progesterone levels.
- The cardiac sphincter is relaxed, leading to regurgitation of acidic gastric contents into the esophagus, which produces chemical esophagitis and heartburn.
- There is diminished gastric secretion and delayed emptying time of the stomach.
- Atonicity of the gut leads to constipation, while diminished peristalsis facilitates greater absorption of food materials.

**Liver and Gallbladder**

- Although there is no histological change in the liver cells, hepatic functions are depressed.
- There is mild cholestasis due to estrogen effects.
- Marked atonicity of the gallbladder occurs due to progesterone.
- This gallbladder atonicity, combined with a high blood cholesterol level during pregnancy, favors gallstone formation.
- Liver function tests such as serum bilirubin, AST, ALT, CPK, and LDH remain unchanged, with the exception of raised alkaline phosphatase (which is largely a placental isoenzyme).
