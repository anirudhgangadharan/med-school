### **1. Introduction and Definition**

A genital fistula is an abnormal communication between the genital tract and the urinary tract (genitourinary fistula) or the bowel (e.g., rectovaginal fistula). The incidence of genitourinary fistula is approximately 0.2–1% among gynecological admissions in developing countries, though it is declining with better obstetric care.

### **2. Etiology (Causes)**

**A. Vesicovaginal Fistula (VVF)** This is the most common type of genitourinary fistula.

- **Obstetrical Causes:** Historically the most common cause in developing countries (80-90%).
    - _Ischemic:_ Prolonged obstructed labor leads to compression of the bladder base between the fetal head and the maternal symphysis pubis $\rightarrow$ ischemic necrosis $\rightarrow$ infection $\rightarrow$ sloughing $\rightarrow$ fistula formation (usually takes 3–5 days to manifest).
    - _Traumatic:_ Direct injury during instrumental vaginal delivery (destructive operations, Kielland forceps) or abdominal operations (cesarean section, cesarean hysterectomy).
- **Gynecological Causes:** Currently accounting for >80% of fistulae in India due to declining obstetric causes.
    - _Operative Injury:_ Anterior colporrhaphy, abdominal or vaginal hysterectomy, and electrothermal injury during laparoscopic surgery.
    - _Malignancy:_ Advanced carcinoma of the cervix, vagina, or bladder.
    - _Radiation:_ Ischemic necrosis due to endarteritis obliterans following radiotherapy for cervical cancer (takes 1–2 years to develop).
    - _Traumatic:_ Fall on a pointed object, pelvic bone fractures, or sexual assault.
    - _Infective:_ Chronic granulomatous lesions like tuberculosis, lymphogranuloma venereum, or schistosomiasis.

**B. Rectovaginal Fistula (RVF)** Abnormal communication between the rectum and vagina.

- **Obstetrical Causes:** Incomplete healing or unrepaired recent Complete Perineal Tear (CPT) is the most common cause. It can also result from obstructed labor due to pressure necrosis of the rectum against a flat sacrum.
- **Gynecological Causes:** Unrecognized trauma during perineorrhaphy or posterior colpotomy, vaginal malignancy, pelvic radiation, diverticulitis, or Crohn’s disease.

### **3. Clinical Features**

**A. Vesicovaginal Fistula**

- **Symptoms:** Continuous escape of urine per vaginam (true incontinence) with no urge to pass urine. If the fistula is small, leakage may only occur in certain positions. Obstetric fistulae symptoms appear 7–14 days post-delivery, whereas direct surgical injury leaks from the first postoperative day. Vesicouterine fistulae may present with cyclical hematuria (menouria or Youssef's syndrome) while maintaining continence.
- **Signs:** Escape of ammonia-smelling watery discharge per vaginam, with sodden and excoriated vulvar skin.

**B. Rectovaginal Fistula**

- **Symptoms:** Involuntary escape of flatus and/or feces into the vagina. Small fistulae may only leak flatus or loose stool.

### **4. Diagnosis and Investigations**

**For Vesicovaginal Fistula:**

- **Speculum Examination:** A Sims' speculum used in the Sims' position provides a clear view of the anterior vaginal wall.
- **Dye Test:** Methylene blue is instilled into the bladder via a catheter. Dye leaking into the vagina confirms the fistula.
- **Three-Swab (Tampon) Test:** Differentiates VVF from ureterovaginal and urethrovaginal fistulae. Three swabs are placed in the vagina (vault, mid, low). Methylene blue is instilled into the bladder. If the middle swab stains blue, it is VVF; if the uppermost swab is wet with urine but unstained with dye, it is a ureterovaginal fistula.
- **Imaging & Endoscopy:** Intravenous urography (IVU), Ultrasound, CT, MRI (for complex fistulae), and Cystourethroscopy (to locate the fistula in relation to ureteric orifices).

**For Rectovaginal Fistula:**

- Confirmed by rectovaginal examination, passing a probe, or introducing methylene blue dye into the rectum. Barium enema, proctoscopy, or colonoscopy may be needed to rule out IBD or malignancy.

### **5. Prevention**

- **Obstetric VVF & RVF:** Adequate antenatal care to screen for "at-risk" mothers, use of the partograph to detect and relieve obstructed labor early. Continuous bladder drainage for 5–7 days following obstructed labor. Identification and proper, meticulous repair of Complete Perineal Tears.
- **Gynecological Fistulae:** Thorough anatomical knowledge, meticulous dissection, avoiding blind clamping of vessels, and preoperative IVU in complex pelvic masses.

### **6. Principles of Management**

**A. Conservative Management**

- **VVF:** For small fistulae diagnosed early, continuous catheterization for 4–8 weeks can lead to spontaneous closure in 50–60% of cases by providing an unobstructed outflow tract for epithelialization.
- **RVF:** Small RVF following obstetric injury may be followed up conservatively for spontaneous healing.

**B. Surgical Management (VVF)**

- **Timing:** The ideal time for repair is 6 weeks to 3 months following delivery or surgery, allowing local infection to subside and general condition to improve. Post-radiation fistulae require a 12-month wait.
- **Route:** The vaginal route is highly preferred due to less blood loss and morbidity.
- **Principles of Repair (Flap Splitting Method):**
    - Perfect asepsis and exposure.
    - Minimal excision of the fibrous scar tissue around the margin.
    - Mobilization of the bladder wall from the vagina for tension-free repair.
    - Suturing the bladder wall in two layers using delayed absorbable sutures (Polyglactin/Vicryl 2-0). The first layer excludes the bladder mucosa. The second layer buries the first.
    - Apposition of the vaginal wall.
    - Watertight closure tested with dye instillation at the end of the procedure.
- **Tissue Grafts:** Large fistulae may require interpositional pedicle grafts (e.g., Martius bulbocavernosus fat pad graft) for new blood supply.

**C. Surgical Management (RVF)**

- Transvaginal fistulotomy or flap method is commonly performed. The scar margin is excised, the vaginal wall is widely separated from the rectal wall for tension-free mobilization, and repaired in layers using Polydioxanone (PDS) 3-0.
- If situated low down, it may be converted into a complete perineal tear and repaired accordingly.
- If situated high up, a preliminary colostomy is done, followed by local repair after 3 weeks, and colostomy closure after another 3 weeks.

**D. Postoperative Care (VVF)**

- **Continuous bladder drainage** by an indwelling catheter is mandatory for 10–14 days.
- Urinary antiseptics based on sensitivity.
- Advice on discharge: Frequent voiding, avoid intercourse for at least 3 months, defer pregnancy for 1 year, and mandatory elective cesarean section for future pregnancies.
