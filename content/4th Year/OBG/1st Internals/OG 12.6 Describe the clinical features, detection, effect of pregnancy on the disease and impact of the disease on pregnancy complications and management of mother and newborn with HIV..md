### **1. Introduction**

Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS) is caused by the Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), an RNA retrovirus that profoundly depletes CD4+ T-lymphocytes, leading to severe cell-mediated immunodeficiency. The vertical transmission of HIV from mother to child (MTCT) is a critical obstetric concern, but with appropriate screening and Antiretroviral Therapy (ART), transmission rates can be reduced to <1%.

### **2. Clinical Features**

Following an incubation period of 1–3 weeks, the clinical presentation progresses through several stages:

- **Primary/Acute Infection (Seroconversion illness):** Presents 1 week to 3 months after exposure with flu-like symptoms including fever, malaise, headache, sore throat, pharyngitis, arthralgia, lymphadenopathy, and a maculopapular rash.
- **Asymptomatic Phase (Clinical Latency):** Follows the primary illness and lasts for a median of 7–10 years.
- **AIDS-Related Complex (ARC) / Advanced Disease:** Presents with weight loss, protracted diarrhea, recurrent oral/genital candidiasis, herpes simplex, pelvic inflammatory disease, and thrombocytopenia.
- **AIDS:** Defined by a CD4+ T-cell count of <200 cells/mm³ or <14% of all lymphocytes, or the presence of AIDS-defining illnesses. These include opportunistic infections (Pneumocystis carinii pneumonia, atypical tuberculosis, cryptosporidiosis, toxoplasmosis) and neoplasms (invasive cervical carcinoma, Kaposi's sarcoma, non-Hodgkin's lymphoma).

### **3. Detection and Investigations**

- **Screening Tests:** Enzyme Immunoassay (EIA) is used to detect antibodies to HIV and P24 antigen ("Combo assay"). It is highly sensitive and inexpensive.
- **Confirmatory Tests:** Initial positive tests must be confirmed using highly specific tests such as the Western blot, Immunofluorescence Assay (IFA), or HIV-1/HIV-2 antibody differentiation immunoassay paired with an HIV-1 Nucleic Acid Test (NAAT/PCR). HIV RNA PCR is the gold standard for diagnosis.
- **Laboratory Monitoring during Pregnancy:**
    - **CD4+ Cell Count:** Baseline at initiation of care and at delivery.
    - **Viral Load (HIV RNA-PCR):** Performed 2-4 weeks after commencing combined Antiretroviral Therapy (cART), once every trimester, at 36 weeks, and at delivery.
    - **Other Labs:** Complete Blood Count (CBC), platelet count, LFTs, serum urea, creatinine, electrolytes, amylase, and G6PD. HIV resistance testing (genotype) should be done before starting treatment.

### **4. Effect of Pregnancy on HIV**

Pregnancy _per se_ has no deleterious effect on the disease progression in HIV-positive women. Furthermore, maternal mortality and morbidity are not inherently increased simply due to pregnancy.

### **5. Impact of HIV on Pregnancy Complications**

- **Obstetric Complications:** While some studies report increased incidences of abortion, prematurity, pre-eclampsia, IUGR, and perinatal mortality in HIV-seropositive mothers, the direct correlation remains inconclusive.
- **Perinatal/Vertical Transmission (MTCT):** Without intervention, transmission rates to the neonate range from 14-25% for HIV-1.
    - **Timing of Transmission:** Transplacental transmission (20% before 36 weeks), during labor and delivery (>80% due to exposure to contaminated birth canal secretions and blood), and postnatally through breastfeeding.
    - **Risk Factors for Transmission:** High maternal viral load, low CD4 count, concurrent STIs/opportunistic infections, prolonged rupture of membranes, chorioamnionitis, vaginal delivery (if viral load is high), invasive intrapartum procedures, and breastfeeding (which doubles the risk of transmission to 14-28%).

### **6. Management of the Mother**

**A. Preconceptional and Antenatal Care**

- **Screening and Counseling:** Voluntary Counseling and Testing Center (VCTC) utilizes an 'Opt Out' approach for all pregnant women. Provide counseling on disease impact, MTCT, mode of delivery, and vaccinations (pneumococcal, hepatitis, Tdap, influenza).
- **Co-infections:** Screen for STIs (Hepatitis B & C, syphilis, Chlamydia, herpes) and opportunistic infections (tuberculosis, cytomegalovirus, toxoplasmosis, candidiasis).
- **Antiretroviral Therapy (HAART/cART):**
    - Goal: Suppress viral multiplication to undetectable levels (<50 copies/mL) to protect maternal health and reduce perinatal transmission.
    - Timing: cART should ideally be started by the 24th week of gestation in all women. For those with a baseline viral load ≤30,000 copies/mL, it can be started in the second trimester; if >100,000 copies/mL or CD4 <200 cells/mm³, it must be started in the first trimester.
    - Regimen: Typically consists of an NRTI backbone (e.g., Abacavir/Lamivudine or Tenofovir/Emtricitabine) plus a third agent (Efavirenz, Atazanavir, Rilpivirine, Darunavir, Raltegravir, or Dolutegravir). Protease Inhibitor (PI) monotherapy is not recommended.
- **Prenatal Diagnosis:** Invasive tests (CVS, amniocentesis) should generally be avoided but may be performed if the viral load is optimally suppressed (<50 copies/mL). Non-invasive screening (NT, serum markers, NIPT) is preferred.

**B. Intrapartum Management**

- **Mode of Delivery:**
    - If viral load is **<50 copies/mL** at 36 weeks: Planned vaginal delivery is safe.
    - If viral load is **>50 copies/mL** at 36 weeks: Planned elective cesarean delivery is recommended at 39 weeks, which reduces the risk of vertical transmission by 50%.
- **Intrapartum Precautions:**
    - Avoid amniotomy and oxytocin augmentation whenever possible.
    - Strictly avoid invasive procedures that break fetal skin (scalp electrodes, scalp blood pH sampling, ventouse/instrumentation).
    - Delayed cord clamping is safe and reduces neonatal anemia.
    - Strict aseptic precautions, PPE kits, protective eyewear, and blunt-tipped needles must be utilized by healthcare workers.
- **Intrapartum Drugs:**
    - Intravenous Zidovudine (ZDV) is administered near delivery if the viral load is >1,000 copies/mL or unknown.
    - Untreated women presenting in labor should urgently receive Nevirapine, Zidovudine, Lamivudine, and Raltegravir alongside IV Zidovudine.

**C. Postpartum Care and Contraception**

- Maternal cART should not be stopped after delivery; care must be continued according to adult guidelines.
- **Contraception:** Barrier methods (condoms) are essential to prevent viral transmission. IUCDs (Copper and LNG-IUS), implants, and injectables are safe and effective. Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs) should be avoided due to drug interactions with antiretroviral agents which affect their efficacy.

### **7. Management of the Newborn**

A neonatologist should be involved in care. The baby may be bathed immediately to remove maternal secretions.

- **Neonatal ART Prophylaxis:** Antiretroviral therapy must be given to all neonates within 4 hours of birth.
    - _Very Low Risk_ (Mother on cART >10 weeks, 2 documented VL <50, and VL <50 at 36 weeks): 2 weeks of Zidovudine monotherapy.
    - _Low Risk_ (Maternal VL <50 at 36 weeks, but other criteria unmet): 4 weeks of Zidovudine monotherapy.
    - _High Risk_ (Maternal birth VL >50): Combination Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP) within 4 hours.
- **Infant Feeding:** In high-income settings, formula milk is recommended to eliminate breast milk transmission. In developing regions, WHO recommends exclusive breastfeeding accompanied by maternal cART (to minimize transmission) to ensure infant nutrition. Monthly viral load reviews of mother and infant are required during breastfeeding and for 2 months after cessation.
- **Follow-Up:** A confirmatory HIV antibody test is done at 18 months. If negative (and the infant is not breastfed), the child is declared free of HIV.
