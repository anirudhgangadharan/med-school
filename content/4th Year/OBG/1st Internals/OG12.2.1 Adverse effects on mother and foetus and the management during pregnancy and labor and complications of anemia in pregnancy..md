### **1. Adverse Effects and Complications on the Mother**

Anemia in pregnancy significantly increases maternal morbidity and mortality. It contributes directly or indirectly to about 20% of maternal deaths in low and middle-income countries. The complications are distributed across the different stages of pregnancy, labor, and puerperium:

**During Pregnancy:**

- **Pre-eclampsia:** Increased incidence, which may be related to underlying hypoproteinemia.
- **Intercurrent Infections:** Pre-existing infections may flare up, and the infection itself further impairs erythropoiesis by depressing the bone marrow.
- **Cardiovascular:** Angina pectoris and Heart Failure (highest risk around 30–32 weeks of gestation).
- **Preterm Labor:** Increased incidence of spontaneous preterm labor and prematurity.
- **Specific Deficiencies:** Atrophic glossitis and Sideropenic dysphagia (Plummer-Vinson syndrome) seen in iron deficiency. Megaloblastic anemia specifically increases the risk of abruptio placentae and miscarriage.

**During Labor:**

- **Uterine Inertia:** Surprisingly, this is _not_ a common association; labor is often short due to a small baby and multiparity.
- **Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH):** A real and life-threatening threat, as the anemic mother cannot tolerate even a minimal or normal amount of blood loss.
- **Congestive Cardiac Failure (CCF):** Typically occurs during labor or immediately following delivery. The blood from the contracting uterine circulation is squeezed into the general circulation, putting additional strain on a myocardium already compromised by hypoxia.
- **Shock:** Even a small amount of hemorrhage may precipitate profound shock or lethal hypoxia.

**During Puerperium:**

- **Infections & Healing:** Puerperal sepsis, subinvolution, and poor or delayed wound healing.
- **Vascular:** Puerperal venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism.
- **Others:** Poor lactation and postpartum depression.

_High-Yield Note: The high-risk periods for sudden maternal death are: (1) 30–32 weeks of pregnancy, (2) During labor, (3) Immediately following delivery, and (4) Anytime in the puerperium, especially 7–10 days postpartum due to cardiac failure or pulmonary embolism._

### **2. Adverse Effects and Complications on the Fetus and Neonate**

The amount of iron transferred to the fetus is generally unaffected unless the mother suffers from severe iron deficiency anemia. However, significant complications include:

- **Growth and Maturity:** Increased incidence of Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR), preterm delivery, and dysmaturity.
- **Fetal Hypoxia:** Severe anemia leads to low fetal oxygenation, reduced amniotic fluid volume, and Intrauterine Death (IUD).
- **Neonatal Anemia:** A baby born at term may not be anemic at birth but has little to no reserve iron, developing anemia in the neonatal period (infancy).
- **Fetal Malformations:** Megaloblastic (folic acid deficiency) anemia is specifically associated with fetal anomalies such as neural tube defects (NTDs), cleft lip, and cleft palate.
- **Perinatal Loss:** The sum effect of these factors leads to a significantly increased perinatal morbidity and mortality.

### **3. Management During Pregnancy**

**A. Prophylactic Management:**

- **Spacing of Birth:** Advise a minimum interval of 2–3 years between pregnancies to replenish lost iron stores.
- **Dietary Prescription:** A balanced diet rich in iron and protein (liver, meat, egg, green leafy vegetables, jaggery). Avoid consuming tea within 1 hour of taking iron tablets.
- **Routine Iron & Folic Acid:** Because only 20% of pregnant women have adequate iron stores (500 mg), routine daily supplementation of 200 mg ferrous sulfate (containing 60 mg elemental iron) along with 1 mg folic acid is highly effective.
- **Eradication of Root Causes:** Treat conditions like hookworm infestation, dysentery, malaria, bleeding piles, and urinary tract infections (UTI).
- **Screening:** Hemoglobin estimation at the 1st antenatal visit, 30th week, and 36th week.

**B. Curative Management:** The choice of therapy depends on the severity of anemia, duration of pregnancy (time available before delivery), and associated complicating factors.

- **Oral Iron Therapy:**
    
    - Drug of choice for most cases. Ferrous preparations (fumarate, gluconate, succinate, or sulfate) are best absorbed.
    - Dose: One tablet (e.g., 200 mg ferrous sulfate) thrice daily after meals, providing ~180 mg of elemental iron daily.
    - Must be continued for at least 100 days postpartum to replenish stores.
    - _Response to therapy:_ Evidenced by a sense of well-being, increased appetite, a rise in Hb (by 2.0 g/dL every 3-4 weeks), and reticulocytosis within 7-10 days.
- **Parenteral Iron Therapy (IM/IV):**
    
    - _Indications:_ Intolerance to oral iron (severe GI side effects), non-compliance, malabsorption syndrome, or severe anemia seen in advanced pregnancy (>32 weeks) where rapid replenishment is required before labor.
    - _Preparations:_ Iron sucrose (IV, requires multiple doses but safe) or Iron dextran (can be given as Total Dose Infusion - TDI).
- **Blood Transfusion:**
    
    - _Indications:_ Severe anemia detected beyond 36 weeks (to urgently improve oxygen-carrying capacity before labor), correction of acute blood loss (APH/PPH), refractory anemia, or associated severe infections.
    - _Product:_ Packed Red Blood Cells (PRBC) are used.
    - _Drawbacks:_ Risk of precipitating premature labor, cardiac failure, pulmonary edema due to circulatory overload, and transfusion reactions.

### **4. Management During Labor**

Labor in an anemic patient must be meticulously managed to prevent cardiac failure, exhaustion, and postpartum hemorrhage.

- **First Stage:**
    - The patient should be nursed in bed in a comfortable position, often propped up.
    - Continuous oxygen inhalation should be provided to ensure adequate maternal and fetal oxygenation.
    - Strict asepsis must be maintained.
- **Delivery (Second & Third Stage):**
    - To prevent PPH, active management of the third stage is critical. Injection Oxytocin 10 IU IM is given to the mother immediately following the delivery of the baby. _Note: Ergometrine is strictly avoided to prevent sudden cardiac overload._
    - Any significant amount of blood loss is promptly compensated by fresh PRBC transfusion.
    - Prophylactic diuretics (e.g., Injection Furosemide 20 mg IV) should be administered alongside transfusions to prevent volume overload and impending cardiac failure.

### **5. Management During Puerperium**

- **Prophylactic Antibiotics:** Administered to prevent puerperal sepsis.
- **Continued Hematinic Therapy:** Pre-delivery iron and folic acid therapy must be continued (for at least 100 days) until the woman completely restores her normal clinical and hematological state.
- **Postnatal Counseling:** The woman should be seen in the postpartum clinic and counseled comprehensively regarding effective methods of contraception to ensure adequate birth spacing.
