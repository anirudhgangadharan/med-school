### **1. PHYSIOLOGY OF GAMETOGENESIS**

Gametogenesis is the process involved in the maturation of the two highly specialized cells, the spermatozoon in the male and the ovum in the female, before they unite to form a zygote.

**Oogenesis** Oogenesis is the process involved in the development of a mature ovum.

- **Origin and Migration:** Primitive germ cells originate from the endoderm of the yolk sac around the end of the 3rd week (or 5-6 weeks of gestation) and migrate to the developing gonadal ridge by amoeboid activity or chemotaxis by the 4th to 6th week. A peptide called telopheron directs this migration.
- **Multiplication:** The germ cells undergo rapid mitotic divisions to differentiate into oogonia. The number of oogonia reaches its maximum at 20 weeks, numbering about 6-7 million.
- **Maturation:** While the majority of oogonia divide, some enter the prophase of the first meiotic division and become primary oocytes. These are surrounded by flat stromal cells, forming primordial follicles. At birth, all oogonia are replaced by primary oocytes, which remain arrested in the resting phase (diplotene stage) between prophase and metaphase. At birth, there are about 2 million primary oocytes, and by puberty, some 400,000 remain. Only about 400 will ovulate during the entire reproductive period.
- **Meiotic Divisions:** The arrested meiotic division is resumed due to the midcycle LH surge. The primary oocyte completes the first meiotic division just prior to ovulation, yielding a secondary oocyte (containing a haploid number of chromosomes, 23,X, and almost all the cytoplasm) and a first polar body. The secondary oocyte begins the second meiotic division but stops at metaphase. This division is completed only after fertilization by a sperm, resulting in a mature ovum (female pronucleus) and a second polar body.

**Spermatogenesis** Spermatogenesis is the process involved in the development of spermatids from primordial male germ cells and their differentiation into spermatozoa. It takes approximately 74 days to complete.

- **Maturation:** Shortly before puberty, primordial germ cells develop into spermatogonia, which differentiate into primary spermatocytes. Primary spermatocytes undergo the first meiotic division to form two secondary spermatocytes (23,X or 23,Y). The second meiotic division results in the formation of four spermatids.
- **Spermiogenesis:** The extensive morphological differentiation of spermatids into mature spermatozoa without further cell division is called spermiogenesis.
- **Capacitation and Acrosome Reaction:** Capacitation is the physiochemical change taking place in the female genital tract (over 2-6 hours) making the sperm hypermotile and capable of fertilizing the oocyte. It involves an influx of Ca++ and cyclic AMP-dependent phosphorylation. The acrosome reaction involves the fusion of the sperm plasma membrane with the outer acrosomal membrane, releasing enzymes like hyaluronidase and acrosin that help the sperm digest the zona pellucida.

### **2. PHYSIOLOGY OF OVULATION**

Ovulation is the process whereby a secondary oocyte is released from the ovary following the rupture of a mature Graafian follicle, making it available for conception. It occurs approximately 14 days prior to the expected next menstrual period.

**Mechanism of Ovulation**

- **Changes in the Follicle:** The Graafian follicle undergoes preovulatory enlargement up to about 20 mm in diameter due to the accumulation of follicular fluid. The cumulus oophorus separates from the granulosa cells and floats freely in the antrum. The follicular wall near the ovarian surface becomes thinner, and a conical projection called the stigma develops. The cumulus escapes out of the follicle by a slow oozing process taking 1-2 minutes, along with follicular fluid.
- **Changes in the Oocyte:** Prior to ovulation, cytoplasmic volume increases, and the arrested first meiotic division is completed with the extrusion of the first polar body.

**Causes of Ovulation**

- **LH Surge (Endocrinal):** A sustained peak level of estrogen (>200 pg/mL) for 24-48 hours in the late follicular phase exerts a positive feedback effect, causing an LH surge from the anterior pituitary. Ovulation occurs approximately 12-16 hours after the LH peak or 32-36 hours after the onset of the LH surge. The LH surge stimulates the completion of the reduction division, initiates luteinization of granulosa cells, and promotes the synthesis of progesterone and prostaglandins.
- **FSH Rise (Endocrinal):** A preovulatory rise in 17-alpha-hydroxyprogesterone facilitates estrogen's positive feedback to induce an FSH surge. This increases the plasminogen activator, converting plasminogen to plasmin, which lyses the follicular wall.
- **Stretching Factor:** Passive stretching leads to necrobiosis of the overlying tissue; the intrafollicular pressure remains static at about 10-15 mm Hg.
- **Contraction of Micromuscles:** Contractions in the theca externa and ovarian stroma occur due to increased prostaglandin secretion.

### **3. PHYSIOLOGY OF FERTILIZATION**

Fertilization is the process of fusion of the spermatozoon with the mature ovum, beginning with sperm-egg collision and ending with the production of a mononucleated single cell called the zygote. It almost always occurs in the ampullary part of the Fallopian tube.

**Approximation of Gametes**

- Following ovulation, the ovum is picked up by the tubal fimbriae and rapidly transported to the ampullary part by muscular contractions and ciliary action.
- The fertilizable lifespan of the oocyte ranges from 12 to 24 hours, while that of the sperm is 48-72 hours.
- Out of millions of sperms deposited, only 300-500 reach the ovum after capacitation.

**Contact and Fusion of Gametes**

- **Penetration:** Complete dissolution of the corona radiata is achieved by the chemical action of hyaluronidase released from the acrosomal cap. The sperm penetrates the zona pellucida and reaches the perivitelline space.
- **Zona Reaction and Oolemma Block:** Once a sperm touches the oolemma and fuses with the oocyte plasma membrane, penetration by other sperms (polyspermy) is prevented by a zona reaction (hardening) and oolemma block, facilitated by the release of cortical granules.
- **Formation of Pronuclei:** The secondary oocyte completes its second meiotic division, extruding the second polar body. The remaining nucleus becomes the female pronucleus. The head and neck of the spermatozoon enter the cytoplasm and become the male pronucleus.
- **Zygote Formation:** The male and female pronuclei unite at the center, restoring the diploid number of chromosomes (46).
- **Sex Determination:** The sex is determined by the sex chromosome supplied by the spermatozoon (X chromosome yields a 46,XX female; Y chromosome yields a 46,XY male).

### **4. PHYSIOLOGY OF IMPLANTATION (NIDATION)**

Implantation is the process by which the blastocyst embeds into the endometrium of the uterus, normally occurring on the anterior or posterior wall of the body near the fundus on the 6th day after fertilization (corresponding to the 20th day of a regular menstrual cycle).

**Changes in the Blastocyst and Endometrium**

- **Zona Hatching:** The blastocyst enlarges, and the zona pellucida becomes stretched, thinned, and gradually disappears (lysis of zona), allowing the trophectoderm cells to interact with the endometrial cells.
- **Implantation Window:** Endometrial receptivity is induced by progesterone, leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF), prostaglandins, and COX-2. It extends from day 20 to day 24 of a normal menstrual cycle.

**Stages of Implantation**

- **Apposition:** Mediated by the formation of pinopods (microvilli projections from the endometrial cell surface) that absorb endometrial fluid. The polar trophoblast cells attach to the endometrium through adhesion molecules like integrins, selectins, and cadherins.
- **Adhesion:** The blastocyst firmly attaches to the endometrial epithelium.
- **Penetration/Invasion:** Actual penetration occurs through the stromal cells, facilitated by the histolytic action of the blastocyst. The syncytiotrophoblast burrows deep inside the stratum compactum of the decidua. Vacuoles appear in the syncytium and fuse to form large lacunae. The syncytium erodes maternal capillaries, allowing maternal blood to enter the lacunar system for aerobic nutrition. This deeper penetration, completely covering the blastocyst on all sides, is called interstitial implantation and is completed by the 10th or 11th day.

**Decidual Reaction**

- The increased structural and secretory activity of the endometrium in response to progesterone following implantation is known as the decidual reaction.
- The endometrium of the pregnant uterus is termed the decidua, which is divided into the decidua basalis (in contact with the base of the blastocyst), decidua capsularis (covering the blastocyst), and decidua vera/parietalis (lining the rest of the uterine cavity).
