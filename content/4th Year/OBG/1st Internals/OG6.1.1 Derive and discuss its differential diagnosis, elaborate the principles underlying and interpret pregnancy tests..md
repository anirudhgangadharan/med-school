### **1. DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS OF PREGNANCY**

The diagnosis of pregnancy is sometimes confused with conditions presenting with amenorrhea and/or an abdominopelvic mass. The differential diagnosis includes:

- **Pseudocyesis (Phantom, Spurious, or False Pregnancy):**
    - It is a psychological disorder where an often infertile woman with an intense desire to have a baby develops a false but firm belief that she is pregnant.
    - The most conspicuous feature is the cessation of menstruation.
    - Obstetric examination, ultrasonography (USG), and immunological tests exclude pregnancy.
- **Cystic Ovarian Tumor:**
    - _Growth:_ Slow-growing, usually taking months to grow.
    - _Symptoms:_ Amenorrhea is usually absent.
    - _Signs:_ The mass feels cystic or tense cystic; there is an absence of Braxton-Hicks contractions and positive signs of pregnancy.
    - _Confirmation:_ Ultrasonography easily confirms the diagnosis.
- **Uterine Fibroid:**
    - _Growth:_ Slow-growing, often taking years.
    - _Symptoms:_ Amenorrhea is absent.
    - _Signs:_ The tumor feels firm or hard (though it may feel cystic if cystic degeneration occurs). Positive signs of pregnancy are absent.
    - _Confirmation:_ USG or immunological pregnancy tests help confirm the diagnosis.
    - _Note:_ Pregnancy with a fibroid can also create confusion; the pregnant uterus feels soft and cystic, while the fibroid is firmer.
- **Full Urinary Bladder:**
    - In cases of chronic retention of urine (e.g., due to a retroverted gravid uterus), the distended bladder may be mistaken for an ovarian cyst, acute hydramnios, or a pregnant uterus.
    - It is strictly suprapubic, tense/cystic, and disappears after catheterization.
- **Encysted Peritonitis:**
    - May present with a history of Koch’s (tubercular) infection and amenorrhea of longer duration.
    - The swelling has ill-defined margins, is usually situated high up, and pelvic examination gives negative findings regarding the uterus.
- **Hematometra:** Accumulation of blood in the uterus, often presenting with primary or secondary amenorrhea and a midline pelvic mass.
- **Ascites:** Differentiated by fullness of the flanks, shifting dullness, and a fluid thrill.

### **2. PRINCIPLES UNDERLYING PREGNANCY TESTS**

- **Antigen-Antibody Reaction:** Pregnancy tests depend on the detection of the antigen, human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), present in maternal urine or serum, using commercially available polyclonal or monoclonal antibodies.
- **Biochemical Basis of hCG:** hCG is a glycoprotein molecule. It consists of two subunits:
    - **Alpha ($\alpha$) subunit:** Identical to the $\alpha$ subunits of pituitary hormones (LH, FSH, and TSH).
    - **Beta ($\beta$) subunit:** Structurally and functionally distinct and highly specific to hCG.
- **Targeting the $\beta$-Subunit:** To avoid cross-reactivity with LH, FSH, or TSH, modern immunological tests utilize antibodies developed specifically against this highly specific $\beta$-subunit.
- **Test Methods:** Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) is the most sensitive and specific method utilized in both laboratory serum testing and point-of-care urine card tests (two-site sandwich immunoassay).

### **3. INTERPRETATION OF PREGNANCY TESTS**

- **Timing of Positivity:** With a highly sensitive test, hCG can be detected in maternal serum or urine 8-9 days after ovulation, which is about 3-4 days before the expected date of menses.
- **Visual Interpretation (Card Tests):** The presence of color bands in both the "control" window and the "test" window indicates a positive result (pregnant).
- **Sensitivity Levels:**
    - _Serum ELISA:_ Can detect hCG concentrations as low as 1-2 mIU/mL, making it positive up to 5 days before the first missed period.
    - _Urine Card Tests (Membrane ELISA):_ Typically sensitive to 30-50 mIU/mL and turn positive on the first day of the missed period.
- **Accuracy:** The accuracy of these tests ranges from 98.6% to 99%. The nonpregnant level of hCG is strictly below 1 mIU/mL.

### **4. CLINICAL UTILITY AND LIMITATIONS**

**Other Clinical Uses of $\beta$-hCG Testing:** Apart from the routine diagnosis of an early intrauterine pregnancy, $\beta$-hCG tests are employed for:

1. Diagnosing ectopic pregnancies.
2. Monitoring pregnancies following _in vitro_ fertilization (IVF) and embryo transfer.
3. Following up cases of gestational trophoblastic diseases (hydatidiform mole and choriocarcinoma).

**False-Positive Results:** False-positive hCG test results are rare but can occur under the following circumstances:

- Exogenous hCG injections (e.g., used for ovulation induction).
- Presence of physiological pituitary hCG.
- hCG-producing neoplasms (tumors of the ovary, lung, bladder, or gastrointestinal tract).
- Renal failure leading to delayed hCG clearance.
- Presence of heterophile antibodies in the maternal serum.

**False-Negative Results & Test Limitations:**

- Testing too early in the gestation period before hCG reaches the detection threshold.
- Routine urine tests are not reliable after 12 weeks of pregnancy due to the natural decline in hCG levels.
- Test accuracy can be negatively affected by the presence of hemoglobin or albumin in the urine, elevated LH, or certain immunological diseases.
