### **1. ADVERSE EFFECTS AND COMPLICATIONS OF ANEMIA**

**Maternal Complications** Anemia either directly or indirectly contributes to about 20% of maternal deaths in low and middle-income countries. The maternal risk periods for sudden death include 30–32 weeks, during labor, immediately following delivery, and 7–10 days postpartum due to cardiac failure or pulmonary embolism.

- **During Pregnancy:**
    - Pre-eclampsia (potentially related to hypoproteinemia).
    - Intercurrent infections flare up, and the infection itself further impairs erythropoiesis by causing bone marrow depression.
    - Heart failure at 30–32 weeks of pregnancy.
    - Preterm labor and prematurity.
    - Other features: Angina pectoris, sideropenic dysphagia (Plummer Vinson syndrome), and atrophic glossitis.
- **During Labor:**
    - Uterine inertia is _not_ a common associate; labor is often short due to a small baby and multiparity.
    - Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH) is a real threat even with minimal amounts of blood loss.
    - Congestive cardiac failure frequently occurs during labor or immediately following delivery due to the sudden autotransfusion of blood from the contracting uterus placing extra strain on a hypoxic myocardium.
    - Shock can be precipitated by even a small amount of hemorrhage due to the pre-existing hypoxic state.
- **During Puerperium:**
    - Increased risk of puerperal sepsis and subinvolution.
    - Poor wound healing and failing lactation.
    - Puerperal venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism.
    - Postpartum depression.

**Fetal and Neonatal Complications** Amount of iron transferred to the fetus is generally unaffected unless the mother suffers from severe iron deficiency anemia.

- Increased incidence of Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR) and preterm delivery.
- In severe anemia, there is low fetal oxygenation, reduced amniotic fluid volume, and a high risk of Intrauterine Fetal Death (IUFD).
- Increased perinatal loss and anemia in infancy due to reduced fetal iron stores.

### **2. MANAGEMENT DURING PREGNANCY (ANTENATAL CARE)**

**Prophylactic Management**

- **Spacing of Births:** A minimum interval of 2 to 3 years between pregnancies is advised to replenish iron stores depleted during childbirth and lactation.
- **Supplementary Iron Therapy:** Since 40% of women have virtually no iron stores, iron and folic acid supplementation is essential for all pregnant women, typically starting from the 16th week.
- **Dietary Advice:** A balanced diet rich in iron and proteins (e.g., liver, meat, eggs, green leafy vegetables) should be recommended. Cooking in iron utensils is advantageous.
- **Eradication of Concomitant Diseases:** Treat hookworm infestations, dysentery, malaria, bleeding piles, and urinary tract infections.
- **Early Detection:** Hemoglobin levels should be routinely checked at the first antenatal visit, at 30 weeks, and finally at 36 weeks to detect falling levels.

**Curative Management** Ideally, all patients with a hemoglobin level of 8 g/100 mL or less (or arbitrarily 7.5 g/dL) should be hospitalized for investigation and treatment. The choice of therapy depends on the severity of anemia, duration of pregnancy (time available before delivery), and associated complicating factors.

- **Oral Iron Therapy:**
    - Best absorbed in the ferrous form (ferrous sulfate, ferrous fumarate, or ferrous gluconate).
    - Side effects include nausea, vomiting, epigastric pain, diarrhea, constipation, and metallic taste.
    - Response is evidenced by a sense of wellbeing, increased appetite, reticulocytosis within 7–10 days, and a rise in hemoglobin levels within 3 weeks.
- **Parenteral Iron Therapy (IM or IV):**
    - _Indications:_ Intolerance to oral iron, non-compliance, severe anemia in advanced pregnancy (>32 weeks) where there is no time for oral iron to act, and faulty absorption (e.g., malabsorption syndromes).
    - _Preparations:_ Iron sucrose is safe and has fewer side effects, though it requires multiple doses compared to iron dextran.
    - _Drawbacks (IM):_ Painful injections, risk of abscess formation, skin staining, and allergic reactions (pyrexia, arthralgia).
- **Blood Transfusion:**
    - _Indications:_ Severe anemia seen in later months (beyond 36 weeks) to improve oxygen-carrying capacity before labor, anemia due to acute blood loss (Antepartum Hemorrhage), refractory anemia, associated infections, or during operative delivery.
    - _Drawbacks:_ Risk of premature labor, cardiac failure with pulmonary edema from circulatory overload, and transfusion reactions.

### **3. MANAGEMENT DURING LABOR (INTRAPARTUM CARE)**

- **First Stage:** The patient should be placed in bed in a comfortable position, often propped up. Provision for continuous oxygen inhalation should be available to combat hypoxia. Strict asepsis must be maintained to prevent infection.
- **Second & Third Stage:** The second stage may be cut short by prophylactic operative delivery (forceps/ventouse) if required to prevent maternal exhaustion.
- **Active Management of Third Stage (AMTSL):** Injection oxytocin 10 IU IM is administered soon following the delivery of the baby to prevent PPH.
- **Fluid & Blood Management:** Any significant blood loss must be compensated with fresh Packed Red Blood Cells (PRBC). Aggressive fluid management is avoided, and diuretics (furosemide 20 mg IV) may be used concurrently with transfusions to prevent volume overload of the heart.

### **4. MANAGEMENT DURING PUERPERIUM**

- **Infection Control:** Prophylactic broad-spectrum antibiotics are given to prevent puerperal sepsis.
- **Ongoing Therapy:** Pre-delivery hematinic therapy (iron and folic acid) must be rigorously continued until the woman restores normal clinical and hematological parameters.
- **Counseling:** Women should be seen in the postpartum clinic for counseling regarding birth spacing and appropriate methods of contraception.
