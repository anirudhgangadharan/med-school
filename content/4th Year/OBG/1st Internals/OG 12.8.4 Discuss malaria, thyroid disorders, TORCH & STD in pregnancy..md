### **1. Malaria in Pregnancy**

**Pathogenesis and Effects on Pregnancy** Malaria in pregnancy is predominantly a tropical disease, and the risk and severity of infection are high due to the immunocompromised state of pregnancy. The infected erythrocytes become rigid, irregular, and sticky, causing blockage of microcirculation.

- **Maternal Complications:** Megaloblastic anemia (due to hemolysis and folate deficiency), thrombocytopenia, hypoglycemia (due to increased glucose consumption by the host and parasites, and insulin secretion stimulated by quinine), metabolic acidosis, jaundice, renal failure, pulmonary edema (RDS), cerebral malaria (convulsions and coma), circulatory collapse, and abnormal bleeding (DIC).
- **Fetal Complications:** Fetal effects are primarily due to high maternal fever or placental parasitization (especially in _P. falciparum_ infections in the second half of pregnancy). Complications include miscarriage, preterm labor, prematurity, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), intrauterine fetal death (IUFD), fetal anemia, and congenital malaria (rare unless the placenta is damaged).

**Management**

- **Prevention & Prophylaxis:** Use of permethrin and pyrethroid sprays or mosquito nets. Chemoprophylaxis involves Chloroquine (300 mg base weekly) or Mefloquine (5 mg/kg/week in the second and third trimesters if chloroquine-resistant).
- **Treatment of Uncomplicated Malaria:** Chloroquine 600 mg base PO followed by 300 mg 12 hours later, then 300 mg daily for the next 2 days. Folic acid 10 mg should be given daily to prevent megaloblastic anemia.
- **Treatment of Complicated Malaria:** Requires Intensive Care Unit (ICU) management. Artesunate IV 2.4 mg/kg at 0, 12, and 24 hours, then daily is highly effective and acts fast. Alternatively, IV Quinine followed by oral therapy is used.

### **2. Thyroid Disorders in Pregnancy**

During pregnancy, there is a stimulatory thyrotropic effect from hCG (which shares an alpha subunit with TSH) that can cause a transient phase of hyperthyroidism in the first trimester, known as gestational transient thyrotoxicosis.

**Hyperthyroidism**

- **Causes:** Immunological (Graves' disease, chronic thyroiditis), non-immunological (nodular thyroid disease), gestational (hyperemesis gravidarum, multiple pregnancy, molar pregnancy), or iatrogenic.
- **Complications:** Maternal risks include miscarriage, preterm delivery, pre-eclampsia, congestive cardiac failure, placental abruption, and thyroid storm. Fetal risks include low birth weight (LBW), FGR, prematurity, stillbirth, and fetal hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism.
- **Diagnosis:** Suppressed TSH with elevated free T4 (FT4) and free T3. Elevated Thyroid Peroxidase Antibodies (TPOAb) act as a marker for Graves' disease. Radioactive iodine (131I) uptake is strictly contraindicated.
- **Management:** Propylthiouracil (PTU) is preferred in the first trimester as Methimazole carries a risk of embryopathy (e.g., choanal atresia, tracheoesophageal fistula, omphalocele). Methimazole can be used later. Propranolol is used for marked tachycardia. Thyroid storm is a life-threatening emergency managed in the ICU with fluids, beta-blockers, antithyroid drugs, Lugol's iodine, and hydrocortisone.

**Hypothyroidism**

- **Causes:** Autoimmune (Hashimoto's thyroiditis), post-thyroidectomy, post-131I treatment, or iodine deficiency.
- **Complications:** Pre-eclampsia, placental abruption, cardiac dysfunction, anemia, LBW, stillbirth, miscarriage, and deficient intellectual/neurologic development of the child.
- **Diagnosis:** Elevated TSH with low FT4 (overt) or normal FT4 (subclinical).
- **Management:** Levothyroxine 2-2.4 µg/kg/day, with doses often needing an increase in the second trimester to maintain normal TSH levels.

### **3. TORCH Infections in Pregnancy**

**Toxoplasmosis**

- **Organism & Effects:** Caused by the protozoan _Toxoplasma gondii_, transmitted via undercooked meat or cat feces. Fetal infection risk increases with gestational age (15% in 1st, 30% in 2nd, 60% in 3rd trimester). Fetal effects include hydrocephalus, ventriculomegaly, ascites, hepatosplenomegaly, chorioretinitis, cerebral calcification, and mental retardation.
- **Diagnosis & Treatment:** Detection of IgM and IgG seroconversion. Fetal diagnosis via amniocentesis PCR. Treatment involves Pyrimethamine (avoid in 1st trimester) + Sulfadiazine + Leucovorin, or Spiramycin for acute infection.

**Rubella (German Measles)**

- **Organism & Effects:** RNA virus. Extremely teratogenic if contracted in the first trimester (50% risk of major anomalies). Causes Congenital Rubella Syndrome (CRS): Sensorineural deafness, cardiac defects (PDA, septal defects), ophthalmic defects (cataracts, retinopathy), and hematologic issues.
- **Diagnosis & Management:** Virus-specific IgM and RT-PCR. Live-attenuated MMR vaccine is strictly contraindicated during pregnancy, and pregnancy should be avoided for 4 weeks post-vaccination.

**Cytomegalovirus (CMV)**

- **Organism & Effects:** DNA virus. Highest transplacental transmission is in the 3rd trimester (30-40%). Consequences include non-immune hydrops, IUGR, microcephaly, intracranial calcification, chorioretinitis, mental retardation, and sensorineural deafness.
- **Diagnosis & Management:** DNA PCR of amniotic fluid, blood, or urine. No routine screening or highly effective prenatal treatment is currently standard.

**Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV)**

- **Organism & Effects:** DNA virus (HSV-1 and HSV-2). Transplacental infection is unusual; the fetus is primarily infected during vaginal delivery through a contaminated birth canal. Disseminated neonatal infection can cause fatal hemorrhagic pneumonitis, DIC, and CNS damage.
- **Management:** Cesarean delivery is indicated for active primary genital HSV infection with intact or recently ruptured membranes. Antiviral therapy (Acyclovir or Valacyclovir) is given for primary and recurrent infections, and suppressive therapy is initiated at 36 weeks.

### **4. Sexually Transmitted Diseases (STDs) in Pregnancy**

**Syphilis**

- **Pathogenesis & Effects:** Caused by _Treponema pallidum_. The basic pathology is obliterative endarteritis leading to bulky placenta and fetal infection. Fetal effects include miscarriage, preterm birth, macerated/fresh stillbirth, non-immune fetal hydrops, and congenital syphilis. Early congenital syphilis presents with maculopapular rash, rhinitis, hepatosplenomegaly, and chorioretinitis; late presents with Hutchinson teeth, saddle nose, saber shins, and 8th nerve deafness.
- **Diagnosis:** Screening via VDRL (positive 6 weeks post-infection). Confirmatory tests include TPHA, EIA, and FTA-ABS.
- **Management:** Benzathine penicillin G (2.4 million units IM). For early syphilis, a single dose; for late syphilis, 3 doses are given. Penicillin-allergic patients require desensitization.

**Gonorrhea and Chlamydia**

- **Gonorrhea:** _Neisseria gonorrhoeae_ infection in pregnancy tends to be more acute. Increases the risk of preterm labor, PROM, and intrapartum/postpartum infection. Causes ophthalmia neonatorum in the newborn. Treated with Ceftriaxone 250 mg IM (single dose).
- **Chlamydia:** _Chlamydia trachomatis_ causes preterm labor, PROM, chorioamnionitis, and neonatal conjunctivitis or pneumonia. Treated with Erythromycin (0.5 g QID for 7-10 days) or Azithromycin (1 g single dose).

**Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) and AIDS**

- **Pathogenesis & Transmission:** HIV is an RNA retrovirus that depletes CD4+ T-cells. Perinatal/vertical transmission occurs transplacentally, intrapartum (highest risk due to exposure to birth canal secretions), and postnatally via breast milk. Risk factors for transmission include high maternal viral load, low CD4 count, prolonged rupture of membranes, and chorioamnionitis.
- **Diagnosis & Antenatal Care:** Opt-out VCTC screening for all pregnant women. Disease progression is monitored via CD4+ counts and HIV RNA viral load.
- **Management (cART):** Combined Antiretroviral Therapy (cART) is recommended for all pregnant women, ideally started by the 24th week, utilizing an NRTI backbone (e.g., Abacavir/Lamivudine or Tenofovir/Emtricitabine) plus a third agent (e.g., Efavirenz, Atazanavir).
- **Intrapartum & Postpartum Care:** Planned elective cesarean delivery at 39 weeks is indicated if the viral load is >50 copies/mL at 36 weeks. If the viral load is <50 copies/mL, vaginal delivery is planned. Intrapartum IV Zidovudine (ZDV) is given if the viral load is >1000 copies/mL or unknown. Neonates must receive ART prophylaxis within 4 hours of birth. Avoidance of breastfeeding is recommended in developed nations to prevent transmission.
