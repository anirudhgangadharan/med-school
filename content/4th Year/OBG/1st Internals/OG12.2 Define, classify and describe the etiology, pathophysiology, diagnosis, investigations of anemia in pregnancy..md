### **1. Definition of Anemia in Pregnancy**

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), anemia in pregnancy is defined as a hemoglobin (Hb) concentration of 11 g/100 mL (11 g/dL) or less, or a hematocrit of <33%. Because maternal plasma volume expands significantly during pregnancy resulting in hemodilution, a hemoglobin level below 10 g/dL at any time during pregnancy is broadly considered as pathological anemia requiring detailed investigation.

**Grading of Anemia (WHO criteria):**

- **Mild:** 10.0 – 10.9 g/dL
- **Moderate:** 7.0 – 9.9 g/dL
- **Severe:** < 7.0 g/dL
- **Very Severe:** < 4.0 g/dL

### **2. Classification of Anemia in Pregnancy**

Anemia in pregnancy is broadly classified into physiological and pathological types:

**A. Physiological Anemia of Pregnancy:** A normal adaptive drop in Hb concentration due to disproportionate plasma volume expansion compared to red cell mass.

**B. Pathological Anemia:**

- **1. Deficiency Anemias (Isolated or Combined):**
    - Iron deficiency (constitutes about 95% of cases).
    - Folic acid deficiency.
    - Vitamin B12 deficiency.
    - Protein deficiency.
- **2. Hemorrhagic Anemias:**
    - _Acute:_ Following bleeding in early pregnancy or Antepartum Hemorrhage (APH).
    - _Chronic:_ Hookworm infestation, bleeding piles.
- **3. Hereditary/Hemoglobinopathies:**
    - Thalassemias.
    - Sickle cell hemoglobinopathies.
    - Hereditary hemolytic anemias (e.g., RBC membrane defects like spherocytosis).
- **4. Bone Marrow Insufficiency:** Hypoplasia or aplasia (due to radiation or drugs).
- **5. Anemia of Infection:** Malaria, tuberculosis, kala-azar.
- **6. Chronic Disease or Neoplasm:** Chronic renal disease, leukemias, lymphomas.
- **7. Hemolytic Anemias:** Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE), HELLP syndrome, autoimmune hemolysis, drug-induced G6PD deficiency.

### **3. Etiology and Risk Factors**

The most common cause of anemia in pregnancy is iron deficiency. The factors leading to this, particularly in tropical regions, are divided into pre-pregnancy and during-pregnancy factors:

**A. Before Pregnancy:**

- **Faulty dietetic habits:** High phosphate and phytic acid in carbohydrate-rich diets form insoluble iron complexes, reducing absorption.
- **Faulty absorption:** Intestinal infestations, intestinal hurry, or hypochlorhydria hinder iron absorption.
- **Iron loss:** Due to heavy menstrual bleeding, repeated pregnancies with short intervals, prolonged lactation, hookworm infestation, malaria, or bleeding piles.

**B. During Pregnancy:**

- **Increased demands:** The total iron requirement in pregnancy is about 1,000 mg. Daily requirements rise to 6-7 mg in the second half of pregnancy, which cannot be met by diet alone.
- **Diminished intake:** Vegetarian diets, loss of appetite, and vomiting of pregnancy.
- **Diminished absorption:** Use of antacids, H2 blockers, and proton pump inhibitors inhibit absorption. Conditions like _H. pylori_ or celiac disease also impair it.
- **Infections:** Conditions like asymptomatic bacteriuria depress erythropoiesis.
- **Excess demand states:** Multiple pregnancy (two-fold increase in demand) and rapidly recurring pregnancies (<2 years interval).

### **4. Pathophysiology**

**Physiological Anemia:**

- Maternal plasma volume increases by 40-50% (about 1,250 mL) and peaks at around 30-34 weeks.
- Red Blood Cell (RBC) mass increases by only 20-30% (about 350 mL).
- This disproportionate increase leads to hemodilution, causing a relative fall in hemoglobin and hematocrit values, creating a physiological normocytic normochromic anemia.
- The advantages of this hemodilution include diminished blood viscosity, which ensures optimum gaseous exchange between maternal and fetal circulation.

**Pathological Anemia (Iron Deficiency):**

- It occurs when there is an absolute depletion of body iron stores, making them inadequate to maintain the demands for erythropoiesis.
- Due to the lack of iron, hemoglobin synthesis is impaired, leading to the production of small, pale-staining red blood cells (microcytic hypochromic anemia).

**Pathological Anemia (Megaloblastic):**

- Caused by the deficiency of folic acid (most common in pregnancy) or Vitamin B12.
- Folic acid and Vitamin B12 are essential for DNA synthesis in erythropoietic cells. Their deficiency leads to deranged red cell maturation and the production of abnormal, large precursors (megaloblasts) in the bone marrow.

### **5. Diagnosis (Clinical Features)**

In mild cases, patients may be asymptomatic, and the condition is detected during routine blood screening. When symptomatic, the features include:

**Symptoms:**

- Easy fatigue, weakness, and exhaustion (earliest manifestations).
- Anorexia and indigestion.
- Palpitation, dyspnea on exertion, giddiness.
- Swelling of the legs (edema).

**Signs on Examination:**

- Pallor of varying degrees (checked in the conjunctiva, tongue, nail beds).
- Glossitis, stomatitis, and angular cheilitis.
- Nail changes: Brittle, fragile, ridged nails, or Koilonychia (spoon-shaped nails).
- Leg edema (may also be due to hypoproteinemia or associated pre-eclampsia).
- Cardiovascular signs: Tachycardia, cardiac enlargement, soft systolic murmur in the mitral area.
- Respiratory signs: Basal crepitations due to pulmonary congestion.
- _Plummer Vinson Syndrome:_ A triad of dysphagia, stomatitis, and iron deficiency anemia.

### **6. Investigations**

Any patient with a hemoglobin level of <= 9 g% should undergo full hematological evaluation to determine the degree, type, and cause of anemia.

**A. To Note the Degree of Anemia:**

- Hemoglobin estimation (Hb).
- Total Red Blood Cell (RBC) count.
- Packed Cell Volume (PCV) / Hematocrit.

**B. To Ascertain the Type of Anemia:**

- **Peripheral Blood Smear:** Essential to study RBC morphology. Iron deficiency shows microcytic hypochromic cells with anisocytosis (variation in size) and poikilocytosis (variation in shape). Megaloblastic anemia shows macrocytes and hypersegmented neutrophils.
- **Hematological Indices:**
    - Mean Corpuscular Volume (MCV): <75 µ³ (or <79 fL) indicates microcytic.
    - Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin Concentration (MCHC): <30% (most sensitive index of iron deficiency).
    - Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin (MCH): <25 pg.
- **Serum Iron Studies:**
    - Serum Ferritin: <30 µg/L (The single most reliable indicator to confirm iron deficiency anemia, as it reflects the main storage protein for iron).
    - Serum Iron: <30 µg/100 mL.
    - Total Iron Binding Capacity (TIBC): Raised (>400 µg/dL).
    - Transferrin Saturation: <=10% to <16%.

**C. To Find Out the Cause of Anemia:**

- **Stool Examination:** Routine in the tropics to rule out helminthic/hookworm infestation.
- **Urine Examination:** Routine and microscopic for RBCs/pus cells, and 'clean catch' midstream urine culture to detect asymptomatic bacteriuria/UTI.
- **Hemoglobin Electrophoresis (HPLC):** Indicated if MCV is low but iron deficiency is excluded, to screen for hemoglobinopathies like Thalassemia or Sickle Cell Trait.
- **Chest X-ray:** If pulmonary tuberculosis is suspected.

**D. Bone Marrow Study (Rarely Indicated):**

- Not a routine test. Indicated only when the anemia does not respond to appropriate therapy (refractory), to diagnose hypoplastic/aplastic anemia, or to detect _Leishman-Donovan_ bodies for kala-azar.
