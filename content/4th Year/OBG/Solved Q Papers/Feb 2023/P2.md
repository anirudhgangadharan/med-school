1. Define abnormal uterine bleeding, mention its causes and management of AUB in 30 year old multiparous lady.

**DEFINITION OF ABNORMAL UTERINE BLEEDING (AUB)** Any uterine bleeding outside the normal volume, duration, regularity, or frequency is considered abnormal uterine bleeding (AUB). Heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB) is a subset of AUB, defined as bleeding that interferes with a woman's physical, emotional, social, and maternal quality of life.

**CAUSES OF ABNORMAL UTERINE BLEEDING** The causes of AUB are systematically classified by the FIGO (2011) acronym **PALM-COEIN**, which subdivides the etiologies into structural and nonstructural categories.

**Structural Causes (PALM):**

- **P** - Polyp (AUB-P).
- **A** - Adenomyosis (AUB-A).
- **L** - Leiomyoma (AUB-L); further subdivided into submucosal myoma (AUB-LSM) and other myoma (AUB-LO).
- **M** - Malignancy and hyperplasia (AUB-M).

**Nonstructural Systemic Causes (COEIN):**

- **C** - Coagulopathy (AUB-C).
- **O** - Ovulatory dysfunction (AUB-O).
- **E** - Endometrial (AUB-E).
- **I** - Iatrogenic (AUB-I).
- **N** - Not yet identified (AUB-N).

---

**MANAGEMENT OF AUB IN A 30-YEAR-OLD MULTIPAROUS LADY** A 30-year-old multiparous woman falls into the reproductive age group (20–40 years). The management protocol is based on her desire for future childbearing, the severity of bleeding, and the underlying pathology.

**1. Initial Assessment and Investigations**

- **Rule out Pregnancy:** Pregnancy must always be excluded beforehand, irrespective of the marital status of the woman.
- **Clinical Evaluation:** A thorough general and systemic examination is done to exclude any systemic, iatrogenic, or organic pelvic pathology.
- **Laboratory Tests:** Blood values including complete blood count (CBC) and coagulation profile (platelet count, prothrombin time, partial thromboplastin time) must be checked to exclude bleeding disorders. Thyroid profile (TSH, T3, T4) should also be assessed.
- **Imaging:** Transvaginal sonography (TVS) or Saline infusion sonography (SIS) is done to detect structural abnormalities like fibroids or adenomyosis.
- **Hysteroscopy and Biopsy:** Hysteroscopy with directed biopsy is the best method to evaluate endometrial pathology and to detect submucous fibroids or polyps. Endometrial sampling should be considered during the childbearing period if the bleeding is acyclic.

**2. General Management**

- Advise rest during the active bleeding phase.
- Provide adequate assurance and sympathetic handling.
- Correct anemia using dietary modifications, hematinics, or blood transfusion if the bleeding is severe.

**3. Medical Management** Medical management is the first line of treatment if no significant structural pathology is found.

- **Nonhormonal Therapy:**
    - _Prostaglandin Synthetase Inhibitors (NSAIDs):_ Mefenamic acid (500 mg TID for 5 days) reduces menstrual blood loss by 25–50% and concurrently relieves dysmenorrhea.
    - _Antifibrinolytic Agents:_ Tranexamic acid (500 mg–1 g 6-hourly for 5 days) counteracts the endometrial fibrinolytic system and reduces blood loss by 50%.
- **Hormonal Therapy:**
    - _Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs):_ Highly effective for cycle regulation and minimizing blood loss. They are the drug of choice if the patient also desires contraception. Treatment is typically continued for 3–6 cycles.
    - _Progestins:_ Norethisterone acetate (5 mg TID) or Medroxyprogesterone acetate (10 mg TID) given cyclically (from day 5 to 25 of the cycle) or continuously to induce endometrial atrophy and organize shedding.
    - _Levonorgestrel Intrauterine System (LNG-IUS):_ Very effective for women who have completed their family or want long-term contraception. It reduces blood loss by 80–100%, induces endometrial atrophy, and acts as a "medical hysterectomy".
- **Management of Acute/Severe Bleeding:** Conjugated equine estrogen 25 mg intravenously every 4 hours or 10 mg/day orally can be given to achieve rapid hemostasis.

**4. Surgical Management** Since the patient is multiparous, if her family is completed and medical treatment fails or is contraindicated, surgical options are considered.

- **Hysteroscopic Surgery:** Resection of localized lesions like endometrial polyps or submucous fibroids.
- **Endometrial Ablation/Resection:** Using laser, rollerball, thermal balloon, or microwave to destroy the endometrium up to the basal layer. This is an excellent option for women who have completed their family, wish to preserve the uterus, and have a normal-sized uterus without significant pathology.
- **Uterine Artery Embolization (UAE):** An interventional radiological option indicated if AUB is caused by large uterine fibroids.
- **Hysterectomy (Abdominal, Vaginal, or Laparoscopic):** Considered the definitive treatment of last resort. It is justified when conservative therapies fail, bleeding severely impairs the quality of life, or if premalignant changes (like atypical hyperplasia) are detected.

2. Discuss in detail about risk factors of CA Cervix and staging of carcinoma cervix and management.

**RISK FACTORS FOR CARCINOMA CERVIX**

Carcinoma cervix is primarily associated with viral and venereal factors. The prolonged effect of carcinogens on the immature cells of the transformation zone leads to malignancy. The key risk factors include:

- **Infectious Agents:**
    - Human Papillomavirus (HPV) is the most critical risk factor. High oncogenic risk types include 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 45, 52, 56, 58, 59, and 68.
    - Other infections include HSV-2, HIV (immunosuppression), and _Chlamydia_.
- **Sexual Behavior:**
    - Early age of first sexual intercourse ($\le$ 16 years).
    - Multiple sexual partners or a husband with multiple sexual partners.
- **Obstetric Factors:**
    - Early age of first pregnancy.
    - Multiparity, and having too many and too frequent births.
- **Socioeconomic and Personal Factors:**
    - Poor genital hygiene and low socioeconomic status.
    - Dietary deficiencies of vitamins A, C, E, and folic acid.
    - Smoking habit.
- **Medical History:**
    - Immunosuppressed state (e.g., HIV-positive individuals).
    - Prolonged use of combined oral contraceptive pills.
    - Inadequate cervical cytology screening.
    - Increasing age.

---

**FIGO STAGING OF CARCINOMA CERVIX (2018)**

Staging is based primarily on clinical examination, supplemented by imaging (r) and pathology (p) to allocate cases accurately.

**STAGE I: The carcinoma is strictly confined to the cervix (extension to the uterine corpus should be disregarded).**

- **Stage IA:** Invasive carcinoma that can be diagnosed only by microscopy with a maximum depth of invasion <5 mm.
    - **IA1:** Measured stromal invasion <3 mm in depth.
    - **IA2:** Measured stromal invasion $\ge$ 3 mm and <5 mm in depth.
- **Stage IB:** Invasive carcinoma with measured deepest invasion $\ge$ 5 mm, lesion limited to the cervix uteri.
    - **IB1:** Invasive carcinoma $\ge$ 5 mm depth of stromal invasion, and <2 cm in greatest dimension.
    - **IB2:** Invasive carcinoma $\ge$ 2 cm and <4 cm in greatest dimension.
    - **IB3:** Invasive carcinoma $\ge$ 4 cm in greatest dimension.

**STAGE II: The carcinoma invades beyond the uterus, but has not extended onto the lower third of the vagina or to the pelvic wall.**

- **Stage IIA:** Involvement limited to the upper two-thirds of the vagina without parametrial involvement.
    - **IIA1:** Invasive carcinoma <4 cm in greatest dimension.
    - **IIA2:** Invasive carcinoma $\ge$ 4 cm in greatest dimension.
- **Stage IIB:** With parametrial involvement but not up to the pelvic wall.

**STAGE III: The carcinoma involves the lower third of the vagina and/or extends to the pelvic wall and/or causes hydronephrosis or nonfunctioning kidney and/or involves pelvic and/or para-aortic lymph nodes.**

- **Stage IIIA:** The carcinoma involves the lower third of the vagina, with no extension to the pelvic wall.
- **Stage IIIB:** Extension to the pelvic wall and/or hydronephrosis or nonfunctioning kidney.
- **Stage IIIC:** Involvement of pelvic and/or para-aortic lymph nodes, irrespective of tumor size and extent.
    - **IIIC1:** Pelvic lymph node metastasis only.
    - **IIIC2:** Para-aortic lymph node metastasis.

**STAGE IV: The carcinoma has extended beyond the true pelvis or has involved (biopsy proven) the mucosa of the bladder or rectum.**

- **Stage IVA:** Spread to adjacent pelvic organs.
- **Stage IVB:** Spread to distant organs.

**MANAGEMENT OF CARCINOMA CERVIX**

Management requires a multidisciplinary approach, taking into account the patient's age, general condition, stage of the disease, and desire for fertility.

**1. Preventive Management**

- **Primary Prevention:**
    - Identifying and educating high-risk individuals.
    - Modifying sexual behavior (delaying sex, limiting partners, promoting condom use).
    - **HPV Vaccination:** Prophylactic vaccines (bivalent, quadrivalent, or nonavalent) given ideally to girls aged 9–26 years to produce immunity against high-risk HPV types.
    - Maintenance of genital hygiene and treatment of STIs.
- **Secondary Prevention:**
    - Population-based screening programs using cervical cytology (Pap smear/LBC), primary HPV testing, or visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA) to detect and treat precancerous lesions.

**2. Pretreatment Evaluation**

- Improve general health, correct anemia and malnutrition.
- Evaluate serum tumor markers (SCCA, CA-125, CEA) to establish baselines.
- Imaging (CT, MRI, PET) for node status and metastasis detection.

**3. Stage-Wise Curative Management**

- **Stage IA1 (Microinvasive, <3 mm depth):**
    
    - _Fertility desired:_ Cervical conization with close follow-up, or Modified Radical Trachelectomy.
    - _Fertility not desired (Elderly):_ Total extra-fascial hysterectomy.
    - _If LVSI is present:_ Modified Radical Hysterectomy with Pelvic Lymphadenectomy or Sentinel Lymph Node Biopsy (SLNB).
- **Stage IA2 ($\ge$ 3 mm, <5 mm depth):**
    
    - _Low risk:_ Modified Radical Trachelectomy OR Modified Radical Hysterectomy + Pelvic Lymphadenectomy or SLNB.
    - _Fertility desired:_ Cervical conization with laparoscopic extraperitoneal pelvic lymphadenectomy OR Radical Trachelectomy with Pelvic Lymphadenectomy.
- **Stage IB1, IB2, and IIA1 (Early Invasive Carcinoma):**
    
    - _Primary Modality:_ Surgery or Radiotherapy (both yield similar survival rates of ~85%).
    - _Surgical approach:_ Radical Hysterectomy (Type III) + Pelvic Lymphadenectomy or SLNB.
    - _Fertility sparing (Stage IB1 <2cm):_ Radical Trachelectomy with pelvic lymph node evaluation.
- **Stage IB3 ($\ge$ 4 cm) and IIA2 (Bulky Tumors):**
    
    - Surgery is discouraged due to high morbidity.
    - _Standard treatment:_ Concurrent platinum-based chemoradiation therapy (CCRT).
    - Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) followed by surgery can be used to shrink the tumor where radiotherapy facilities are scarce.
- **Stage IIB, III, and IVA (Locally Advanced Cervical Cancer):**
    
    - _Standard Treatment:_ Concurrent Chemoradiation Therapy (CCRT). External Beam Radiation Therapy (EBRT) combined with Brachytherapy.
    - Systemic chemotherapy is integrated for stages IIIC2 and above.
    - _Stage IVA (Central disease only, without pelvic wall/distant spread):_ Pelvic exenteration may be considered but carries a poor prognosis.
- **Stage IVB (Distant Metastasis):**
    
    - CCRT utilizing Carboplatin-paclitaxel, cisplatin-topotecan, gemcitabine, or vinorelbine.
    - Palliative chemotherapy and/or palliative radiotherapy to control pain and hemorrhage.
    - Supportive/hospice care focusing on symptom control, pain management (morphine), and quality of life.

3. Supports of uterus.

**SUPPORTS OF THE UTERUS**

The normal position of the uterus is anteverted and anteflexed. The uterine supports are designed to hold the organ in this anatomical position and prevent its downward descent through the natural urogenital hiatus in the pelvic floor.

The supports of the uterus are biomechanically grouped into a functional **three-tier system**:

### **1. Upper Tier (Positional Support)**

The uppermost supports primarily maintain the uterus in its normal anteverted position.

- **Structures Involved:**
    - Endopelvic fascia covering the uterus.
    - Round ligaments.
    - Broad ligaments with intervening pelvic cellular tissues.
- **Function:** The broad and round ligaments act as "guy ropes" that provide a steadying effect on the uterus. They **do not** have any action in preventing the downward descent (prolapse) of the uterus.

### **2. Middle Tier (Primary/Strongest Support)**

This tier constitutes the strongest and most vital support of the uterus. It consists of a hammock-like arrangement of condensed pelvic cellular tissues (endopelvic fascia) reinforced by plain muscle fibers.

- **Pericervical Ring:** A collar of fibroelastic connective tissue that completely encircles the supravaginal cervix. It acts as an anchoring hub for all major ligaments and stabilizes the cervix at the level of the interspinous diameter.
- **Mackenrodt’s Ligaments (Cardinal / Transverse Cervical Ligaments):** These fan-shaped condensations extend from the lateral supravaginal cervix and upper vaginal wall to the parietal fascia covering the obturator internus on the lateral pelvic wall. They provide lateral stabilization to the cervix and act as the primary vascular conduits of the uterus.
- **Uterosacral Ligaments:** Formed by the condensation of peritoneum, they extend from the posterolateral surface of the cervix (at the level of the internal os) to the periosteum of the 2nd, 3rd, and 4th sacral vertebrae. They hold the cervix posteriorly at the level of the ischial spines, which mechanically maintains the uterus in an anteflexed position and suspends the vagina over the levator plate.
- **Pubocervical Fascia (Bladder Pillar):** Extends from the anterolateral supravaginal cervix and pericervical ring to the back of the pubic bone and arcus tendineus.

### **3. Inferior Tier (Indirect Support)**

This tier provides indirect support by forming a robust base that holds up the upper structures and closes the pelvic outlet.

- **Pelvic Floor Muscles (Levator Ani):** A strong, fatigue-resistant striated muscle slung like a hammock around the midline pelvic effluents. It supports the pelvic viscera, guards the hiatus urogenitalis, and strongly counteracts the downward thrust of increased intra-abdominal pressure.
- **Levator Plate:** A thick band of connective tissue and muscle formed by the fusion of the medial fibers of the two levator ani muscles (anococcygeal raphe). It forms a horizontal supportive shelf upon which the uterus, upper vagina, and rectum rest. Due to its horizontal position, it physically prevents the prolapse of genital organs.
- **Perineal Body (Obstetrical Perineum):** A solid, pyramidal-shaped fibromuscular structure at the central point of the perineum. It receives attachments from 9 muscles (like the hub of a wheel) and is crucial for stabilizing the pelvic floor and maintaining the normal vaginal axis.
- **Urogenital Diaphragm:** Works in conjunction with the perineal body and levator ani to reinforce the inferior support system.

4. PCOS.
**POLYCYSTIC OVARIAN SYNDROME (PCOS)**

**DEFINITION AND EPIDEMIOLOGY** Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome (PCOS) is a heterogeneous, multifactorial, and polygenic endocrine disorder characterized primarily by excessive androgen production by the ovaries. It is the most common endocrine disorder in women of reproductive age, with an incidence of 0.5–4%, and is highly prevalent (20–30%) among young women in this group.

**DIAGNOSTIC CRITERIA (ASRM/ESHRE 2018)** Diagnosis is based on the presence of **any two of the following three criteria** (after excluding other etiologies like congenital adrenal hyperplasia, thyroid dysfunction, hyperprolactinemia, and Cushing's syndrome):

1. Oligo-ovulation and/or anovulation.
2. Hyperandrogenism (clinical and/or biochemical).
3. Polycystic ovaries on ultrasound.

**PATHOLOGY OF THE OVARY**

- **Macroscopic:** The ovaries are typically enlarged with an ovarian volume $>10 \text{ cm}^3$. The capsule is thickened and pearly white in color.
- **Microscopic:** Thickening of the tunica albuginea is observed. There is the presence of multiple (typically $>12$ to $\ge 20$) peripherally arranged follicular cysts measuring 2–9 mm in diameter. The stroma is increased, demonstrating theca cell hypertrophy (stromal hyperthecosis). There is no evidence of a corpus luteum due to anovulation.

**ETIOPATHOGENESIS AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY** The exact pathophysiology involves a vicious cycle of endocrine disturbances across multiple compartments:

- **Hypothalamic-Pituitary Abnormality:** There is an increased pulse frequency of Gonadotropin-Releasing Hormone (GnRH), which leads to a preferential increase in the pulse frequency and amplitude of Luteinizing Hormone (LH). This results in tonically elevated LH levels and an altered LH:FSH ratio (often $>3:1$). FSH remains low due to negative feedback from chronically elevated estrogens and inhibin.
- **Anovulation and Hyperandrogenism:** Low FSH arrests follicular growth at 2–10 mm, preventing dominant follicle selection. The tonically elevated LH stimulates theca cell hypertrophy, leading to excessive androgen production (androstenedione and testosterone). Defective aromatization creates an androgenic microenvironment within the follicle, arresting maturation.
- **Obesity and Insulin Resistance (IR):** Central (android) obesity is a major environmental insult. Hyperinsulinemia directly stimulates theca cells to produce more androgens and inhibits the hepatic synthesis of Sex Hormone-Binding Globulin (SHBG), thereby increasing the levels of free, biologically active androgens.
- **Tonic Hyperestrogenic State:** Excess androstenedione undergoes peripheral aromatization in adipose tissue to estrone ($E_1$). This cumulative unbound estrone and estradiol creates a tonic hyperestrogenic state, sensitizing the pituitary to GnRH and stimulating endometrial hyperplasia.

**CLINICAL FEATURES**

- **Menstrual Abnormalities (70%):** Patients typically present with oligomenorrhea, amenorrhea, or dysfunctional uterine bleeding (DUB).
- **Infertility:** Due to chronic oligo-ovulation or anovulation.
- **Obesity (50%):** Characterized by android (central) obesity, measured by elevated BMI and waist-hip ratio.
- **Hyperandrogenism (70%):** Manifests clinically as hirsutism, acne, and alopecia. Frank virilism (deep voice, clitoromegaly) is extremely rare and should raise suspicion of an androgen-producing tumor.
- **Acanthosis Nigricans:** Thickened, hyperpigmented, velvety skin changes over the nape of the neck, inner thighs, groin, and axilla, which are clinical markers of severe insulin resistance.
- **HAIR-AN Syndrome:** A severe subset characterized by **H**yper**A**ndrogenism, **I**nsulin **R**esistance, and **A**canthosis **N**igricans.

**INVESTIGATIONS**

- **Ultrasonography (TVS is preferred):** Reveals enlarged ovarian volume ($\ge 10 \text{ cm}^3$), increased stroma, and $\ge 20$ (or $>12$) peripherally arranged small follicles measuring 2–9 mm (resembling a "necklace").
- **Hormone Assays:**
    - **LH and FSH:** Elevated LH and increased LH:FSH ratio ($>3:1$).
    - **Androgens:** Raised serum testosterone ($>1.5 \text{ ng/mL}$) and Dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS); low SHBG.
    - **Estrogens:** Raised estrone and estradiol.
    - **Prolactin:** Mildly elevated in 20% of cases.
- **Metabolic Profile:**
    - **Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT):** Impaired in 31% of patients, with frank diabetes in 7.5%.
    - **Insulin:** Raised fasting serum insulin ($>25 \text{ \mu IU/mL}$) and fasting glucose/insulin ratio $<4.5$ indicate insulin resistance.
    - **Fasting Lipid Profile:** Evaluation for dyslipidemia.

**LONG-TERM CONSEQUENCES AND HEALTH RISKS** If left untreated, patients with PCOS are at a high risk for:

- **Metabolic Syndrome:** Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (10%), hypertension, dyslipidemia (decreased HDL, increased LDL and triglycerides).
- **Cardiovascular:** Increased risk of coronary artery disease and cardiovascular morbidity.
- **Neoplastic:** Persistently elevated unopposed estrogen significantly increases the risk of endometrial hyperplasia and endometrial cancer. Ovarian cancer risk is also increased.
- **Pregnancy Complications:** Increased incidence of early pregnancy loss/miscarriage.
- **Other:** Obstructive sleep apnea due to obesity.

**MANAGEMENT PROTOCOL** Management must be highly individualized based on the patient's primary complaints (obesity, infertility, hirsutism, or menstrual irregularities).

**1. General Measures & Metabolic Correction:**

- **Weight Reduction & Lifestyle:** First-line treatment for obese patients. Weight reduction of just 2–5% improves hyperandrogenism, insulin resistance, and menstrual cyclicity.
- **Insulin Sensitizers (Metformin):** Dose is typically 500 mg thrice daily. Improves insulin sensitivity, reduces hyperandrogenemia, lowers BMI, corrects lipid abnormalities, and restores ovulation.

**2. Management of Menstrual Abnormalities (Fertility Not Desired):**

- **Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs):** Treatment of choice. They regulate the cycle, suppress LH, increase SHBG (reducing free androgens), and provide endometrial protection against hyperplasia/carcinoma.
- **Cyclic Progestins:** Used to induce withdrawal bleeding and prevent endometrial hyperplasia.

**3. Management of Hyperandrogenism (Hirsutism & Acne):**

- **COCs:** Specifically those containing low-androgenic progestins like drospirenone or desogestrel.
- **Antiandrogens:** Must be used alongside contraception. Options include:
    - _Cyproterone acetate_ (2 mg combined with ethinyl estradiol) inhibits gonadotropin secretion and blocks androgen receptors.
    - _Spironolactone_ (50–200 mg/day) acts as an aldosterone antagonist and blocks androgen receptors at the hair follicle.
    - _Flutamide_ (nonsteroidal androgen receptor antagonist) or _Finasteride_ (5$\alpha$-reductase inhibitor).
- **Cosmetic measures:** Epilation, electrolysis, or laser hair removal for existing terminal hair. Topical retinoids or benzoyl peroxide for acne.

**4. Management of Subfertility (Ovulation Induction):**

- **First-line drugs:** Clomiphene citrate or Letrozole (aromatase inhibitor).
- **Adjuvant Metformin:** Highly effective in restoring ovulation when combined with Clomiphene in insulin-resistant patients.
- **Gonadotropins (Pure FSH / hMG + hCG):** Second-line for Clomiphene-resistant cases. Requires strict monitoring (TVS and estradiol) due to the high risk of severe Ovarian Hyperstimulation Syndrome (OHSS) and multiple pregnancies.

**5. Surgical Management:**

- **Laparoscopic Ovarian Drilling (LOD):** Indicated for clomiphene-resistant, hyperandrogenic anovulatory women. Involves 4–6 punctures per ovary using diathermy or laser vaporization (depth 2–4 mm). It destroys androgen-producing stroma, lowers systemic/intraovarian androgen levels, and restores spontaneous ovulation without the risk of OHSS. Replaces historical wedge resection.
- **Bariatric Surgery:** Recommended for morbidly obese patients (BMI $>35 \text{ kg/m}^2$) who fail medical therapy.
- **In Vitro Fertilization (IVF):** Reserved for refractory cases.

5. Leucorrhoea diagnosis and management.

**LEUCORRHOEA: DIAGNOSIS AND MANAGEMENT**

**DEFINITION AND CRITERIA** Leucorrhoea (or leukorrhea) is strictly defined as an excessive normal vaginal discharge. To be classified as leucorrhoea, the discharge must fulfil the following specific criteria:

- The excess secretion is evident from persistent vulvar moistness, staining of the undergarments (which turns brownish-yellow on drying), or the need to wear a vulvar pad.
- It is non-purulent and non-offensive.
- It is non-irritant and never causes pruritus.

**ETIOLOGY AND CAUSES** The causes of leucorrhoea can be physiological or pathological (non-infective):

- **Physiological Excess:** Normal secretion increases in conditions associated with high oestrogen levels. These include:
    - Puberty.
    - Menstrual cycle changes, specifically around ovulation and in the premenstrual phase.
    - Pregnancy.
    - Sexual excitement (due to abundant secretion from Bartholin’s glands).
- **Cervical Causes:** Non-infective cervical lesions can produce excessive secretion that pours out at the vulva. Such lesions include cervical ectopy (erosion), chronic cervicitis, mucous polyps, and ectropion (exposure of cervical glands to the vagina).
- **Vaginal Causes:** Increased vaginal transudation occurs in conditions associated with increased pelvic congestion. These include:
    - Uterine prolapse.
    - Acquired retroverted uterus.
    - Chronic pelvic inflammation.
    - Oral contraceptive 'pill' use.
    - Vaginal adenosis.
- **General Factors:** Ill health is a significant cause of excessive discharge, as it leads to excess exfoliation of the superficial vaginal cells.

**DIAGNOSIS AND CLINICAL EVALUATION** Evaluation requires a detailed history and systematic physical examination to differentiate leucorrhoea from infective or neoplastic causes.

- **Detailed History:** Must cover the duration of the discharge, any prior episodes, contraceptive practice, and the presence of associated symptoms like dysuria, dyspareunia, or pelvic pain.
- **Physical Examination:** Involves general health assessment and abdominal examination to detect any mass or tenderness.
- **Inspection of the Vulva:** To check for the nature of the discharge and to rule out any ulcers.
- **Speculum Examination:** Essential to detect any structural pathology in the cervix or vagina (e.g., ectopy, polyps).
- **Bimanual Pelvic Examination:** To detect any foreign body, adnexal tenderness, or pelvic mass.

**INVESTIGATIONS** To rule out specific infections and malignancies, the following investigations should be organised:

- Microbiological study of the discharge.
- Papanicolaou (Pap) smear.
- Urine for routine examination (RE) and culture & sensitivity (CS).
- PCR/NAAT (Nucleic Acid Amplification Testing) for specific pathogens.

**MANAGEMENT** The management of leucorrhoea is highly dependent on identifying and treating the underlying cause, alongside providing reassurance:

- **General Measures:** Improvement of general health, providing assurance, and adopting a sympathetic attitude towards the patient's ailments are often enough to cure the state.
- **Local Hygiene:** Meticulous maintenance of local perineal hygiene must be advised.
- **Treatment of Cervical Factors:** Persistent cervical lesions causing troublesome discharge require surgical treatments such as electrocautery, cryosurgery, or trachelorrhaphy.
- **Treatment of Pelvic Lesions:** Pelvic pathologies (e.g., prolapse, pelvic congestion) producing vaginal leucorrhoea require appropriate targeted therapy.
- **Contraceptive Pill Users:** If the symptom is severely annoying, the patient may have to temporarily stop the use of the oral contraceptive 'pill'.
- **Infection Control:** If investigations reveal a specific infection, targeted antimicrobial therapy must be instituted.

6. Medical management of Ectopic pregnancy.

**MEDICAL MANAGEMENT OF ECTOPIC PREGNANCY**

Medical management has become a preferred conservative approach for unruptured ectopic pregnancies, aiming to significantly reduce operative morbidity, hospital stay, and cost while improving the chances of subsequent intrauterine pregnancy.

**1. Patient Selection Criteria for Medical Therapy** Careful selection is crucial for the success of medical management. The patient must fulfill the following criteria:

- Asymptomatic and compliant.
- Hemodynamically stable.
- Serum β-hCG level less than 3,000 IU/L.
- Tubal diameter less than 4 cm without any fetal cardiac activity.
- No evidence of intra-abdominal hemorrhage.

**2. Drugs Used**

- **Systemic Therapy:** Methotrexate (MTX) is the most commonly used drug. Actinomycin can also be used.
- **Direct Local Therapy (Salpingocentesis):** Under sonographic or laparoscopic guidance, chemotherapeutic agents are injected directly into the ectopic mass. Drugs used include Methotrexate, Potassium chloride (KCl), Prostaglandin (PGF2α), Hyperosmolar glucose, or Actinomycin.

**3. Methotrexate (MTX) Therapy**

- **Mechanism of Action:** MTX is a folic acid antagonist. It blocks the enzyme dihydrofolate reductase, preventing the conversion of dihydrofolate to tetrahydrofolate (the active form of folic acid). This blocks the synthesis of DNA, RNA, and proteins in the rapidly dividing trophoblast cells.
- **Success Rate:** The overall success rate of MTX therapy is 90%.

**Contraindications to Methotrexate:**

- **Absolute Contraindications:** Breastfeeding, hepatic, renal, or hematologic dysfunction, immunodeficiency state, significant anemia, sensitivity to MTX, and active pulmonary disease.
- **Relative Contraindications:** Embryonic cardiac motion, Gestational sac >3.5 cm.

**4. Treatment Regimens**

**A. Single-Dose Regimen**

- **Dose:** Methotrexate 50 mg/m² given intramuscularly (IM) as a single dose.
- **Monitoring:**
    - Measure serum β-hCG levels on Day 4 and Day 7 after administration.
    - If the decline in β-hCG between Day 4 and Day 7 is ≥ 15%, the patient is followed up weekly with serum β-hCG until levels drop to less than 10 mIU/mL.
    - If the decline is < 15%, a second dose of MTX 50 mg/m² is administered on Day 7.

**B. Multidose Regimen**

- **Indications:** Used for advanced gestations, those with embryonic cardiac activity, or cases failing the single-dose therapy.
- **Dose:** MTX 1 mg/kg IM on Days 1, 3, 5, and 7, alternating with Leucovorin (folinic acid) 0.1 mg/kg IM on Days 2, 4, 6, and 8.
- **Monitoring:** Serum β-hCG is monitored weekly until the level falls to less than 5.0 mIU/mL. Outpatient monitoring is generally preferred unless additional safety is needed.

**5. Side Effects of Medical Management**

- Side effects are more common with the multidose regimen.
- **Most common:** Flatulence.
- **Others:** Mucositis, gastroenteritis, hepatitis, anemia, and bone marrow depression.
- **Pain:** Patients may complain of abdominal pain following therapy due to the death of trophoblast cells and the separation of the ectopic pregnancy from the tubal wall. This generally subsides on its own.

**6. Medical Management in Uncommon Ectopic Pregnancies**

- **Interstitial Pregnancy:** Systemic or local Methotrexate can be used in hemodynamically stable patients.
- **Cervical Pregnancy:** Managed with systemic MTX or local injection of KCl.
- **Ovarian Pregnancy:** Systemic MTX can be used when the risk of surgery is high.
- **Cesarean Scar Pregnancy:** Managed with systemic (IM) MTX or local injection directly into the gestation sac.
- **Heterotopic Pregnancy:** Methotrexate is strictly reserved for cases where the concurrent intrauterine pregnancy is nonviable or the patient does not wish to continue the pregnancy. Otherwise, local injection of KCl or hyperosmolar glucose (feticide) with aspiration of the ectopic sac is done to preserve the intrauterine pregnancy.

7. Endometrial biopsy.
**ENDOMETRIAL BIOPSY / SAMPLING**

**Introduction** Endometrial biopsy or sampling is a rapid, safe, and inexpensive diagnostic procedure frequently performed to evaluate the histological and functional state of the endometrium. It can be performed as an outpatient procedure without anaesthesia or as an inpatient surgical procedure (Dilatation and Curettage).

**Indications** **1. Diagnostic Indications:**

- **Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (AUB) / Dysfunctional Uterine Bleeding (DUB):** To determine the functional state of the endometrium and exclude organic lesions.
- **Infertility Work-up:** To detect evidence of ovulation and evaluate the functional integrity of the corpus luteum.
- **Postmenopausal Bleeding:** Mandatory evaluation to exclude endometrial malignancy or hyperplasia.
- **Suspected Endometrial Tuberculosis:** To obtain tissue for histopathology, AFB culture, and PCR.
- **Hormone Replacement Therapy (HRT):** For periodic screening or evaluating abnormal bleeding during HRT.
- **Pathologic Amenorrhea:** To assess endometrial responsiveness (e.g., excluding uterine synechiae).
- **Chorionepithelioma (Choriocarcinoma):** For histological diagnosis, though deep myometrial lesions may be missed.

**2. Therapeutic / Combined Indications:**

- Arresting acute episodes of excessive dysfunctional uterine bleeding.
- Removal of endometrial polyps, retained intrauterine contraceptive devices (IUCD), or incomplete abortion products.

**Instruments Used**

- **Pipelle (Endometrial Sampler):** A thin plastic cannula (2–4 mm diameter) with an internal plunger. It is the instrument of first choice for outpatient sampling. Suction is created when the plunger is withdrawn, obtaining adequate tissue with a failure rate of less than 8%.
- **Sharman’s Curette:** Used in infertility workups as an outpatient procedure to obtain a strip of endometrium for hormonal reflection study.
- **Vabra Aspirator:** Another suction device used for outpatient endometrial sampling.
- **Uterine Curettes:**
    - _Sharp curette:_ Used for benign lesions (infertility, DUB, TB endometritis).
    - _Blunt curette:_ Used in suspected endometrial carcinoma or choriocarcinoma to prevent uterine perforation.
- **Hysteroscopy-directed biopsy:** The gold standard, allowing direct visualization and targeted biopsy of focal lesions like polyps or submucous fibroids that blind procedures might miss.

**Timing of the Biopsy**

- **Infertility (to check ovulation):** Ideally performed on the 21st–23rd day of a regular 28-day cycle. Barrier contraception must be used during this cycle to prevent accidental conception.
- **Irregular Cycles:** Done within 24 hours of the onset of menstruation.
- **Menorrhagia / Irregular Shedding:** 5–6 days prior to the period or 5–6 days after the period starts.
- **Acyclic or Continuous Bleeding:** Can be done at any time, or soon after the bleeding starts.

**Interpretation and Histological Findings**

- **Evidence of Ovulation:** Characterised by secretory changes due to progesterone action on an oestrogen-primed endometrium. The earliest histological evidence is **subnuclear vacuolation** of the glandular epithelium, appearing 36–48 hours after ovulation.
- **Luteal Phase Defect (LPD):** Diagnosed when the histological dating of the endometrium lags behind the actual cycle date by at least 3 days (e.g., day 22 appearance on day 25 of the cycle). This lag must be proven in two consecutive cycles.
- **Anovulatory Cycle (DUB):** Shows a proliferative or hyperplastic endometrium (e.g., 'Swiss cheese' pattern) with complete absence of secretory changes.
- **Endometrial Tuberculosis:** Typical findings include epithelioid cells and giant cell systems. For TB, one part of the specimen is sent in formalin for histopathology, and another part in normal saline for culture (Löwenstein-Jensen media), AFB microscopy, guinea pig inoculation, and PCR (CBNAAT).
- **Endometrial Carcinoma:** Accurate in 91–99% of cases when evaluating postmenopausal bleeding. Findings range from atypical complex hyperplasia to frank adenocarcinoma.

**Complications and Limitations**

- **Blind Procedure Limitations:** Blind sampling (Pipelle or D&C) can miss focal intrauterine pathology, such as endometrial polyps or submucous fibroids, in 10–20% of women.
- **Asherman’s Syndrome:** Vigorous or overzealous sharp curettage can destroy the basal layer of the endometrium, leading to uterine synechiae (adhesions), which presents as secondary amenorrhea or hypomenorrhea.
- **Future Obstetric Risks:** Damage to the endometrium increases the risk of morbidly adherent placenta in subsequent pregnancies.
- **Surgical Complications:** Risks associated with D&C include uterine perforation, brisk haemorrhage, cervical tear, and flaring up of pelvic infections.

8. Adenomyosis.
**DEFINITION** Adenomyosis, also known as endometriosis interna, is a condition characterized by the ingrowth of both the glandular and stromal components of the endometrium directly into the myometrium. It can manifest as a diffuse or focal lesion.

**ETIOPATHOGENESIS AND RISK FACTORS**

- **Patient Profile:** It is predominantly seen in parous women (90%) and usually manifests in women over the age of 40 (80%).
- **Causes:** The exact cause is unknown, but it is heavily associated with repeated childbirths, vigorous uterine curettage, and excess estrogenic effects.
- **Associated Pathology:** It coexists with pelvic endometriosis in about 40% of cases, and nearly 60% of women have coexistent uterine myomas or endometrial hyperplasia.
- **Pathogenesis:** The disease develops when endometrial glands and stroma breach the endometrial-myometrial interface (EMI). Because the submucosa is normally absent, the glands come into direct contact with the myometrium, forming deep nests. This disruption of the inner myometrial "junctional zone" (JZ) is thought to be triggered by trauma to the deeper endometrium (e.g., repeated curettage), genetic predisposition, or altered immune responses.

**PATHOLOGY**

- **Gross (Naked Eye) Appearance:** There is a diffuse, symmetrical enlargement of the uterus, typically not exceeding the size of a 12–14 weeks pregnant uterus (a large orange), with the posterior wall often more thickened than the anterior wall. Unlike a uterine fibroid, there is **no capsule** surrounding the growth. The cut surface presents a characteristic dark, trabeculated appearance with visible cystic dark spaces and hemorrhagic blood spots.
- **Microscopic Appearance:** Histological examination reveals endometrial-like glandular tissue surrounded by stromal cells deep within the myometrium, accompanied by marked myometrial hyperplasia and hemosiderin-laden phagocytic cells.

**CLINICAL FEATURES** Approximately 50% of patients remain asymptomatic, with the condition discovered incidentally on histological examination. When symptomatic, the classic triad includes:

- **Menorrhagia (70%):** The most common symptom, caused by an increased uterine cavity surface area, associated endometrial hyperplasia, and inadequate uterine contractions due to the distortion of the junctional zone.
- **Dysmenorrhea (30%):** Patients experience progressively increasing, colicky secondary dysmenorrhea. The pain often begins a week before menstruation and characteristically continues even after the period is over, primarily due to retrograde uterine contractions, local tissue edema, and prostaglandins.
- **Dyspareunia and Urinary Frequency:** Arise due to the enlarged, tender, and bulky uterus.
- **Infertility and Miscarriage:** There is a higher incidence of infertility and repeated pregnancy loss due to abnormal subendometrial myometrial function, retrograde contractions, impaired sperm transport, and reduced endometrial receptivity.

**SIGNS**

- Abdominal examination may reveal a hypogastric mass occupying the midline, but it rarely exceeds 14 weeks in gestational size.
- Bimanual pelvic examination typically reveals a uniformly, diffusely enlarged uterus that is **soft and tender** (a key differentiating feature from a fibroid, which is usually firm, irregularly enlarged, and nontender).

**DIAGNOSIS AND INVESTIGATIONS**

- **Transvaginal Sonography (TVS) & Color Doppler:** Findings include a heterogeneous echotexture, a hypoechoic myometrium with multiple small cysts giving a characteristic "honeycomb appearance," increased vascularity within the myometrium, and an ill-defined endometrial echo.
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** MRI is highly accurate and is the gold standard for differentiating adenomyosis from uterine fibroids. Diagnostic criteria include an asymmetrical thickness of the uterine walls (posterior > anterior), striated projections extending from the endometrium, small myometrial hypoechoic cysts, and crucially, a **thickened Junctional Zone (JZ) $\geq$ 12 mm**.

**TREATMENT** Medical therapy for adenomyosis is often unsatisfactory, making surgery the definitive treatment in most progressive cases.

- **Medical Management:**
    - NSAIDs are commonly prescribed to control pain and bleeding.
    - Hormone therapy (e.g., progestins or cyclic estrogen-progestin) generally offers little benefit.
    - However, the Levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine system (LNG-IUS) or a Danazol-loaded IUD has been found to significantly improve symptoms of menorrhagia and dysmenorrhea with minimal side effects.
- **Conservative Surgery:** Options for women desiring to retain their uterus include adenomyomectomy, uterine mass reduction (via laparotomy or laparoscopy), or Uterine Artery Embolization (UAE) to relieve symptoms.
- **Definitive Surgery:** Total hysterectomy (with or without bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy) is the definitive and most appropriate treatment for parous, aged women who have completed their families.

9. Screening for cervical cancer.
**SCREENING FOR CERVICAL CANCER**

**Introduction and Objectives** The primary objective of screening is to detect precancerous lesions (Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia - CIN) at an early, treatable stage and to reduce the incidence and mortality from cervical cancer. Cervical cancer has a long preinvasive phase, taking 10–15 years for the progression of a premalignant condition to invasive carcinoma. The widespread use of the Pap smear has effectively reduced the incidence of cervical cancer by 80% and the associated mortality by 70%.

**Screening Modalities** **1. Exfoliative Cervical Cytology (Pap Smear):**

- **Conventional Pap Smear:** Considered the gold standard for screening. The cervix is exposed using a Cusco’s bivalve speculum without the use of lubricants, as lubricants can distort cell morphology. A sample is collected using an Ayre’s spatula from the ectocervix (squamocolumnar junction) and an endocervical brush from the endocervix. The cells are evenly spread on a glass slide and immediately fixed with 95% ethyl alcohol to prevent drying artifacts.
- **Liquid-Based Cytology (LBC):** This superior, newer technique uses a plastic broom to sample both the ectocervix and endocervix simultaneously. The broom head is rinsed or broken off into a transport liquid medium. Cells are separated by centrifugation to prepare thin-layer smears.
- _Advantages of LBC:_ It provides an even distribution of abnormal cells without clustering, eliminates obscuring blood/mucus, reduces false-positive/false-negative or unsatisfactory smears, and allows for simultaneous High-Risk HPV-DNA testing from the same liquid medium.

**2. HPV-DNA Testing:**

- Human Papillomavirus (HPV) infection, specifically high-risk oncogenic types (16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, 58), is the causative agent in >99% of CIN and invasive cancers.
- Screening detects high-risk HPV-DNA using Hybrid Capture-2 (HC-2) or Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) techniques. It is highly sensitive and used either as a primary screening method or as an adjunct (co-testing) to cytology.

**3. Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid (VIA):**

- Used predominantly in low-and-middle-income countries (LMICs) as a "downstaging screening" or "see and treat" method.
- The cervix is inspected 30–60 seconds after the application of 5% acetic acid. Acetic acid acts as a mucolytic agent and coagulates high nuclear protein found in CIN.
- Women exhibiting opaque "acetowhite" lesions are referred for colposcopic evaluation or immediate treatment like cryotherapy.

**Screening Guidelines & Schedule (ACOG, FOGSI, ACS)**

- **Initiation:** Initial screening should begin at 25 years (30 years by the Government of India), regardless of the onset of sexual activity.
- **Age 25–29 years:** Pap testing (cytology alone) every 3 years.
- **Age 30–65 years:** Co-testing (Pap smear + High-Risk HPV testing) every 5 years OR Pap testing alone every 3 years.
- **Age > 65 years:** Routine screening can be discontinued provided the woman has had normal negative testing over the past 10 years and no history of high-grade dysplasia (CIN 2 or 3) in the past 20 years.
- **Exceptions (High-Risk Groups):** Women with HIV seropositivity, immunosuppression, or a history of in-utero DES exposure require annual screening.
- **Post-Hysterectomy:** Follow-up Pap screening is not needed if a total hysterectomy was performed for benign conditions. Women with a subtotal hysterectomy require usual screening. Vault smears are indicated if hysterectomy was done for persistent high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSIL).
- **Pregnancy:** Cervical cytology screening should be a routine part of antenatal care.

**The Bethesda System (TBS) for Cytology Reporting (2014)** Cervical cytology is universally reported using The Bethesda System.

- **Specimen Adequacy:** Classified as Satisfactory or Unsatisfactory (due to scant cellularity, contamination, or absence of endocervical cells).
- **Squamous Cell Abnormalities:**
    - **ASC-US:** Atypical Squamous Cells of Undetermined Significance.
    - **ASC-H:** Atypical Squamous Cells, cannot exclude High-grade lesion.
    - **LSIL:** Low-grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion (Correlates with HPV infection and CIN 1).
    - **HSIL:** High-grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion (Correlates with CIN 2, CIN 3, and Carcinoma in situ).
    - **SCC:** Squamous Cell Carcinoma.
- **Glandular Cell Abnormalities:** Atypical Glandular Cells (AGC), Adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS), and Adenocarcinoma.

**Triage and Management of Abnormal Screening Results**

- **ASC-US:** Reflex HPV testing is recommended. If the HPV test is positive, colposcopy is indicated. If negative, routine screening is resumed at 5 years.
- **LSIL:** In women >30, if both cytology and HPV testing are negative, repeat in 5 years. If HPV is positive, colposcopy and directed biopsy are performed. If HPV is negative but cytology is positive, repeat the cytology in 1 year.
- **ASC-H and HSIL:** All women must be evaluated immediately with colposcopy and directed biopsy, as the risk of underlying CIN 2/3 is high (up to 69% for HSIL).
- **AGC:** Carries a high risk of underlying endometrial or cervical neoplasia (3-17%). Requires immediate colposcopy accompanied by endocervical and endometrial sampling.

10. Ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome.
**OVARIAN HYPERSTIMULATION SYNDROME (OHSS)**

**Definition** Ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS) is an iatrogenic and potentially life-threatening complication of ovulation induction (superovulation), characterized by multiple follicular development and massive ovarian enlargement following human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) stimulation. It occurs most frequently with conception cycles and typically appears about 3 to 6 days after the ovulating dose of hCG is administered.

**Pathophysiology** The exact mechanism is poorly understood, but the core pathology involves increased capillary permeability due to the release of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and the activation of the nitric oxide (NO) system. A variety of chemical mediators, including cytokines, prorenin, and renin, are also stimulated following hCG administration. This increased permeability leads to the leakage of fluid from the peritoneal and ovarian surfaces (third spacing), which results in ascites, hypovolemia, oliguria, and electrolyte imbalance.

**Risk Factors**

- Young age (<30 years).
- Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome (PCOS).
- High serum Estradiol (E2) >2,500 pg/mL.
- Rapidly rising serum E2 levels (>75% rise from the previous day).
- Ovarian ‘necklace sign’ on ultrasonography (USG), indicating multiple small follicles.
- hCG administration and the occurrence of multiple pregnancy.

**Clinical Features and Classification** Depending on the severity, OHSS is classified into four clinical grades:

- **Mild (10–20%):** Presents with abdominal bloating and mild pain. The ovarian size is <5 cm.
- **Moderate (5–10%):** Characterized by nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea. Ultrasonographic (USG) evidence of ascites is present, and ovarian size ranges from 8–12 cm, but laboratory values remain normal.
- **Severe (1–2%):** Manifests with clinical ascites and sometimes hydrothorax. Complications include Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS) and hemoconcentration (hematocrit >45%, WBC count >15,000/mL). Patients present with oliguria alongside raised serum creatinine, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, liver dysfunction, and anasarca. The ovarian size is >12 cm.
- **Critical (Rare):** Presents with tense ascites and severe end-organ dysfunction. Laboratory findings reveal a hematocrit >55%, WBC count >25,000/mL, and oliguria with serum creatinine >1.6 mg/dL. Life-threatening complications such as renal failure and thromboembolic events may occur. Ovarian size remains >12 cm.

**Investigations**

- Transvaginal Sonography (TVS) is used to assess ovarian volume, visualize multiple follicles, and evaluate the extent of ascites.
- Laboratory workup includes a complete hemogram (to check for hemoconcentration), liver function tests (LFTs), renal function tests (RFTs), serum electrolytes, and a coagulation profile.
- Electrocardiography (ECG) and strict urine output monitoring are required.
- A chest X-ray (with pelvic shielding) and oxygen saturation monitoring are indicated if there is respiratory compromise.

**Prevention** While complete prevention may not be possible, severity can be significantly reduced using the following strategies:

- Utilizing GnRH antagonists for pituitary downregulation.
- Employing a low starting dose of gonadotropins in high-risk women.
- Administering metformin cotreatment during gonadotropin stimulation in women with PCOS.
- Ensuring close monitoring of superovulation cycles using TVS and serum estradiol estimation.
- **Coasting:** Withholding the ovulatory dose of hCG in susceptible cases, cancelling the treatment cycle, or delaying the hCG injection.
- Performing follicular aspiration after hCG administration and cryopreserving oocytes or embryos for future use.
- Using dopamine agonists like Cabergoline, which is effective as it inhibits the action of VEGF.
- Aspiration of immature oocytes for in vitro maturation (IVM).
- Providing luteal phase support with progesterone instead of hCG.

**Management** The management of OHSS is primarily conservative and supportive. Moderate and severe cases necessitate hospital admission.

- **Fluid Therapy:** Oral fluids are continued to prevent hemoconcentration and maintain renal perfusion. Intravenous normal saline at 150 mL/hour is administered when the hematocrit is >45%.
- **Volume Expanders:** Human albumin (50 mL of 25%) may be administered intravenously to correct hypovolemia, and the dose may be repeated.
- **Symptomatic Relief:** Abdominal paracentesis may be performed under USG guidance to relieve severe respiratory distress caused by ascites. Pain is managed with paracetamol or pethidine.
- **Intensive Care Management (ICM):** Severe cases require ICU admission to aggressively correct hypovolemia, electrolyte imbalances, metabolic acidosis, blood coagulopathy, and to manage specific complications like renal failure.
- **Surgical Intervention:** Surgery is rarely indicated in the management of OHSS.


11. Course of ureter and its relations.
**PELVIC URETER: COURSE, RELATIONS AND APPLIED ANATOMY**

**Introduction and Dimensions**

- **Extent:** The pelvic ureter is a retroperitoneal structure extending from its crossing over the pelvic brim down to its termination in the urinary bladder.
- **Dimensions:** It measures approximately 13 cm in length and has a diameter of 5 mm.

**Course and Anatomical Relations** The course of the pelvic ureter can be systematically traced from the pelvic brim to the bladder:

- **Entry into the Pelvis:** It enters the pelvis by crossing in front of the bifurcation of the common iliac artery, lying over the sacroiliac joint. At this level, it lies behind the root of the mesentery on the right side and behind the apex of the mesosigmoid on the left side.
- **Downward Course (Lateral Pelvic Wall):** It descends in direct contact with the peritoneum, lying anterior to the internal iliac artery. It lies behind the ovary and forms the posterior boundary of the ovarian fossa.
- **Forward Course (Pelvic Floor):** Upon reaching the level of the ischial spine, the ureter changes direction to course forwards and medially over the pelvic floor along the base of the broad ligament.
- **Relation to Uterine Artery:** As it travels medially, the ureter is crossed anteriorly (from above) by the uterine artery.
- **Relation to Cervix and Vagina:** The ureter enters the ureteric tunnel (tunnel of Wertheim) within the cardinal ligament and lies very close to the supravaginal part of the cervix, maintaining a distance of about 1.5 cm lateral to it. It then traverses a short distance over the anterior vaginal fornix.
- **Entry into Bladder:** Finally, it pierces the lateral angle of the urinary bladder and courses obliquely through the bladder wall for about 2 cm before opening into the base of the trigone.

**Normal Constrictions of the Pelvic Ureter** The pelvic ureter is comparatively constricted at three specific anatomical sites:

1. Where it crosses the pelvic brim.
2. Where it is crossed by the uterine artery.
3. In its intravesical part (where it traverses the bladder wall).

**Histological Structure** From outside inwards, the ureter consists of:

- **Outer Coat:** Fibers derived from the visceral layer of the pelvic fascia.
- **Muscle Coat:** Consists of three layers: outer and inner longitudinal layers, and an intermediate circular layer.
- **Mucous Layer:** Lined by transitional epithelium.

**Blood Supply**

- **Arterial:** It receives segmental blood supply from nearly all the visceral branches of the anterior division of the internal iliac artery, including the uterine, vaginal, vesical, middle rectal, and superior gluteal arteries.
- **Venous:** Drainage corresponds to the arterial supply.

**High-Yield Applied Anatomy (Surgical Importance)**

- **Surgical Identification:** During pelvic surgery, the ureter is identified by its pale glistening appearance, the presence of longitudinal vessels on its surface, and the elicitation of peristalsis upon mechanical stimulation.
- **Vulnerable Sites for Surgical Injury:** Because of its close proximity to the genital organs, the ureter is highly prone to injury (0.5-1% of pelvic operations) during procedures like abdominal hysterectomy. The common sites of injury are:
    1. At the infundibulopelvic ligament (where it runs parallel to the ovarian vessels).
    2. By the side of the cervix during clamping of the cardinal ligament and descending cervical artery.
    3. At the vaginal angle as it traverses over the anterior fornix.
    4. Deep in the pelvis, lateral to the uterosacral ligament.
    5. During pelvic peritonization, as the ureter lies in the posterior leaf of the peritoneum.

12. Hormonal contraception.
**HORMONAL CONTRACEPTION**

Hormonal contraception is one of the most effective and widely used reversible methods of family planning. They primarily involve the use of synthetic estrogens and progestins, or progestins alone, administered via various routes.

**CLASSIFICATION OF HORMONAL CONTRACEPTIVES**

1. **Oral:**
    - Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs) – Monophasic, biphasic, triphasic, extended use.
    - Progestin-Only Pills (POPs / Minipill).
    - Emergency (Postcoital) Pills.
    - Non-steroidal Pills (Centchroman).
2. **Parenteral (Injectables):** Depot Medroxyprogesterone Acetate (DMPA), Norethisterone Enanthate (NET-EN), Combined monthly injectables.
3. **Subdermal Implants:** Implanon/Nexplanon, Norplant-II (Jadelle).
4. **Devices/Rings:** Levonorgestrel-Intrauterine System (LNG-IUS), Vaginal rings.
5. **Transdermal Patch:** Delivers hormone directly through the skin.

---

### **1. COMBINED ORAL CONTRACEPTIVES (COCs)**

COCs are the most effective reversible method of contraception, containing a combination of estrogen and progestin.

- **Composition:** Estrogen is typically ethinyl estradiol (EE). The progestin component determines the "generation" of the pill (e.g., 2nd generation: Levonorgestrel; 3rd generation: Desogestrel, Gestodene; 4th generation: Drospirenone). Low-dose pills contain less than 50 µg of EE.
- **Mode of Action:**
    1. **Inhibition of Ovulation:** Synergistically act on the hypothalamo-pituitary axis. Estrogen suppresses FSH and GnRH (preventing follicular growth), while progestin suppresses the LH surge (preventing ovulation) via negative feedback.
    2. **Cervical Mucus Alteration:** Progestin makes the cervical mucus thick, viscid, and scanty, preventing sperm penetration.
    3. **Endometrial Hypoplasia:** Causes stromal edema and regression of glands, making the endometrium non-receptive to blastocyst implantation.
    4. **Tubal Motility:** Alters tubal transport and motility.
- **Dosage Schedule:** 1 tablet daily, preferably at bedtime, for 21 days starting on day 1 of the cycle, followed by a 7-day hormone-free interval (or 7 iron/dummy pills).
- **Missed Pill Rule:** If one pill is missed (late up to 24 hours), it should be taken immediately, and the next pill taken at the scheduled time.
- **Non-Contraceptive Health Benefits:** Regulates menstrual cycle, reduces dysmenorrhea (40%), menorrhagia (50%), and premenstrual syndrome (PMS). It protects against pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), ectopic pregnancy, endometriosis, functional ovarian cysts, benign breast disease, and reduces the risk of endometrial and ovarian cancers by 50%.
- **Side Effects:**
    - _Minor:_ Nausea, vomiting, breast tenderness (mastalgia), weight gain, breakthrough bleeding, and acne.
    - _Major:_ Venous thromboembolism (VTE), arterial thrombosis (MI, stroke), cholestatic jaundice, and a slight transient increase in breast cancer risk.
- **Absolute Contraindications (WHO Category 4):** History of arterial/venous thrombosis, severe hypertension, active liver disease/jaundice, estrogen-dependent neoplasm (breast cancer), undiagnosed vaginal bleeding, pregnancy, and breastfeeding within 6 weeks postpartum.
- **Drug Interactions:** Drugs that induce liver microsomal enzymes (e.g., rifampicin, barbiturates, phenytoin, carbamazepine) accelerate hormone clearance and reduce COC efficacy, requiring backup contraception.

---

### **2. PROGESTIN-ONLY PILLS (POPs / MINIPILL)**

POPs contain very low doses of progestin (e.g., Levonorgestrel 75 µg, Desogestrel 75 µg) and are devoid of estrogen.

- **Mode of Action:** Predominantly works by thickening cervical mucus to impede sperm penetration and causing endometrial atrophy. Ovulation is inhibited in only about 2% of cases (though Desogestrel inhibits ovulation in 97% of cycles).
- **Dosage:** Must be taken daily continuously from the first day of the cycle, at the exact same time every day, with no break.
- **Advantages:** Eliminates estrogen-related side effects. It does not affect breast milk composition or volume, making it the ideal **"Lactation Pill"**. Can be used in women with hypertension, diabetes, epilepsy, and smoking habits.
- **Disadvantages:** Irregular vaginal bleeding/spotting is common. Strict timing is crucial; a delay of >3 hours (or >12 hours for Desogestrel) requires backup contraception for 48 hours.

---

### **3. INJECTABLE PROGESTINS**

Long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs) administered parenterally.

- **Preparations:**
    - _DMPA (Depot Medroxyprogesterone Acetate):_ 150 mg deep IM every 3 months. A sub-Q preparation (Sayana Press 104 mg) is also available for 3-monthly use.
    - _NET-EN (Norethisterone Enanthate):_ 200 mg IM every 2 months.
- **Mode of Action:** Strongly inhibits the midcycle LH peak (blocking ovulation), thickens cervical mucus, and causes endometrial atrophy.
- **Advantages:** Highly effective (Failure rate 0-0.3 HWY), safe in lactation, prevents endometrial cancer, and reduces sickle cell pain crises. Menorrhagia is effectively controlled.
- **Disadvantages:** Irregular bleeding followed by amenorrhea, weight gain, delayed return of fertility (6-12 months after discontinuation), and reversible loss of bone mineral density (BMD) with long-term use.

---

### **4. SUBDERMAL IMPLANTS**

Single or multiple hormone-releasing rods placed subdermally in the non-dominant arm.

- **Preparations:**
    - _Nexplanon (Implanon):_ Single rod containing 68 mg Etonogestrel, effective for 3 years.
    - _Norplant-II (Jadelle):_ Two rods containing Levonorgestrel, effective for 3 years.
- **Mode of Action:** Inhibits ovulation in 90% of cycles, thickens cervical mucus, and causes endometrial atrophy.
- **Advantages:** Extremely high efficacy (Pearl Index 0.01), considered as "reversible sterilization", rapid return of fertility upon removal.
- **Disadvantages:** Frequent irregular spotting, bleeding, or amenorrhea. Efficacy is reduced by enzyme-inducing drugs.

---

### **5. EMERGENCY CONTRACEPTION (POSTCOITAL PILLS)**

Used to prevent pregnancy after unprotected sexual intercourse (UPSI), condom rupture, or missed pills.

- **Levonorgestrel (LNG):** 1.5 mg single dose (or 0.75 mg two doses, 12 hours apart). Most effective if taken within 72 hours of UPSI, but can be used up to 120 hours. Failure rate is 0.6-2.6%.
- **Ulipristal Acetate (UPA):** 30 mg single oral dose. It is a Selective Progesterone Receptor Modulator (SPRM) taken within 120 hours. Superior to LNG as it delays ovulation even just before the LH surge and inhibits implantation.
- **Mifepristone (RU 486):** 100 mg single dose within 5 days of UPSI. Acts as an antiprogesterone to prevent implantation.
- **Yuzpe Regimen:** High-dose combined oral pills (0.25 mg LNG + 50 µg EE) – 2 tablets taken stat and 2 tablets after 12 hours. Often causes significant nausea and vomiting.

---

### **6. CENTCHROMAN (SAHELI / CHHAYA)**

- **Description:** A nonsteroidal, non-hormonal, oral contraceptive containing Ormeloxifene (30 mg).
- **Mode of Action:** Acts as a Selective Estrogen Receptor Modulator (SERM). It possesses strong anti-estrogenic and weak estrogenic activity. It creates asynchrony between the developing zygote and the endometrium, thereby preventing implantation. It **does not** inhibit ovulation.
- **Dosage:** One tablet (30 mg) twice a week for the first 3 months, followed by once a week thereafter.
- **Advantages:** Highly safe, devoid of cardiovascular/metabolic side effects, no teratogenicity, safe during lactation, and provides immediate return of fertility upon discontinuation.





13. Palm coien classification.
**PALM-COEIN CLASSIFICATION OF ABNORMAL UTERINE BLEEDING (FIGO - 2011)**

**INTRODUCTION** The PALM-COEIN classification is a universally accepted, systematic system introduced by the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) in 2011 to categorize the causes of Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (AUB) in non-gravid women of reproductive age. It replaces older, ambiguous terminologies like Dysfunctional Uterine Bleeding (DUB).

The system divides the etiologies of AUB into **nine main categories**, grouped broadly into two halves:

1. **PALM:** Structural or histological causes (diagnosed by imaging or histopathology).
2. **COEIN:** Non-structural systemic causes.

---

### **A. STRUCTURAL CAUSES (PALM)**

These are structural abnormalities that can be evaluated via imaging techniques (like USG or MRI) or histopathological examination.

- **P - Polyp (AUB-P):** Includes endometrial or cervical polyps. Diagnosis is generally made via transvaginal sonography (TVS), saline infusion sonography (SIS), or hysteroscopy.
- **A - Adenomyosis (AUB-A):** Endometrial glands and stroma extending into the myometrium. Diagnosis is primarily made by sonography, though MRI is considered superior.
- **L - Leiomyoma (AUB-L):** Uterine fibroids. Because myomas are common and may not always be the cause of bleeding, this category is further subdivided into two critical groups based on their relationship to the endometrial cavity:
    - **$AUB-L_{SM}$ (Submucosal myoma):** At least one submucosal fibroid affecting the cavity.
    - **$AUB-L_O$ (Other myoma):** Myomas (like intramural or subserosal) that do not affect the endometrial cavity.
- **M - Malignancy and Hyperplasia (AUB-M):** Includes atypical endometrial hyperplasia and female genital tract cancers (vulva, vagina, cervix, endometrium, uterus, tubes, and ovary). Malignancy is observed in about 4.5% of postmenopausal women presenting with bleeding symptoms.

---

### **B. NON-STRUCTURAL CAUSES (COEIN)**

These represent underlying systemic, endocrine, or iatrogenic abnormalities that cannot be localized through standard anatomical imaging.

- **C - Coagulopathy (AUB-C):** Encompasses systemic bleeding disorders. Includes von Willebrand disease (most common), prothrombin deficiency, hemophilias A & B, idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP), leukemia, platelet deficiency, and inherited coagulopathies (factors V, VII, X, XI, XIII). It also includes women presenting with AUB while on anticoagulation therapy (heparin or LMWH). _High Yield:_ Nearly 20% of adolescent girls presenting with AUB have an underlying coagulation disorder.
- **O - Ovulatory Dysfunction (AUB-O):** Manifests primarily as anovulatory cycles, resulting in continued, unopposed estrogenic stimulation of the endometrium without the stabilizing effect of progesterone. Frequently seen at the extremes of reproductive age (adolescents and perimenopausal women) due to dysfunction of the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis, as well as in conditions like PCOS, hyperprolactinemia, and thyroid dysfunction.
- **E - Endometrial (AUB-E):** AUB occurring in the context of regular ovulatory cycles. It is thought to result from primary dysfunction of local endometrial hemostasis. Factors include abnormal endometrial production of prostaglandins (e.g., deficiency of vasoconstrictive $PGF_{2\alpha}$ or excessive production of vasodilatory $PGE_2$ and $PGI_2$). Chronic inflammatory changes or subclinical infection (e.g., _Chlamydia trachomatis_) also fall into this category.
- **I - Iatrogenic (AUB-I):** Bleeding triggered by medical interventions or devices. Common causes include breakthrough bleeding from combined oral contraceptives (COCs), erratic use of steroidal pills or vaginal rings, and bleeding associated with Intrauterine Contraceptive Devices (IUCDs) or the Levonorgestrel Intrauterine System (LNG-IUS).
- **N - Not yet classified / Not otherwise specified (AUB-N):** Rare or poorly defined causes that do not fit into the other categories. This includes trauma or foreign bodies in the genital tract.

---

### **NOTATION SYSTEM**

The FIGO classification utilizes a binary subscript system to denote the presence (1) or absence (0) of each specific etiology, allowing for accurate documentation of multiple co-existing pathologies.

- **Example 1:** A patient whose abnormal bleeding is caused strictly by adenomyosis is documented as: **AUB: $P_0 A_1 L_0 M_0 – C_0 O_0 E_0 I_0 N_0$**.
- **Example 2:** A patient presenting with AUB due to both an endometrial polyp and ovulatory dysfunction is documented as: **AUB: $P_1 A_0 L_0 M_0 – C_0 O_1 E_0 I_0 N_0$**.


14. Red degeneration of fibroid.
**RED DEGENERATION OF FIBROID (CARNEOUS DEGENERATION)**

**INTRODUCTION AND DEFINITION** Red degeneration, also known as carneous degeneration, is a specific type of secondary change that predominantly affects a large uterine fibroid. It characteristically occurs during the second half of pregnancy and the puerperium, affecting approximately 5–10% of fibroids in this state. Because partial recovery of the tissues is possible, this condition is also referred to as necrobiosis.

**ETIOPATHOGENESIS**

- **Cause:** The exact cause remains unknown, but it is highly probable to be vascular in origin, arising from circulatory inadequacy as the tumor rapidly grows during pregnancy.
- **Role of Infection:** Infection does **not** play any role in the pathogenesis of red degeneration.

**PATHOLOGY**

- **Gross (Naked Eye) Appearance:** On cut section, the tumor exhibits dark areas with a characteristic "raw-beef" appearance. It often contains cystic spaces within it. The tissue frequently emits a fishy odor, which is attributed to the presence of fatty acids. The distinct red coloration is imparted by the presence of hemolyzed red blood cells and hemoglobin within the tissue.
- **Microscopic Appearance:** Histological examination shows clear evidence of cellular necrosis. The blood vessels within the fibroid are found to be thrombosed; however, actual extravasation of blood into the tissues is unlikely.

**CLINICAL FEATURES** The patient usually presents in the later months of pregnancy or during the postpartum period with the following features:

- **Pain:** Acute, severe pain in the lower abdomen over the site of the fibroid.
- **Constipation:** May be present as an associated symptom.
- **Signs:** Localized tenderness and marked rigidity over the tumor mass are the hallmark signs on abdominal examination.

**DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS** The acute abdominal presentation of red degeneration can mimic several other acute obstetric and surgical emergencies, including:

- Acute appendicitis.
- Twisted ovarian tumor/cyst.
- Disturbed ectopic pregnancy.
- Abruptio placentae.
- Acute cholecystitis.
- Chorioamnionitis and labor.

**INVESTIGATIONS**

- **Blood Count:** Characteristically reveals leukocytosis.
- **Ultrasonography (USG) & Color Doppler:** USG confirms the diagnosis of the fibroid with certainty, and Color Doppler is helpful in evaluating the vascularity and central degenerative changes.
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** MRI is highly accurate for diagnosing the condition, determining the exact dimensions and location of the fibroid, assessing its relation to placental implantation, and identifying the specific type of degeneration.

**MANAGEMENT** The fundamental principle of managing red degeneration during pregnancy is strictly **conservative**. Surgery is actively discouraged.

- **Medical/Conservative Management:**
    - The patient must be put to strict bed rest.
    - Adequate analgesics and sedatives are frequently given to relieve the acute pain.
    - Antibiotics (e.g., Ampicillin 500 mg capsule thrice daily for 7 days) are often prescribed.
    - _Prognosis with medical therapy:_ The acute symptoms are usually self-limiting and tend to clear off completely within 10 days of conservative management.
- **Surgical Management:**
    - There is no need for laparotomy in the vast majority of cases.
    - **Contraindication:** Myomectomy during pregnancy is preferably avoided because it carries an extremely high risk of profuse, uncontrollable hemorrhage during the operation.
    - **Exception:** Surgical intervention (removal) is occasionally indicated only if a pedunculated subserous fibroid undergoes torsion.


15. Bartholin’s abscess.
**BARTHOLIN’S ABSCESS**

**INTRODUCTION AND PATHOGENESIS** Bartholin’s abscess is the direct end result of acute bartholinitis. The pathogenic process involves the blockage of the Bartholin's duct due to fibrosis, which causes the inflammatory exudates to become pent up inside, leading to the formation of an abscess. If left untreated, the abscess may spontaneously burst through the lower vaginal wall, leaving behind an open sinus tract with periodic discharge.

**ETIOLOGICAL AGENTS** The infection is typically polymicrobial.

- While _Gonococcus_ is often suspected, other pyogenic organisms are more commonly involved.
- Common pathogens include _Escherichia coli_, _Staphylococcus_, _Streptococcus_, _Chlamydia trachomatis_, _Bacteroides_, and _Peptostreptococcus_.

**CLINICAL FEATURES** **Symptoms:**

- Intense local pain and discomfort in the vulvar region.
- The pain is usually severe enough that the patient cannot walk or even sit.
- Fever is often an associated constitutional symptom.

**Signs:**

- A unilateral, tender swelling is present beneath the posterior half of the labium majus, expanding medially towards the posterior part of the labium minus.
- The overlying skin appears distinctly red and edematous.

**MANAGEMENT** The treatment protocol involves both medical and surgical approaches depending on the stage of the abscess:

**1. Conservative and Medical Management:**

- Complete rest is imposed.
- Pain is relieved by administering appropriate analgesics and advising daily sitz baths.
- Systemic antibiotics are instituted, such as Ampicillin 500 mg orally every 8 hours, or Tetracycline if a _Chlamydia_ infection is suspected.

**2. Surgical Management (Definitive):**

- **Incision and Drainage (I&D):** The abscess must be drained at the earliest opportunity before it undergoes spontaneous rupture.
- **Catheter Drainage:** Catheter drainage of the abscess cavity following the incisional drainage is also a recognized method of treatment.

**3. Management of Recurrent Cases:**

- In cases of recurrent Bartholin’s abscess, complete surgical excision of the Bartholin's gland and its duct is the definitive treatment.
- This excision must strictly be performed during the quiescent (non-infected) phase after the acute infection has been completely controlled. Excision is particularly indicated for persistent or recurrent infections, especially when they occur in women beyond the age of 40.

16. Tests for ovulation.
**TESTS FOR OVULATION**

The various methods used in clinical practice to detect ovulation are broadly classified into three categories: Indirect (presumptive), Direct, and Conclusive evidences.

### **A. INDIRECT (PRESUMPTIVE) EVIDENCES**

These are the most commonly used methods in clinical practice.

**1. Menstrual History**

- A history of regular normal menstrual blood loss between the ages of 20–35 years is highly suggestive of ovulatory cycles.
- Presence of mid-menstrual pain (Mittelschmerz syndrome) or spotting.
- Presence of premenstrual syndrome (PMS) or primary dysmenorrhea.

**2. Basal Body Temperature (BBT) Charting**

- **Principle:** Progesterone, secreted after ovulation, is thermogenic (primarily due to an increase in norepinephrine production).
- **Procedure:** The patient records her oral temperature daily upon waking up, before getting out of bed.
- **Observation:** A **"biphasic pattern"** indicates ovulation. The body temperature rises by 0.5–1°F (0.2–0.5°C) following ovulation and sustains throughout the second half of the cycle. A monophasic pattern indicates anovulation.

**3. Hormonal Estimation**

- **Serum Progesterone:** Estimation is done on day 8 and day 21 of a 28-day cycle. A rise in value from $<1 \text{ ng/mL}$ in the follicular phase to $>5-6 \text{ ng/mL}$ in the mid-luteal phase (day 21) strongly suggests adequate ovulation and luteal function.
- **Serum LH:** Daily midcycle estimation to detect the LH surge. Ovulation occurs about 34–36 hours after the onset of the LH surge, and 10–12 hours after the LH peak.
- **Urinary LH:** Over-the-counter LH kits detect the midcycle LH surge in urine. Ovulation usually occurs within 14–24 hours (and almost always within 48 hours) of colour change.
- **Serum Estradiol:** Shows a midcycle peak approximately 24–36 hours prior to ovulation.

**4. Sonography (Transvaginal - TVS)**

- Serial TVS during the midcycle can precisely track the growing Graafian follicle until it reaches its mature preovulatory diameter of 18–20 mm.
- **Signs of recent ovulation:** Disappearance or collapse of the dominant follicle and the presence of free fluid in the pouch of Douglas.

**5. Endometrial Biopsy (Endometrial Sampling)**

- **Timing:** Performed on the 21st–23rd day of the cycle (barrier contraception must be used in that cycle).
- **Observation:** Presence of secretory changes confirms ovulation. The earliest histological evidence is **subnuclear vacuolation** of the glandular epithelium, which appears 36–48 hours after ovulation.

**6. Cervical Mucus Study**

- **Fern Test:** The characteristic fern tree pattern of cervical mucus, seen in the oestrogen-dominated preovulatory phase, completely disappears after the 22nd day of an ovulatory cycle due to the action of progesterone.
- **Spinnbarkeit (Stretchability):** The stretchability of the mucus is lost following ovulation.

**7. Vaginal Cytology**

- Smears taken on day 25 or 26 show a shift of the Maturation Index (MI) to the left.
- The smear reveals characteristic progesterone effects: a preponderance of intermediate cells with folded edges (navicular cells) found in clumps against a dirty background.

---

### **B. DIRECT EVIDENCES**

**1. Laparoscopy**

- Direct visualization of the ovaries reveals a recent corpus luteum or the stigma of ovulation.
- Detection of the ovum from aspirated peritoneal fluid from the pouch of Douglas.

---

### **C. CONCLUSIVE EVIDENCE**

**1. Pregnancy**

- The occurrence of pregnancy is the surest and only conclusive proof of ovulation.


17. Hysteroscopy.
**HYSTEROSCOPY**

**INTRODUCTION** Hysteroscopy is an endoscopic procedure that allows direct visualization inside the uterine (endometrial) cavity with the aid of a fiberoptic telescope. It is considered the gold standard for visualizing the uterine cavity and tubal ostia and is used for both diagnostic and therapeutic (operative) purposes.

**INSTRUMENTS USED**

- **Telescope:** Rigid telescopes are commonly used. Diameters vary from 3–5 mm for diagnostic purposes to 5–7 mm for operative procedures. The viewing angle may be straight (0°) or fore-oblique (30°, 70°, or 90°). Flexible telescopes (can bend up to 110°) are advantageous for easy uterine entry and tubal cannulation.
- **Telescope Sheath:** A smaller sheath (5 mm) is used for diagnostic purposes, while a larger operative sheath (7–10 mm) with separate inflow and outflow channels is required for surgery.
- **Distending Media:** Used to separate the uterine walls to provide a panoramic view.
    - **Gas:** Carbon dioxide ($CO_2$) is safe and commonly used for diagnostic purposes, but visibility is poor in the presence of bleeding.
    - **Liquid:** Normal saline is used for both diagnostic and operative procedures (compatible with bipolar electrosurgery). Glycine is required if monopolar electrosurgery is used. A pressure of 50–70 mm Hg is maintained for adequate distension.
- **Accessory Instruments:** Scissors, grasping forceps, monopolar and bipolar electrodes (roller ball, cutting loop, and coagulation needle).
- **Imaging:** A high-resolution camera, monitor, and a high-intensity light source (Xenon or mercury halide) are used.

**TIMING AND PREPARATION**

- **Timing:** It should preferably be done in the postmenstrual (proliferative) phase when the endometrium is thin and the uterus is normal-sized.
- **Pretreatment:** For operative procedures like endometrial ablation or transcervical resection of the endometrium (TCRE), preoperative thinning of the endometrium for 4–6 weeks using Danazol, GnRH analogs, or progestogens is recommended.
- **Anesthesia:** Diagnostic hysteroscopy is done as an outpatient procedure under a paracervical block with 1% xylocaine. Operative hysteroscopy requires general or regional (spinal/epidural) anesthesia.

**INDICATIONS**

**1. Diagnostic Hysteroscopy:**

- **Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (AUB):** Evaluation of menorrhagia and postmenopausal bleeding.
- **Infertility:** Especially when associated with an abnormal hysterosalpingogram (filling defect, synechiae).
- **Müllerian Anomalies:** To diagnose arcuate, subseptate, septate, bicornuate, or didelphys uterus.
- **Recurrent Miscarriage:** To evaluate the cavity for submucous fibroids, polyps, or Asherman's syndrome.
- **Misplaced IUCD:** To locate and retrieve an intrauterine device with missing threads.
- **Others:** Chronic pelvic pain evaluation, visualizing the transformation zone (microcolpohysteroscopy).

**2. Operative (Therapeutic) Hysteroscopy:**

- **Polypectomy and Myomectomy:** Resection of endometrial polyps and submucous leiomyomas.
- **Adhesiolysis (Synechiolysis):** For treating intrauterine adhesions (Asherman's syndrome).
- **Endometrial Ablation/Resection (TCRE):** Using a laser, rollerball, or resectoscope to treat patients with dysfunctional uterine bleeding (DUB) as an alternative to hysterectomy.
- **Metroplasty:** Resection of a uterine septum (often under concurrent laparoscopic guidance).
- **Tubal Cannulation:** To release proximal tubal obstruction.
- **Sterilization:** Transcervical insertion of micro-inserts (Essure) into the tubal ostia, or destroying the interstitial portion of the tubes.

**RCOG LEVELS OF HYSTEROSCOPIC PROCEDURES (1994)**

- **Level 1 (Diagnostic):** Diagnostic hysteroscopy + targeted biopsy, removal of a simple polyp or IUCD.
- **Level 2 (Minor Operative):** Proximal tubal cannulation, minor adhesiolysis, removal of a pedunculated fibroid or large polyp.
- **Level 3 (Complex Operative):** Septum resection, major synechiolysis, TCRE, resection of submucous leiomyoma.

**CONTRAINDICATIONS**

- **Pelvic Infection (Acute PID):** Distension media can flush infected material through the fallopian tubes into the peritoneal cavity.
- **Pregnancy:** (Except to remove an IUCD with missing threads).
- **Cervical Cancer:** Risk of excessive bleeding from friable tissue.
- **Cardiopulmonary Disorders:** Patients are at high risk for fluid overload and pulmonary edema.
- **Severe Cervical Stenosis:** Increases the risk of cervical trauma/perforation.

**COMPLICATIONS** **1. Perioperative (Immediate) Complications:**

- **Distension Media-Related:** Fluid overload leading to pulmonary or cerebral edema. Use of glycine can cause ammonia toxicity, hyponatremia, and hypo-osmolar states. A fluid deficit of $\ge 500 \text{ mL}$ is alarming and requires strict monitoring. $CO_2$ gas can cause gas embolism.
- **Surgical Trauma:** Uterine perforation (most common), hemorrhage, and cervical tears.
- **Thermal Injury:** Electrosurgical or laser burns to adjacent intra-abdominal organs (bowel, bladder).

**2. Late Complications:**

- **Failure of Treatment:** Recurrence of AUB following TCRE.
- **Infectious/Obstructive:** Hematometra and pyometra secondary to cervical stenosis (especially if ablation is done too close to the internal os).
- **Syndromes:** Post-ablation tubal sterilization syndrome (PATSS).



18. Complications of hysterectomy.

**COMPLICATIONS OF HYSTERECTOMY**

Hysterectomy is a major surgical procedure. Its complications can be broadly classified into intraoperative and postoperative (immediate, late, and remote) complications.

**1. INTRAOPERATIVE COMPLICATIONS**

- **Hemorrhage:** Can occur due to slippage of ligatures or injury to major vessels.
- **Visceral Injury:** Accidental injury to the intestine, bladder, or ureter. Ureteric injury is particularly common during clamping of the cardinal ligament, infundibulopelvic ligament, or vaginal angles.
- **Anesthetic Hazards:** Include atelectasis, pulmonary edema, and embolism.

**2. POSTOPERATIVE COMPLICATIONS**

**A. Immediate Complications (Within first 24–48 hours)**

- **Hemorrhagic Shock (Hypovolemia):** Due to primary hemorrhage (slipping of the ligature, usually at the vaginal angle). This requires prompt resuscitation and resuturing under anesthesia.
- **Urinary Complications:**
    - **Retention of urine:** Very common, occurring due to pain, spasm of pelvic floor muscles, or tissue edema.
    - **Anuria:** May be prerenal (inadequate fluid replacement) or postrenal (bilateral ureteric obstruction or ligation).
    - **Cystitis:** Usually secondary to catheterization.

**B. Late Complications (Within days to weeks)**

- **Secondary Hemorrhage:** Typically occurs between 7 to 14 days postoperatively and is secondary to sepsis/infection at the vaginal vault. Internal bleeding from a sloughing uterine or ovarian artery is rare.
- **Infections and Pyrexia:** Fever may result from:
    - Vault cellulitis and pelvic abscess.
    - Abdominal wound infection or hematoma.
    - Pulmonary infections (atelectasis, pneumonia).
    - Peritonitis or septic thrombophlebitis.
- **Wound Complications:** Hematomas in the rectus sheath or pelvis, wound dehiscence (more common with vertical incisions in high-risk patients), and rarely, fatal necrotizing fasciitis.
- **Thromboembolic Disorders:** Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE) are significant risks following major pelvic surgery.
- **Urinary Incontinence & Fistulas:**
    - **True incontinence:** Manifesting 7–14 days after surgery is typically due to ischemic sloughing and necrosis of the bladder or ureters, leading to a Vesicovaginal Fistula (VVF) or Ureterovaginal Fistula.
    - **Overflow incontinence:** Due to prolonged overdistension of the bladder.
- **Gastrointestinal Dysfunction:** Paralytic ileus or mechanical intestinal obstruction due to post-surgical adhesions.

**C. Remote / Delayed Complications**

- **Vault Granulation:** More common when catgut is used, less with Vicryl.
- **Vault Prolapse:** Secondary vaginal vault prolapse can occur (often accompanied by an enterocele), though it is less common after abdominal hysterectomy compared to vaginal hysterectomy.
- **Incisional Hernia:** More frequent with midline vertical incisions than with low transverse incisions.
- **Sexual Dysfunction:** Dyspareunia due to vaginal shortening or vault scarring.
- **Psychological Symptoms:** Depression or altered body image.
- **Fallopian Tube Prolapse:** Prolapse of the fallopian tube through the vaginal vault (rare).

**3. SPECIFIC COMPLICATIONS BASED ON ROUTE/EXTENT OF SURGERY**

- **Radical Hysterectomy (Wertheim's Operation):** Carries higher morbidity including severe bladder dysfunction/atony (due to parasympathetic and sympathetic nerve damage), lymphocyst formation in the pelvis, lymphedema of the legs, and higher rates of ureteric (1%) and vesicovaginal (0.5%) fistulas.
- **Vaginal Hysterectomy:** Immediate specific risks include vault cellulitis, pelvic abscess, and a higher propensity for late secondary vault prolapse.


19. Levonorgestrel IUCD
**LEVONORGESTREL INTRAUTERINE CONTRACEPTIVE DEVICE (LNG-IUCD / LNG-IUS)**

**INTRODUCTION** The Levonorgestrel-Intrauterine System (LNG-IUS) is a highly effective, safe, and long-acting reversible contraceptive (LARC) device. It is a T-shaped device with a polydimethyl-siloxane membrane around its vertical stem that acts as a steroid reservoir to release levonorgestrel locally into the uterine cavity.

**TYPES AND PREPARATIONS** There are primarily four types of LNG-IUDs currently in use:

- **Mirena and Liletta (52 mg LNG):** These measure 32 mm x 32 mm and contain a total of 52 mg of levonorgestrel. They initially release 20 µg of LNG per day. Mirena is approved for 5 years and Liletta for 6 years, but both are likely effective for up to 7 years. The trailing strings for Mirena are tan, while Liletta's are blue.
- **Kyleena (19.5 mg LNG):** Smaller in size (28 mm x 30 mm), initially releases 17.5 µg LNG daily, and is effective for 5 years. Its trailing strings are blue.
- **Skyla (13.5 mg LNG):** Also smaller (28 mm x 30 mm), initially releases 14 µg LNG daily, and is effective for 3 years. Its trailing strings are tan.
- _Note:_ The smaller devices (Kyleena and Skyla) are particularly suitable for nulliparous women and cause lower rates of amenorrhea compared to the 52 mg devices. Skyla and Kyleena also feature a silver ring at the junction of their stem and arms to aid in imaging.

**MECHANISM OF ACTION** The LNG-IUS acts predominantly in the uterine cavity and has both pre- and post-fertilization effects. Its local actions include:

1. **Endometrial Suppression:** It induces strong, uniform suppression and atrophy of the endometrium, leading to stromal decidualization and endometrial cell inactivation. This prevents blastocyst implantation.
2. **Cervical Mucus Alteration:** The continuous release of progestin makes the cervical mucus very thick and scanty, which severely impedes sperm motility and prevents access to the upper genital tract.
3. **Tubal Motility:** It decreases tubal motility, further interfering with sperm and ovum transport.
4. **Anovulation:** While it primarily acts locally with minimal systemic absorption, it can cause anovulation and insufficient luteal phase activity in some women (anovulation is more common with the 52 mg device than the 13.5 mg device). The serum estradiol concentrations generally remain unaffected.

**CONTRACEPTIVE EFFICACY**

- The contraceptive efficacy of LNG-IUS is extremely high and is comparable to surgical sterilization.
- The failure (pregnancy) rate is **0.1 to 0.2 per 100 women years**.

**NON-CONTRACEPTIVE / THERAPEUTIC BENEFITS (HIGH YIELD)** The LNG-IUS is uniquely beneficial for many gynecological conditions and is often considered an alternative to surgical interventions.

- **Heavy Menstrual Bleeding (HMB) / DUB:** Recommended as a first-line therapy for HMB in the absence of structural abnormalities. It significantly reduces menstrual blood loss by 80–100% and is often termed a **"medical hysterectomy"**.
- **Dysmenorrhea and Pelvic Pain:** Markedly reduces severe dysmenorrhea, pelvic pain, and premenstrual tension syndrome (PMS).
- **Endometriosis and Adenomyosis:** Highly effective in treating the symptoms of endometriosis (especially rectovaginal) and adenomyosis.
- **Endometrial Hyperplasia and Carcinoma:** Protects the endometrium from hyperplasia. It can be used as a fertility-sparing treatment in early-stage endometrial carcinoma.
- **Hormone Replacement Therapy (HRT):** Provides excellent endometrial protection when combined with estrogen for HRT during the perimenopausal transition or in postmenopausal women.
- **Coagulopathies:** Successfully manages Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (AUB) in women with inherited bleeding disorders (like von Willebrand disease) or those on anticoagulation therapy.
- **Reduced Ectopic Risk:** The risk of ectopic pregnancy is significantly reduced (0.02 per 100 women years).

**CONTRAINDICATIONS** **Absolute General Contraindications:**

- Pregnancy or suspected pregnancy.
- Undiagnosed genital tract bleeding.
- Acute pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) or STIs current or within the last 3 months.
- Distortion of the uterine cavity (e.g., large fibroids >12 weeks size or congenital malformations).
- Postpartum/postabortal endometritis in the last 3 months or infected abortion.

**Specific Contraindications for LNG-IUS:**

- Active hepatic tumor or severe hepatocellular disease.
- Current breast cancer.
- Severe arterial disease.

**DISADVANTAGES AND COMPLICATIONS**

- **Menstrual Irregularities:** Spotting and irregular bleeding are common in the initial months. With the 52 mg device, nearly 50% of women develop amenorrhea and 25% develop oligomenorrhea after 24 months of use. While clinically harmless, this must be counseled beforehand to ensure patient compliance.
- **Expense:** It is relatively expensive compared to copper devices.
- **Expulsion and Malpositioning:** Like all IUDs, there is a risk of spontaneous expulsion or malpositioning over long durations, which may lead to method failure.
- **Uterine Perforation:** Rare (about 1 in 1,000 insertions), mostly occurring at the time of insertion, particularly in lactating women.
- **Systemic Progestin Effects:** Although systemic absorption is minimal, some women may experience minor progestogenic side effects like acne, mastalgia, or mood changes. Simple ovarian cysts may form but do not require surgical intervention.

**INSERTION TIMING AND TECHNIQUE**

- **Timing:** Can be inserted during the interval period (preferably 2-3 days after the menstrual period ends), immediately postabortal, immediately postpartum (within 10 minutes of placental expulsion or intracesarean), or during lactational amenorrhea.
- **Technique:** Insertion is an outpatient procedure done under strict aseptic "no-touch" techniques without anesthesia. The device is loaded into the inserter tube, passed through the cervix to the fundus, and released by pulling back the slider on the handle. The threads are trimmed leaving about 3 cm outside the external os.

20. CA-125

**CA-125 (CANCER ANTIGEN 125)**

**INTRODUCTION AND NATURE**

- CA-125 is a glycoprotein that serves as a highly significant serum tumor marker in gynecological oncology and practice.
- **Normal Value:** A value of more than 35 U/mL is generally considered suggestive of epithelial ovarian cancer.
- **Half-life:** The serum half-life of CA-125 is 20 days.

**CLINICAL APPLICATIONS** **1. In Ovarian Malignancy:**

- **Screening and Diagnosis:** CA-125 is primarily used for the screening of epithelial (nonmucinous) cancers of the ovary. For early diagnosis, multimodal screening combining serum CA-125 with Transvaginal Sonography (TVS) is employed.
- **Risk of Malignancy Index (RMI):** CA-125 is a vital parameter in calculating the RMI to triage adnexal masses. The formula is RMI = U × M × CA-125 (where U is the ultrasound score and M is menopausal status). An RMI value of >250 indicates a 75% risk of cancer and necessitates full staging laparotomy at a specialized oncology center.
- **Monitoring Therapy:** It is an essential marker for monitoring tumor response during chemotherapy. The serum level of CA-125 falls following successful surgical resection of the tumor or effective chemotherapy.
- **Prognostic Value:** Persistently elevated levels during or after treatment indicate bulky residual disease, tumor recurrence, or clones that are resistant to chemotherapy.
- **Advanced Screening Algorithms:** Longitudinal biomarker algorithms tracking CA-125 over time are now considered more important than a single predefined cut-off value. Additionally, combining CA-125 with the HE 4 (Human Epididymis 4 protein) biomarker is superior to any single marker for diagnosing and managing early-stage ovarian serous cancers.

**2. In Endometriosis:**

- A moderate elevation of serum CA-125 is typically noticed in patients with severe endometriosis.
- While not specific for initial diagnosis, it is highly helpful in assessing the therapeutic response, following up on proved cases, and detecting recurrence after medical or surgical therapy.

**3. In Other Gynecological Cancers:**

- **Endometrial Carcinoma:** An elevated level of CA-125 indicates advanced disease.
- **Fallopian Tube Cancer & Uterine Carcinosarcoma:** Serum CA-125 levels are frequently elevated and act as a useful marker to monitor the disease response to treatment.

**CONDITIONS ASSOCIATED WITH RAISED CA-125 (DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS)** A major limitation of CA-125 is that it is not a tumor-specific antigen; it can be elevated in a variety of malignant, benign, and physiological conditions.

- **Malignant Conditions:**
    - Gynecological: Epithelial ovarian tumors (serous and endometrioid), endometrial carcinoma, fallopian tube cancer, and cervical adenocarcinoma.
    - Non-Gynecological: Carcinomas of the breast, colon, pancreas, stomach, liver, and lung.
- **Benign and Physiological Conditions:**
    - Endometriosis and Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID).
    - Uterine fibroids (leiomyomata) and pelvic peritonitis.
    - Physiological states such as pregnancy and mid-menstruation.
    - Medical conditions like pancreatitis.
    - It may also be mildly elevated in about 1% of completely normal women.

21. Meig’s syndrome.

**MEIGS' SYNDROME**

**DEFINITION** Meigs' syndrome is a classic, high-yield clinical triad consisting of:

1. A benign solid ovarian tumor.
2. Ascites.
3. Right-sided hydrothorax (pleural effusion).

**ASSOCIATED OVARIAN PATHOLOGIES** While the syndrome is most classically associated with an **ovarian fibroma** (which is the most common benign solid tumor of the ovary), it can also be seen in association with other specific benign ovarian tumors. These include:

- Brenner tumor.
- Thecoma.
- Granulosa cell tumor.

_High-Yield Note:_ True Meigs' syndrome is relatively rare, observed in only about 1% of cases of ovarian fibromas. However, the isolated development of ascites is quite common in these tumors; nearly 50% of patients with an ovarian fibroma will develop ascites when the size of the tumor exceeds 6 cm.

**CLINICAL PRESENTATION** Patients typically present with a combination of gynecological, gastrointestinal, and respiratory symptoms:

- Presence of a firm, solid pelvic mass on bimanual or abdominal examination.
- Abdominal distension and fullness secondary to the accumulation of ascitic fluid.
- Respiratory embarrassment, shortness of breath, or dullness to percussion on the right side of the chest due to the right-sided hydrothorax.
- The exact pathophysiological mechanism responsible for the transudation of fluid causing the ascites and the right-sided pleural effusion remains unknown.

**DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS: PSEUDO-MEIGS' SYNDROME** It is critically important in an exam to differentiate true Meigs' syndrome from Pseudo-Meigs' syndrome.

- **Pseudo-Meigs' syndrome** is the term used when the exact same clinical manifestations (ascites and hydrothorax) occur in association with pelvic conditions _other_ than the specific benign ovarian tumors listed above.
- Common examples of conditions causing Pseudo-Meigs' syndrome include uterine leiomyomas (fibroids), malignant ovarian tumors, or other pelvic malignancies.

**MANAGEMENT AND PROGNOSIS**

- **Treatment:** The definitive management of Meigs' syndrome is strictly surgical. It involves the surgical excision of the offending ovarian tumor (e.g., oophorectomy or cystectomy depending on the patient's age and the specific tumor).
- **Prognosis and Hallmark Feature:** The most defining and remarkable characteristic of Meigs' syndrome is that there is a **spontaneous and complete remission** of both the ascites and the right-sided hydrothorax following the surgical removal of the ovarian tumor. Because the underlying tumors are benign, the long-term prognosis after surgery is excellent.


22. Fothergill’s operation.

**FOTHERGILL’S OPERATION (MANCHESTER OPERATION)**

**INTRODUCTION AND INDICATIONS** Fothergill's operation (also known as Manchester operation) is a restorative surgical procedure designed to correct uterine descent associated with cystocele and rectocele.

- **Primary Indications:** It is indicated when preservation of the uterus and reproductive function is desired, or when symptoms are primarily due to vaginal prolapse associated with elongation of the supravaginal cervix.

**COMPOSITE STEPS OF THE OPERATION** The operation consists of five principal components:

1. Preliminary Dilatation and Curettage (D&C).
2. Amputation of the cervix.
3. Plication of Mackenrodt's ligaments in front of the cervix.
4. Anterior colporrhaphy.
5. Colpoperineorrhaphy.

**DETAILED OPERATIVE STEPS**

- **Preliminary D&C:** The uterine sound provides an idea about the elongation of the cervix. Dilatation of the cervical canal facilitates the passage of sutures during the covering of the amputated cervix, ensures adequate uterine drainage, and prevents postoperative cervical stenosis. Curettage is done to remove unhealthy endometrium.
- **Incision and Dissection:** The initial steps mirror an anterior colporrhaphy up to pushing up the bladder. The anterior transverse incision is extended posteriorly across the posterior cervicovaginal junction, and the vaginal wall is dissected off from the cervix.
- **Securing Ligaments:** The Mackenrodt’s ligaments along with the descending cervical arteries are clamped at a higher level, cut, and replaced by a ligature (chromic catgut No. '1'). Any concurrent enterocele must be searched for and repaired.
- **Amputation of Cervix:** The cervix is amputated at the calculated level. If future reproduction is desired, a low amputation is performed.
- **Covering the Cervical Stump:** The posterior lip of the amputated cervix is covered by a vaginal flap using a **Sturmdorf suture** or by Bonney’s method.
- **Plication of Mackenrodt’s Ligaments (Fothergill's Stitch):** The cut ends of the Mackenrodt’s ligaments are sutured to the anterior surface of the cervix. This is typically done using **Fothergill’s stitch**, which shortens the ligaments, elevates the cervix to its normal position, and anteverts the uterus.
    - _Sequence of Fothergill's stitch:_ Vaginal skin at the Fothergill’s lateral point $\rightarrow$ Mackenrodt’s ligament $\rightarrow$ cervical tissue (outside inwards) $\rightarrow$ cervical tissue (inside outwards) $\rightarrow$ opposite Mackenrodt’s ligament $\rightarrow$ opposite vaginal skin.
- **Completion:** The pubocervical fascia is approximated (as in anterior colporrhaphy), redundant vaginal mucosa is excised, the vaginal margins are sutured, and finally, a posterior colpoperineorrhaphy is performed. Toileting of the vagina is done, an optional vaginal pack is placed, and a self-retaining catheter is introduced.

**COMPLICATIONS** The complications are broadly categorized into intraoperative, postoperative, and late:

- **During Operation:**
    - Hemorrhage.
    - Injury to the bladder and rectum.
- **Postoperative:**
    - Retention of urine or cystitis.
    - Hemorrhage (primary or secondary).
    - Infection.
- **Late Complications (Obstetric & Gynecological):**
    - Cervical stenosis leading to hematometra.
    - Infertility.
    - Cervical incompetency (leading to mid-trimester miscarriages).
    - Cervical dystocia during labor.
    - Dyspareunia.
    - Recurrence of the prolapse.
