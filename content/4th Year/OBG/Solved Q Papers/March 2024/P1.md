1.	Postpartum IUCD

**DIAGNOSIS**

- **Pre-eclampsia with severe features (Imminent Eclampsia / Acute Fulminant Pre-eclampsia)**.
- **Justification:** The patient is a primigravida (high-risk group) at 37 weeks of gestation, presenting with a systolic blood pressure $\ge$ 160 mm Hg and ominious symptoms (headache indicating central nervous system dysfunction, and vomiting indicating possible hepatic involvement or impending convulsions).

**INVESTIGATIONS**

**Maternal Investigations:**

- **Urine:** Urinalysis for proteinuria (Protein/Creatinine ratio $>0.3$ or dipstick $\ge$ 1+). Note: A 24-hour urine collection for protein may be done but should not delay delivery.
- **Hematological:** Complete Blood Count (CBC) including Hematocrit (to check for hemoconcentration) and Platelet count (to rule out thrombocytopenia $<100,000/\mu L$).
- **Liver Function Tests (LFTs):** Serum transaminases (AST, ALT), LDH (to rule out hepatocellular injury or HELLP syndrome), and serum bilirubin.
- **Renal Function Tests (RFTs):** Serum creatinine (value $\ge$ 1.1 mg/dL indicates severe disease) and Serum uric acid ($\ge$ 4.5 mg/dL).
- **Coagulation Profile:** Prothrombin Time (PT), Activated Partial Thromboplastin Time (APTT), and Fibrinogen (indicated if platelet count is $<100,000/\mu L$ or liver enzymes are raised).
- **Ophthalmoscopic Examination:** To check for retinal spasm, edema, exudates, hemorrhages, or papilledema.

**Fetal Investigations:**

- **Ultrasonography (USG):** For fetal growth profile (BPD, HC, AC, FL), Estimated Fetal Weight (EFW), and Amniotic Fluid Index (to rule out oligohydramnios).
- **Antenatal Fetal Monitoring:** Non-Stress Test (NST), Biophysical Profile (BPP), and umbilical/middle cerebral artery Doppler flow velocimetry.

**MANAGEMENT**

**1. Immediate/General Measures:**

- **Hospitalization:** Mandatory admission in an intensive care or eclampsia room.
- **Position:** Rest in a left lateral decubitus position to prevent vena caval compression and improve uteroplacental perfusion.
- **Monitoring:** Regular monitoring of maternal pulse, BP (4 times/day minimum), respiratory rate, reflexes, and continuous Electronic Fetal Monitoring (EFM). Insert a Foley catheter to strictly monitor fluid intake and hourly urine output.

**2. Pharmacological Management:**

- **Antihypertensive Therapy:** Indicated to bring BP down to a safe level (Target Systolic: 140–160 mm Hg; Diastolic: 90–100 mm Hg) to prevent maternal cerebrovascular accidents.
    - _First-line drugs:_ Labetalol (10–20 mg IV every 10 min), Hydralazine (5 mg IV every 30 min), or oral Nifedipine (10–20 mg).
- **Anticonvulsant Prophylaxis:** Since the patient has headache and vomiting (ominous signs), Magnesium Sulfate ($MgSO_{4}$) is the drug of choice to prevent eclamptic seizures.
    - _Regimen:_ 4 g IV loading dose (over 3-5 mins), followed by a maintenance dose of 1 g/hr IV infusion or IM regimen.

**3. Definitive/Obstetric Management (Delivery):**

- Since the patient is at **37 weeks of gestation** with severe features, **immediate delivery is the definitive treatment**, irrespective of the Bishop score.
- **Method of Delivery:**
    - _Induction of Labor:_ If the cervix is favorable, low rupture of membranes (ARM) along with oxytocin infusion is the method of choice. If unfavorable, cervical ripening with $PGE_{2}$ gel followed by ARM is done.
    - _Cesarean Section:_ Indicated if there is a tendency for a prolonged induction-delivery interval, an unfavorable cervix, fetal distress, or other obstetric complications.
- **Intrapartum Care:** Epidural analgesia is preferred. The second stage of labor is cut short using prophylactic forceps or ventouse. Active management of the third stage of labor must be conducted using oxytocin (ergometrine is avoided to prevent a sudden spike in BP).

**4. Postpartum Management:**

- Watch closely in a high dependency unit for at least 48 hours.
- Continue $MgSO_{4}$ infusion for 24 hours postpartum to prevent postpartum eclampsia.
- Continue oral antihypertensives (e.g., nifedipine or labetalol) if the BP remains elevated (>150/100 mm Hg).

**COMPLICATIONS**

**Maternal Complications:**

- _Immediate:_ Eclampsia (convulsions), abruptio placentae, HELLP syndrome, acute renal failure (oliguria/anuria), pulmonary edema, cerebral hemorrhage, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), postpartum hemorrhage, retinal detachment/blindness, and maternal death.
- _Remote (Long-term):_ Recurrent pre-eclampsia in future pregnancies (25%), chronic hypertension, ischemic heart disease, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, and chronic renal disease.

**Fetal and Neonatal Complications:**

- Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR) due to chronic placental insufficiency.
- Intrauterine hypoxia and birth asphyxia.
- Intrauterine Fetal Death (IUFD).
- Increased need for Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) admission and high perinatal mortality.



2. Diagnosis and management of Post Partum Hemorrhage.
**POSTPARTUM HEMORRHAGE (PPH)**

**DEFINITION**

- **Clinical (Practical) Definition:** Any amount of bleeding from or into the genital tract following the birth of the baby up to the end of the puerperium, which adversely affects the general condition of the patient (evidenced by a rise in pulse rate and falling blood pressure).
- **Quantitative Definition:** Excessive bleeding following delivery of >500 mL in a vaginal delivery or >1000 mL in a cesarean delivery, accompanied by signs and symptoms of hypovolemia. Visual estimation of blood loss is often inaccurate; hence clinical signs must be considered.

**CLASSIFICATION** **1. Based on Timing:**

- **Primary (Early) PPH:** Hemorrhage occurs within 24 hours following the birth of the baby (majority occur within 2 hours). It is further subdivided into:
    - _Third-stage hemorrhage:_ Bleeding occurs before the expulsion of the placenta.
    - _True postpartum hemorrhage:_ Bleeding occurs subsequent to the expulsion of the placenta.
- **Secondary (Late) PPH:** Hemorrhage occurs beyond 24 hours and within the puerperium (usually between the 8th and 14th day).

**2. Based on Amount of Blood Loss:**

- **Minor:** < 1 Liter.
- **Major:** > 1 Liter.
- **Severe:** > 2 Liters.

**ETIOLOGY (THE "4 Ts")** **1. Tone (Atonic Uterus - 80%):**

- Failure of the myometrium at the placental site to contract and retract.
- _Causes:_ Grand multiparity, overdistension of uterus (multiple pregnancy, polyhydramnios, big baby >4 kg), severe anemia (<9 g/dL), prolonged labor, prolonged anesthesia, precipitate labor, uterine fibroid/malformation, antepartum hemorrhage, and use of tocolytics (MgSO4, nifedipine). **2. Trauma (20%):**
- Lacerations of the cervix, vagina, perineum, or paraurethral region.
- Rupture of the uterus or broad ligament hematoma.
- Bleeding is usually revealed but can be concealed (vulvovaginal hematoma). **3. Tissue (5-10%):**
- Retained bits of placenta (missing cotyledons), membranes, or blood clots.
- Morbidly adherent placenta (accreta, increta, percreta). **4. Thrombin (Coagulopathy - 1%):**
- Diminished procoagulants or increased fibrinolytic activity.
- _Causes:_ Abruptio placentae, severe pre-eclampsia, HELLP syndrome, amniotic fluid embolism, intrauterine fetal death (IUFD), or idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura.

---

**DIAGNOSIS AND CLINICAL EVALUATION**

- **Visual Bleeding:** Usually visible outside as a slow trickle or brisk hemorrhage. Can be concealed as a hematoma.
- **Hemodynamic Assessment (Rule of 30 & Shock Index):**
    - _Rule of 30:_ Fall in systolic BP by 30 mm Hg, rise in HR by 30 bpm, rise in respiratory rate >30/min, drop in Hb/hematocrit by 30%, or urine output <30 mL/hr indicates at least a 30% loss of blood volume (moderate to severe shock).
    - _Shock Index (SI):_ Heart Rate / Systolic BP. A normal SI is 0.5–0.7; an SI of 0.9–1.1 indicates major acute blood loss.
- **Differentiating Atonic vs. Traumatic PPH:**
    - _Atonic:_ Uterus feels flabby and soft; becomes hard on massaging.
    - _Traumatic:_ Uterus is firm, hard, and well-contracted, but bright red bleeding continues.

---

**PROPHYLAXIS (PREVENTION)**

- **Antenatal:** Correct anemia (Hb >10 g/dL), identify high-risk cases and ensure delivery in an equipped hospital, mandatory blood grouping, and ultrasound localization of the placenta to rule out placenta accreta.
- **Intranatal:** Active Management of Third Stage of Labor (AMTSL) reduces PPH by 60%.
    - Administer Oxytocin 10 IU IM/IV or Carbetocin 100 µg IV within 1 minute of baby's delivery.
    - Controlled cord traction.
    - Careful exploration of the uterovaginal canal and 2-hour postpartum observation.

---

**MANAGEMENT OF PRIMARY PPH** The "Golden Hour" is critical; resuscitation must begin immediately to ensure the best chance of survival.

**1. Immediate Resuscitative Measures:**

- Call for extra help and a multidisciplinary team.
- Establish two large-bore (14-gauge) IV lines. Keep the patient flat and warm.
- Catheterize the bladder with a Foley catheter to monitor output.
- Send blood for typing, cross-matching (at least 2 units), CBC, and coagulation screen.
- _Volume Replacement:_ Rapidly infuse crystalloids (up to 2L, e.g., Normal Saline/Ringer's) and colloids (1-2L). Administer PRBCs and FFP (ratio of 4 units FFP to 6 units PRBC) to prevent dilutional coagulopathy.
- Monitor pulse, BP, Respiratory rate, and SpO2 every 15-30 minutes.

**2. Medical Management (For Atonic PPH):**

- **Fundal Massage:** Vigorously massage the uterus to make it hard and express clots.
- **Uterotonics:**
    - _Oxytocin:_ 10–20 IU in 500 mL normal saline infused at 40–60 drops/min.
    - _Methergine:_ 0.2 mg IV slowly or IM (Avoid in hypertension/severe pre-eclampsia).
    - _Carboprost (15-methyl PGF2α):_ 250 µg IM every 15 minutes (Max 8 doses or 2 mg). Contraindicated in asthma.
    - _Misoprostol (PGE1):_ 600–1000 µg given rectally, buccally, or sublingually.
    - _Carbetocin:_ 100 µg IV/IM.
- **Antifibrinolytics:** _Tranexamic acid:_ 1 g IV (slowly over 10 mins). Can be repeated in 30 minutes if needed.

**3. Mechanical & Refractory Management:**

- Ensure the placenta and membranes are complete. If pieces are retained, perform manual exploration of the uterus under general anesthesia.
- **Bimanual Compression:** The right hand is introduced into the vagina (clenched into a fist in the anterior fornix) while the left hand exerts counter-pressure over the abdomen, squeezing the uterus until tone is regained.
- **Balloon Tamponade:** First-line surgical intervention for atonic PPH. A Foley, Bakri, or Condom catheter is inserted into the uterine cavity and inflated with 200–500 mL of normal saline. Kept in place for 4-6 hours. Avoids hysterectomy in 78% of cases.
- **Tight Intrauterine Packing:** 5-meter long gauze is packed uniformly from the fundus downwards. Removed after 24 hours.

**4. Surgical Management (Stepwise Devascularization):** When medical and mechanical methods fail:

- **Uterine Artery Embolization (UAE):** Performed by interventional radiology using gelfoam.
- **B-Lynch Compression Sutures & Multiple Square Sutures:** Places mechanical compression on the uterus.
- **Arterial Ligation:**
    1. Bilateral ligation of the uterine arteries.
    2. Bilateral ligation of the utero-ovarian anastomotic vessels.
    3. Bilateral ligation of the anterior division of the internal iliac arteries.
- **Hysterectomy (Subtotal/Total):** The ultimate life-saving measure if uncontrollable hemorrhage persists. Decision should not be delayed, especially in cases of placenta accreta or uterine rupture.

**5. Management of Traumatic PPH:**

- Immediate exploration of the uterovaginal canal under good lighting and general anesthesia.
- Direct visualization and repair of cervical, vaginal, or perineal lacerations with hemostatic absorbable sutures (Catgut/Vicryl).

---

**MANAGEMENT OF SECONDARY PPH**

- **Etiology:** Retained bits of placental cotyledons/membranes (most common), wound sepsis, endometritis, separation of slough, or rarely choriocarcinoma.
- **Diagnosis:** Bleeding is bright red, of varying amount, and accompanied by features of sepsis and subinvolution.
- **Management:**
    - _Supportive:_ Blood transfusion, IV antibiotics (e.g., clindamycin and metronidazole), and IV Methergine 0.2 mg.
    - _Surgical (Active):_ Explore the uterus urgently under General Anesthesia. Remove retained products using ovum forceps and perform gentle curettage with a flushing curette. Send materials for histopathology.
    - _Severe Cases:_ May require laparotomy, internal iliac artery ligation, or hysterectomy if secondary to a sloughing uterine wound (e.g., post-cesarean).


3. Hyperemesis gravidarum.
**HYPEREMESIS GRAVIDARUM (HG)**

**DEFINITION** Hyperemesis Gravidarum is a severe type of vomiting in pregnancy which has a deleterious effect on the health of the mother and/or incapacitates her in day-to-day activities. The triad of adverse effects due to protracted nausea and vomiting of pregnancy (NVP) includes:

1. > 5% loss of pre-pregnancy weight.
    
2. Dehydration.
3. Electrolyte imbalance. It can also lead to metabolic acidosis due to starvation or metabolic alkalosis due to the loss of hydrochloric acid from vomiting.

**INCIDENCE** The incidence has markedly fallen over the last 30 years and is now rare in hospital practice (less than 1 in 1,000 pregnancies). This reduction is due to better family planning (fewer unplanned pregnancies), early antenatal clinic visits, and the availability of potent antihistaminic and antiemetic drugs.

**ETIOLOGY AND HIGH-RISK FACTORS** The exact etiology is obscure, but it is primarily limited to the first trimester, peaking around 9 weeks and resolving by 20 weeks (90%). High-risk factors include:

- Primigravidae (more common in the first pregnancy) with a 15% tendency to recur in subsequent pregnancies.
- Younger age and low body mass.
- History of motion sickness, migraine, or nausea while on combined oral contraceptive pills (COCs).
- Familial history (mother and sisters suffering from the same).
- Conditions with high human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) levels, such as hydatidiform mole and multiple pregnancy.
- Unplanned pregnancies.

**THEORIES OF PATHOGENESIS**

- **Hormonal:** Excess hCG or higher biological activity of hCG is strongly associated, evidenced by peak vomiting coinciding with peak hCG levels. High serum estrogen and progesterone excess (which causes relaxation of the cardiac sphincter and impaired gastric motility) are also implicated. Other involved hormones include thyroxine, prolactin, leptin, and adrenocortical hormones. Subclinical or clinical hyperthyroidism (transient thyrotoxicosis) is seen in up to 60% of women with HG.
- **Neurogenic/Psychological:** Once vomiting starts, neurogenic elements likely aggravate the condition.
- **Morbid Changes:** The morbid pathological changes observed are secondary to starvation and dehydration.

**CLINICAL COURSE AND FEATURES** Cases are grouped into Early and Late stages based on severity:

- **Early Stage:** Vomiting occurs throughout the day, curtailing normal activities, but there is no evidence of dehydration or starvation.
- **Late Stage (Severe):** Evidences of dehydration and starvation appear.
    - _Symptoms:_ Increased frequency of vomiting with retching, diminished urine output (oliguria), epigastric pain, and constipation.
    - _Signs of Dehydration & Ketoacidosis:_ Dry coated tongue, sunken eyes, acetone smell in breath, tachycardia, hypotension, and a rise in temperature. Jaundice is a late and rare feature.

**BIOCHEMICAL AND METABOLIC CHANGES**

- Hyponatremia and hypokalemia.
- Low serum urea level and raised hematocrit (due to hemoconcentration).
- Ketonuria with metabolic alkalosis (from loss of stomach acid).
- Metabolic acidosis develops in severe cases due to starvation and ketoacidosis.

**INVESTIGATIONS**

1. **Ultrasonography (TVS):** Essential to confirm the viability of the pregnancy and to exclude multiple pregnancy or hydatidiform mole.
2. **Urine Analysis:** To check for quantity, specific gravity, acetone, protein, bile, and urobilinogen.
3. **Biochemical Blood Tests:** Serum electrolytes (Na+, K+, Cl-), urea, uric acid, and liver enzymes.
4. **Hematological Tests:** Hematocrit (to assess hemoconcentration).
5. **Thyroid Profile:** Serum TSH, T3, and free T4 to rule out transient thyrotoxicosis.
6. **ECG:** Indicated if there is an abnormal serum potassium level.
7. **Ophthalmoscopic Examination:** Required in seriously ill patients; retinal hemorrhage and detachment are the most unfavorable signs.

**COMPLICATIONS** **Maternal:**

1. _Neurologic:_ Wernicke's encephalopathy (due to Vitamin B1/thiamine deficiency presenting with blurred vision, ataxia, nystagmus, and confusion), pontine myelinolysis, peripheral neuritis, Korsakoff's psychosis, and depression.
2. _Gastrointestinal:_ Stress ulcers in the stomach, Mallory-Weiss syndrome (esophageal tear and bleeding from severe retching).
3. _Hepatic/Renal:_ Jaundice, hepatic failure, acute renal failure.
4. _Others:_ Convulsions, coma, hypoprothrombinemia (due to Vitamin K deficiency).

**Fetal:** The fetus usually remains unaffected once the condition resolves, but there is an 18% risk of low birth weight and preterm birth.

**MANAGEMENT** The core principles of management are: maintaining hydration, controlling vomiting, correcting fluid/electrolyte imbalance and metabolic disturbances, and preventing serious complications.

**1. Hospitalization:** Indicated if there is protracted NVP despite oral antiemetics, continued vomiting with ketonuria, weight loss >5%, or presence of comorbidities.

**2. Fluid Therapy and Diet:**

- Oral feeding is withheld for at least 24 hours after the cessation of vomiting.
- Fast IV hydration (1–1.5 L) with normal saline and potassium (if not contraindicated) is administered to rectify dehydration, ketoacidosis, and electrolyte imbalances.
- Once vomiting subsides, oral feeding is gradually reintroduced, starting with dry carbohydrates (biscuits, toast) in small, frequent feeds.

**3. Pharmacotherapy (Antiemetics):**

- _First Line:_ Cyclizine (50 mg PO/IM/IV 8 hourly), Prochlorperazine (Stemetil), Promethazine, Chlorpromazine, or a safe combination of Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine 25 mg) and Doxylamine (25 mg).
- _Second Line:_ Metoclopramide (5-10 mg 8 hourly) which stimulates gastric motility, Domperidone, or Ondansetron (4-8 mg 6-8 hourly).
- _Third Line:_ Corticosteroids (Hydrocortisone 100 mg twice daily IV, converting to oral prednisolone 40-50 mg daily once improved) for intractable vomiting or hypotension.

**4. Nutritional & Preventive Supplementation:**

- Parenteral Thiamine (Vitamin B1) 100 mg IV daily is strictly administered to prevent Wernicke's encephalopathy.
- Other supplements include Vitamin B6, Vitamin C, and Vitamin B12.

**5. Additional Measures:**

- Proton pump inhibitors (IV) are used to prevent stress ulcers.
- Thromboprophylaxis (Low Molecular Weight Heparin - LMWH) is indicated due to the risk of hemoconcentration.
- A multidisciplinary approach involving an obstetrician, physician, nutritionist, and sometimes a psychiatrist is required.
- _Termination of pregnancy_ is very rarely indicated nowadays.


4. Puerperal sepsis.
**PUERPERAL SEPSIS (PUERPERAL INFECTION)**

**DEFINITION**

- **Puerperal Sepsis:** An infection of the genital tract which occurs as a complication of delivery.
- **Puerperal Pyrexia:** A rise of temperature reaching 100.4°F (38°C) or more (measured orally) on two separate occasions and 24 hours apart (excluding the first 24 hours) within the first 10 days following delivery. Puerperal pyrexia is considered to be due to genital tract infection (puerperal sepsis) unless proved otherwise,.

**PREDISPOSING FACTORS** The cervicovaginal mucous membrane is damaged during normal delivery, and the placental site is converted into an open wound with blood clots acting as excellent culture media.

- **Antepartum factors:** Anemia, preterm labor, premature rupture of the membranes (PROM), prolonged rupture of membranes (≥ 18 hours), diabetes, and immunocompromised states (e.g., HIV).
- **Intrapartum factors:** Repeated vaginal examinations, traumatic vaginal delivery, antepartum or postpartum hemorrhage, retained bits of placental tissue or membranes, prolonged or obstructed labor, cesarean delivery, and dehydration/ketoacidosis.

**MICROBIOLOGY** Most genital tract infections are polymicrobial (a mixture of aerobic and anaerobic organisms),.

- **Aerobes:** Group A and B beta-hemolytic _Streptococcus_, _Staphylococcus aureus_ (including MRSA), _E. coli_, _Klebsiella_, _Proteus_, _Pseudomonas_, _Gardnerella vaginalis_,.
- **Anaerobes:** _Peptostreptococcus_, _Peptococcus_, _Bacteroides fragilis_, _Fusobacteria_, _Mobiluncus_, _Clostridia_.
- **Others:** _Chlamydia_, _Mycoplasma_.

**MODES OF INFECTION**

1. **Endogenous:** Organisms present in the genital tract before delivery (e.g., Anaerobic _Streptococcus_).
2. **Autogenous:** Organisms present elsewhere in the body (skin, throat) migrate via the bloodstream or are introduced by the patient herself.
3. **Exogenous:** Infection contracted from outside sources such as the hospital environment or attendants (e.g., Beta-hemolytic _Streptococcus_, _Staphylococcus_, _E. coli_),.

**PATHOLOGY AND SPREAD OF INFECTION**

- **Primary Sites:** Perineum, vagina, cervix, and uterus (Endomyometritis),.
- **Spread of Infection:**
    - **Pelvic cellulitis (parametritis):** Direct, lymphatic, or hematogenous spread to pelvic cellular tissues.
    - **Peritonitis:** Common following metritis after cesarean delivery.
    - **Salpingitis & Pelvic Abscess:** Spread to fallopian tubes or encysted pus in the pouch of Douglas.
    - **Septic Pelvic Thrombophlebitis (SPT):** Hypercoagulable state leads to thrombus in deep pelvic veins, which becomes infected and releases septic microemboli.
    - **Septicemia and Septic Shock:** Metastatic infection or endotoxin release causing multiorgan failure, ARDS, or DIC,.

**CLINICAL FEATURES**

- **Local Infection (Wound):** Slight temperature rise, malaise, headache, red and swollen local wound, purulent discharge, or wound disruption.
- **Uterine Infection (Mild):** Temperature >100.4°F, pulse >90 bpm, offensive and copious lochial discharge, and a tender, subinvoluted uterus.
- **Uterine Infection (Severe):** Acute onset with high temperature, chills and rigors, rapid pulse out of proportion to temperature, abdominal pain, scanty and odorless lochia, and a tender/soft uterus.
- **Spreading Infection:** Pelvic tenderness (peritonitis), indurated mass (parametritis), bulging fluctuant mass in the pouch of Douglas (pelvic abscess), or severe systemic signs of septicemia/toxic shock syndrome,,.

**INVESTIGATIONS** A case of puerperal pyrexia is considered genital sepsis unless proved otherwise,.

1. **Swabs:** High vaginal and endocervical swabs for aerobic and anaerobic culture and antibiotic sensitivity.
2. **Urine:** "Clean catch" midstream specimen for analysis and culture.
3. **Blood Tests:** Total and differential WBC count, hemoglobin estimation, serum lactate (level ≥4 mmol/L indicates tissue hypoperfusion), and platelets,.
4. **Blood Culture:** Indicated if fever is associated with chills and rigors.
5. **Imaging:** Pelvic ultrasound (to detect retained products, pelvic abscess, or color Doppler for venous thrombosis), CT/MRI (for pelvic vein thrombosis), and Chest X-ray.

**PROPHYLAXIS**

- **Antenatal:** Improve nutritional status and correct anemia; eradicate any septic foci.
- **Intranatal:** Strict surgical asepsis during delivery, prophylactic antibiotics during cesarean section,.
- **Postnatal:** Aseptic precautions for at least 1 week, isolation of infected mothers/babies, and restricted visitors,.

**TREATMENT**

- **General Care:** Isolate the patient, maintain adequate fluid and calorie intake via IV infusion, correct anemia with blood transfusion if needed, and use an indwelling catheter to monitor urine output. Maintain a strict chart for vital signs and lochia.
- **Medical Management (Antibiotics):**
    - Empirical therapy should be started immediately: Gentamicin (2 mg/kg IV loading dose, followed by 1.5 mg/kg IV every 8 hours) + Clindamycin (900 mg IV every 8 hours) + Metronidazole (500 mg IV every 8 hours).
    - _Severe sepsis:_ Piperacillin-tazobactam or carbapenem plus clindamycin for broadest coverage,.
    - _MRSA infection:_ Vancomycin or teicoplanin.
    - Continue antibiotics until the infection is controlled for at least 7-10 days.
    - _Septic Pelvic Thrombophlebitis:_ IV Heparin for 7-10 days alongside antibiotics.
- **Surgical Management:**
    - **Perineal Wound:** Removal of sutures, wound debridement, sitz baths, and secondary suturing once healthy granulation tissue develops,.
    - **Retained Uterine Products:** Surgical evacuation under antibiotic coverage if the diameter is >3 cm.
    - **Pelvic Abscess:** Drainage by colpotomy under ultrasound guidance.
    - **Laparotomy/Hysterectomy:** Indicated in cases of unresponsive peritonitis, ruptured tubo-ovarian abscess, gangrenous uterus, or gas gangrene infection,.
    - **Necrotizing Fasciitis:** Aggressive wound scrubbing, extensive tissue debridement, and high-dose broad-spectrum IV antibiotics,.


5. Intra partum Fetal monitoring.

**DEFINITION AND OBJECTIVES** Intrapartum Fetal Evaluation (IFM) is the continuous or intermittent surveillance of the fetal condition during labor. The primary objective is to detect fetal hypoxia and initiate appropriate management depending on its severity, thereby preventing intrapartum fetal organ damage, hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, or fetal death.

**PHYSIOLOGICAL BASIS OF INTRAPARTUM STRESS** Even during normal labor, the fetus is subjected to stress because uterine contractions temporarily curtail uteroplacental circulation (peak myometrial pressure of 120 mm Hg exceeds the spiral artery pressure of 90 mm Hg). Additionally, contractions can cause intermittent umbilical cord compression, leading to interrupted blood flow and hypoxia.

**METHODS OF FETAL MONITORING** Intrapartum fetal monitoring is broadly classified into Clinical, Biophysical, and Biochemical methods.

**1. Clinical Monitoring**

- **Method:** Intermittent auscultation of the Fetal Heart Rate (FHR) using an ordinary stethoscope, fetoscope, or handheld Doppler.
- **Frequency:** Auscultation should be done every 30 minutes in the first stage of labor (then every 15 minutes), and every 5 minutes in the second stage.
- **Technique:** The FHR should be counted for a full 60 seconds, particularly before and immediately following a uterine contraction.
- **Normal Findings:** The normal FHR ranges from 110 to 160 beats per minute (bpm).
- **Signs of Fetal Distress:** FHR >160 bpm (tachycardia) or <100 bpm (bradycardia) lasting for more than 10 minutes, delayed return to the normal rate after a contraction, and FHR irregularity.
- **Meconium-Stained Liquor (MSL):** Hypoxia causes a vagal response leading to increased peristaltic activity and relaxation of the anal sphincter, resulting in the passage of meconium. Thick (pea soup) meconium indicates fetal jeopardy.
- **Limitations:** Periodic observation may overlook transient abnormalities, is subject to human error, and can be difficult in maternal obesity or polyhydramnios.

**2. Biophysical Monitoring**

- **Continuous Electronic Fetal Monitoring (EFM) / Cardiotocography (CTG):**
    - **Indications:** Maternal high-risk conditions (hypertension, diabetes, previous cesarean, antepartum hemorrhage, induced labor, thick MSL) and fetal high-risk conditions (FGR, oligohydramnios, multiple pregnancy, abnormal auscultatory FHR).
    - **Methods:** Can be performed externally (Doppler transducers on the maternal abdomen recording FHR and uterine contractions) or internally (fetal ECG tracing using a spiral scalp electrode after rupture of membranes).
    - **Advantages:** It provides accurate monitoring of uterine contractions, allows early detection of hypoxia, offers a medicolegal record, and significantly reduces the incidence of neonatal seizures by 50%.
    - **Limitations:** EFM has a high false-positive rate leading to unnecessary obstetric interventions (a 63% increase in cesarean delivery and a 15% increase in operative vaginal delivery), requires trained personnel, and limits maternal mobility.
- **Induced Fetal Stimulation Tests:**
    - Methods include Vibroacoustic Stimulation (VAS), fetal scalp stimulation via digital stroking, and halogen light stimulation.
    - The presence of FHR accelerations following stimulation indicates a normal blood pH and the absence of fetal acidosis.

**3. Biochemical Monitoring**

- **Fetal Scalp Blood Sampling (FBS):** Used to corroborate fetal CTG abnormalities by assessing fetal blood pH.
    - **pH > 7.25:** Reassuring; continue monitoring labor.
    - **pH 7.21 - 7.25:** Borderline; repeat the test within 30 minutes.
    - **pH < 7.20:** Abnormal; indicates fetal acidosis and the need for urgent delivery.
- **Lactate Estimation:** Lactate values correlate with FBS. <4.1 mmol/L is normal, 4.2-4.9 mmol/L is borderline, and >4.9 mmol/L is abnormal.

**4. Fetal Electrocardiogram (ECG) / ST-Segment Analysis (STAN)**

- Analyzes the ST segment and T-wave amplitude (T/QRS ratio).
- An increase in the T/QRS ratio (>0.25) or biphasic ST complexes combined with a nonreassuring CTG indicates fetal metabolic acidosis.
- Combining ST-segment analysis with CTG reduces operative delivery rates compared to using CTG alone.

**INTERPRETATION OF CTG (RCOG / NICE CATEGORIZATION)**

- **Category I (Normal/Reassuring):** Baseline FHR of 110-160 bpm, moderate baseline variability (5-25 bpm), no late or variable decelerations, and early decelerations may or may not be present.
- **Category II (Suspicious/Indeterminate):** Includes tracings with one nonreassuring feature while the rest are reassuring (e.g., dropping baseline, absent accelerations).
- **Category III (Pathological/Abnormal):** Absent baseline variability combined with recurrent late or variable decelerations, bradycardia lasting at least 10 minutes, or a sinusoidal pattern lasting at least 20 minutes.

**TYPES OF DECELERATIONS**

- **Early Deceleration:** Uniform, coincides with the peak of uterine contraction, and is caused by fetal head compression (vagal nerve activation) which is benign.
- **Late Deceleration:** Begins after the onset of the contraction, with the nadir occurring after the peak of the contraction; it strongly suggests uteroplacental insufficiency and fetal hypoxia.
- **Variable Deceleration:** V-shaped, variable in timing and shape, and indicates umbilical cord compression.

**MANAGEMENT OF NONREASSURING FETAL STATUS (NRFS)** NRFS (formerly known as fetal distress) indicates impending fetal hypoxia and acidosis.

- **Nonsurgical Measures (Intrauterine Resuscitation):**
    - Place the mother in a left lateral position to relieve aortocaval compression and improve uteroplacental perfusion.
    - Administer Oxygen (6-8 L/min) via a face mask to improve fetal oxygen saturation.
    - Administer rapid IV infusion of crystalloids (Ringer's solution) to correct maternal dehydration or hypotension (especially post-epidural).
    - Stop oxytocin infusion immediately to alleviate strong uterine contractions and improve fetal oxygenation.
    - Consider amnioinfusion to relieve cord compression in cases of severe variable decelerations.
- **Surgical Measures:**
    - If the FHR pattern remains nonreassuring despite resuscitation, or if fetal metabolic acidosis is confirmed (FBS pH < 7.20), immediate delivery is mandatory.
    - Cesarean delivery (Category I) should be performed, with the gold standard for the decision-to-delivery interval being 30 minutes to prevent irreversible neurological injury.

6. External Cephalic Version.
**EXTERNAL CEPHALIC VERSION (ECV)**

**Definition** External Cephalic Version (ECV) is a manipulative procedure performed entirely through the maternal abdominal wall to change an oblique or transverse lie into a longitudinal lie, or to bring the favorable cephalic pole into the lower pole of the uterus.

**Indications**

- Breech presentation.
- Transverse or oblique lie.

**Timing of Version**

- It is generally performed at **36 weeks in primigravidae** and **37 weeks in multigravidae**.
- Performing it earlier increases the chances of spontaneous reversion, while later attempts may be difficult due to the increasing size of the fetus and decreasing amniotic fluid.

**Contraindications**

- **Antepartum Hemorrhage (APH):** Placenta previa or placental abruption (due to the risk of placental separation).
- **Fetal Factors:** Fetal compromise/distress, hyperextended head (stargazing fetus), large fetus (>3.5 kg), severe Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR), dead fetus, or major congenital anomalies.
- **Maternal Factors:** Severe pre-eclampsia, contracted pelvis, congenital malformation of the uterus (e.g., septate or bicornuate uterus), oligohydramnios, or Rhesus isoimmunization.
- **Pregnancy Factors:** Multiple pregnancy, ruptured membranes, or previous cesarean deliveries (>1). _Note: A previous single Lower Segment Cesarean Section (LSCS) is not an absolute contraindication for ECV_.
- _Note: Breech with extended legs (frank breech) is NOT a contraindication for version_.

**Predictors of Success** The overall success rate of ECV is about 65% (60% in multiparae and 40% in primigravidae). Success is more likely in cases with:

- Multigravidity.
- Non-engagement of the breech.
- Use of tocolysis.
- Maternal weight <65 kg.
- Posteriorly located placenta.
- Complete breech presentation.
- Adequate liquor (Amniotic Fluid Index >10).
- Average-sized baby.

**Procedure / Steps**

1. **Preliminaries:** Informed consent is taken. The procedure is performed in a labor delivery complex. The patient is asked to empty her bladder and lie supine with shoulders slightly raised and thighs slightly flexed.
2. **Pre-procedure Assessment:** Real-time ultrasound is done to confirm the presentation, rule out anomalies, and check liquor volume. A reactive Non-Stress Test (NST) must be documented.
3. **Tocolysis:** A tocolytic drug (e.g., subcutaneous terbutaline 0.25 mg) may be administered to relax the uterus and increase the success rate.
4. **The "Forward Roll" Maneuver:**
    - The breech is mobilized using both hands toward the iliac fossa on the side of the fetal back.
    - The podalic pole is grasped by one hand and the head by the other.
    - Firm, intermittent pressure is exerted in opposite directions to keep the fetal trunk flexed. The head is pushed down toward the pelvis and the breech toward the fundus until the lie becomes transverse.
    - The hands are then changed to prevent crossing, and pressure continues until the head is brought to the lower pole.
5. **Intra-procedure Monitoring:** Fetal wellbeing is monitored intermittently with Doppler or ultrasound. The procedure must be abandoned if significant fetal distress occurs or if it fails after 4 attempts.

**Post-Procedure Care**

- A reactive NST should be obtained after completing the maneuver. Transient bradycardia is common but should settle within 10 minutes.
- The patient is observed for 30 minutes for any signs of vaginal bleeding, premature rupture of membranes, or onset of labor.
- **Rh-negative nonimmunized women** must be given 100 µg of anti-D gammaglobulin intramuscularly to prevent alloimmunization.

**Complications and Dangers**

- Premature onset of labor.
- Premature rupture of the membranes (PROM).
- Placental abruption and bleeding.
- Cord entanglement around fetal parts or formation of a true knot, which can lead to fetal hypoxia and intrauterine fetal death.
- Fetomaternal bleed and alloimmunization.
- Amniotic fluid embolism.
- Rupture of the uterus.

**Benefits of Successful ECV**

- Reduces the incidence of breech presentation at term and avoids the complications associated with vaginal breech delivery.
- Significantly reduces the incidence of cesarean delivery by about 50%.
- Reduces both fetal and maternal morbidity.


7. GDM screening.

**GESTATIONAL DIABETES MELLITUS (GDM) SCREENING**

**1. General Recommendations and Timing**

- **Universal Screening:** All pregnant women should be screened for GDM irrespective of risk factors.
- **Routine Timing:** Screening is routinely performed between **24 and 28 weeks of gestation**.
- **Early Screening:** Recommended during the first trimester or early pregnancy for women presenting with high-risk factors.

**2. Risk Factors for GDM (Indications for Early Screening)** Early screening is warranted if the patient has any of the following high-risk factors:

- **Obstetric History:** Previous GDM, previous birth of a macrosomic/overweight baby (≥4 kg), unexplained perinatal loss, or previous stillbirth with autopsy revealing pancreatic islet hyperplasia.
- **Family History:** Positive family history of diabetes (parents, siblings, uncles, aunts, or grandparents).
- **Present Pregnancy Complications:** Polyhydramnios, recurrent vaginal candidiasis, or persistent glycosuria (glycosuria on two or more occasions after 20 weeks dictates a mandatory Glucose Tolerance Test).
- **Maternal Factors:** Age >30 years, obesity, Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS), hypertension (≥140/90 mm Hg), or high-risk ethnic group (East Asian, Pacific island ancestry).

**3. Screening Strategies and Procedures**

**A. One-Step Procedure (DIPSI Guidelines)** _Highly relevant for Indian clinical practice._

- **Method:** Administer **75 g oral glucose** irrespective of the time of the last meal.
- **Diagnostic Cut-off:** A 2-hour venous plasma glucose value of **>140 mg/dL** is diagnostic of GDM. _(Note: The text mentions DIPSI criteria awaits reconsideration, but it remains a standard 1-step procedure)_.

**B. Two-Step Procedure (ACOG Guidelines)**

- **Step 1 (Screening - Oral Glucose Challenge Test):** A 50 g oral glucose load is given irrespective of the time of day or last meal.
    - **Cut-off:** A 1-hour plasma glucose value of **≥140 mg/dL** (or whole blood ≥130 mg/dL) is considered positive.
- **Step 2 (Diagnostic - Oral Glucose Tolerance Test):** If Step 1 is positive, a 100 g (or 75 g WHO/IADPSG) 2-hour Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT) is performed.

**4. Diagnostic Criteria Values** According to the text, the diagnosis of GDM is confirmed if values meet or exceed specific thresholds:

- **Standard Plasma Glucose Values:** Fasting Blood Sugar (FBS) ≥5.6 mmol/L (100 mg/dL) and 2-hour plasma glucose ≥7.8 mmol/L (140 mg/dL).
- **IADPSG Criteria (75 g OGTT):** Fasting: 92 mg/dL, 1-hour: 180 mg/dL, 2-hour: 153 mg/dL. Diagnosis is confirmed if **one or more** of these thresholds are met or exceeded.

**5. Additional Screening Marker (HbA1c)**

- For high-risk women undergoing early screening, the value of **HbA1c** is estimated.
- An HbA1c value of **≥6.5%** suggests overt (pre-gestational) diabetes rather than GDM. Women with overt diabetes require a comprehensive ultrasound to detect fetal malformations related to embryogenesis.

8. Modified Bishop score.

**Introduction and Purpose** The Bishop's preinduction cervical scoring system is a clinical tool used to assess the readiness (ripeness) of the cervix and to predict the success of induction of labor.

**Parameters Assessed** The scoring system evaluates five specific components. Four are related to the cervix and one relates to the fetal head:

4. Cervical Position
5. Station of the Fetal Head

9. IUGR-Risk factors.

**FETAL GROWTH RESTRICTION (FGR) / INTRAUTERINE GROWTH RESTRICTION (IUGR) - RISK FACTORS**

The etiology of Fetal Growth Restriction (FGR) is multifactorial and can be broadly classified into maternal, fetal, placental, toxic, and unknown factors.

**1. Maternal Factors**

- **Constitutional:** Small women, slim body type, and specific maternal genetic and racial backgrounds.
- **Demographic & Physical:** Extremes of maternal age (>40 years is a major risk, $\ge$35 years is a minor risk), nulliparity, maternal BMI <20 or >35, and poor weight gain during pregnancy.
- **Nutritional & Environmental:** Severe anemia, hemoglobinopathies, malnutrition, and hypoxemia (due to maternal lung disease).
- **Medical Disorders:** Early-onset severe hypertension, chronic hypertension, long-standing diabetes mellitus with vascular disease, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), antiphospholipid syndrome (APLS), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and inflammatory bowel syndrome (IBS).
- **Obstetric History:** Previous pregnancy affected by FGR or pre-eclampsia, prior stillbirth, maternal or paternal history of being Small for Gestational Age (SGA), short pregnancy intervals (<6 months) or prolonged intervals ($\ge$60 months), and conception via IVF (singleton pregnancy).
- **Current Pregnancy Complications:** Heavy vaginal bleeding similar to menses is a major risk factor.

**2. Fetal Factors (Intrinsic)**

- **Genetic & Chromosomal:** Genetic syndromes, aneuploidy, triploidy, trisomy 13, trisomy 18, microdeletion duplications, and single site mutations.
- **Structural:** Presence of congenital structural anomalies.
- **Infections:** Congenital intrauterine infections, particularly TORCH infections and Cytomegalovirus (CMV).
- **Gestation Type:** Multiple pregnancy (twins or higher-order).

**3. Placental and Umbilical Cord Factors**

- **Placental:** Poor uterine blood flow (maternal vascular malperfusion), placental infarction (5%), placental abruption, mosaicism, diabetes with vascular disease, and placental inflammation (villitis).
- **Umbilical Cord:** Single umbilical artery, velamentous cord insertion, increased cord length, and true cord knots.

**4. Toxins and Teratogens**

- Maternal exposure to teratogenic drugs or toxins, including smoking, alcohol, cocaine, and heroin, significantly impairs fetal growth.

**5. Unknown Factors**

- In approximately 40% of all FGR cases, the exact etiology remains unknown.

**HIGH-YIELD PREDICTIVE MARKERS FOR FGR:**

- **Biochemical Markers:** Low Placental Growth Factor (PLGF), low Pregnancy-Associated Plasma Protein-A (PAPP-A) (specifically <0.4 MoM), and high Alpha-Fetoprotein (AFP) in the first/second trimester.
- **Ultrasonographic (USG) Markers:** Uterine Artery (UtA) Pulsatility Index (PI) >95th percentile, bilateral UtA diastolic notching, and abnormal placental morphology.


10. Polyhydraminos.
**POLYHYDRAMNIOS**

**DEFINITION**

- **Anatomical:** A state where the liquor amnii exceeds 2,000 mL.
- **Clinical:** Excessive accumulation of liquor amnii causing discomfort to the patient and/or when imaging help is needed to substantiate the clinical diagnosis of the lie and presentation of the fetus.
- **Sonographic:** Amniotic Fluid Index (AFI) > 25 cm (more than the 95th percentile for gestational age) and a Maximum Vertical Pocket (MVP) > 8 cm.

**INCIDENCE AND CLASSIFICATION**

- **Incidence:** Occurs in 1 to 2% of pregnancies; more common in multiparae than in primigravidae.
- **Clinical Types**:
    1. **Chronic (Most Common):** Onset is insidious, taking a few weeks to accumulate. It is 10 times more common than the acute variety.
    2. **Acute (Extremely Rare):** Sudden onset within a few days, typically occurring before 20 weeks.
- **Grading by MVP**:
    - Mild: MVP 8–11 cm (79% of cases).
    - Moderate: MVP 12–15 cm (16% of cases).
    - Severe: MVP >16 cm (5% of cases).

**ETIOLOGY** The exact mechanism is speculative but usually involves deficient absorption or excessive production of amniotic fluid.

- **Fetal Anomalies (20%):**
    - _Anencephaly:_ Present in about 50% of cases due to transudation from exposed meninges, absent fetal swallowing reflex, and suppression of fetal antidiuretic hormone (ADH) leading to excessive urination.
    - _Open spina bifida:_ Due to increased transudation from the meninges.
    - _Gastrointestinal:_ Esophageal or duodenal atresia (prevents swallowing of liquor).
    - _Others:_ Facial clefts, neck masses, hydrops fetalis (Rh isoimmunization or nonimmune), fetal TORCH/parvovirus B19 infections, aneuploidy, genetic syndromes, and muscular dystrophy.
- **Placental Causes:** Chorioangioma of the placenta (leads to increased transudation).
- **Multiple Pregnancy:** 10 times more common than overall incidence. It is especially common in monozygotic twins (usually affecting the second sac) and in Twin-Twin Transfusion Syndrome (TTTS), where the recipient twin develops polyhydramnios.
- **Maternal Causes:**
    - _Diabetes (30% of cases):_ Maternal hyperglycemia causes fetal hyperglycemia, leading to fetal diuresis and hydramnios.
    - _Cardiac or renal disease:_ May lead to placental edema and increased transudation.
- **Idiopathic:** No specific cause is found in 50–60% of cases.

**CLINICAL FEATURES**

- **Symptoms:** Primarily due to mechanical causes.
    - Respiratory: Dyspnea; the patient may need to remain in a sitting position to breathe.
    - Cardiovascular: Palpitations.
    - Venous compression: Edema of the legs and vulva, varicosities, and hemorrhoids.
- **Signs:**
    - **General Examination:** Dyspneic state, evidence of pre-eclampsia (hypertension, edema, proteinuria).
    - **Abdominal Examination:**
        - _Inspection:_ Abdomen is markedly enlarged and globular with fullness at the flanks. Skin is tense, shiny, and shows large striae.
        - _Palpation:_ Fundal height is greater than the period of amenorrhea. Abdominal girth around the umbilicus is more than the normal term average (100 cm). Fluid thrill can be elicited in all directions. Fetal parts and presentation cannot be well-defined. External ballottement is elicited very easily.
        - _Auscultation:_ Fetal heart sounds (FHS) are not heard distinctly with a stethoscope, though detectable by Doppler.
    - **Internal Examination:** The cervix is pulled up, partially taken up, or dilated, and tense, bulging membranes can be felt.

**INVESTIGATIONS**

- **Ultrasonography (USG):** Confirms diagnosis (MVP > 8 cm, AFI > 25 cm), excludes multiple fetuses, determines fetal lie and presentation, and diagnoses fetal structural/congenital malformations.
- **Blood Tests:** ABO and Rh grouping (to exclude isoimmunization), postprandial sugar, or Glucose Tolerance Test (GTT) to rule out diabetes.
- **Amniotic Fluid Analysis:** Estimation of maternal/amniotic alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) which is markedly elevated in open neural tube defects.

**DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS**

- Twin pregnancy.
- Pregnancy with a huge ovarian cyst (Hingorani's sign helps differentiate).
- Maternal ascites.

**COMPLICATIONS**

- **Maternal (During Pregnancy):** Increased incidence of pre-eclampsia (25%), malpresentation, premature rupture of membranes (PROM), preterm labor, accidental hemorrhage (placental abruption due to sudden decompression), and intrauterine fetal death (IUFD).
- **Maternal (During Labor):** Early rupture of membranes, cord prolapse, uterine inertia, increased need for operative delivery, retained placenta, and postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) due to severe uterine atony and shock.
- **Maternal (Puerperium):** Subinvolution and increased puerperal morbidity due to infection.
- **Fetal/Neonatal:** Perinatal mortality is highly increased (~50%), mostly due to prematurity, congenital abnormalities (40%), cord prolapse, and hydrops fetalis.

**MANAGEMENT** **Principles:** Relieve maternal symptoms, identify the underlying cause, and prevent or manage complications.

- **Mild Polyhydramnios (MVP 8-11 cm):** Usually transient in the mid-trimester. Requires no specific treatment other than extra bed rest.
- **Severe Polyhydramnios (MVP ≥ 16 cm):** Requires immediate hospitalization in a high-risk unit.
    - **Supportive Therapy:** Bed rest with a backrest. Treat associated medical conditions (diabetes, pre-eclampsia). Diuretics are of little value. Indomethacin (COX-2 inhibitor) can be used for a short period to reduce fetal urine output, but prolonged use risks premature closure of the ductus arteriosus.
- **Definitive Obstetric Management:**
    - **With Fetal Abnormality:** Referral to a Maternal-Fetal Medicine (MFM) unit. Pregnancy is terminated irrespective of the gestational age. Amniocentesis is done to drain liquor, followed by induction via vaginal PGE2 gel and low artificial rupture of membranes (ARM).
    - **Without Fetal Abnormality (Uncomplicated):**
        - _Responsive to treatment:_ Expectant management; await spontaneous delivery at term.
        - _Unresponsive & <37 weeks (with maternal distress):_ Therapeutic amniocentesis (amnioreduction). Fluid is drained slowly (~500 mL/hour) to relieve mechanical distress. Repeated if fluid reaccumulates. Stopped when AFI < 25 cm.
        - _Unresponsive & >37 weeks:_ Induction of labor. Amniocentesis is done to drain liquor -> check for favorable longitudinal lie -> start stabilizing oxytocin infusion -> perform low ARM. This prevents sudden decompression, which could cause abruption or cord prolapse.
- **Intrapartum Care:**
    - Internal examination immediately after the rupture of membranes to exclude cord prolapse.
    - Oxytocin infusion if uterine contractions are sluggish.
    - _Active management of the third stage:_ Intravenous methergine 0.2 mg or oxytocin is given with the delivery of the anterior shoulder to prevent atonic PPH.

**ACUTE POLYHYDRAMNIOS** An extremely rare variant occurring before 20 weeks.

- **Associations:** Monozygotic twins (TTTS) or placental chorioangioma.
- **Presentation:** Acute abdomen features—abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting.
- **Treatment:** Mostly results in spontaneous abortion. In severe TTTS, repetitive amnioreduction or laser ablation may improve outcomes.

11. Vaginal birth after caesarian section (VBAC)
**VAGINAL BIRTH AFTER CESAREAN SECTION (VBAC) / TRIAL OF LABOR AFTER CESAREAN (TOLAC)**

**INTRODUCTION & DEFINITIONS**

- **TOLAC (Trial of Labor After Cesarean):** It is a planned attempt to deliver vaginally by a woman who has had a previous cesarean delivery.
- **VBAC (Vaginal Birth After Cesarean):** It refers to the successful vaginal delivery in a woman undergoing TOLAC.
- Instead of performing a repeat cesarean delivery for all cases, TOLAC is highly encouraged for women with prior low transverse cesarean deliveries. The overall success rate of TOLAC is 72–75%.

**PREDICTORS OF SUCCESS FOR VBAC (FAVORABLE FACTORS)** The likelihood of a successful VBAC is higher in the presence of the following factors:

- Prior vaginal delivery (87% success rate).
- Prior successful VBAC (85–90% success rate).
- Spontaneous onset of labor in the current pregnancy (80% success rate).
- Cervical dilatation >4 cm on admission (86% success rate).
- Prior cesarean section was for a non-recurring indication, such as fetal distress (73% success) or breech presentation (89% success).
- Lower estimated fetal weight (higher fetal weight lowers the success rate).
- _Note: Women who are obese (BMI > 30) or elderly have lower success rates_.

**BENEFITS OF SUCCESSFUL VBAC** Compared to an Elective Repeat Cesarean Section (ERCS), a successful VBAC offers numerous benefits:

- Avoidance of major abdominal surgery and decreased maternal morbidity (such as infections).
- Reduced length of hospital stay.
- Decreased need for blood transfusion.
- Avoidance of the risks associated with multiple repeat cesarean deliveries in future pregnancies, specifically abnormal placentation (Placenta Previa and Placenta Accreta Spectrum/PAS), and the subsequent need for peripartum hysterectomy.
- Decreased risk of neonatal Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS) compared to ERCS (2-3% vs. 4-5%).

**RISKS OF UNSUCCESSFUL TOLAC** If a trial of labor fails and ends in an emergency cesarean, the risks are higher than in an ERCS:

- **Uterine rupture (0.5–1%)** or uterine scar dehiscence.
- Hemorrhage requiring successive operations and increased need for blood transfusion.
- Increased risk of hysterectomy due to uterine rupture.
- Perinatal complications: Low Apgar score, admission to NICU, Hypoxic Ischemic Encephalopathy (HIE), neonatal death, and rarely stillbirth.

**SELECTION CRITERIA FOR TOLAC (INDICATIONS)** Scrupulous selection of cases is paramount for a safe outcome. Criteria include:

1. One (RCOG) or two (ACOG) previous lower segment transverse scars.
2. Non-recurring indication for the previous cesarean section.
3. Clinical and/or ultrasonographic assessment confirming an adequate pelvis and sound scar integrity.
4. Availability of continuous labor monitoring (Electronic Fetal Monitoring - EFM).
5. Availability of resources (anesthesia, blood transfusion, and operation theater) for an emergency Category I cesarean section within 30 minutes of the decision.
6. Informed consent from the woman explaining the benefits and risks.

**CONTRAINDICATIONS FOR TOLAC (INDICATIONS FOR REPEAT CESAREAN)**

1. Previous classical, inverted T-shaped, or J-shaped uterine incision (due to a high risk of rupture: 2-5%).
2. Previous more than two lower segment cesarean sections.
3. Contracted pelvis or suspected cephalopelvic disproportion (CPD).
4. History of prior uterine rupture.
5. Presence of obstetric or medical complications (e.g., severe pre-eclampsia, placenta previa, malpresentation, big baby).
6. Cases admitted as an emergency with symptoms suggestive of scar rupture (unusual abdominal pain, scar tenderness, bleeding).
7. Limited hospital resources or patient refusal for TOLAC.

**MANAGEMENT PROTOCOL FOR TOLAC**

**1. Antenatal Care and Admission:**

- Patients are considered 'high-risk' and require regular antenatal supervision to check for pain, scar tenderness, or bleeding.
- Women with a lower segment transverse scar should be admitted electively at 38 weeks of gestation for proper assessment and formulation of the delivery method.

**2. Management of First Stage of Labor:**

- **Induction:** Spontaneous onset of labor is highly desired. If induction is required, mechanical methods like Foley's catheterization are safe. **The use of prostaglandins (e.g., PGE1/misoprostol) is strictly contraindicated** as it significantly increases the risk of uterine scar rupture.
- **Preparation:** Secure an intravenous line with Ringer's solution, and send a blood sample for grouping and cross-matching.
- **Monitoring:** Labor progress must be meticulously mapped on a partograph. Continuous Electronic Fetal Monitoring (EFM) is desirable. The appearance of a non-reassuring fetal heart rate pattern, significant variable decelerations, or prolonged bradycardia is an early warning sign of uterine rupture.
- **Augmentation:** Oxytocin for augmentation can be used selectively and judiciously, though it does increase the risk of uterine scar rupture and subsequent cesarean section.
- **Analgesia:** Epidural analgesia is not contraindicated. It does not delay the progress of labor nor mask the signs of scar rupture.

**3. Management of Second Stage of Labor:**

- A prolonged second stage must be avoided.
- **Prophylactic operative vaginal delivery** (forceps or ventouse/vacuum extraction) with an episiotomy is widely used to cut short the second stage and reduce stress on the uterine scar.

**4. Management of Third Stage of Labor:**

- The placenta is delivered normally.
- **Routine manual exploration of the uterine scar is not universally recommended** today. It is selectively performed only if there is continued excessive vaginal bleeding or unexplained maternal hypotension despite a well-contracted uterus.

**WARNING SIGNS OF SCAR DEHISCENCE / RUPTURE** The trial of labor must be immediately abandoned in favor of an emergency laparotomy/cesarean section if any of the following signs appear:

- Non-reassuring fetal heart rate (fetal distress, particularly prolonged bradycardia).
- Acute, severe abdominal pain or suprapubic pain persisting _in between_ uterine contractions.
- Loss of station of the presenting part on vaginal examination.
- Cessation of previously adequate uterine contractions.
- Abnormal vaginal bleeding or hematuria.
- Maternal tachycardia, hypotension, or frank shock.


12. Postpartum IUCD
**POSTPARTUM INTRAUTERINE CONTRACEPTIVE DEVICE (PPIUCD)**

**Introduction** Postpartum Intrauterine Contraceptive Device (PPIUCD) is a highly effective, safe, and long-acting reversible contraceptive (LARC) method used for the spacing of births. The Copper IUD (e.g., Cu-T 380A) is considered the most effective form of postpartum contraception.

**Timing of Insertion** PPIUCD can be inserted at specific times following childbirth:

- **Post-placental:** Inserted within 10 minutes after the expulsion of the placenta following a vaginal delivery.
- **Intracesarean:** Inserted during a cesarean section, immediately after the removal of the placenta and before the closure of the uterine incision.
- **Early Postpartum:** Inserted within 48 hours after delivery, before the patient is discharged from the hospital.
- **Extended Postpartum / Interval:** If not inserted within the first 48 hours, insertion is usually delayed until 4-6 weeks postpartum. Insertion between 48 hours and 4 weeks is categorized as MEC-3, whereas anytime after 4 weeks is MEC-1.

**Instrument Used for Insertion**

- **Kelly's Long Forceps:** This is a long metallic instrument with a smooth curve close to the fenestrated blades and no catch on the handle, specifically used for postplacental insertion of Cu-T 380A into the puerperal uterus.
- _Advantages of Kelly's Forceps:_ Its length enables the precise placement of the IUCD at the fundus of the bulky puerperal uterus; the transverse serrations on the blades provide a good grip; and the absence of a catch protects the IUCD from being crushed.

**Advantages of PPIUCD**

- **Immediate and Long-term Protection:** It provides immediate action and long-term contraceptive protection (e.g., 10 years for Cu-T 380A).
- **Ideal for Poor Follow-up:** It offers special benefits to women who have limited or no access to routine postpartum care.
- **Cost-Effective and Convenient:** There is no need for frequent hospital visits, it incurs no further cost, and it boasts high continuation rates with good user satisfaction.
- **Lactation Friendly:** It is absolutely safe for lactating women, as it does not have any adverse estrogen-related effects on breast milk volume or composition.
- **Reversibility:** There is an immediate return to fertility following its removal.

**Disadvantages and Complications**

- **High Expulsion Rate:** The spontaneous expulsion rate is slightly high (5-10%) following puerperal or postabortal insertions compared to interval insertions.
- **Uterine Perforation:** While overall rare (1 in 1,000 insertions), partial or complete perforation is more liable to be met during the lactational period when the uterus remains soft and small.
- **Missing Threads:** The threads may not be visible through the cervical os if the device is pulled up by the involuting uterus, or if it is expelled outside unnoticed by the patient.

**Contraindications Specific to Postpartum Period**

- Postpartum endometritis or infected abortion in the last 3 months.
- Acute pelvic infection or sexually transmitted infections (STIs).
- Undiagnosed genital tract bleeding.
- Significant immunosuppression.
- Distortion of the shape of the uterine cavity (e.g., congenital malformations or fibroids).

**HEMATOLOGICAL CHANGES IN PREGNANCY**

During pregnancy, the maternal hematological system undergoes significant physiological adaptations to meet the demands of the growing fetus, protect the mother from blood loss during delivery, and ensure optimal uteroplacental perfusion. 

**1. BLOOD VOLUME**
*   **Total Blood Volume:** Markedly raised, starting from the 6th week of pregnancy. It expands rapidly and reaches a maximum of 40–50% above the nonpregnant level at 30–34 weeks, after which it remains static until delivery. The total volume increases from approximately 4000 mL to 5500 mL (an increment of ~1500 mL).
*   **Plasma Volume:** Starts increasing by 6 weeks and plateaus at 30 weeks. The maximum increase is to the extent of 40–50% (an addition of ~1250 mL, increasing from 2500 mL to 3750 mL). The increase is greater in multigravidae, multiple pregnancies, and with larger babies.

**2. RED BLOOD CORPUSCLES (RBC) AND HEMOGLOBIN**
*   **RBC Mass:** Begins to increase at about 10 weeks and continues until term without plateauing. The RBC volume increases by 20–30% (from 1400 mL to 1750 mL, an addition of 350 mL). Iron supplementation can increase the RBC mass by 30%.
*   **Erythropoiesis:** Regulated by increased fetal demand for oxygen; maternal erythropoietin levels are raised, and the reticulocyte count increases by 2%. 
*   **Hemoglobin Mass:** The total hemoglobin mass increases by 18–20% (from 475 g to 560 g, an addition of 85 g). 

**3. HEMODILUTION AND PHYSIOLOGICAL ANEMIA**
*   **Pathophysiology:** Because the increase in plasma volume (40–50%) is disproportionately greater than the increase in RBC volume (20–30%), a state of hemodilution occurs.
*   **Indices:** This leads to an apparent fall in hemoglobin concentration by about 2 g% near term and a decrease in the hematocrit (PCV) value from 38% to 32%. This is termed *physiological anemia of pregnancy*.
*   **Advantages of Relative Hemodilution:** 
    1.  Diminished blood viscosity ensures optimum gaseous exchange between maternal and fetal circulations.
    2.  Protects the mother against the adverse cardiovascular effects of supine and erect postures.
    3.  Protects the mother against the adverse effects of blood loss during delivery (less RBC loss per volume of blood shed).

**4. LEUKOCYTES AND IMMUNE SYSTEM**
*   **WBC Count:** Neutrophilic leukocytosis occurs, with the count increasing to about 8,000/mm³ during pregnancy, and reaching up to 20,000/mm³ during labor. This may be driven by rising estrogen and cortisol levels.
*   **Immune Modulation:** The maternal immune system modulates away from a cell-mediated (Th1) cytotoxic response toward increased humoral and innate (Th2) immune responses to prevent fetal rejection.

**5. PLASMA PROTEINS**
*   **Total Protein:** Increases from 180 g (nonpregnant) to 230 g near term.
*   **Concentration:** Due to hemodilution, the overall plasma protein concentration decreases from 7 g% to 6 g%. 
*   **Fractions:** Albumin concentration falls significantly (from 4.3 to 3 g%), while globulin levels slightly increase (from 2.7 to 3 g%).
*   **A:G Ratio:** The normal Albumin:Globulin ratio of 1.7:1 is diminished to 1:1. 
*   **Effect:** This reduction in protein concentration leads to diminished blood viscosity and a decrease in colloid osmotic tension, contributing to physiological edema.

**6. BLOOD COAGULATION FACTORS (HYPERCOAGULABLE STATE)**
Pregnancy is a hypercoagulable state designed to control blood loss and ensure hemostasis after placental separation.
*   **Fibrinogen (Factor I):** Levels increase by 50%, rising from 200–400 mg/dL to 300–600 mg/dL.
*   **Increased Factors:** Activities and plasma levels of factors I, VII, VIII, IX, and X are markedly increased. 
*   **Unchanged/Decreased Factors:** Levels of factors II, V, and XII remain unchanged or are mildly increased. Factors XI and XIII are slightly decreased. 
*   **Platelets:** The platelet count remains static or shows a slight physiological fall to the extent of 15% (gestational thrombocytopenia) due to hemodilution and increased platelet consumption, but this does not cause pregnancy complications.
*   **Fibrinolytic Activity:** Markedly depressed during pregnancy and labor, returning to normal within 1 hour after the delivery of the placenta.
*   **Clotting Time:** Remains unaffected.

**7. ERYTHROCYTE SEDIMENTATION RATE (ESR)**
*   The ESR increases markedly (four-fold) from 10 mm/h to 40 mm/h, primarily due to the elevated fibrinogen and globulin levels. Because of this physiological rise, ESR has little diagnostic value during pregnancy.

**8. IRON INDICES**
*   **Serum Iron:** Slightly lowered (65–75 µg/100 mL).
*   **Total Iron Binding Capacity (TIBC):** Increased to 300–400 µg/100 mL.
*   **Saturation Percentage:** Drops to less than 16%. 
*   *Note:* The total iron requirement during pregnancy is approximately 1,000 mg, necessitating routine supplementation.

14. Puerperal sterilization.
**PUERPERAL STERILIZATION (TUBECTOMY)**

**DEFINITION & TIMING** Puerperal sterilization is a permanent method of female surgical contraception performed during the puerperium.

- **Ideal Timing:** It is typically performed **24 to 48 hours following delivery**.
- It can also be done concurrently with a cesarean section.

**INDICATIONS**

1. **Family Planning:** Principal indication for couples who have completed their desired family size (commonly a two-child norm).
2. **Socio-economic:** Adopted by individuals to limit family size due to socio-economic constraints.
3. **Medicosurgical Indications:** Medical conditions where repeated pregnancies pose a severe threat to the mother’s life (e.g., severe heart disease, chronic renal disease, uncontrolled diabetes, or severe hypertension).
4. **Obstetric Indications:** Concurrently done during a third repeat cesarean section or to avoid future obstetric complications.
5. **Risk-reducing Salpingectomy:** To reduce the risk of serous ovarian and peritoneal cancers.

**PRE-STERILIZATION COUNSELING & CONSENT** Thorough counseling is a mandatory prerequisite:

- Must involve both partners.
- Counsel regarding the **permanency** of the procedure and the issue of reversibility (reversal is technically difficult and carries an increased risk of ectopic pregnancy).
- Discuss the failure rate (0.7% overall) and potential risks/side effects.
- Provide options for alternative Long-Acting Reversible Contraceptives (LARCs) like Cu-T 380A or LNG-IUS.
- **Informed written consent** must be obtained prior to the procedure.

**SURGICAL APPROACH & PROCEDURE**

- **Route:** Minilaparotomy (Mini-Lap) or conventional laparotomy. _Note: Laparoscopic sterilization should NOT be performed within 6 weeks of delivery due to the enlarged, bulky uterus_.
- **Anesthesia:** Local anesthesia (with premedication like pethidine and phenergan), spinal, or general anesthesia.
- **Incision:** A small transverse or vertical incision is made **two fingers' breadth (1 inch) below the fundal height** (which is easily palpable per abdomen during the puerperium).

**Common Techniques of Tubal Occlusion:**

1. **Pomeroy’s Technique (Most Common & Gold Standard):**
    - The fallopian tube is identified by its fimbrial end.
    - A loop of the tube is made at the junction of the proximal and middle third (isthmus and ampulla).
    - The base of the loop is ligated firmly with **No. '0' chromic catgut** (an absorbable suture).
    - About 1–1.5 cm of the loop distal to the ligature is excised.
    - _Mechanism of success:_ As the catgut absorbs, the cut ends pull apart, become independently sealed, and separate widely, preventing recanalization.
    - _Failure rate:_ Lowest among methods (0.1–0.5%).
2. **Irving’s Method:** The tube is ligated and divided. The medial cut end is buried into the posterior uterine myometrium, and the distal end is buried in the mesosalpinx.
3. **Madlener’s Technique:** The loop is crushed and tied with non-absorbable silk without excision. _Abandoned today due to a high failure rate (1.5–7%)_.
4. **Kroener’s Method:** Fimbriectomy (removal of the fimbrial end).

**ADVANTAGES OF PUERPERAL TIMING**

- **Technical Simplicity:** The fundus is high up in the abdomen, making the tubes easily accessible through a small sub-umbilical incision.
- **Convenience:** The patient is already hospitalized for delivery; hospital stay and rest cover recovery from both childbirth and surgery simultaneously.

**COMPLICATIONS** **1. Immediate (Surgical/Anesthetic):**

- Wound infection or peritonitis.
- Hemorrhage (primary or slipping of ligature), broad ligament hematoma.
- Injury to adjacent viscera (rectum, bladder, or blood vessels).
- Anesthetic hazards.
- _Mortality:_ Extremely low (approx. 7 per 100,000 for puerperal ligations).

**2. Remote (Long-term):**

- **Method Failure:** Overall failure rate is 0.7%. If pregnancy occurs following a failed tubal sterilization, there is a very high risk of it being an **ectopic pregnancy**.
- **Post-Ligation Syndrome:** Characterized by chronic pelvic pain, congestive dysmenorrhea, menorrhagia/irregular periods, and cystic ovaries (likely due to vascular disruption in the mesosalpinx).
- Incisional hernia.
- Psychological regret and alteration in libido.

**REVERSIBILITY** If the patient later desires fertility, reversal requires microsurgical tubotubal anastomosis. While anatomic patency and pregnancy rates after microsurgical reversal of clips/rings can be up to 80%, the rate is lower for resection methods, and the risk of ectopic pregnancy remains elevated. In vitro fertilization (IVF) is an alternative to surgical reversal.

15. HELLP

**HELLP SYNDROME**

**INTRODUCTION & DEFINITION**

- **HELLP** is an acronym for **H**emolysis, **E**levated **L**iver enzymes, and **L**ow **P**latelet count.
- It is a severe complication or variant of pre-eclampsia, occurring in 10–15% of severe pre-eclampsia cases.
- Importantly, HELLP syndrome can be diagnosed even in the absence of maternal hypertension or proteinuria.

**PATHOPHYSIOLOGY**

- The underlying pathology is widespread endothelial injury and dysfunction, which leads to thrombocytopenia and a rise in fibrin degradation products.
- **Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia** is the hallmark of the triad of HELLP syndrome.
- Parenchymal necrosis of the liver (starting at the periphery of the lobule due to thrombosis of arterioles) causes an elevation in hepatic enzymes.

**CLINICAL FEATURES**

- Patients commonly present with nausea, vomiting, and epigastric or right upper quadrant pain.
- Right upper quadrant pain is specifically due to the stretching of Glisson's capsule surrounding the liver.

**DIAGNOSTIC CRITERIA & INVESTIGATIONS** The diagnosis requires biochemical and hematological evaluation demonstrating the triad:

1. **Hemolysis (H):** Abnormal peripheral blood smear showing schistocytosis and burr cells, elevated Lactic Dehydrogenase (LDH) >600 IU/L, and elevated serum bilirubin >1.2 mg/dL.
2. **Elevated Liver Enzymes (EL):** Serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels >70 IU/L.
3. **Low Platelets (LP):** Thrombocytopenia defined as a platelet count <100,000/mm³.

**COMPLICATIONS**

- **Maternal:** Subcapsular liver hematoma formation, which may progress to spontaneous liver rupture causing sudden hypotension and hemoperitoneum. Other severe risks include Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) (15%), abruptio placentae, acute renal failure, pulmonary edema, severe ascites, Adult Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS), sepsis, and maternal death (up to 25%).
- **Perinatal:** High morbidity and a perinatal mortality rate ranging between 5% and 60%, largely due to preterm delivery, prematurity, Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS), and sepsis.

**DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS**

- HELLP syndrome must be differentiated from Acute Fatty Liver of Pregnancy (AFLP), Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (HUS), and Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP).
- _Note:_ In HELLP, hypertension and proteinuria are typically more prominent than in AFLP, whereas AFLP presents with severe hypoglycemia and hyperuricemia; TTP and HUS often present with more profound renal failure or neurological abnormalities.

**MANAGEMENT** The principles of management are similar to those of severe pre-eclampsia and eclampsia. A multidisciplinary approach in an Intensive Care Unit (ICU) is required.

**1. Medical Management:**

- **Antiseizure Prophylaxis:** Intravenous Magnesium Sulfate ($MgSO_{4}$) is the drug of choice and is highly recommended to prevent eclamptic seizures.
- **Corticosteroid Therapy:** Administration of corticosteroids is crucial. It improves maternal outcomes (increases platelet count and urinary output) and perinatal outcomes (enhances fetal pulmonary maturity, and decreases the incidence of intraventricular hemorrhage and necrotizing enterocolitis).
- **Blood Products:** Platelet transfusion is indicated if the maternal platelet count falls below 50,000/mm³.
- **Antihypertensives:** Strict blood pressure control using agents like Labetalol, Hydralazine, or Nifedipine is required if hypertension is severe.

**2. Obstetric Management (Delivery):**

- Careful assessment of maternal and fetal status is required, followed by expeditious delivery, which is the definitive treatment.
- Cesarean section is the most common mode of delivery.
- Epidural anesthesia can be safely administered provided the maternal platelet count is >70,000/mm³.
- Expectant management (bed rest, plasma volume expansion, antithrombotics, and steroids) is selectively reserved for pregnancies <34 weeks, provided there is no multiorgan dysfunction.

**3. Postpartum Care & Prognosis:**

- The patient must be closely monitored in an ICU until there is a clear improvement in platelet count, urine output, blood pressure, and liver enzymes.
- Women who have had HELLP syndrome face a recurrence risk of 3–19% in subsequent pregnancies. They also have a higher risk for future complications like chronic hypertension and cardiovascular disease.


16. APGAR Score.

**APGAR SCORE**

**INTRODUCTION**

- Designed by Dr. Virginia Apgar in 1953, the Apgar score is a clinical scoring procedure used for the simple understanding and evaluation of the cardiopulmonary status of a newborn baby.

**TIMING OF ASSESSMENT**

- Classically, the rating is recorded at **1 minute** and **5 minutes** after birth.
- If the score remains significantly depressed at 5 minutes, it should be evaluated again after 15 minutes.
- _Note:_ In certain circumstances where the newborn is severely compromised, it is inappropriate to delay resuscitative efforts just to wait for the 1-minute Apgar score to be obtained.

**PARAMETERS AND SCORING SYSTEM** The score evaluates five parameters, each given a score of 0, 1, or 2, making a maximum total score of 10.

|Parameters (Signs)|Score 0|Score 1|Score 2|
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
|**Respiratory Effort**|Absent|Slow, irregular|Good, crying effort|
|**Heart Rate**|Absent|<100 bpm|>100 bpm|
|**Muscle Tone**|Flaccid|Flexion of extremities|Active body movements|
|**Reflex Irritability**|No response|Grimace|Cough or sneeze|
|**Color**|Blue, pale|Body pink, extremities blue|Complete pink|

**INTERPRETATION OF THE SCORE** Based on the total score out of 10, the newborn's clinical state is classified as follows:

- **8–10:** No depression (essentially normal).
- **5–7:** Mild depression.
- **3–4:** Moderate depression.
- **0–2:** Severe depression.

**CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE AND PROGNOSIS**

- **Long-term Correlation:** The 5-minute Apgar score is of more clinical value than the 1-minute score for long-term neurological correlation.
- **Sequelae of Asphyxia:** An Apgar score of 0–3 extending up to 20 minutes of age is a major prognostic factor for severe sequelae and multiorgan failure following perinatal asphyxia.
- **Limitations:** The Apgar score alone should _not_ be taken as definitive evidence of long-term neurological damage (such as Cerebral Palsy). Cord blood pH is considered a better parameter to assess fetal oxygenation status.

17. Missed abortion.

**MISSED ABORTION (MISSED MISCARRIAGE)**

**DEFINITION**

- It is a clinical condition where the fetus is dead and retained inside the uterus for a variable period. It is also referred to as early fetal demise.

**PATHOLOGY & ULTRASONOGRAPHIC (USG) FINDINGS**

- The exact causes for the prolonged retention of the dead fetus inside the uterus remain unclear.
- Pathologically, the liquor amnii gets absorbed, and the placenta becomes pale, thin, and sometimes adherent.
- **Transvaginal Sonography (TVS) Diagnostic Criteria:**
    - Crown-Rump Length (CRL) > 7 mm with no cardiac motion seen.
    - Mean Sac Diameter (MSD) > 25 mm with no visible embryo (anembryonic sac).
    - Presence of a collapsing gestational sac, an irregular amnion, and yolk sac calcification strongly indicate embryonic demise.

**CLINICAL FEATURES** The patient usually initially presents with features of a threatened miscarriage, which is subsequently followed by:

1. Persistence of a brownish vaginal discharge.
2. Subsidence of the subjective symptoms of pregnancy (e.g., morning sickness, breast tenderness).
3. Absence of fetal heart sounds (FHS) upon examination with a Doppler ultrasound.
4. Real-time ultrasonography revealing a definitive absence of fetal cardiac motion and fetal movements.

**COMPLICATIONS**

- The complications are generally similar to those seen in Intrauterine Fetal Death (IUFD).
- Blood coagulation disorders may occur, though they are less likely to develop in early missed miscarriages compared to late fetal demise.

**MANAGEMENT** The management approach can be expectant, medical, or surgical, largely depending on the gestational size of the uterus.

**1. When the Uterus is Less Than 12 Weeks:**

- **Expectant Management:** Many women will undergo spontaneous expulsion of the conceptus without intervention.
- **Medical Management:** Prostaglandin E1 (Misoprostol) 800 µg is administered vaginally in the posterior fornix. This dose can be repeated after 24 hours if necessary. Expulsion usually occurs within 48 hours.
- **Surgical Management:** Suction evacuation or dilatation and evacuation is performed either as the primary definitive treatment or if the medical method fails. Care must be taken as there is a risk of brisk hemorrhage and damage to the uterine walls during the operation.

**2. When the Uterus is More Than 12 Weeks:**

- **Medical Management (First-line):** Prostaglandins are more effective than oxytocin for induction in these cases.
    - _Combined Regimen:_ Tablet Mifepristone 200 mg orally, followed by Misoprostol 800 mg via buccal, vaginal, or sublingual route.
    - _Alternative Regimen:_ Misoprostol 800 mg alone by any route (buccal, vaginal, or sublingual).
    - _Oxytocin Infusion:_ 10–20 units of oxytocin in 500 mL of normal saline infused at 30 drops/min. If this fails, escalating doses up to a maximum of 200 units may be used with careful monitoring.
- **Surgical Management:** Dilatation and evacuation can be performed once the cervix is adequately softened with PGE1. If the cervix is unfavorable, it is dilated using mechanical dilators or a laminaria tent, followed by the slow evacuation of the uterine cavity.

**Follow-up Care:**

- Following medical expulsion, an ultrasonography must be performed to document an completely empty uterine cavity.
- If the ultrasound shows retained tissues, an Evacuation of the Retained Products of Conception (ERPC) must be done.


18. Maternal mortality ratio.
**MATERNAL MORTALITY RATIO (MMR)**

**DEFINITIONS**

- **Maternal Death:** Death of a woman while pregnant or within 42 days of the termination of pregnancy, irrespective of the duration and the site of pregnancy, from any cause related to or aggravated by the pregnancy or its management, but not from accidental or incidental causes.
- **Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR):** It is expressed as the number of maternal deaths per 100,000 live births.
- **Maternal Mortality Rate:** Indicates the number of maternal deaths divided by the number of women of reproductive age (15–49 years), expressed per 100,000 women of reproductive age per year.

**EPIDEMIOLOGY & STATISTICS**

- **Global Target (SDG 2030):** To reduce the global average MMR to less than 70 per 100,000 live births, with no country exceeding 140/100,000 live births.
- **India:** The MMR in India has significantly declined to 99 per 100,000 live births (2020).
- **Lifetime Risk:** The probability of dying from pregnancy-related complications for a woman in her reproductive age. In India, it is presently 0.3%.

**CAUSES OF MATERNAL DEATHS** The causes are broadly classified into three categories:

**1. Direct Obstetric Deaths (75%)** Resulting from complications of pregnancy, delivery, or their management.

- **Hemorrhage (20–25%):** Mostly Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH), antepartum hemorrhage (abruptio placentae, placenta previa), and ectopic pregnancy.
- **Hypertensive Disorders (15–20%):** Severe pre-eclampsia and eclampsia.
- **Puerperal Sepsis / Infection (11%):** Associated with premature rupture of membranes, prolonged, and obstructed labor.
- **Obstructed Labor (9%):** Due to cephalopelvic disproportion, abnormal lie, or malpresentation.
- **Unsafe Abortion (8%):** Lack of safe abortion services and post-abortion care.
- _Note: Suicidal death is also considered a direct death._

**2. Indirect Obstetric Deaths (25%)** Resulting from conditions present before or developed during pregnancy, aggravated by the physiological effects of pregnancy and labor.

- **Anemia (15–20%):** The most important single indirect cause of maternal death in developing countries.
- **Viral Hepatitis (5–10%):** Endemic in India; deaths mostly occur in the last trimester due to hepatic coma and coagulation failure.
- **Other Medical Disorders:** Cardiac disease, diabetes, thyroid disease, malaria, and HIV.

**3. Non-Obstetric or Fortuitous Deaths** Deaths due to accidental or incidental causes like typhoid, accidents, and other infectious diseases.

**RISK FACTORS & THE "THREE DELAYS" MODEL**

- **Associated Factors:** Extremes of age (>35 years risk is 3-4 times higher), grand multiparity (Para 5 or above), low socioeconomic status, and lack of antenatal care.
- **The Three Delays:** The Government of India identifies three major delays contributing to maternal mortality:
    1. **Delay-I:** Delay in recognizing the problem and deciding to seek care (lack of birth preparedness).
    2. **Delay-II:** Delay in reaching the health facility (lack of transport/referral facility).
    3. **Delay-III:** Delay in receiving treatment at the center (due to unequipped health facilities, lack of trained personnel, blood, or medicines).

**PREVENTIVE MEASURES & MANAGEMENT** Most maternal deaths (80%) are preventable through effective policy interventions and limited investment.

- **Essential Obstetric Care:** Basic antenatal, intranatal, and postnatal care with continuous risk assessment.
- **Skilled Birth Attendant (SBA):** Presence of an SBA at every delivery and maintenance of "three cleans" to minimize sepsis.
- **Emergency Obstetric Care (EmOC):** Provision of comprehensive EmOC at First Referral Units (FRUs) including cesarean delivery, blood transfusion, manual removal of placenta, and anesthetic services.
- **Family Planning & Safe Abortion:** Preventing unwanted pregnancies and unsafe abortions by providing accessible, client-oriented contraceptive services.
- **Correction of Anemia:** Routine iron-folic acid supplementation and admission for severe anemia (Hb < 7 g/dL).
- **Maternal Death Audits:** Confidential Enquiry into Maternal Death (CEMD) and Maternal Deaths Surveillance and Response (MDSR) to evaluate causes and avoidable factors.
- **National Programs:** Successful implementation of Reproductive and Child Health (RCH) care, Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakaram (JSSK), and LaQshya (Labor room quality improvement).


19. Tocolysis.
**TOCOLYSIS / TOCOLYTIC AGENTS**

**DEFINITION AND OBJECTIVES** Tocolytics are drugs used to inhibit or arrest uterine contractions, primarily to delay preterm labor and delivery. The main objectives of short-term tocolytic therapy (usually 48 hours) are:

1. To allow time for the administration of maternal corticosteroids (which enhance fetal lung maturation and reduce the incidence of respiratory distress syndrome, intraventricular hemorrhage, necrotizing enterocolitis, and patent ductus arteriosus).
2. To permit the in utero transfer of the fetus to a tertiary care center equipped with advanced Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) facilities.

**CONTRAINDICATIONS FOR TOCOLYSIS** Tocolysis is withheld if continuation of pregnancy poses a greater risk than preterm delivery.

- **Maternal:** Uncontrolled diabetes, thyrotoxicosis, severe hypertension, cardiac disease, and hemorrhage in pregnancy (e.g., abruptio placentae or placenta previa).
- **Fetal:** Nonreassuring fetal status (fetal distress), intrauterine fetal death (IUFD), lethal congenital malformations, and gestational age $\ge$ 34 weeks.
- **Obstetric/Others:** Premature rupture of membranes (PROM), chorioamnionitis, and cervical dilatation greater than 4 cm.

---

**CLASSIFICATION OF TOCOLYTIC AGENTS** The commonly used drugs include Calcium channel blockers, Betamimetics, Magnesium sulfate, Prostaglandin synthetase inhibitors, Oxytocin receptor antagonists, and Nitric oxide donors.

**1. Calcium Channel Blockers (e.g., Nifedipine, Nicardipine, Verapamil)**

- **Mechanism of Action:** Blocks the entry of calcium into the cell, reducing intracellular free calcium necessary for muscle contraction. It is considered equally effective to Magnesium sulfate.
- **Dose:** Nifedipine 10–20 mg orally (not sublingually) every 3–6 hours.
- **Side Effects:** Maternal flushing, headache, nausea, hypotension, and tachycardia. Fetal/neonatal tachycardia.
- _Precaution:_ Simultaneous use with Magnesium sulfate or betamimetics should be avoided due to synergistic and hazardous effects.

**2. Betamimetics (e.g., Terbutaline, Ritodrine, Isoxsuprine)**

- **Mechanism of Action:** Stimulates $\beta_2$ receptors, activating adenylate cyclase and cAMP. This reduces intracellular free calcium and inhibits the activation of Myosin Light Chain Kinase (MLCK), leading to smooth muscle relaxation.
- **Dose:**
    - _Terbutaline:_ 0.25 mg subcutaneously (SC) every 3–4 hours.
    - _Ritodrine:_ IV infusion at 50 $\mu$g/min, increased by 50 $\mu$g every 10 min until contractions cease, maintained for 12 hours after cessation.
- **Side Effects:** High maternal side effects, especially parenterally, including tachycardia, palpitation, pulmonary edema, hypotension, cardiac failure, hyperglycemia, hypokalemia, and hyperinsulinemia. Fetal tachycardia and heart failure.
- _Contraindications:_ Maternal cardiac disease and diabetes.

**3. Magnesium Sulfate ($MgSO_4$)**

- **Mechanism of Action:** Acts by competitive inhibition of calcium ions at the motor end plate, reducing calcium influx. It also acts as a neuroprotector against cerebral palsy when given before 32 weeks.
- **Dose:** Loading dose of 4–6 g IV (10–20% solution) over 20–30 min, followed by a maintenance IV infusion of 1–2 g/hr. Continued for 12 hours after contractions stop.
- **Side Effects:** Maternal flushing, perspiration, headache, and muscle weakness. Fetal side effects include decreased FHR variability.
- _Precaution:_ Monitor respiratory rate, knee jerks, and urine output. Antidote is Calcium Gluconate. Contraindicated in myasthenia gravis and impaired renal function.

**4. Prostaglandin Synthetase Inhibitors / NSAIDs (e.g., Indomethacin, Sulindac)**

- **Mechanism of Action:** Cyclo-oxygenase inhibitor. Reduces synthesis of prostaglandins (PGs), which in turn reduces intracellular free $Ca^{++}$ and inhibits myometrial contractions.
- **Dose:** Loading dose 50 mg orally (PO) or per rectum (PR), followed by 25 mg every 6 hours for 48 hours.
- **Side Effects:** Maternal heartburn, GI bleeding, asthma, renal injury, and platelet dysfunction. Fetal side effects include premature constriction of the ductus arteriosus, oligohydramnios, and neonatal pulmonary hypertension.
- _Contraindications:_ Hepatic disease, active peptic ulcer, and coagulation disorders.

**5. Oxytocin Receptor Antagonists (e.g., Atosiban)**

- **Mechanism of Action:** Blocks myometrial oxytocin receptors. It inhibits intracellular calcium release and the release of prostaglandins, thereby inhibiting myometrial contractions.
- **Dose/Usage:** IV infusion 300 $\mu$g/min. Usually used between 24 and 32 weeks.
- **Side Effects:** Nausea, vomiting, and rarely chest pain.

**6. Nitric Oxide Donors (e.g., Glyceryl Trinitrate - GTN)**

- **Mechanism of Action:** Acts as a smooth muscle relaxant.
- **Side Effects:** Headache and hypotension. Can be administered via patches.

---

**OTHER OBSTETRIC INDICATIONS FOR TOCOLYSIS** Apart from preterm labor, tocolytic agents are utilized in the following clinical scenarios:

- **External Cephalic Version (ECV):** To relax the hypertonic/irritable uterus and increase the success rate of version (e.g., Terbutaline 0.25 mg SC).
- **Uterine Tachysystole / Hyperstimulation:** To reverse excessive uterine contractions (e.g., induced by oxytocin or prostaglandins) causing nonreassuring fetal status (Injection Terbutaline 0.25 mg SC is used).
- **Precipitate Labor:** To suppress hyperactive uterine contractions if a patient has a history of precipitate labor.

20. Antihypertensives in pregnancy.

**ANTIHYPERTENSIVES IN PREGNANCY**

**INDICATIONS AND OBJECTIVES**

- **Primary Indication:** Antihypertensive drugs are essential for women with severe pre-eclampsia or chronic hypertension (Blood Pressure $\ge$ 160/110 mm Hg) to protect the mother from life-threatening complications such as eclampsia, cerebral hemorrhage, stroke, cardiac failure, and placental abruption.
- **Target BP:** The aim is to reduce the BP to a safe level, maintaining the systolic BP between 140–160 mm Hg and diastolic BP between 90–100 mm Hg. If there is a risk of target organ damage (e.g., renal failure), BP should be maintained at $\le$ 140 mm Hg systolic.
- **Caution:** Routine use of antihypertensives in mild or moderate hypertension is generally not favored, as aggressively lowering the maternal blood pressure can diminish uteroplacental perfusion, which may be detrimental to the fetus.

**FIRST-LINE DRUGS FOR HYPERTENSIVE EMERGENCIES (ACUTE THERAPY)** These drugs are used to rapidly lower BP in severe pre-eclampsia or eclampsia.

**1. Labetalol**

- **Mechanism:** Combined $\alpha$ and $\beta$ adrenergic receptor blocking agent.
- **Dose (IV):** 10–20 mg IV over 2 minutes; followed by 20–80 mg every 10–30 minutes until the desired effect is achieved (Maximum dose: 220–300 mg). Alternatively, 40 mg/hour continuous infusion.
- **Side Effects:** Tremors, headache, and maternal hypotension. May cause neonatal bradycardia.
- **Contraindications:** Asthma, myocardial disease, congestive cardiac failure, heart block, and significant bradycardia.

**2. Hydralazine**

- **Mechanism:** Direct vascular smooth muscle relaxant causing peripheral arteriolar vasodilatation, thereby increasing cardiac output and renal blood flow.
- **Dose (IV):** 5–10 mg IV over 2 minutes, repeated every 20–30 minutes if needed (Maximum dose: 20–30 mg).
- **Side Effects:** Maternal reflex tachycardia, hypotension, palpitations, headache, fluid retention, lupus-like syndrome, and abnormal FHR tracings. Neonatal thrombocytopenia may occur.
- _Note:_ Diuretics may be used concurrently for sodium retention, and propranolol for arrhythmias.

**3. Nifedipine**

- **Mechanism:** Calcium channel blocker; causes direct arteriolar vasodilatation by inhibiting slow inward calcium channels.
- **Dose (Oral):** 10 mg orally (never sublingual), repeated in 30 minutes if necessary.
- **Side Effects:** Flushing, reflex tachycardia, palpitations, headache, hypotension, and inhibition of labor.
- _Caution:_ Simultaneous use with Magnesium Sulfate ($MgSO_4$) can be hazardous due to synergistic hypotensive and neuromuscular blocking effects.

**DRUGS FOR MAINTENANCE THERAPY (CHRONIC HYPERTENSION)**

**1. Methyldopa**

- **Mechanism:** Central and peripheral antiadrenergic action. It is considered highly effective and safe for both mother and fetus.
- **Dose:** 250 mg orally twice daily (BID), which can be increased up to 1 g four times daily (QID) depending on the response.
- **Side Effects:** Postural hypotension, excessive sedation, hemolytic anemia, sodium retention, positive Coombs' test, and postpartum depression. Fetal intestinal ileus may occur.
- **Contraindications:** Hepatic disorders and psychiatric patients. It is avoided postpartum due to the risk of exacerbating postpartum depression.

**2. Labetalol (Oral)**

- **Dose:** 100 mg orally BID/TID, which can be increased up to 2400 mg daily.

**3. Nifedipine (Oral)**

- **Dose:** 5–10 mg TID, maximum 60–120 mg/day OR 10–20 mg every 4–6 hours (max 240 mg/24h).

**DRUGS USED AS A LAST RESORT (ICU SETTING ONLY)**

**1. Sodium Nitroprusside**

- **Mechanism:** Direct arterial and venous vasodilator.
- **Dose:** IV infusion of 0.25–5 (or up to 8) $\mu$g/kg/min.
- **Precautions:** Used only for severe hypertensive crisis unresponsive to other drugs, for a very short duration (e.g., 10 minutes).
- **Side effects:** Severe maternal hypotension, nausea, vomiting. Causes fetal toxicity due to cyanide and thiocyanate metabolites.

**2. Nitroglycerin**

- **Mechanism:** Relaxes mainly venous but also arterial smooth muscle.
- **Dose:** IV infusion of 5 $\mu$g/min, increasing every 3–5 minutes up to 100 $\mu$g/min.
- **Side effects:** Tachycardia, headache, methemoglobinemia. Contraindicated in hypertensive encephalopathy as it increases intracranial pressure.

**CONTRAINDICATED ANTIHYPERTENSIVES IN PREGNANCY**

- **ACE Inhibitors (e.g., Captopril, Enalapril) & Angiotensin Receptor Blockers (ARBs) (e.g., Telmisartan):** These are **absolutely contraindicated** during pregnancy (FDA Category X).
- **Teratogenic Effects:** Use during pregnancy causes fetal renal tubular dysgenesis, neonatal renal failure, oligohydramnios, pulmonary hypoplasia, IUGR, and CVS/CNS malformations.
- _Note:_ Women on ACE inhibitors or ARBs must be switched to safer alternatives during preconceptional counseling or immediately upon confirming pregnancy.

**POSTPARTUM MANAGEMENT & LACTATION**

- Antihypertensive therapy must be continued postpartum if the blood pressure remains elevated (Systolic >150 mm Hg or Diastolic >100 mm Hg).
- For breastfeeding mothers, oral Labetalol, Nifedipine, or Enalapril (an ACE inhibitor safe _after_ delivery) are the preferred choices.
- Methyldopa is avoided in the puerperium due to the high risk of postpartum depression.


21. Stages of labour.
**STAGES OF LABOR**

The events of normal labor are conventionally divided into four stages.

**1. FIRST STAGE OF LABOR (Cervical Stage)**

- **Definition:** It starts from the onset of true labor pain and ends with the full dilatation of the cervix (10 cm).
- **Duration:** The average duration is 12 hours in primigravidae and 6 hours (WHO criteria note up to 10 hours) in multiparae.
- **Main Events:** The primary events are the progressive dilatation and effacement (taking up) of the cervix, along with the full formation of the lower uterine segment.
- **Phases:** It is structurally divided into two phases:
    - _Latent Phase:_ The period from the onset of true labor pain until cervical dilatation reaches 5 cm (WHO guidelines) or 6 cm (ACOG guidelines). The average duration is 8.6 hours in primigravidae and 5.3 hours in multiparae.
    - _Active Phase:_ Characterized by more rapid cervical dilatation, starting from 5 cm (WHO) and continuing until full cervical dilatation.

**2. SECOND STAGE OF LABOR (Stage of Expulsion)**

- **Definition:** It begins with the full dilatation of the cervix and ends with the expulsion of the fetus from the birth canal.
- **Duration:** The average duration is 2 hours (WHO notes up to 3 hours) in primigravidae and 30 minutes (WHO notes up to 2 hours) in multiparae.
- **Main Events:** This stage is chiefly concerned with the descent and delivery of the fetus through the maternal pelvis.
- **Phases:** It consists of two distinct phases:
    - _Propulsive (Passive) Phase:_ Starts from full cervical dilatation and continues until the fetal head descends and touches the pelvic floor.
    - _Expulsive (Active) Phase:_ Characterized by the mother experiencing an irresistible, involuntary urge to bear down (push), which culminates in the delivery of the baby.

**3. THIRD STAGE OF LABOR (Placental Stage)**

- **Definition:** It begins immediately after the expulsion of the fetus and ends with the expulsion of the placenta and fetal membranes (afterbirths).
- **Duration:** The average duration is about 15 minutes for both primigravidae and multiparae. However, with Active Management of the Third Stage of Labor (AMTSL), this duration is significantly reduced to approximately 5 minutes.
- **Main Events:** Comprises three key steps: the separation of the placenta from the decidua basalis, its descent into the lower uterine segment or vagina, and finally its complete expulsion.
- **Hemostasis Mechanism:** Bleeding from the placental site is naturally controlled by the effective contraction and retraction of the myometrium, which acts as "living ligatures" to clamp torn uterine sinuses, supported further by thrombosis and myotamponade.

**4. FOURTH STAGE OF LABOR (Stage of Observation)**

- **Definition:** It is a critical period of observation lasting for at least 1 hour following the complete expulsion of the afterbirths.
- **Purpose:** To closely monitor both the mother and the newborn to ensure their stability and well-being.
- **Main Events:** Involves vigilant monitoring of maternal vitals (recording pulse and blood pressure), assessing uterine retraction (ensuring the uterus remains firm and well-contracted), and strictly checking for any abnormal vaginal bleeding to promptly identify and manage postpartum hemorrhage.


22. Methods of delivering after coming head in vaginal breech delivery.
**METHODS OF DELIVERING THE AFTERCOMING HEAD IN VAGINAL BREECH DELIVERY**

The delivery of the aftercoming head is the most crucial stage of breech delivery. The ideal time interval between the delivery of the umbilicus and the birth of the mouth should preferably be 5 to 10 minutes.

There are three common and highly effective methods to deliver the aftercoming head.

**1. BURNS-MARSHALL METHOD** This is a widely practiced method where the baby's body is used to facilitate delivery.

- **Technique:** The baby is allowed to hang down by its own weight.
- An assistant applies firm suprapubic pressure with the flat of the hand in a downward and backward direction (directed more towards the fetal sinciput) to promote flexion of the head.
- When the nape of the fetal neck becomes visible under the pubic arch, the operator grasps the baby by the ankles with a finger placed in between them.
- Maintaining steady traction, the fetal trunk is swung in an upward and forward direction towards the mother's abdomen, forming a wide arc.
- Simultaneously, the operator’s left hand guards the perineum, allowing it to slip successively off the face and brow.
- Once the mouth is cleared from the vulva, the mucus in the mouth and pharynx is promptly cleared using a mucus sucker, and the trunk is depressed to deliver the rest of the head.

**2. FORCEPS DELIVERY** Forceps can be used routinely for the aftercoming head once it is in the pelvic cavity.

- **Instrument of choice:** _Piper forceps_ is specially designed for this purpose, characterized by an absent pelvic curve, a reverse pelvic curve, and long parallel shanks that permit the baby's body to rest against it. Ordinary long-curved forceps (e.g., Das's variety) can also be used.
- **Advantages:** Traction force is applied directly to the fetal head rather than being transmitted through the vulnerable cervical spine, flexion is better maintained, and the airway can be cleared of mucus more effectively during the process.
- **Technique:** The baby is allowed to hang by its weight, aided by suprapubic pressure. An assistant lifts the baby's legs and trunk upwards to facilitate the introduction of the forceps blades from below.
- Traction must follow the axis of the birth canal. The head must be delivered very slowly (taking over 1 minute) to reduce rapid compression-decompression forces, which helps prevent intracranial bleeding.

**3. MALAR FLEXION AND SHOULDER TRACTION (Modified Mauriceau-Smellie-Veit Technique)**

- **Grip:** The baby is placed resting on the operator’s supinated left forearm, with its limbs hanging freely on either side.
- The index and middle fingers of the left hand are placed over the fetal malar bones on either side to maintain flexion of the head (in the original method, the finger was placed inside the mouth, which is now modified to avoid injury).
- The pronated right hand is placed over the baby's shoulders—the ring and little fingers on the right shoulder, the index finger on the left shoulder, and the middle finger resting on the suboccipital region.
- **Traction:** Downward and backward traction is applied until the nape of the neck appears under the pubic arch. An assistant must apply continuous suprapubic pressure during this time to maintain head flexion.
- The fetus is then swung upward and forward toward the maternal abdomen, sequentially releasing the face and brow, followed by depressing the trunk to release the vertex and occiput.

**MANAGEMENT OF COMPLICATIONS (ARRESTED AFTERCOMING HEAD)**

- **Arrest at the Brim:** Usually due to a deflexed head. It is managed by malar flexion and shoulder traction accompanied by strong suprapubic pressure to negotiate the transverse diameter. _Note: Forceps are contraindicated for a high, unengaged head_.
- **Arrest in the Cavity:** Best managed by Forceps delivery or Malar flexion and shoulder traction.
- **Arrest at the Outlet:** Often due to a rigid perineum or deflexed head. Handled by a liberal episiotomy followed by forceps application or the Mauriceau-Smellie-Veit maneuver.
- **Entrapment in an Incompletely Dilated Cervix:** The cervix is manually pushed up while traction is applied to the fetal trunk via malar flexion and shoulder traction (the "shoe-horn method"). If it fails, Duhrssen's incisions may be made at the 2 o'clock and 10 o'clock positions on the cervix.
- **Occiput Posterior Position of the Head:** Managed via the **Modified Prague Maneuver**. One hand supports the fetal shoulders from below, while the other hand gently lifts the trunk upwards towards the maternal abdomen, flexing the head within the birth canal and allowing the occiput to deliver slowly over the perineum.
