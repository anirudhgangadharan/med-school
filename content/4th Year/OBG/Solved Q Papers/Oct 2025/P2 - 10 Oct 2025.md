1. 1. Discuss the physiology of menstruation with the help of a diagram. Define primary amenorrhoea and enumerate its causes.
**PHYSIOLOGY OF MENSTRUATION**

**Definition** Menstruation is the visible manifestation of cyclic, physiologic uterine bleeding due to shedding of the endometrium, following the invisible interplay of hormones mainly through the hypothalamo-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis.

- **Normal parameters:** Cycle interval of 21–35 days (mean 28 days), duration of flow 4–5 days, and average blood loss of 35 mL (ranging from 20–80 mL), with nearly 70% of the loss occurring in the first 2 days.

**Prerequisites for Normal Menstruation**

1. Normal female chromosomal pattern (46,XX).
2. Actively coordinated hypothalamo-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis.
3. Responsive endometrium to ovarian hormones (estrogen and progesterone).
4. Patent outflow tract (uterovaginal canal).
5. Active support of thyroid and adrenal glands.

**Phases of the Menstrual Cycle** The physiology can be understood through the integrated events in the ovary and the endometrium:

**A. Ovarian Cycle**

1. **Follicular Phase:** At the beginning of the cycle, low levels of estrogen and inhibin allow a rise in Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH) from the anterior pituitary. Rising FSH rescues a cohort of follicles and selects a dominant follicle. Under the synergistic effect of FSH and estrogen, granulosa cells multiply, and Luteinizing Hormone (LH) receptors are formed.
2. **Ovulation:** The dominant follicle secretes high levels of estradiol. Once estradiol reaches a critical level (>200 pg/mL) sustained for ~48 hours, it exerts a positive feedback effect, triggering the **LH surge**. Ovulation occurs approximately 32–36 hours after the onset of the LH surge or 10–12 hours after the LH peak. The LH surge stimulates the completion of the first meiotic division of the oocyte and the rupture of the follicular wall.
3. **Luteal Phase:** Following ovulation, the ruptured follicle transforms into the corpus luteum, which secretes abundant progesterone and estrogen. Progesterone peaks about 8 days after the LH peak, exerting negative feedback on FSH and LH. The lifespan of the corpus luteum is 12–14 days in an infertile cycle.

**B. Endometrial Cycle**

1. **Regenerative Phase:** Begins before menstruation ceases and completes 2–3 days after. The superficial epithelium and glands regenerate from the basal zone.
2. **Proliferative Phase (Days 5–14):** Driven by rising ovarian estrogens. Endometrial glands become tubular, stromal cells become compact, and spiral vessels extend to the subepithelial region. Endometrial thickness reaches 10–12 mm.
3. **Secretory Phase (Days 15–28):** Driven by combined estrogen and progesterone. Progesterone acts on the estrogen-primed endometrium, producing secretory changes. **Subnuclear vacuolation** appears (earliest histologic evidence of ovulation). Glands become corkscrew-shaped with "saw-toothed" epithelium, and vessels undergo marked spiraling.

**Mechanism of Menstrual Bleeding**

- **Hormone Withdrawal:** In the absence of pregnancy, the corpus luteum demises, causing a sharp fall in estrogen and progesterone levels.
- **Vasospasm and Ischemia:** Hormone withdrawal leads to intense spasm of the spiral arterioles, causing stasis and tissue anoxemia in the functional zone of the endometrium.
- **Enzymatic Autodigestion:** Leukocyte infiltration occurs. Breakdown of tissues is mediated by proteolytic enzymes (Matrix Metalloproteinases - MMPs), which are stimulated by inflammation.
- **Role of Prostaglandins:** The endometrium synthesizes prostaglandins. $PGF_{2\alpha}$ acts as a potent vasoconstrictor and causes myometrial contraction, further causing endometrial necrosis and sloughing.

**PRIMARY AMENORRHEA**

**Definition** Primary amenorrhea is defined as the failure of menstruation to occur by the age of **16 years**, regardless of the presence of normal secondary sexual characters, OR by the age of **14 years** in the absence of growth or development of secondary sexual characters.

**Causes of Primary Amenorrhea** The etiopathogenesis of primary amenorrhea can be classified into the following high-yield groups:

**1. Hypergonadotropic Hypogonadism (Primary Ovarian Failure) (43%)**

- **Chromosomal Abnormalities:**
    - Turner’s syndrome (45,X0) - The most common cause.
    - Mosaic states (e.g., 45,X0/46,XX).
    - Pure gonadal dysgenesis (46,XX or 46,XY).
- **Resistant Ovarian Syndrome (Savage’s syndrome):** Follicles are present but resistant to gonadotropins due to FSH receptor defects.
- **Enzyme Deficiencies:** e.g., $17\alpha$-hydroxylase deficiency.
- **Metabolic disorders:** Galactosemia (causes premature ovarian failure due to toxic effects on follicles).

**2. Developmental Defects of the Genital Tract (30%)**

- **Müllerian Agenesis/Dysgenesis:** Mayer-Rokitansky-Küster-Hauser (MRKH) syndrome (absence of uterus and vagina with normal ovaries and 46,XX karyotype).
- **Outflow Tract Obstruction (Cryptomenorrhea):**
    - Imperforate hymen.
    - Transverse vaginal septum.
    - Atresia of the upper-third of the vagina and cervix.
- **Androgen Insensitivity Syndrome (Testicular Feminization Syndrome):** 46,XY karyotype with tissue receptor defect for androgens; presents with female phenotype, absent uterus, and blind vagina.

**3. Hypogonadotropic Hypogonadism (Central/Hypothalamic-Pituitary Causes) (27%)**

- **Constitutional Delay:** Physiologic delayed puberty.
- **Hypothalamic Dysfunction:** Due to severe stress, extreme weight loss, excessive strenuous exercise, or anorexia nervosa.
- **Kallmann’s Syndrome:** Isolated gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) deficiency associated with anosmia (olfactory bulb agenesis).
- **CNS Lesions/Tumors:** e.g., Craniopharyngioma, which suppresses GnRH secretion.

**4. Dysfunction of Thyroid and Adrenal Cortex**

- **Adrenal:** Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia (Adrenogenital syndrome).
- **Thyroid:** Severe hypothyroidism / Cretinism.

**5. Systemic Illness and Metabolic Disorders**

- Severe malnutrition, chronic anemia, and extreme weight loss.
- Chronic infectious diseases like Tuberculosis.
- Juvenile diabetes.

**6. Unresponsive Endometrium**

- **Congenital Uterine Synechiae:** Often tubercular in origin.
- **Receptor Defect:** Congenital unresponsive endometrium (estrogen receptor mutation - very rare).

2. Discuss the etiology, clinical features and the management of vesicovaginal fistula.
**VESICOVAGINAL FISTULA (VVF)**

**DEFINITION** Vesicovaginal fistula (VVF) is an abnormal communication between the bladder and the vagina, leading to the involuntary escape of urine into the vaginal canal. It is the most common type of acquired genitourinary fistula.

**ETIOLOGY** The causes of VVF are broadly classified into obstetrical, gynecological, and congenital.

**1. Obstetrical Causes (80-90% of cases in developing countries)**

- **Ischemic (Most Common):** Prolonged compression of the bladder base between the fetal head and the symphysis pubis during obstructed labor causes pressure necrosis, leading to sloughing and fistula formation. This typically presents 3 to 5 days following delivery.
- **Traumatic:** Direct injury during instrumental vaginal deliveries (destructive operations, difficult forceps with Kielland) or during abdominal operations like cesarean section or cesarean hysterectomy. Direct traumatic fistulae present with leakage immediately after delivery.

**2. Gynecological Causes (Accounts for >80% of cases currently in India)**

- **Operative Injury:** Iatrogenic injury during abdominal/vaginal hysterectomy, anterior colporrhaphy, or from electrothermal injury during laparoscopic pelvic surgery.
- **Malignancy:** Direct spread from advanced carcinoma of the cervix, vagina, or bladder.
- **Radiation:** Ischemic necrosis due to radiation-induced endarteritis obliterans (typically presents 1-2 years post-radiotherapy).
- **Traumatic:** Fall on a sharp pointed object, pelvic bone fractures, or road traffic accidents.
- **Infective:** Chronic granulomatous lesions like vaginal tuberculosis or lymphogranuloma venereum.

**3. Congenital Causes**

- Extremely rare; occurs due to abnormal fusion of the Müllerian eminence with the vesicourethral part of the cloaca.

---

**CLINICAL FEATURES**

- **Symptoms:**
    - The classic symptom is the continuous escape of urine per vaginam (true incontinence) with a complete loss of the normal urge to void.
    - If the fistula is very small, urine may leak only in certain positions, and the patient may still be able to pass urine normally.
    - Associated severe pruritus vulvae and soreness.
    - _Timing of onset:_ 7-14 days postpartum in ischemic obstetric fistulae; immediately (day 1) in direct surgical trauma.
    - _Associated symptoms:_ Secondary amenorrhea, foot-drop (nerve compression in obstructed labor), or complete perineal tear.
- **Signs:**
    - **Vulvar Inspection:** Scent of ammonia, with sodden and excoriated vulvar skin.
    - **Speculum Examination:** Using a Sims’ speculum with the patient in Sims’ position (Sims' triad) balloons the vagina, providing an excellent view of the anterior vaginal wall to visualize the size, site, and number of fistulae. Bladder mucosa may be seen prolapsing through large defects.
    - **Metal Catheter Test:** A metal catheter passed through the external urethral meatus into the bladder will come out through the fistula, confirming VVF and ensuring urethral patency.

---

**DIAGNOSIS & INVESTIGATIONS** Diagnosis is usually clinical, but tiny fistulae require specific confirmatory tests:

- **Three-Swab (Tampon) Test:** Highly reliable to differentiate VVF from ureterovaginal and urethrovaginal fistulae. Three cotton swabs are placed in the upper, middle, and lower vagina. Methylene blue dye is instilled into the bladder via a catheter. If only the **middle swab** is stained blue, it confirms VVF.
- **Methylene Blue Dye Test:** Dye instilled into the bladder is seen leaking through the anterior vaginal wall defect.
- **Examination Under Anesthesia (EUA):** With the patient in knee-chest position, bubbles of air escape through the tiny fistula upon coughing.
- **Imaging:** Intravenous Urography (IVU) or CT scanning is mandatory to rule out a coexisting ureterovaginal fistula (present in 10% of VVF cases).
- **Cystourethroscopy:** Assesses the exact level, size, number of fistulae, and their proximity to the ureteric orifices.

---

**MANAGEMENT**

**1. Preventive Management**

- Adequate antenatal care to screen for risks of obstructed labor.
- Use of the partograph for early detection of obstruction and timely operative intervention.
- Continuous bladder drainage for 5-7 days following obstructed labor to prevent ischemic necrosis.

**2. Conservative (Immediate) Management**

- If a small fistula (2 mm to 2 cm) is detected early, continuous bladder catheterization for 4-8 weeks can lead to spontaneous closure in 50-60% of cases.

**3. Surgical Management (Definitive Repair)**

- **Timing of Surgery:** The ideal time for obstetric VVF repair is **6 weeks to 3 months** after delivery, allowing the general condition to improve, tissue edema to subside, and local infection to resolve. Surgical fistulae recognized within 24 hours can be repaired immediately; otherwise, wait 10-12 weeks. Radiation fistulae require a waiting period of at least 12 months.
- **Preoperative Preparation:** Treat vulvar excoriation with barrier creams, eradicate urinary tract infections (start appropriate antibiotics 3-5 days prior), and evaluate renal function.
- **Route of Repair:**
    - _Vaginal route_ is preferred due to less blood loss, lower morbidity, and shorter hospital stay.
    - _Abdominal route_ (transperitoneal/transvesical) is indicated for high/fixed vault fistulae, fistulae very close to ureteric orifices, large/complex fistulae, or prior failed repairs.
- **Surgical Procedures:**
    - **Flap-Splitting Method (Most Common):** The vaginal mucosa is dissected off the bladder wall around the fistula. The scar tissue at the margins is minimally excised. The bladder wall is closed without tension in two layers using delayed absorbable sutures (Vicryl 2-0), strictly _excluding_ the bladder mucosa. The vaginal mucosa is closed with interrupted sutures. Water-tight closure is confirmed by instilling dye at the end of the procedure.
    - **Saucerization (Sims' technique):** For very small fistulae, paring the margins and placing interrupted stitches without extensive tissue dissection.
    - **Latzko Technique:** A partial colpocleisis (obliteration of the vaginal vault) suitable for small, high, post-hysterectomy vault fistulae.
    - **Interpositional Grafts:** For large or complex fistulae, a Martius graft (bulbocavernosus muscle and labial fat pad) or omental pedicle graft brings a new blood supply and fills the dead space.

**4. Postoperative Care**

- **Continuous Bladder Drainage:** Crucial for success. Maintain an indwelling Foley's catheter for 10-14 days to keep the bladder completely decompressed.
- Ensure high fluid intake and administer appropriate urinary antiseptics/antibiotics.
- **Advice on Discharge:** Advise frequent voiding, strictly avoid intercourse for 3 months, and defer pregnancy for at least 1 year. Any future pregnancy must have a mandatory hospital delivery via elective cesarean section.

3. Evaluation of secondary infertility
**DEFINITION AND TIMING OF EVALUATION** Secondary infertility indicates that a woman has had a previous pregnancy but fails to conceive subsequently. The couple should be investigated after one year of regular, unprotected intercourse with adequate frequency (4–5 times a week). The interval for evaluation is shortened to 6 months if the woman is older than 35 years or the man is older than 40 years. Both partners must be present for the first visit and clinical examination.

**CLINICAL EVALUATION**

**1. History Taking (Female):** In cases of secondary infertility, careful evaluation of the previous obstetric and surgical history is of paramount importance.

- **Previous Obstetric History:** Details of past abortions, deliveries, and puerperium must be elicited. A history of postabortal or puerperal sepsis is a major risk factor, as ascending infection can cause severe tubal damage and tubal blockage.
- **Surgical History:** A history of vigorous postabortal or puerperal uterine curettage strongly points towards the development of intrauterine adhesions or uterine synechiae (Asherman's syndrome). Any history of abdominal or pelvic surgery should be noted as it predisposes to pelvic adhesions.
- **Menstrual History:** A wide spectrum of abnormalities ranging from hypomenorrhea to oligomenorrhea or amenorrhea may be present. These indicate disturbed hypothalamo-pituitary-ovarian axis, thyroid/adrenal dysfunction, or endometrial destruction (synechiae).
- **Contraceptive Practice:** Previous use of an Intrauterine Contraceptive Device (IUCD) should be noted as it carries a risk of introducing Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID).
- **Medical History:** Inquire about a history of genital tuberculosis, sexually transmitted infections, or diabetes.

**2. History Taking (Male):**

- Inquire regarding a history of mumps orchitis after puberty, diabetes, recurrent chest infections (bronchiectasis), sexually transmitted diseases, relevant surgeries like herniorrhaphy, and sexual dysfunction (erectile/ejaculatory defects).

**3. Physical Examination:**

- **General & Systemic (Female):** Evaluate Body Mass Index (BMI), as both obesity and marked weight loss affect fertility. Look for signs of endocrinopathies such as hirsutism, acne, acanthosis nigricans, galactorrhea, or thyroid dysfunction.
- **Gynecological Examination:** Speculum examination is done to detect abnormal cervical discharge for culture. Bimanual examination assesses uterine size, position, and mobility, and checks for adnexal masses, tenderness, or nodules in the pouch of Douglas (suggestive of pelvic endometriosis or tuberculosis).
- **Male Examination:** Assess testicular volume using an orchidometer (should be at least 20 mL) and examine for the presence of varicocele in the upright position.

**INVESTIGATIONS** The fundamental approach relies on noninvasive or minimally invasive methods prior to major invasive ones. The basic investigations are grouped into three main categories: semen analysis, confirmation of ovulation, and confirmation of tubal patency.

**I. Male Factor Evaluation**

- **Semen Analysis (WHO 2010 Criteria):** This is the first step of investigation. The sample is collected after 2–3 days of abstinence and examined within 2 hours.
    - _Normal Reference Values:_ Volume $\ge$ 1.5 mL, Sperm concentration $\ge$ 15 million/mL, Total progressive forward motility $\ge$ 32%, Normal morphology $\ge$ 4%, and viability $\ge$ 58%.
- **Further Tests:** If semen analysis is persistently abnormal, hormonal assays (Serum FSH, LH, testosterone, prolactin, TSH), testicular biopsy, or karyotyping/genetic testing (for Y chromosome microdeletions) are indicated.

**II. Female Factor Evaluation** **A. Diagnosis of Ovulation**

- **Serum Progesterone:** Estimation is done in the mid-luteal phase (day 21 of a 28-day cycle). A value greater than 6 ng/mL is a presumptive sign of ovulation.
- **Serial Transvaginal Sonography (TVS):** Folliculometry during the midcycle tracks the growth of the dominant follicle (reaches 18–20 mm prior to ovulation). Rupture is confirmed by a collapsed follicle and free fluid in the pouch of Douglas.
- **Urinary LH Kit:** Detects the midcycle LH surge, predicting ovulation within 14–24 hours.
- **Basal Body Temperature (BBT):** Demonstrates a "biphasic pattern" due to the thermogenic effect of progesterone secreted by the corpus luteum.
- **Endometrial Biopsy:** Rarely done solely for ovulation tracking today, but a biopsy on day 21–23 showing secretory endometrium (with subnuclear vacuolation appearing 36–48 hours post-ovulation) confirms ovulation and evaluates the luteal phase.

**B. Tubal and Peritoneal Evaluation** Tubal factors account for 25–35% of female infertility and are common in secondary infertility due to acquired infections.

- **Hysterosalpingography (HSG):** Performed between day 7 and day 10 of the cycle. It evaluates the internal contour of the uterine cavity and tubal patency (demonstrated by free peritoneal spillage of the radio-opaque dye). It is strictly contraindicated in the presence of active pelvic infection.
- **Laparoscopy and Chromopertubation:** Considered the "gold standard" for evaluating the tubal and peritoneal factors. It allows direct visualization of tubal patency via methylene blue dye spillage, and detects peritubal adhesions, fimbrial agglutination, hydrosalpinx, and pelvic endometriosis.
- **Sonohysterosalpingography (SIS):** A noninvasive alternative where normal saline is infused transcervically while performing TVS to observe fluid flowing through the tubes into the peritoneal cavity.

**C. Uterine Evaluation** Uterine factors (10% of female infertility) are particularly relevant in secondary infertility due to acquired conditions like Asherman’s syndrome or submucous fibroids.

- **Hysteroscopy:** The gold standard for direct visualization of the uterine cavity. It accurately diagnoses and simultaneously treats intrauterine synechiae (adhesions), submucous fibroids, and endometrial polyps.
- **Transvaginal Sonography (TVS) and SIS:** SIS is highly superior in outlining intracavitary focal pathologies such as polyps and submucous myomas.

**D. Ovarian Reserve Testing** Crucial for women over 35 years of age or those with a history of ovarian surgery.

- **Antral Follicle Count (AFC):** Measured by TVS on cycle day 2–4. An AFC of less than 10 (normal 10–20) strongly predicts poor ovarian reserve and a poor response to ovulation induction.
- **Serum Anti-Müllerian Hormone (AMH):** Levels $<0.5$ ng/mL suggest decreased ovarian reserve, whereas levels $>2$ ng/mL suggest a good reserve. AMH can be tested on any day of the cycle.
- **Day 2/3 Serum FSH and Estradiol:** An elevated basal FSH ($\ge$ 10–15 IU/L) alongside elevated estradiol indicates diminishing ovarian reserve.

**E. Endocrine and Metabolic Evaluation**

- Based on clinical suspicion (e.g., PCOS, galactorrhea), specific assays including Serum TSH, Prolactin, FSH, LH, androgens (Testosterone, DHEAS), and an oral glucose tolerance test (for insulin resistance) should be performed.

4. Principles of myomectomy
**DEFINITION** Myomectomy is the enucleation of myomata from the uterus, leaving behind a potentially functioning organ capable of future reproduction.

**OBJECTIVES**

- To preserve reproductive function in young women desirous of pregnancy.
- To comply judiciously with the wish to preserve menstrual function in parous women.
- To improve menorrhagia and correct anemia.
- As an alternative to hysterectomy in selected cases.

**INDICATIONS**

- Persistent uterine bleeding/menorrhagia not responding to medical therapy.
- Excessive pain or pressure symptoms.
- Fibroid size >12 weeks in a woman desirous of having a baby.
- Distortion of the uterine cavity causing unexplained infertility.
- Recurrent pregnancy loss or miscarriage due to fibroid.
- Rapidly growing myoma during follow-up.
- Subserous pedunculated fibroid.

**PREREQUISITES BEFORE SURGERY**

- **Hysteroscopy or Hysterosalpingography (HSG):** To exclude any submucous fibroid, polyp, or tubal block.
- **Endometrial Biopsy/Hysteroscopy:** In cases of irregular cycles, to rule out endometrial carcinoma.
- **Husband's Semen Analysis:** To confirm male fertility.

**CONTRAINDICATIONS**

- **Absolute:** Infected fibroid, growth of myoma after menopause, suspected malignant change (sarcoma), pelvic or endometrial tuberculosis.
- **Relative:** Parous women where hysterectomy is safer and more definitive, functionless fallopian tubes (e.g., bilateral hydrosalpinx), during pregnancy or cesarean section.

**MEASURES TO CONTROL OPERATIVE BLOOD LOSS**

- **Preoperative Medical Therapy:** GnRH analogs, Danazol, or selective progesterone receptor modulators given for 3 months reduce the size, vascularity, and operative blood loss.
- **Vasoconstrictive Agents:** Synthetic vasopressin (20 units diluted in 20–100 mL of normal saline) injected into the myometrium overlying the myoma. Needle aspiration prior to injection is imperative to avoid intravascular injection. Causes marked blanching.
- **Tourniquets:** A soft plastic tube passed through an avascular part of the broad ligament at the level of the uterine isthmus to occlude the uterine and ovarian vessels.
- **Mechanical Clamps:** Victor Bonney’s myomectomy clamp placed around the uterine vessels and round ligaments at the level of the internal os.

**PRINCIPLES AND STEPS OF SURGERY**

- **Uterine Incision:** A single linear or elliptical incision in the midline on the anterior wall of the uterus is preferred. This avoids injury to the tubes and ovaries and allows lateral fibroids to be removed by tunneling without adding multiple scars. Posterior wall fibroids can also be removed via a transcavity approach to avoid posterior adhesions.
- **Incision Depth:** Deepened through the myometrium and the pseudocapsule until the myoma is reached.
- **Enucleation:** The myoma is grasped with a single-toothed vulsellum or a myoma screw for traction. Dissection must be strictly intracapsular (in the plane between the myoma and the capsule) using sharp and blunt dissection to minimize blood loss.
- **Obliteration of the Dead Space:** The myoma bed must be meticulously obliterated using interrupted mattress or figure-of-eight sutures (1-0 Vicryl). Multiple layers of sutures ("tire stitch") may be required for deep beds.
- **Endometrial Cavity Repair:** If the cavity is opened, it is repaired with a running suture of 4–0 or 5–0 delayed absorbable material.
- **Serosal Approximation:** The uterine serosa is approximated with a thin absorbable suture (4-0). Adhesion barriers like Interceed (oxidized cellulose) or Gore-Tex may be used.
- _Note on large fundal myomas:_ Bonney’s hood operation may be done, where the capsule is trimmed and sewn over the anterior uterine wall after enucleation.

**COMPLICATIONS**

- **Immediate:** Intraperitoneal hemorrhage due to imperfect hemostasis; inadvertent need for hysterectomy if bleeding is uncontrolled or myometrial injury is extensive.
- **Postoperative:** Postoperative fever (hematoma or tissue injury), adhesion formation, injury to bladder/ureter/fallopian tubes.

**PROGNOSIS & LONG-TERM OUTCOMES**

- **Pregnancy Rate:** Following myomectomy, the pregnancy rate is about 40–60%.
- **Recurrence:** The risk of recurrence and persistence of fibroids is 30–50%, and the risk of relaparotomy is 20–25%.
- **Obstetric Management:** Pregnancy following a myomectomy requires mandatory hospital delivery due to the rare but significant risk of uterine scar rupture.

5. GnRh analogues in gynecology.
**GnRH ANALOGUES IN GYNECOLOGY**

**Introduction and Classification** Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) analogues are synthesized by the substitution of amino acids at different positions of the natural GnRH decapeptide molecule. This modification overcomes the very short half-life of natural GnRH and alters its biological functions. They are classified into two categories:

- **GnRH Agonists:** Synthesized by amino acid substitutions at the 6 and 10 positions. They possess a longer half-life (3–8 hours) and are 15 to 200 times more potent than natural GnRH.
- **GnRH Antagonists:** Synthesized by the modification of amino acids at positions 1, 2, 3, 6, and 10.

**Common Preparations and Routes of Administration**

- **Agonists:**
    - Buserelin (Intranasal, Subcutaneous: 200–600 µg/day).
    - Nafarelin (Intranasal: 400–800 µg/day).
    - Goserelin / Zoladex (Subcutaneous depot implant: 3.6 mg every 28 days).
    - Leuprorelin acetate (Subcutaneous or Intramuscular depot: 3.75 mg every 28 days).
    - Triptorelin (Intramuscular: 3 mg every 28 days).
- **Antagonists:**
    - Cetrorelix and Ganirelix (Subcutaneous injection; minimum effective dose is 0.25 mg).
    - Elagolix (Oral preparation, used at 150–250 mg/day).

**Mechanism of Action**

- **GnRH Agonists:** When administered continuously, they initially stimulate the anterior pituitary, resulting in a transient increased secretion of FSH and LH (upregulation), known as the "flare effect". After 1 to 3 weeks of continuous exposure, profound suppression of gonadotropin secretion occurs (downregulation) due to the loss of pituitary GnRH receptor sensitivity. This produces a hypogonadotropic hypogonadal state, effectively creating a "medical hypophysectomy".
- **GnRH Antagonists:** They competitively bind and completely block pituitary GnRH receptors, causing immediate suppression of gonadotropins without any initial stimulatory "flare effect".

**Clinical Indications in Gynecology**

- **Endometriosis:** By creating a medical hypophysectomy and a hypoestrogenic state, GnRH analogues induce atrophy of ectopic endometrial implants. They are highly effective in relieving chronic pelvic pain and reducing adhesion formation.
- **Uterine Leiomyoma (Fibroid):** The induced hypoestrogenic state causes shrinkage of the tumor volume by about 50–60% over 3–6 months. It is primarily used preoperatively to reduce tumor size, decrease tumor vascularity, and minimize blood loss during surgery, facilitating laparoscopic/hysteroscopic myomectomy or vaginal hysterectomy. (Note: The fibroid regrows upon cessation of therapy).
- **Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART) / IVF:** Used for controlled ovarian stimulation (COS). Downregulation of the pituitary prevents a premature LH surge. Agonists are used in "long" or "short (flare)" protocols, while antagonists are increasingly used to immediately block premature ovulation.
- **Dysfunctional Uterine Bleeding (DUB) / Heavy Menstrual Bleeding (HMB):** Used as a short-term therapy to induce amenorrhea, allowing the patient to recover from severe anemia while awaiting definitive surgery.
- **Prior to Endometrial Resection/Ablation (TCRE):** Used 4–6 weeks preoperatively to suppress endometrial growth and thin the endometrium, which facilitates the surgical procedure.
- **Precocious Puberty:** The drug of choice for GnRH-dependent (central) precocious puberty. It suppresses the premature activation of the HPO axis, halts the development of secondary sexual characteristics, and slows down accelerated bone maturation, preventing premature epiphyseal closure to preserve adult height.
- **Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS) / PMDD:** Used for both diagnosis (administering a 3-month depot to see if symptoms resolve) and treatment by suppressing the ovarian cycle (medical oophorectomy).
- **Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome (PCOS):** Used for ovulation induction in refractory cases where endogenous LH levels are tonically elevated.
- **Hirsutism:** Suppresses excess ovarian steroid production (testosterone) by inhibiting LH secretion in severe or refractory idiopathic cases.
- **Advanced Breast Carcinoma:** Used in premenopausal women with estrogen-dependent tumors.

**Side Effects and Hazards** The side effects are predominantly related to the induced hypogonadotropic and hypoestrogenic state.

- **Vasomotor & Menopausal Symptoms:** Hot flashes (most common), vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, headache, and depression.
- **Bone Loss:** Significant decrease in both trabecular (lumbar spine) and cortical (femoral neck) bone mineral density (osteoporosis) if used continuously for more than 6 months.
- **Others:** Acne, muscle pain, back pain, and dry skin.

**Add-Back Therapy** To minimize the adverse hypoestrogenic side effects (especially bone demineralization and severe vasomotor symptoms) during long-term use (>6 months), "add-back therapy" is instituted. This consists of daily administration of a low-dose combined estrogen (e.g., conjugated equine estrogen 0.625 mg) and a progestin (e.g., medroxyprogesterone 2.5 mg).

**High-Yield Comparison: Agonist vs. Antagonist**

- **Initial Flare Effect:** Present in agonists; completely absent in antagonists.
- **Suppression of LH Surge:** Takes a long duration (weeks) for agonists; immediate suppression for antagonists.
- **Estrogen Deficiency Symptoms:** Commonly present and pronounced with agonists; largely absent and better tolerated with antagonists.
- **Pituitary Function Recovery:** Slow following agonist cessation; quick recovery following antagonist therapy.
- **Risk of Ovarian Hyperstimulation Syndrome (OHSS) in IVF:** Present with agonists; significantly lower risk with antagonists.



6. Cryptomenorrhea
**DEFINITION**
Cryptomenorrhea is a condition characterized by the periodic shedding of the endometrium and bleeding, where the menstrual blood fails to come out from the genital tract due to an obstruction in the outflow passage.

**CAUSES**
*   **Congenital:** Imperforate hymen (the most common cause), transverse vaginal septum, and atresia of the upper-third of the vagina and cervix.
*   **Acquired:** Stenosis of the cervix following amputation, conization, or deep cauterization. It can also occur due to secondary vaginal atresia following a neglected and difficult vaginal delivery.

**PATHOPHYSIOLOGY**
*   If the site of obstruction is low down in the vagina (e.g., imperforate hymen), the accumulated blood sequentially results in **hematocolpos** (collection of blood in the vagina), followed by **hematometra** (blood distending the uterine cavity), and eventually **hematosalpinx** (blood distending the fallopian tubes after the fimbrial ends are blocked by adhesions).
*   If the obstruction is at the level of the cervix, it directly produces hematometra and subsequent hematosalpinx.
*   A large hematocolpos causes marked elongation of the urethra, which mechanically leads to the retention of urine.
*   In neglected cases, retrograde passage of menstrual blood through the fallopian tubes may result in pelvic endometriosis.

**CLINICAL FEATURES**
*   **Symptoms:**
    *   In congenital types, the patient (usually aged 13–15 years) presents with primary amenorrhea and experiences periodic, cyclical lower abdominal pain.
    *   Urinary symptoms, particularly frequency, dysuria, or acute retention of urine, are highly associated and may be the primary presenting feature.
    *   In acquired cases, the patient complains of amenorrhea dating back to the surgical or obstetric event, along with periodic lower abdominal pain.
*   **Signs:**
    *   **Abdominal Examination:** A uniform, globular mass is felt in the hypogastrium. 
    *   **Vulvar Inspection:** In cases of imperforate hymen, a tense, bulging membrane with a characteristic bluish coloration is visible.
    *   **Rectal Examination:** Confirms the fullness of the ballooned vagina and the enlarged uterine mass.

**INVESTIGATIONS**
*   **Ultrasonography (USG):** Readily confirms the clinical diagnosis by visualizing the hematometra and hematocolpos. 

**MANAGEMENT**
*   **Imperforate Hymen:** The treatment of choice is a **cruciate incision** over the bulging hymen under strict asepsis, allowing the thick, tarry accumulated blood to drain completely. 
*   **Cervical Stenosis:** Simple dilatation of the cervix is sufficient to drain the collected blood.
*   **Vaginal Atresia:** Reconstructive surgery is required to establish communication between the uterovaginal canal and the newly created tract to maintain patency.


7. Causes and evaluation of post menopausal bleeding
**DEFINITION AND SIGNIFICANCE** Bleeding per vagina following established menopause is called postmenopausal bleeding. The same importance is also given to cases where normal menstruation continues beyond the age of 55 years. The clinical significance of postmenopausal bleeding must never be underestimated, as approximately one-third of these cases are due to genital malignancy.

A cardinal clinical rule is that a case of postmenopausal bleeding is considered to be due to endometrial carcinoma unless proved otherwise. Finding a benign condition to account for the bleeding does not negate the need for a thorough investigation to rule out carcinoma, because benign and malignant lesions may coexist.

**CAUSES OF POSTMENOPAUSAL BLEEDING** The etiology can be broadly classified based on the anatomical site of the lesion:

**1. Uterine / Endometrial Causes**

- **Senile (Atrophic) Endometritis:** Thin, atrophic endometrium loses its defense, leading to infection and bleeding.
- **Atrophic Endometrium:** Simple atrophy without infection.
- **Endometrial Hyperplasia:** Often due to unopposed estrogen stimulation.
- **Genital Malignancy:** Endometrial carcinoma is the most critical cause to rule out. Sarcoma of the uterus is another highly malignant cause.
- **Uterine Polyps:** Endometrial mucous polyps or fibroid polyps.
- **Dysfunctional Uterine Bleeding (DUB)**.
- **Tubercular Endometritis**.

**2. Ovarian / Tubal Causes**

- **Estrogen-producing Ovarian Tumors:** Granulosa cell tumor of the ovary, which causes endometrial hyperplasia and bleeding.
- **Carcinoma of the Fallopian Tube:** A rare malignancy causing watery or blood-stained discharge.
- **Ovarian Malignancy**.

**3. Cervical Causes**

- **Carcinoma of the Cervix**.
- **Cervical Erosion (Ectopy) and Cervical Polyps**.

**4. Vaginal and Vulval Causes**

- **Senile (Atrophic) Vaginitis:** Thinning of vaginal epithelium prone to trauma and infection.
- **Decubitus Ulcer:** Secondary to pelvic organ prolapse.
- **Carcinoma of the Vagina and Vulva**.

**5. Other Causes**

- **Urethral Lesions:** Urethral caruncle, urethral polyp, prolapsed urethral mucosa, or urethral carcinoma.
- **Iatrogenic:** Withdrawal bleeding following exogenous estrogen intake (Hormone Replacement Therapy).
- **Foreign Body:** Retained and forgotten pessary or Intrauterine Contraceptive Device (IUCD).
- **Idiopathic:** In about 25% of cases, the exact cause remains unknown, though this incidence is decreasing with the wider use of hysteroscopy.

---

**EVALUATION AND INVESTIGATIONS** The initial step is to definitively establish that the bleeding is vaginal in origin, and not hematuria or bleeding per rectum.

**1. Detailed History**

- Note the exact age of menopause and the menstrual pattern prior to menopause.
- Assess the amount of bleeding and the number of bleeding episodes.
- Inquire about the sensation of something coming down the introitus (suggestive of prolapse and decubitus ulcer).
- Elicit any urinary problems like dysuria or frequency of urination.
- Obtain a history of exogenous estrogen (HRT) intake; even if present, full investigations must be carried out to exclude malignancy.
- Family history of endometrial and/or ovarian carcinoma (first-degree relatives), which may suggest Lynch syndrome.

**2. Clinical Examination**

- **General and Systemic Examination:** Evaluate for obesity, hypertension, and diabetes, which together form the "corpus cancer syndrome" predisposing to endometrial carcinoma. Examine the breasts and regional lymph nodes.
- **Vulval Inspection:** Carefully inspect the vulva for any growth, and take a biopsy if present. Separate the labia to inspect the urethral meatus for a caruncle, polyp, or mucosal prolapse.
- **Speculum Examination:** Note the condition of the cervix and vaginal vault. If any visible cervical growth is present, a biopsy must be taken for histology. If the cervix looks apparently healthy, a cervical and endocervical smear should be taken to exclude dysplasia or Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia (CIN).
- **Bimanual Pelvic Examination:** Assess the uterus; it may be normal, atrophic, or enlarged (suggestive of pyometra, fibroid, or sarcoma). Note the mobility of the uterus and palpate for any adnexal mass (ovarian tumor or infective mass).
- **Rectal Examination:** Done to corroborate the bimanual findings and assess the parametrium.

**3. Special Investigations**

- **Transvaginal Sonography (TVS) and Color Doppler:** This is highly accurate due to its proximity to the target tissue. An endometrial thickness of less than 5 mm (or $\le$ 4 mm) indicates an atrophic endometrium and has a 100% negative predictive value for endometrial pathology. A thick, polypoid endometrium (9-10 mm), irregular texture, or fluid within the uterine cavity requires further evaluation to exclude malignancy.
- **Saline Infusion Sonography (SIS):** More accurate than plain TVS in detecting focal intracavitary pathology like polyps or submucous fibroids, allowing for directed biopsy.
- **Endometrial Sampling (Biopsy):** This is mandatory to exclude endometrial malignancy, especially if the endometrial thickness is >4 mm. It is safely done as an outpatient procedure using a soft, flexible plastic suction cannula like the Pipelle or a Sharman curette. It has an accuracy of 91-99% in detecting cancer and correlates well with curettings.
- **Hysteroscopy and Directed Biopsy:** Helps in the direct visualization of the endometrium and taking a targeted biopsy from the offending site. It prevents missing focal lesions like polyps or small submucous fibroids that might escape a blind curette.
- **Fractional Curettage:** The definitive method of diagnosis and staging if outpatient endometrial biopsy is nondiagnostic, if there is cervical stenosis, or if cervical cytology is negative. It involves endocervical curettage first, followed by uterine cavity curettage. If a pyometra is detected, the curettage is withheld for about 1 week to allow drainage and antibiotic coverage, avoiding the risk of uterine perforation and spreading peritonitis.
- **Cytology:** Papanicolaou (Pap) smear is not a highly reliable diagnostic test for endometrial carcinoma, being positive in only 30% of cases. However, Liquid-Based Cytology (LBC) or Endocervical Curettage (ECC) may detect glandular abnormalities.
- **Advanced Imaging (CT, MRI, PET):** Computed Tomography (CT) and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) are useful in selected cases where malignancy is confirmed or highly suspected, to assess myometrial invasion, extrauterine spread, and lymph node status.
- **Tumor Markers:** Serum CA 125 levels should be assessed; an elevated level indicates advanced disease or ovarian pathology.

8. Bacterial vaginosis.
**BACTERIAL VAGINOSIS**

**Definition and Etiopathogenesis** Bacterial vaginosis (BV) is the most common cause of symptomatic vaginitis, accounting for 15–50% of cases.

- It is a synergistic polymicrobial infection.
- The normal acidic vaginal flora is disrupted, leading to a marked decrease in protective lactobacilli.
- This allows the overgrowth of _Gardnerella vaginalis_ (previously _Haemophilus vaginalis_) along with multiple anaerobic organisms such as _Bacteroides_ species, _Peptococcus_ species, _Mobiluncus_, _Prevotella_, and _Mycoplasma hominis_.
- The term "vaginosis" is preferred over "vaginitis" because histological examination reveals an absence of underlying vaginal inflammation.

**Risk Factors**

- Multiple or new sexual partners.
- Early age of sexual intercourse.
- Cigarette smoking.
- Use of Intrauterine Contraceptive Devices (IUCD).
- Immunosuppression.

**Clinical Features**

- **Vaginal Discharge:** The classic presentation is a malodorous, homogeneous, grayish-white to green-yellow vaginal discharge that is adherent to the vaginal wall.
- **Odor:** Characteristic "fishy" smell.
- **Lack of inflammation:** Unlike other genital infections, pruritus (itching) and irritation are usually absent.

**Diagnosis** The diagnosis of BV is primarily clinical. Bacterial vaginal culture has no role in establishing the diagnosis.

- **Amsel’s Diagnostic Criteria:** The presence of at least **three out of the four** following criteria is required for a presumptive diagnosis:
    1. Homogeneous vaginal discharge.
    2. Vaginal pH > 4.5 (tested via litmus paper).
    3. **Positive Whiff test:** A fishy (amine) odor is released when a drop of the vaginal discharge is mixed with a 10% potassium hydroxide (KOH) solution.
    4. **Presence of Clue cells (>20% of cells):** Clue cells are vaginal squamous epithelial cells heavily coated with coccobacilli, giving them a granular or stippled appearance where the normal cell borders are obscured.
- **Gram Stained Vaginal Smear (Hay/Ison Criteria):** Demonstrates a preponderance of _Gardnerella_ or _Mobiluncus_ morphotypes with few or completely absent lactobacilli.

**Complications** Bacterial vaginosis is associated with significant gynecological and obstetrical morbidities:

- **Gynecological:**
    - Recurrent infections leading to Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID).
    - Development of PID following an abortion.
    - Vaginal cuff cellulitis following a hysterectomy.
    - Decreased success rates with In Vitro Fertilization (IVF) treatments.
- **Obstetrical:**
    - Second-trimester miscarriage and pregnancy loss before 20 weeks.
    - Preterm Premature Rupture of Membranes (PPROM).
    - Preterm labor (associated with a 4-fold rise in risk).
    - Postpartum endometritis.

**Management and Treatment (CDC Guidelines)** Cure rates are approximately 80% with appropriate antimicrobial therapy.

- **First-line therapy:** Metronidazole 500 mg orally, twice daily (BID) for 7 days.
- **Alternative oral therapies:** Tinidazole 2 g orally for 3 days, OR Clindamycin 300 mg orally BID for 7 days.
- **Topical therapy:** Clindamycin ovules 100 mg intravaginally, applied once at bedtime for 3 days.
- **Partner Management:** The sexual partner should be treated simultaneously to improve outcomes and reduce recurrence.


9. Symptoms and signs of benign ovarian tumour
**SYMPTOMS AND SIGNS OF BENIGN OVARIAN TUMOUR**

**Patient Profile**

- **Age:** Benign tumors predominantly manifest in the late childbearing period, although dermoid cysts (90%) and mucinous cyst adenomas are common during the reproductive period. Dermoid cysts are also more common during pregnancy (10%).
- **Parity:** Unlike fibroids, there is no correlation between benign ovarian tumors and the parity of the patient.

**Symptoms**

- **Asymptomatic:** Most benign ovarian tumors remain entirely asymptomatic and are accidentally discovered by a physician during routine abdominal palpation, pelvic examination, laparoscopy, or laparotomy.
- **Abdominal Swelling:** The patient may notice a gradually increasing mass in the lower abdomen, which characteristically takes months to grow.
- **Abdominal Discomfort:** Patients often complain of a sense of heaviness or a dull aching pain in the lower abdomen.
- **Pressure Symptoms:** If the tumor becomes huge enough to fill the entire abdominal cavity, it can produce cardiorespiratory embarrassment or gastrointestinal symptoms such as nausea or indigestion.
- **Menstrual Symptoms:** The menstrual pattern typically remains unaffected. However, if the tumor is hormone-producing, abnormalities may arise: feminizing tumors (like granulosa cell tumors) can cause menorrhagia, postmenopausal bleeding, or precocious puberty, whereas masculinizing tumors (like Sertoli-Leydig cell tumors) can cause amenorrhea.

**Signs**

**1. General Examination**

- The general condition of the patient usually remains unaffected.
- In cases of huge mucinous cyst adenomas, the patient may become cachectic due to significant protein loss.
- Pitting edema of the legs may be present when a huge tumor presses on the great pelvic veins.

**2. Abdominal Examination**

- **Inspection:** There is visible bulging of the lower abdomen, and the abdominal wall moves freely over the mass with respiration. The mass may be situated centrally or on one side. In cases of a huge tumor filling the entire abdomen, the umbilicus becomes everted, veins under the skin become visible, but the flanks characteristically remain flat, which differentiates it from ascites.
- **Palpation:**
    - **Feel:** The mass is typically cystic or tense cystic, as benign solid tumors (like fibroma, thecoma, and Brenner tumor) are rare.
    - **Mobility:** The mass is freely mobile from side to side but restricted from above downwards, unless the tumor has a long pedicle.
    - **Borders:** The upper and lateral borders are well-defined, but the lower pole is difficult to reach, signifying its pelvic origin.
    - **Surface and Tenderness:** The surface is smooth but may be grooved in lobulated tumors, and the mass is usually not tender.
- **Percussion:** The percussion note is dull in the center over the tumor and resonant in the flanks. A fluid thrill may be elicited if the cyst walls are thin and the content is watery. If coexisting ascites and right-sided hydrothorax are present alongside a benign solid tumor (fibroma), it is termed Meigs' syndrome.
- **Auscultation:** A friction rub may sometimes be present over the tumor.

**3. Pelvic (Bimanual) Examination** Bimanual examination is crucial for confirming the uterine or adnexal origin of the mass:

- The uterus can be felt separately from the mass.
- A distinct groove can be felt between the uterus and the mass.
- Movement of the mass per abdomen does not transmit movement to the cervix, differentiating it from a uterine mass.
- On elevating the mass per abdomen, the cervix remains in a stationary position.
- The lower pole of the cyst can be palpated through the vaginal fornices.
- If a cyst is felt lying anterior to the uterus, it is highly likely to be a dermoid cyst.

10. Endometrial sampling methods.
**ENDOMETRIAL SAMPLING**

**Definition** Endometrial sampling is a rapid, safe, and inexpensive diagnostic test frequently performed as an outpatient (OPD) procedure. It involves obtaining a sample of the endometrial lining for histological evaluation without the need for general anesthesia or hospital admission.

**Indications**

- **Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (AUB) / Dysfunctional Uterine Bleeding (DUB):** To evaluate the functional state of the endometrium and exclude organic lesions.
- **Perimenopausal or Postmenopausal Bleeding:** Mandatory investigation to exclude endometrial hyperplasia or endometrial carcinoma.
- **Infertility Workup:** To determine the occurrence of ovulation and to diagnose luteal phase defect (LPD).
- **Hormone Replacement Therapy (HRT):** For periodic screening of the endometrium during HRT.
- **Suspected Infections:** To detect endometrial tuberculosis.

**Methods and Instruments for Endometrial Sampling**

**1. Pipelle Endometrial Sampler (Instrument of First Choice)**

- **Description:** A thin, flexible plastic suction cannula (2–4 mm in diameter) containing an internal plunger.
- **Procedure:** Inserted transcervically as an outpatient procedure without the need for cervical dilatation or anesthesia. When the internal plunger is withdrawn, adequate endometrial tissue is aspirated into the cannula due to the vacuum/suction created.
- **Advantages:** It has a high diagnostic accuracy, detecting endometrial cancer in 91–99% of cases, and the procedure failure rate is less than 8%.
- **Disadvantages:** As it is a blind procedure, focal intrauterine pathologies such as endometrial polyps or submucous fibroids cannot be detected.

**2. Sharman’s Curette**

- A small metallic curette utilized primarily in infertility workups.
- It is performed as an outpatient procedure without anesthesia to obtain a narrow strip of endometrium, which is sufficient to study the hormonal reflection and secretory changes.

**3. Vabra Aspirator**

- A suction device used for aspiration biopsy of the endometrium in an outpatient setting.

**4. Hysteroscopy and Directed Biopsy**

- Considered the best method to evaluate endometrial pathology in AUB.
- Provides direct visualization of the entire uterine cavity, allowing targeted biopsies from specific offending sites.
- **Advantage:** Detects focal lesions (e.g., submucous fibroids, polyps) that are frequently missed by blind sampling methods.

**5. Dilatation and Curettage (D&C) / Fractional Curettage**

- **Indications for D&C:** Employed when outpatient endometrial biopsy (EB) results are nondiagnostic, when EB is technically impossible due to cervical stenosis, or when a large tissue mass is required for histological studies.
- **Fractional Curettage:** The definitive method to detect the extent of a malignant growth. The orderly steps involve: (1) Endocervical curettage, (2) Introduction of a uterine sound, (3) Dilatation of the internal os, and (4) Uterine curettage at the fundus and body. The specimens are sent in separate containers for precise histological staging.

**Timing of Endometrial Sampling**

- **Infertility / Ovulation Detection:** Biopsy is classically done on the **21st to 23rd day** of a regular 28-day cycle. If cycles are irregular, it is performed within 24 hours of the onset of menstruation. Barrier contraception must be prescribed during the biopsy cycle to prevent the disruption of an accidental conception.
- **Cyclic AUB (Menorrhagia / Irregular shedding):** Done 5–6 days prior to the period, 5–6 days after the period starts, or soon after the period starts.
- **Acyclic / Continuous Bleeding:** Can be performed at any time or soon after the bleeding begins.

**Interpretation of Findings**

- **Ovulation Confirmation:** The appearance of secretory activity in the endometrial glands indicates a functional corpus luteum. **Subnuclear vacuolation** of the glandular epithelium is the earliest histological evidence of ovulation, appearing 36–48 hours post-ovulation.
- **Luteal Phase Defect:** Diagnosed if the histological dating of the endometrium lags behind the actual chronological cycle day by more than 2 days.
- **Malignancy / Hyperplasia:** The sample may reveal simple hyperplasia, complex hyperplasia (with or without atypia), or frank endometrial carcinoma, guiding the definitive surgical or medical management.


11. Management of PCOD.

**MANAGEMENT OF POLYCYSTIC OVARIAN DISEASE (PCOD / PCOS)**

Management of PCOS is highly individualized and is dictated by the patient's primary presenting symptoms: obesity, menstrual abnormalities, hyperandrogenism (hirsutism/acne), or subfertility.

**1. Management of Obesity and Metabolic Syndrome**

- **Weight Reduction & Lifestyle Modification:** This is the first line of treatment. A weight reduction of even 2–5% improves the metabolic syndrome, insulin resistance, hyperandrogenemia, and restores reproductive function and spontaneous ovulation.
- **Insulin Sensitizing Agents:**
    - **Metformin:** The dose is usually 500 mg thrice daily. It decreases circulating insulin and androgen levels, reduces weight and BMI, improves lipid profiles (lowers LDL), and reduces the risk of developing diabetes.
    - Thiazolidinediones (e.g., pioglitazone, rosiglitazone) may be used in cases resistant to metformin.
- **Bariatric Surgery:** Indicated for morbidly obese women who fail to lose weight with lifestyle measures. It significantly improves anovulation, insulin resistance, and hirsutism.

**2. Management of Menstrual Abnormalities (When Fertility is NOT Desired)** Chronic anovulation leads to unopposed estrogen, posing a risk for endometrial hyperplasia and carcinoma.

- **Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs):** This is the treatment of choice to prevent endometrial hyperplasia, regulate cycles, and control abnormal uterine bleeding (AUB).
- **Mechanism:** Progestin suppresses LH secretion from the pituitary, while the estrogen component increases sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), which binds free testosterone, thereby reducing hyperandrogenemia.

**3. Management of Hyperandrogenism (Hirsutism, Acne, Alopecia)** Medical management needs at least 6 months to show benefits because the life cycle of a hair follicle is 6 months.

- **Combined Oral Contraceptives:** COCs containing newer, less androgenic progestins (e.g., drospirenone, desogestrel) are best suited for controlling hirsutism and acne.
- **Antiandrogens:** Used when COCs alone are insufficient.
    - **Cyproterone Acetate:** A potent antiandrogen used in combination with ethinyl estradiol (2 mg cyproterone + 35 µg EE). It competes for androgen receptors and inhibits 5α-reductase.
    - **Spironolactone:** A potassium-sparing diuretic that acts as an androgen receptor antagonist and inhibits 5α-reductase directly. Dose is 50–200 mg/day.
    - **Finasteride:** A 5α-reductase inhibitor given at a dose of 5 mg daily.
    - **Flutamide:** A nonsteroidal androgen receptor antagonist (250 mg/day).
- **Glucocorticoids:** Dexamethasone (0.25–0.5 mg at bedtime) can be used to suppress adrenal androgen production.
- **Cosmetic / Local Measures:** Eflornithine hydrochloride cream (13.9%) inhibits hair growth. Physical removal via laser therapy, electrolysis, or waxing is required for already existing terminal hair.

**4. Management of Subfertility (Ovulation Induction)** Prior correction of weight and hyperinsulinemia (using Metformin) significantly improves the response to ovulation-inducing drugs.

- **Letrozole:** An aromatase inhibitor given at a dose of 2.5 mg or 5 mg daily for 5 days (from Day 3 to Day 7 of the cycle). It reduces estrogen negative feedback, raises FSH, and increases ovarian sensitivity. It is widely used as a primary drug, yielding pregnancy rates comparable to or better than Clomiphene, with low multiple pregnancy rates.
- **Clomiphene Citrate (CC):** Administered at 50 mg/day (up to 250 mg/day) for 5 days. Highly successful for inducing ovulation, especially when combined with Metformin.
- **Gonadotropins (hMG / purified FSH):** Indicated for clomiphene/letrozole-resistant cases. Patients with PCOS are highly sensitive, so a careful, low-dose step-up protocol with strict ultrasonographic folliculometry is mandatory to prevent Ovarian Hyperstimulation Syndrome (OHSS) and multiple pregnancies.
- **Surgical Management - Laparoscopic Ovarian Drilling (LOD):** Indicated for clomiphene-resistant cases. Using electrodiathermy or laser, 4 to 6 punctures (up to 2–4 mm deep) are made in each ovary. It reduces systemic and intraovarian androgen levels and restores spontaneous ovulation, with less risk of OHSS compared to gonadotropins. It has entirely replaced bilateral wedge resection.
- **Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART) / IVF:** Considered for refractory cases or when there are concomitant factors for infertility.

12. Symptoms, signs and differential diagnosis of fibroid uterus.
**SYMPTOMS OF FIBROID UTERUS**

The majority of uterine fibroids (about 75%) remain entirely asymptomatic and are discovered incidentally during routine pelvic examination or ultrasound. When symptomatic, the clinical presentation depends largely on the anatomic location (site is more important than size) and the size of the tumor.

**1. Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (AUB) (30%)** This is the most common symptom, typically presenting as:

- **Menorrhagia (Heavy Menstrual Bleeding):** It is the classic symptom of symptomatic fibroids, presenting with progressively increasing blood loss. It is especially conspicuous in submucous and interstitial (intramural) fibroids. The pathogenesis of menorrhagia includes:
    - Interference with normal rhythmic uterine contractility due to the interposition of the fibroid.
    - Congestion and dilatation of the subjacent endometrial venous plexuses caused by tumor obstruction.
    - Endometrial hyperplasia secondary to hyperestrinism (anovulation).
    - Imbalance of prostanoids, specifically a relative deficiency of Thromboxane $A_2$ ($TXA_2$) compared to Prostacyclin ($PGI_2$).
    - Increased surface area of the bleeding endometrium.
- **Metrorrhagia (Irregular Bleeding):** Bleeding outside the normal cycle may occur due to ulceration of a submucous fibroid or fibroid polyp, torn vessels from the sloughing base of a polyp, or associated endometrial carcinoma.

**2. Dysmenorrhea**

- **Congestive Dysmenorrhea:** Caused by associated pelvic congestion or coexisting endometriosis.
- **Spasmodic Dysmenorrhea:** Associated with the uterine effort to extrude a submucous fibroid polyp from the cavity.

**3. Subfertility (30%)** Fibroids can be a major complaint in women presenting with infertility. Contributing factors include:

- _Uterine factors:_ Distortion or elongation of the uterine cavity (impeding sperm ascent), impaired rhythmic uterine contractions during intercourse (affecting sperm transport), congestion of endometrial venous plexuses (defective implantation), and endometrial atrophy/ulceration over submucous fibroids (defective nidation).
- _Tubal factors:_ Cornual block due to the specific location of the fibroid, or marked elongation of the fallopian tube over a large fibroid.
- _Ovarian factors:_ Associated anovulation.

**4. Pain (Lower Abdomen or Pelvis)** Fibroids are generally painless tumors. When pain occurs, it is usually due to complications such as:

- Degeneration (e.g., Red degeneration during pregnancy).
- Torsion of a subserous pedunculated fibroid.
- Extrusion of a fibroid polyp through the cervix.
- Associated pelvic pathologies like endometriosis or pelvic inflammatory disease (PID).

**5. Pressure Symptoms** These are rare in body fibroids but depend on the specific location:

- _Posterior wall fibroid:_ May become impacted in the pelvis, causing constipation, dysuria, or retention of urine.
- _Broad ligament fibroid:_ Can cause ureteric compression leading to hydroureter, hydronephrosis, and subsequent pyelitis.
- _Cervical fibroids:_ Anterior cervical fibroids cause prominent bladder symptoms (frequency, retention); posterior cervical fibroids cause rectal symptoms (constipation); lateral cervical fibroids can cause vascular obstruction (hemorrhoids, leg edema) and push the ureter laterally.

**6. Abdominal Swelling / Lump** The patient may perceive a sense of heaviness or palpate a lump in the lower abdomen, even in the absence of other symptoms.

**7. Pregnancy Complications** Increased incidence of recurrent pregnancy loss (miscarriage, preterm labor), intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), labor dystocia, and postpartum hemorrhage (PPH).

---

**SIGNS OF FIBROID UTERUS**

**1. General Examination** Varying degrees of pallor may be evident, proportional to the magnitude and duration of heavy menstrual blood loss.

**2. Abdominal Examination** The tumor must be at least 14 weeks of pregnancy size to be palpable per abdomen.

- **Inspection:** A lower abdominal swelling may be visible.
- **Palpation:**
    - _Feel:_ Firm, leaning towards hard. It may feel cystic if cystic degeneration has occurred.
    - _Margins:_ Well-defined all around, except the lower pole which extends into the pelvis and cannot be reached.
    - _Surface:_ Usually nodular and irregular due to multiple fibroids; may be smooth in a solitary, uniformly enlarged fibroid.
    - _Mobility:_ Mobility is restricted from above downwards but the mass can be moved from side to side.
- **Percussion:** The swelling is dull on percussion.
- **Auscultation:** A hissing sound (uterine souffle) may be heard over a vascular fibroid, synchronous with the maternal pulse.

**3. Pelvic (Bimanual) Examination** This confirms that the mass is of uterine origin:

- The uterus is irregularly enlarged by the swelling felt per abdomen.
- The uterus cannot be felt separate from the swelling, meaning no groove is palpable between the uterus and the mass.
- Movement of the tumor per abdomen is transmitted to the cervix, causing it to move.
- _Exception:_ A subserous pedunculated fibroid has a pedicle and may not transmit movement to the cervix, frequently leading to confusion with an ovarian tumor.
- A submucous fibroid may cause a completely symmetrical enlargement of the uterus.

---

**DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS**

A fibroid uterus must be differentiated from other causes of a pelvic or abdominal mass, or symmetrical uterine enlargement.

**1. Pregnancy**

- _Differentiating features:_ Pregnancy is accompanied by amenorrhea and positive signs of pregnancy (e.g., fetal heart sounds, fetal movements). On palpation, the pregnant uterus feels soft and cystic, whereas a fibroid feels firm and hard. Ultrasound is confirmatory.

**2. Adenomyosis**

- _Differentiating features:_ Adenomyosis typically causes diffuse, symmetrical enlargement (rarely exceeding 14 weeks size), whereas fibroids are usually nodular and irregular. Adenomyosis presents with menorrhagia _and_ severe, progressively increasing dysmenorrhea. The uterus feels soft and exquisitely tender in adenomyosis, as opposed to firm and non-tender in fibroids. MRI reveals a thickened junctional zone (>12 mm) in adenomyosis.

**3. Ovarian Tumor**

- _Differentiating features:_ Ovarian tumors grow much faster (in months) compared to fibroids (which take years). An ovarian cyst feels cystic or tense cystic. On bimanual examination, an ovarian tumor is felt _separate_ from the uterus, a groove is palpable between the uterus and the mass, and movement of the abdominal mass does not move the cervix.

**4. Full Urinary Bladder**

- _Differentiating features:_ Often mistaken for a pelvic cyst or tumor. A full bladder is strictly suprapubic, cystic, has ill-defined margins, and causes an urge to void upon pressure. It completely disappears following catheterization.

**5. Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (Tubo-Ovarian Mass)**

- _Differentiating features:_ Presents with a history of fever, vaginal discharge, and acute/chronic pelvic pain. On examination, the mass is usually bilateral, tender, and associated with cervical motion tenderness.

**6. Other Causes of Symmetrical Uterine Enlargement**

- **Hematometra / Pyometra / Lochiometra:** Symmetrical enlargement often accompanied by amenorrhea and cyclical pain (hematometra) or fever and foul discharge (pyometra).
- **Malignancy (Carcinoma body of uterus, Sarcoma, Choriocarcinoma):** Differentiated by rapid growth, postmenopausal bleeding, or preceding history of a vesicular mole. Sarcomatous change in a fibroid is suspected with sudden rapid enlargement and postmenopausal bleeding.

13. Dermoid cyst of ovary
**DERMOID CYST OF OVARY (MATURE CYSTIC TERATOMA)**

**Definition and Origin**

- A dermoid cyst is a mature cystic teratoma, which is a benign germ cell tumor of the ovary.
- It arises from a single germ cell arrested after the first meiotic division, and the karyotype in the majority of cases is 46,XX.
- Although called a "dermoid", it is a misnomer as it typically contains mature tissue elements from all three embryonic germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm).

**Incidence**

- It constitutes about 30–40% of all ovarian tumors and 97% of all ovarian teratomata.
- It is the most common ovarian tumor during pregnancy (constituting 20–40% of ovarian tumors in pregnancy) and is also the most common neoplastic cyst in premenarcheal girls.
- It is bilateral in 15–20% of cases.

**Pathology**

- **Gross / Naked Eye Appearance:**
    - The cyst is usually of moderate size (typically <10 cm) with a tense and smooth capsule.
    - **Cut Section:** The cyst predominantly contains thick, pultaceous sebaceous material (sebum) mixed with hair. It may also contain clear cerebrospinal fluid derived from neural tissues.
    - **Rokitansky’s Protuberance:** A characteristic solid area projecting into the cyst cavity. It is covered by skin with sweat and sebaceous glands, and it is the site where teeth, bones, and hair arise. Histological sections must be taken from this specific area to rule out malignancy.
- **Microscopic Appearance:**
    - The cyst wall is lined by stratified squamous epithelium and, in some places, by granulation tissue, transitional, or columnar epithelium.
    - _Ectodermal elements (most common):_ Skin, hair, teeth, nerve tissue, glial tissue, and sebaceous material.
    - _Mesodermal elements:_ Bone, cartilage, and muscle.
    - _Endodermal elements:_ Thyroid, salivary gland, bronchus, and intestine.

**Special Variants and Associations**

- **Struma Ovarii:** A rare monodermal teratoma composed predominantly of thyroid tissue (less than 3% of mature teratomas), which may be associated with clinical hyperthyroidism.
- **Strumal Carcinoids:** Tumors with argentaffin cells that secrete serotonin and bradykinin, leading to Carcinoid Syndrome (episodic facial flushing, diarrhea, abdominal pain, bronchospasm).
- It can also be associated with autoimmune hemolytic anemia.

**Clinical Features**

- About 50% of dermoid cysts remain entirely asymptomatic.
- When large, they may present with a lump in the lower abdomen, dull aching pain, or heaviness.
- **Classic Pelvic Finding:** On bimanual examination, if an ovarian cyst is palpated lying _anterior_ to the uterus, it is highly likely to be a dermoid cyst.

**Complications**

1. **Torsion of the Pedicle (15–20%):** It is the most common complication of a dermoid cyst. This is predisposed by its moderate size, round contour, high fat content (making it lighter and freely mobile), and a long pedicle.
2. **Malignant Change (1–2%):** The risk of malignancy is very low. When it occurs, Squamous Cell Carcinoma is the most common histological type.
3. **Rupture (1%):** Uncommon, but spontaneous or traumatic rupture spills highly irritating sebaceous material into the peritoneal cavity, causing a severe chemical/irritant peritonitis.
4. **Infection:** May occur following torsion, typically from adherent intestines or uterine tubes.

**Diagnosis & Investigations**

- **Ultrasonography (USG):** Highly characteristic features include:
    - Fat-fluid or hair-fluid levels seen as distinct linear demarcations.
    - Accentuated lines and dots representing floating hair.
    - **Rokitansky's protuberance** visible as an echogenic region.
    - **"Tip of the iceberg" sign:** Created by amorphous echogenic interfaces of fat, hair, and tissue that cast a shadow and obscure structures behind it.
- **Plain X-ray Abdomen:** May reveal a radio-opaque shadow of teeth or bones, providing direct evidence of a dermoid.
- **CT Scan / MRI:** Shows a mixture of low-density areas (fat), high-density areas (dental elements or calcifications), and fat-fluid levels.

**Management**

- **Young women (desirous of fertility):** The treatment of choice is **Ovarian Cystectomy** with preservation of the normal healthy ovarian tissue.
- **Surgical Approach:** Laparoscopic surgery is preferred. It is critical to use an endobag for retrieval and to perform copious pelvic irrigation with normal saline to wash out any spilled sebaceous contents, avoiding chemical peritonitis.
- **Perimenopausal/Older women:** Total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (TAH with BSO) or ovariotomy (salpingo-oophorectomy) may be done depending on the patient's age and intraoperative findings.

14. HPV vaccines
**HUMAN PAPILLOMAVIRUS (HPV) VACCINES**

**Introduction** HPV vaccines are prophylactic vaccines developed from the capsid coat of the Human Papilloma Virus, possessing high immunogenicity. They are primarily used to produce local and humoral immunity against HPV infection, which is the major causative factor for cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) and cervical cancer.

**Types of HPV Vaccines**

1. **Bivalent Vaccine (Cervarix):** Protects against high-risk oncogenic HPV types 16 and 18.
2. **Quadrivalent Vaccine (Gardasil):** Protects against HPV types 6, 11, 16, and 18.
3. **Nonavalent Vaccine (Gardasil 9):** Protects against 9 types of HPV: 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58.

**Mechanism of Action**

- The vaccines induce the production of neutralizing antibodies (IgG, IgA).
- These antibodies work locally at the cervix by preventing the attachment and entry of the virus into the cervical epithelium.

**Target Population and Timing**

- **Target Age:** Vaccines are ideally administered to girls aged 9–26 years. They are approved for school girls (12–18 years) and women (16–25 years).
- **Optimal Timing:** The impact of the vaccine is greatest when given to females who are not already infected; thus, it is highly recommended for adolescent girls (9–13 years) before the onset of sexual activity.

**Dosage and Route of Administration**

- **Route:** Administered intramuscularly (IM) over the deltoid muscle.
- **Schedule (WHO Guidelines):**
    - **Females < 15 years:** 2-dose schedule (0 and 6 months).
    - **Females $\ge$ 15 years:** 3-dose schedule (0, 1 to 2, and 6 months). Specifically, the bivalent vaccine is given at 0, 2, and 6 months, while the quadrivalent is given at 0, 1, and 6 months.
    - **Immunocompromised women:** Must receive the 3-dose schedule.

**Efficacy and Benefits**

- **Cervical Cancer:** Effective in preventing about 90% of cervical cancers. The nonavalent vaccine can prevent CIN II by 96%.
- **Genital Warts:** The inclusion of types 6 and 11 (in quadrivalent and nonavalent) prevents 90% of condyloma acuminata (genital warts).
- **Other Malignancies:** Prophylactic vaccination against types 16 and 18 can prevent about one-third of vulvar cancers, and the vaccines are also used for the prevention of vaginal cancers.
- **Cross-protection:** Both bivalent and quadrivalent vaccines offer some cross-protection against other high-risk HPV types, such as 31, 33, and 45.
- **Duration:** The vaccines are effective for at least 7.5 years.

**Important Clinical Considerations**

- **Nature of Vaccine:** The vaccines are strictly prophylactic and do not have therapeutic effects against pre-existing HPV infections or established CIN.
- **Need for Continued Screening:** Because the vaccines are type-specific and do not protect against all oncogenic HPV types, routine cervical cytology (Pap smear) screening must be continued even in vaccinated women.
- **Pregnancy & Lactation:** HPV vaccination is not recommended during pregnancy, although it has not been found to be harmful. It is, however, completely safe to administer during lactation.
- **Safety:** The vaccines are safe and well-tolerated by patients.

15. Symptoms and signs and menopause
**SYMPTOMS AND SIGNS OF MENOPAUSE**

While the majority of women experience no symptoms other than the permanent cessation of menstruation, a significant number develop manifestations of estrogen deficiency. The symptoms and signs can be classified into acute/intermediate symptoms, late metabolic concerns, and physical organ changes.

**I. SYMPTOMS OF MENOPAUSE**

**1. Menstrual Changes (Menopausal Transition)**

- **Patterns of Cessation:** Menstruation may cease abruptly (rare), or there may be a gradual decrease in both the amount and duration of blood flow.
- **Irregularities:** Cycles may become shorter or irregular with or without excessive bleeding.

**2. Vasomotor Symptoms (Most Characteristic)**

- **Hot Flashes:** Characterized by a sudden feeling of heat followed by profuse sweating. It mainly affects the upper body and lasts for 1–10 minutes.
- **Night Sweats:** Hot flashes occurring at night can cause severe sleep disturbances and insomnia.
- **Associated Features:** Palpitations, fatigue, and weakness.

**3. Urogenital Symptoms**

- **Vaginal:** Estradiol deficiency leads to vaginal dryness, pruritus, leukorrhea, and vaginal infections. Minimal trauma may cause vaginal bleeding due to atrophic epithelial changes.
- **Urinary:** Features include urgency, dysuria, recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs), and stress incontinence.
- **Sexual Dysfunction:** Dyspareunia (painful intercourse) due to lack of lubrication, along with a decreased sexual desire (loss of libido).

**4. Psychological and Cognitive Symptoms**

- **Mood Disturbances:** Irritability, mood swings, anxiety, depression, and dysphoria.
- **Cognitive Decline:** Poor memory, inability to concentrate, and an increased risk of dementia or Alzheimer's disease.

**5. Skin, Hair, and Somatic Symptoms**

- **Skin:** Thinning, loss of elasticity, and wrinkling. Characteristic "purse string" wrinkling around the mouth and "crow feet" around the eyes occur due to reduced collagen content.
- **Hair:** Loss of pubic and axillary hair, along with slight balding.
- **Others:** Backaches, joint aches, and weight gain.

**6. Long-Term Health Concerns (Late Symptoms)**

- **Osteoporosis:** Loss of bone mass by 2–5% per year for the first 5 to 10 years, leading to back pain, loss of height, kyphosis, and fragility fractures (commonly in the vertebra, femoral neck, and wrist).
- **Cardiovascular & Cerebrovascular Disease:** Increased risk of ischemic heart disease, coronary artery disease, and strokes due to atherosclerotic changes and hypercoagulable states induced by estrogen deficiency.

---

**II. SIGNS (ORGAN CHANGES)**

The physical signs of menopause are primarily due to the progressive atrophy of estrogen-dependent tissues:

**1. Ovaries and Fallopian Tubes**

- **Ovaries:** Shrink in size, become wrinkled, and appear white. The cortex thins out, and the medullary components increase.
- **Fallopian Tubes:** Muscle coat becomes thinner, plicae become less prominent, and the cilia completely disappear.

**2. Uterus and Cervix**

- **Uterus:** Becomes significantly smaller. The ratio between the uterine body and the cervix reverts to the prepubertal 1:1 ratio.
- **Endometrium:** Becomes thin and atrophic.
- **Cervix:** Cervical secretions become highly scanty.

**3. Vagina**

- The vaginal canal becomes narrower due to a gradual loss of elasticity.
- The vaginal epithelium thins out, and the rugae flatten progressively.
- Glycogen and Doderlein’s bacilli disappear, shifting the vaginal pH to an alkaline state.
- Vaginal cytology shows a maturation index of 10/85/5 (parabasal/intermediate/superficial cells), indicating low estrogen.

**4. Vulva and Pelvic Floor**

- **Vulva:** Shows gross features of atrophy. The labia become flattened, and the introitus narrows.
- **Pelvic Cellular Tissues:** Loss of muscle tone and thinning of supporting ligaments lead to pelvic relaxation, predisposing the woman to uterine descent or prolapse.

**5. Breasts**

- Breast fat is reabsorbed, and glandular tissues atrophy.
- The nipples decrease in size, and the breasts ultimately become flat and pendulous.

16. Branches of the internal iliac artery
**INTERNAL ILIAC ARTERY: ORIGIN AND RELATIONS**

- **Origin:** It is one of the terminal bifurcations of the common iliac artery. The bifurcation occurs over the sacroiliac articulation.
- **Length:** It measures approximately 2 cm.
- **Relations:** The ureter lies anteriorly, and the internal iliac vein lies posteriorly.
- **Divisions:** It soon divides into an anterior division and a posterior division. Only the anterior division supplies the pelvic viscera.

**BRANCHES OF THE INTERNAL ILIAC ARTERY** The branches are anatomically classified into visceral (supplying the pelvic organs) and parietal (supplying the musculoskeletal walls) branches arising from the anterior and posterior divisions.

**I. ANTERIOR DIVISION** The anterior division gives off both visceral and parietal branches.

- **Visceral Branches:**
    
    1. **Superior vesical artery**.
    2. **Uterine artery:** Arises either directly from the internal iliac artery or in common with the obliterated umbilical artery.
    3. **Inferior vesical artery:** Supplies the bladder, terminal ureter, middle-third of the vagina, and the urethra.
    4. **Middle rectal artery:** Arises either directly from the anterior division or in common with the inferior vesical artery; it supplies the lower-third of the vagina and the rectum.
    5. **Vaginal artery:** Arises either directly from the anterior division or as a branch from the uterine artery.
- **Parietal Branches:**
    
    1. **Inferior gluteal artery**.
    2. **Obturator artery**.
    3. **Internal pudendal artery:** Leaves the pelvic cavity along with its vein and pudendal nerve through the greater sciatic foramen and re-enters the ischiorectal fossa to lie in the pudendal canal (Alcock’s canal).

**II. POSTERIOR DIVISION** The posterior division exclusively supplies parietal structures.

- **Visceral Branches:**
    - Nil (There are no visceral branches from the posterior division).
- **Parietal Branches:**
    1. **Iliolumbar artery**.
    2. **Lateral sacral artery**.
    3. **Superior gluteal artery**.


17. Follow up of vesicular mole.
**FOLLOW-UP OF VESICULAR (HYDATIDIFORM) MOLE**

**Objectives of Follow-Up** Routine follow-up is mandatory for all cases for a minimum of 6 months to 1 year. The primary objective is the early diagnosis of persistent Gestational Trophoblastic Neoplasia (GTN) or choriocarcinoma, which occurs in 15–20% of complete mole cases.

**Schedule of Visits**

- **Initial Phase:** Check-ups are done at weekly intervals until the serum $\beta$-hCG level becomes negative or undetectable. This normalization usually occurs by 4 to 8 weeks.
- **Subsequent Phase:** Once the hCG level is negative, the patient is followed up at monthly intervals for 6 months, and then every 2–3 months for the next 6 months (totaling at least 1 year of follow-up).

**Clinical Evaluation at Each Visit**

- **History Taking:** The patient must be specifically enquired about relevant symptoms such as irregular vaginal bleeding, continuing amenorrhea, persistent cough, breathlessness, or hemoptysis.
- **Physical & Pelvic Examination:**
    - Assess the involution of the uterus.
    - Palpate for ovarian size (theca lutein cysts usually regress spontaneously within 2 months).
    - Carefully inspect for any metastatic malignant deposits, especially in the lower third of the anterior vaginal wall (suburethral).

**Investigations**

- **Serum $\beta$-hCG Assay:** This is the most critical investigation and must be carried out at every visit to monitor all isoforms of hCG.
- **Ultrasonography (USG):** Pelvic USG or examination is done one week after the molar evacuation.
- **Chest X-ray:** If the pre-evacuation chest radiograph was normal, it is repeated only if the patient develops respiratory symptoms or if the serum hCG titer plateaus or rises.

**Contraceptive Advice** The patient must strictly avoid pregnancy for at least 1 year during the follow-up period, as a new pregnancy will cause a rise in hCG, masking the development of GTN.

- **Recommended Methods:** Barrier methods (condoms) can be safely used. Injectable progestins (DMPA) or progestin implants are also safe and effective. Surgical sterilization is an excellent alternative for women who have completed their family.
- **Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs):** These can be safely prescribed only _after_ the serum hCG values have returned to normal.
- **Contraindicated Method:** Intrauterine Contraceptive Devices (IUCDs) are strictly contraindicated due to a high risk of uterine perforation and frequent association with irregular vaginal bleeding, which can create severe clinical confusion with choriocarcinoma.

**Unfavorable Manifestations (High-Risk Signs of GTN)** During follow-up, the appearance of the following signs indicates the development of persistent GTN or malignancy:

1. Persistent ill health.
2. Irregular vaginal bleeding or subinvolution of the uterus.
3. Appearance of respiratory symptoms or secondary vaginal metastases.
4. Chest radiograph showing "cannon ball" shadows (pulmonary metastasis).
5. Serum $\beta$-hCG titers remain persistently elevated or show re-elevation after an initial negative report.

**FIGO Diagnostic Criteria for Postmolar GTN** Intervention or chemotherapy is initiated if follow-up reveals any of the following criteria:

- $\ge$ 4 values of plateaued hCG ($\pm10%$) over a period of at least 3 weeks (e.g., Days 1, 7, 14, 21).
- A rise in hCG levels of $>10%$ across $>3$ values over at least 2 weeks time.
- Persistence of detectable hCG beyond 6 months after the evacuation of the mole.
- Histologic diagnosis of choriocarcinoma.


18. Complications of laparoscopy
**COMPLICATIONS OF LAPAROSCOPY**

The complications of laparoscopy can be broadly classified into three main groups based on their etiology: (A) Specific to laparoscopy itself, (B) Due to anesthesia, and (C) Common to any surgical procedure.

**A. Complications Specific to Laparoscopy Itself**

1. **Extraperitoneal Insufflation:**
    - Leads to surgical emphysema, omental emphysema, and cardiac arrhythmias.
2. **Injury to Blood Vessels:**
    - Injury to mesenteric and omental vessels, or major pelvic/abdominal vessels (aorta, inferior vena cava, and iliac vessels).
    - Inferior epigastric vessels may be injured specifically during the insertion of accessory trocars.
3. **Injury to Viscera (Bowel, Bladder, Ureter):**
    - **Bowel Injury:** Can occur with the Veress needle or trocar, especially in the presence of severe intra-abdominal adhesions. Thermal bowel injuries often go unrecognized intraoperatively, and a delay in diagnosis can be life-threatening.
    - **Mechanism of Damage:** Can be mechanical (during dissection) or thermal (due to electrical or laser energy).
4. **Electrosurgical Complications:**
    - Thermal injury due to electrode burns or insulation defects, commonly affecting the bowel and ureter.
5. **Gas (Carbon Dioxide) Embolism:**
    - A fatal complication resulting in severe hypotension and cardiac arrhythmias.

**B. Anesthetic Complications (Peculiar to Laparoscopy)**

1. **Hypoventilation:** Created by basal lung compression and reduced diaphragmatic excursion, which is a direct consequence of the pneumoperitoneum combined with the steep Trendelenburg position.
2. **Hypercarbia and Metabolic Acidosis:** Occurs when $CO_2$ is absorbed systemically from the pneumoperitoneum.
3. **Cardiovascular:** Tachycardia and arrhythmias.
4. **Pulmonary:** Basal lung atelectasis.
5. **Others:** Esophageal intubation, aspiration, and cardiac arrest.

**C. Complications Common to Any Surgical Procedure**

1. **Hemorrhage**.
2. **Infection**.
3. **Wound Dehiscence**.
4. **Port Site Hernia**.

**Mortality / Fatality**

- The death rate in diagnostic laparoscopy is approximately 5 per 100,000 procedures, though this rate is markedly reduced to almost zero with surgical experience.
- The primary causes of death associated with laparoscopy are cardiac arrest, gas embolism, and the delayed consequences of intestinal injuries.

19. Causes of Hirsuitism.
**DEFINITION** Hirsutism is defined as the excessive growth of androgen-dependent sexual hair (terminal hair) in the facial and central parts of the body (male pattern) that worries a female. It represents one of the early manifestations in the spectrum of virilism.

**PATHOPHYSIOLOGY** Hirsutism is caused by an interplay of factors involving androgen production and target-organ sensitivity. The basic mechanisms include:

- Increased concentration of serum androgens, especially testosterone.
- Decreased levels of sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), which results in an increased fraction of free, biologically active testosterone.
- Increased activity of the $5\alpha$-reductase enzyme in the skin and hair follicles, which converts testosterone into the highly potent dihydrotestosterone (DHT).
- Increased responsiveness of the target organ (skin/pilosebaceous unit) to normal circulating androgens, which is often influenced by ethnic or familial background.

**CAUSES OF HIRSUTISM (HYPERANDROGENISM)** The etiology of hirsutism can be systematically classified into the following high-yield categories:

**1. Ovarian Causes**

- **Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome (PCOS):** The most common endocrine disorder causing hirsutism in females of reproductive age.
- **Androgen-secreting Neoplasms:** Sertoli-Leydig cell tumors (androblastoma), Hilus cell tumors, and Lipoid cell tumors.
- **Other Ovarian Conditions:** Stromal hyperthecosis and Luteoma of pregnancy.

**2. Adrenal Causes**

- **Adrenal Hyperplasia:** Both congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) and late-onset (non-classic) adrenal hyperplasia.
- **Cushing’s Syndrome:** Due to excess glucocorticoid and androgen production.
- **Adrenal Tumors:** Adenomas or carcinomas secreting excess androgens.

**3. Pituitary Causes**

- **Cushing's Disease:** Pituitary tumors (microadenomas) secreting excess Adrenocorticotropic Hormone (ACTH), which secondarily stimulates the adrenal cortex to overproduce cortisol and androgens.
- **Acromegaly:** Pituitary tumors secreting excess growth hormone.

**4. Exogenous / Iatrogenic Causes (Drug Therapy)** Iatrogenic hirsutism is caused by the administration of medications that have androgenic properties or alter hormone binding. Common offending drugs include:

- Androgens and anabolic steroids.
- Synthetic progestogens and certain combined oral contraceptives.
- Danazol (an isoxazole derivative with androgenic properties).
- Other medications: Phenytoin, diazoxide, and cortisone/corticosteroids.

**5. Obesity and Metabolic Syndromes**

- **Android Obesity:** Characterized by central fat distribution, leading to insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, and subsequent androgen excess.
- **HAIR-AN Syndrome:** A specific triad consisting of Hyperandrogenism, Insulin Resistance, and Acanthosis Nigricans.

**6. Physiological and Life-Stage Associated Causes**

- Mild hirsutism is not infrequently observed during physiological states such as puberty and pregnancy.
- **Postmenopause:** During the postmenopausal period, there is a marked decrease in SHBG levels (especially in obese patients), which results in elevated free testosterone and resultant hirsutism.

**7. Idiopathic Causes**

- Idiopathic hirsutism occurs when serum androgen levels are normal and menstrual cycles are regular. It is primarily due to the increased sensitivity of the pilosebaceous unit (PSU) to circulating androgens. This category is often strongly correlated with genetic, familial, or ethnic predispositions.


20. Six indications of Colposcopy
**INDICATIONS OF COLPOSCOPY**

Colposcopy is an _in-vivo_ clinical examination of the cervix, vagina, and vulva using a low-power binocular microscope (6–16 times magnification). It evaluates the terminal vascular network and morphological changes of the tissue to identify the exact site for a targeted biopsy.

The high-yield six major indications for colposcopy in gynecological practice are:

**1. Abnormal Cervical Cytology (Pap Smear)** It is the primary indication for colposcopic evaluation. It is strictly recommended for women presenting with:

- High-Grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesions (HSIL) or Atypical Squamous Cells, cannot exclude HSIL (ASC-H).
- Atypical Glandular Cells (AGC).
- Atypical Squamous Cells of Undetermined Significance (ASC-US) or Low-Grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesions (LSIL) that are positive for high-risk HPV-DNA.

**2. Clinically Suspicious Cervix** A visually abnormal, unhealthy, or suspicious-looking cervix on per speculum examination mandates a colposcopy to rule out malignancy, even if the Pap smear cytology report is negative/normal.

**3. Persistent High-Risk HPV Infection** Women who persistently test positive for high-risk Human Papillomavirus (HR HPV-DNA) test results (e.g., HPV 16, 18) require colposcopy to detect any underlying Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia (CIN).

**4. Unexplained Vaginal Bleeding** Patients presenting with unexplained postcoital (contact) bleeding or abnormal intermenstrual bleeding should undergo colposcopy to rule out local cervical pathology or early invasive carcinoma.

**5. Evaluation of Vulvar and Vaginal Neoplasia** Colposcopy is indicated to properly magnify, delineate, and evaluate precancerous conditions of the lower genital tract, specifically Vulvar Intraepithelial Neoplasia (VIN) and Vaginal Intraepithelial Neoplasia (VaIN).

**6. Directed Biopsy and Post-treatment Surveillance** Colposcopy is indicated to guide a targeted biopsy exactly from abnormal areas (such as acetowhite epithelium, punctation, mosaicism, or atypical vessels). It is also indicated for the strict follow-up surveillance (usually at 6, 12, and 24 months) of women who have been previously treated for CIN via ablative or excisional methods.


21. Risk factors for ovarian carcinoma

**RISK FACTORS FOR OVARIAN CARCINOMA**

**1. Genetic and Familial Factors** Familial predisposition is a highly significant risk factor, accounting for approximately 10–15% of all epithelial ovarian cancers.

- **BRCA Mutations (Hereditary Breast Ovarian Cancer Syndrome):** Accounts for 80–95% of all familial ovarian cancers. Mutations in tumor suppressor genes **BRCA1** (chromosome 17q21) and **BRCA2** (chromosome 13q12) are strongly implicated. The estimated lifetime risk of developing ovarian cancer is 50% with a BRCA1 mutation and 25% with a BRCA2 mutation.
- **Lynch Syndrome (Hereditary Nonpolyposis Colorectal Cancer - HNPCC):** An autosomal dominant disorder caused by DNA mismatch repair gene mutations. Women with Lynch II syndrome have a 12% lifetime risk for developing ovarian cancer.
- **Family History:** Having a first-degree relative (mother, sister, or daughter) or second-degree relative (aunt, grandmother) affected by breast, endometrial, ovarian, or colorectal cancer increases the risk.

**2. Reproductive and Menstrual Factors**

- **Nulliparity and Infertility:** Nulligravidas and women with absolute or relative infertility carry a higher risk.
- **Incessant Ovulation Theory:** Proposed by Fathalla, this theory suggests that repeated ovulatory trauma to the ovarian epithelial lining promotes carcinogenesis.
- **Prolonged Reproductive Span:** Early menarche and late menopause increase the total number of ovulatory cycles a woman experiences, thereby increasing the risk.
- **Fertility Drugs:** The use of ovulation-inducing drugs contributes to the risk, tying into the incessant ovulation theory.

**3. Age and Demographics**

- **Age:** The risk increases with advancing age, with the peak incidence group being 40–60 years. Approximately 60% of ovarian neoplasms in postmenopausal women are malignant.
- **Geography:** The highest incidence is recorded in industrialized countries (e.g., Sweden, USA, and UK).
- **Postmenopausal Palpable Ovary:** An ovarian volume >8 cm³ in a postmenopausal woman is considered a high-risk factor requiring thorough investigation.

**4. Medical and Gynecological History**

- **Previous Malignancy:** A history of a previously removed benign ovarian tumor or breast carcinoma.
- **Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID):** Associated with an increased risk.
- **Dysgenetic Gonad:** Predisposes individuals specifically to malignant germ cell tumors like gonadoblastoma and dysgerminoma.

**5. Environmental and Lifestyle Factors**

- **Obesity:** Women with a Body Mass Index (BMI) > 30 are at higher risk.
- **Diet and Habits:** High dietary fat, coffee, tobacco, and alcohol use have been implicated in the pathogenesis.
- **Chemical Exposure:** Environmental exposure to talc and working in asbestos-related industries are associated with increased risk.

---

**PROTECTIVE FACTORS** _(Factors that decrease the risk of developing ovarian carcinoma)_ Conditions that suppress ovulation provide a protective effect against ovarian cancer:

- **Hormonal Contraception:** The use of Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs) significantly reduces the risk by about 50%, and this protective effect persists for 10–15 years even after stopping the pills. Depot Medroxyprogesterone Acetate (DMPA) is also protective.
- **Reproductive Factors:** Multiparity (repeated pregnancies) and breastfeeding provide rest to the ovaries from continuous ovulation, reducing the risk.
- **Surgical Factors:** Tubal ligation, opportunistic or prophylactic bilateral salpingectomy, and hysterectomy significantly reduce the risk.
- **Lifestyle:** A low-fat and high-fiber diet acts as a protective factor.

22. Progesterone in gynaecology

**PROGESTERONE IN GYNECOLOGY**

**Introduction and Physiology** Progesterone is a natural C-21 steroid hormone produced mainly by the theca lutein cells of the corpus luteum, the placenta during pregnancy, and in small amounts by the adrenal cortex. Daily production is 2–3 mg in the follicular phase and 20–30 mg in the luteal phase. It acts on an estrogen-primed endometrium to produce secretory changes, induces myohyperplasia, diminishes myometrial contractility, and makes cervical mucus thick and viscid to prevent sperm penetration.

**Classification of Progestogens** Progestogens (or progestins) are synthetic compounds having the properties of progesterone, classified by their structural derivatives:

- **Pregnane derivatives:** $17\alpha$-hydroxyprogesterone caproate, Medroxyprogesterone acetate (MPA), Cyproterone acetate.
- **Norpregnane derivatives:** Nomegestrol.
- **Retroprogesterone (Stereoisomer):** Dydrogesterone (does not inhibit ovulation).
- **19-Nortestosterone derivatives:** Estranes (Norethisterone) and Gonanes (Levonorgestrel, Desogestrel, Gestodene, Norgestimate).
- **Spironolactone derivatives:** Drospirenone (has antimineralocorticoid and antiandrogenic properties).

**Diagnostic Uses**

- **Progesterone Challenge Test:** Used in the investigation of secondary amenorrhea. Medroxyprogesterone acetate 10 mg daily is given orally for 5 days. If withdrawal bleeding occurs, it proves an intact hypothalamo-pituitary-ovarian axis, adequate endogenous estrogen (>40 pg/mL), a responsive endometrium, and a patent uterovaginal canal.
- **Estrogen-Progesterone Challenge Test:** Used to exclude uterine pathology (like uterine synechiae) if the progesterone challenge test fails.

**Therapeutic Indications (Pharmacotherapeutics)**

**1. Contraception**

- **Progestin-Only Pill (Minipill):** Safe during lactation as it does not affect breast milk.
- **Emergency Contraception:** Levonorgestrel 1.50 mg as a single dose or two 0.75 mg doses 12 hours apart, taken within 72-120 hours of unprotected coitus.
- **Injectables:** Depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA) 150 mg every 3 months or Norethisterone enanthate (NET-EN) 200 mg every 2 months.
- **Implants & Devices:** Subdermal implants (Implanon) and Levonorgestrel-Intrauterine System (LNG-IUS / Mirena).

**2. Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (AUB) / Dysfunctional Uterine Bleeding (DUB)**

- Progestins are highly effective in anovulatory bleeding by causing organized shedding of the endometrium up to the basal layer.
- Norethisterone or MPA (5 mg thrice daily) is given to arrest acute bleeding, followed by cyclic therapy from day 5 to 25 to regulate the cycle.
- LNG-IUS is recommended as a first-line medical therapy for heavy menstrual bleeding, reducing blood loss by 80–100% by inducing endometrial glandular atrophy.

**3. Endometriosis**

- Progestogens induce a hyperprogestogenic-hypoestrogenic state, causing decidualization, fibrosis, and atrophy of ectopic endometrial tissues.
- Drugs commonly used include MPA, dydrogesterone, norethisterone, dienogest, or LNG-IUS continuously for 6–9 months to produce amenorrhea.

**4. Endometrial Hyperplasia and Carcinoma**

- Progestins counteract unopposed estrogen stimulation, preventing and treating endometrial hyperplasia.
- They are used in well-differentiated (Grade I) endometrial carcinoma and recurrent carcinoma with good steroid (progesterone) receptor status.
- $17\alpha$-hydroxyprogesterone caproate (1,000 mg IM) or high-dose MPA is utilized for this purpose.

**5. Luteal Phase Defect (LPD) and Luteal Support in ART**

- Progesterone is crucial for supporting implantation and early pregnancy.
- Micronized progesterone (100-200 mg thrice daily orally or vaginally) or IM progesterone in oil is given starting after ovulation or oocyte retrieval until 10-12 weeks of gestation.

**6. Hormone Replacement Therapy (HRT)**

- In postmenopausal women with an intact uterus, progestin (e.g., MPA 2.5–5 mg/day) MUST be combined with estrogen therapy to counterbalance the risk of estrogen-induced endometrial hyperplasia and carcinoma.

**7. Other Gynecological Uses**

- **Dysmenorrhea:** Dydrogesterone 5 mg starting from day 5 for 20 days relieves pain by inhibiting uterine contractions without suppressing ovulation.
- **Postponement of Menstruation:** Norethisterone 5 mg thrice daily is started at least 3 days prior to the expected date of menstruation and continued until the desired withdrawal date.
- **Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS):** Dydrogesterone may be tried in the luteal phase to relieve symptoms.

**Mechanism of Antiestrogenic Action on Endometrium** Progestins effectively halt endometrial growth through several mechanisms:

- They decrease the synthesis of estrogen receptors in the endometrium.
- They induce the enzyme estradiol dehydrogenase (17-$\beta$-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase), which converts potent estradiol to the less potent estrone.
- They inhibit the mitotic and proliferative activity of endometrial cells.

**Side Effects of Progestogens**

- **General/Metabolic:** Nausea, leg cramps, mastalgia, weight gain (due to salt and water retention), depression, and mood changes (possibly due to altered tryptophan metabolism).
- **Androgenic Effects (seen with older progestins like Levonorgestrel):** Acne, hirsutism, virilism, scanty periods, and loss of libido.
- **Lipid Profile:** Progestins can unfavorably increase Low-Density Lipoprotein (LDL) and decrease High-Density Lipoprotein (HDL) levels.
