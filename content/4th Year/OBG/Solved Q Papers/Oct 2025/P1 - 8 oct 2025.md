1. Describe stages of labor. Discuss management and complications of third stage of labor.
**STAGES OF LABOR**

Labor is defined as the series of events that take place in the genital organs in an effort to expel the viable products of conception (fetus, placenta, and membranes) out of the womb through the vagina into the outer world. Conventionally, the events of labor are divided into three stages, followed by a fourth stage of observation.

**1. First Stage (Cervical Stage)**

- **Definition:** It starts from the onset of true labor pain and ends with full dilatation of the cervix (10 cm).
- **Duration:** The average duration is 12 hours in primigravidae and 6 hours in multiparae.
- **Phases:** It consists of a latent phase (up to 5 cm of cervical dilatation) and an active phase (from 5 cm to 10 cm of cervical dilatation).
- **Main Events:** The principal events are the effacement and dilatation of the cervix, and the full formation of the lower uterine segment to prepare the birth canal for fetal expulsion.

**2. Second Stage**

- **Definition:** It starts from the full dilatation of the cervix and ends with the expulsion of the fetus from the birth canal.
- **Duration:** The average duration is 2 hours in primigravidae and 30 minutes in multiparae.
- **Phases:** It has two phases:
    - _Propulsive (passive) phase:_ From full cervical dilatation until the head touches the pelvic floor.
    - _Expulsive (active) phase:_ Characterized by maternal bearing-down efforts, ending with the delivery of the baby.

**3. Third Stage**

- **Definition:** It begins after the expulsion of the fetus and ends with the expulsion of the placenta and membranes (afterbirths).
- **Duration:** Its average duration is about 15 minutes in both primigravidae and multiparae, but is reduced to 5 minutes with active management.

**4. Fourth Stage**

- **Definition:** It is the stage of observation for at least 1 hour after the expulsion of the afterbirths.
- **Events:** During this period, maternal vitals (pulse, blood pressure), uterine retraction, and vaginal bleeding are monitored, and the baby is examined to ensure both are well.

---

**MANAGEMENT OF THE THIRD STAGE OF LABOR**

The third stage is the most crucial stage of labor. A previously uneventful first and second stage can become abnormal within a minute with disastrous consequences, primarily postpartum hemorrhage (PPH). The principles of management are to ensure strict vigilance and follow management guidelines strictly to prevent complications.

There are two methods of management currently in practice:

1. Active Management (Preferred)
2. Expectant (Physiological) Management

**I. Active Management of the Third Stage of Labor (AMTSL)** This is the preferred routine management for all women in labor as it reduces the incidence of PPH by 60%.

- **Principle:** To excite powerful uterine contractions within 1 minute of the delivery of the baby by giving a parenteral oxytocic.
- **Advantages:** Minimizes blood loss to approximately one-fifth, shortens the duration of the third stage to half, reduces maternal anemia, and lessens the need for blood transfusion and PPH management.
- **Steps of AMTSL:**
    1. **Administration of Uterotonics:** Injection Oxytocin 10 units IM (preferred), or injection methergine 0.2 mg IM, or tablet Misoprostol 600 µg (orally or rectally) is administered to the mother within one minute of the birth of the baby.
    2. **Delayed Cord Clamping:** The cord is clamped and cut.
    3. **Controlled Cord Traction (CCT):** The placenta is delivered by CCT (Brandt-Andrews technique) availing the first uterine contraction.
    4. **Uterine Massage:** The uterus is massaged immediately after the delivery of the placenta to incite uterine contractions.
    5. **Examination:** Careful examination of the placenta and membranes for completeness, and examination of the genital tract to exclude and repair any injury.

**II. Expectant (Physiological) Management**

- **Principle:** Watchful expectancy for the traditional signs of placental separation (lengthening of the cord, gush of blood, suprapubic bulge).
- **Process:** Spontaneous expulsion is awaited with the aid of gravity or maternal bearing-down efforts. If the patient fails to expel the placenta, one can wait safely up to 10 minutes if there is no bleeding. As the placenta passes through the introitus, it is grasped and twisted to strip the membranes intact.

---

**COMPLICATIONS OF THE THIRD STAGE OF LABOR**

Fatal complications may appear unexpectedly during the third stage. The important complications include:

**1. Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH)**

- **Definition:** Bleeding from the genital tract in excess of 500 mL following the birth of the baby.
- **Causes (The 4 Ts):**
    - _Tone (Atony):_ Poor uterine retraction due to prolonged labor, anesthesia, mismanaged third stage, or uterine fibroids.
    - _Tissue:_ Retained tissues, bits of placenta, or blood clots causing imperfect uterine retraction.
    - _Trauma:_ Injury to the cervix, vagina, perineum, paraurethral region, or rupture of the uterus.
    - _Thrombin:_ Blood coagulation disorders, acquired or congenital.
- **Management:** Empty the uterus of its contents (removal of placenta), massage the uterus to make it hard, replace blood/manage shock, and administer oxytocics (Oxytocin, Methergine, PGF2a, or Misoprostol). In refractory cases, bimanual compression, uterine balloon tamponade, or surgical interventions (hemostatic sutures, internal iliac artery ligation, hysterectomy) may be required.

**2. Retained Placenta**

- **Definition:** The placenta is said to be retained when it is not expelled out even 30 minutes after the birth of the baby (WHO defines it as 15 minutes).
- **Causes:**
    - Placenta completely separated but retained due to poor voluntary expulsive efforts.
    - Simple adherent placenta due to uterine atonicity.
    - Morbid adherent placenta (Placenta Accreta, Increta, Percreta).
- **Management:** Wait with watchful expectancy if there is no bleeding. If separated, express via CCT. If unseparated or accompanied by bleeding, manual removal of the placenta under general anesthesia is indicated.

**3. Uterine Inversion**

- **Definition:** A rare, life-threatening complication where the uterus is turned inside out partially or completely. Incidence is about 1 in 20,000 deliveries.
- **Etiology:** May be spontaneous (fundal attachment of placenta combined with increased intra-abdominal pressure) or iatrogenic (pulling the cord when the uterus is atonic, or applying fundal pressure while the uterus is relaxed).
- **Dangers:** Profound neurogenic shock, hemorrhage, and pulmonary embolism.
- **Management:** Immediate replacement of the inverted fundus under general anesthesia by steady firm pressure, followed by the administration of oxytocics once the uterus is correctly repositioned to maintain contraction.

**4. Shock**

- Can be hemorrhagic (due to massive blood loss) or non-hemorrhagic (such as neurogenic shock from uterine inversion). Requires immediate resuscitation, fluid-electrolyte correction, and blood transfusion.

**5. Pulmonary Embolism**

- Embolism caused either by amniotic fluid or by air, presenting as sudden maternal collapse and severe respiratory distress.

2. Define gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). Discuss adverse fetal and neonatal consequences of GDM.
**DEFINITION OF GESTATIONAL DIABETES MELLITUS (GDM)**

Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) is defined as carbohydrate intolerance of variable severity with onset or first recognition during the present pregnancy.

- **Onset:** It typically presents late in the second trimester or during the third trimester.
- **Scope:** This definition applies regardless of whether the condition persists after pregnancy or whether insulin is required for treatment. It also includes women who may have had previously unrecognized overt diabetes prior to pregnancy.

**ADVERSE CONSEQUENCES OF GDM**

The maternal metabolic disturbance in GDM significantly impacts fetal development and neonatal transition, primarily driven by maternal hyperglycemia leading to fetal hyperinsulinemia. Notably, the risk of congenital fetal anomalies is _not_ increased in true GDM, as the carbohydrate intolerance develops after the critical period of fetal organogenesis in the first trimester.

**1. Fetal Consequences**

- **Fetal Macrosomia:** Occurs in 40–50% of cases, characterized by a birth weight greater than 4 kg or >90th percentile for gestational age. According to the Pedersen hypothesis, maternal hyperglycemia crosses the placenta, causing fetal hyperglycemia, which stimulates fetal pancreatic islet cell hypertrophy and hyperplasia. The resulting fetal hyperinsulinemia drives excessive carbohydrate utilization and fat accumulation (adiposity). Elevated maternal free fatty acids also cross the placenta, accelerating fetal triglyceride synthesis and further contributing to adiposity.
- **Polyhydramnios:** Complicates 25–50% of GDM pregnancies. The exact mechanism involves fetal hyperglycemia inducing fetal osmotic diuresis (polyuria), which subsequently increases the amniotic fluid volume.
- **Preterm Birth:** High risk of preterm labor and delivery, often occurring secondary to uterine overdistension from polyhydramnios or associated infections.
- **Intrauterine Fetal Death (IUFD):** Sudden, unexplained fetal death is a severe risk, particularly in cases with poor glycemic control, fetal macrosomia, or superimposed pre-eclampsia. The pathogenesis is multifactorial, ultimately culminating in fetal hypoxia and lactic acidemia. Fetal hyperinsulinemia and hyperglycemia increase the fetal oxygen demand, while placental villus edema, fetal polycythemia, and hyperviscosity further compromise oxygen delivery.

**2. Intrapartum Fetal Complications**

- **Birth Injuries:** Macrosomia significantly increases the risk of cephalopelvic disproportion, prolonged labor, and shoulder dystocia. Consequently, fetuses are at a high risk for traumatic birth injuries, including brachial plexus injuries (Erb's palsy).
- **Fetal Distress:** Fetal hypoxia and acidosis can develop rapidly during labor.

**3. Neonatal Consequences**

- **Neonatal Hypoglycemia:** Affects approximately 50% of infants, particularly macrosomic babies. After the umbilical cord is clamped, the continuous supply of maternal glucose abruptly ceases, but the neonate's hyperplastic beta cells continue to secrete high levels of insulin, leading to a rapid and precipitous drop in plasma glucose levels (<35–40 mg/dL).
- **Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS):** Fetal hyperinsulinemia acts antagonistically to cortisol, blocking its maturational effect on type II pneumocytes. This delays the synthesis of surfactant phospholipids, causing RDS even in late preterm or term infants.
- **Neonatal Hyperbilirubinemia:** Seen in 25–50% of cases. It occurs due to the breakdown of an excessive red blood cell mass (polycythemia) and hepatic immaturity.
- **Neonatal Polycythemia:** Chronic intrauterine hypoxia stimulates increased fetal erythropoietin production, leading to polycythemia, which can cause hyperviscosity syndrome.
- **Electrolyte Imbalances:** Neonates are at high risk for hypocalcemia (≤7 mg/dL) and hypomagnesemia (<7 mg/dL).
- **Neonatal Cardiomyopathy:** Poorly controlled GDM can lead to hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, classically involving the interventricular septum. This is primarily driven by excessive fetal insulin, which can lead to obstructive cardiac failure and right ventricular hypertrophy (often resolving postpartum).

**4. Long-Term Infant Consequences**

- **Childhood and Adult Obesity:** Infants born to mothers with GDM have a two-fold increased risk of becoming overweight or obese later in life.
- **Metabolic Syndrome and Type 2 Diabetes:** These infants face a four-fold increased risk of developing metabolic syndrome, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes mellitus in adulthood.


3. Couvelaire uterus
**COUVELAIRE UTERUS (UTEROPLACENTAL APOPLEXY)**

**Definition** Couvelaire uterus, also known as uteroplacental apoplexy, is a specific pathological entity first described by Couvelaire. It is typically encountered in association with a severe form of concealed abruptio placentae. It is characterized by the massive intravasation (percolation) of blood into the uterine musculature, extending outward up to the serous coat of the uterus.

**Diagnosis** The condition cannot be diagnosed clinically; it can only be diagnosed by direct visual inspection of the uterus during a laparotomy (e.g., during a cesarean section).

**Gross (Naked Eye) Pathology**

- **Color:** The uterus classically assumes a dark port-wine color, which may present as patchy or diffuse discoloration.
- **Site of Origin:** The extravasation tends to occur initially at the uterine cornu before spreading to other areas, most prominently over the placental implantation site.
- **Hemorrhagic Spread:** Subperitoneal petechial hemorrhages are found beneath the uterine peritoneum and may extend outward into the broad ligament.
- **Associated Findings:** There may be free blood within the peritoneal cavity or the formation of a broad ligament hematoma. On occasion, the uterine serosa may split entirely, allowing blood to spill directly into the peritoneal cavity.

**Microscopic Pathology**

- **Muscle Necrosis:** The uterine muscle fibers overlying the affected area undergo necrosis.
- **Muscular Dissociation:** There is extensive infiltration of blood and fluid between the muscle bundles. This muscular dissociation predominantly occurs within the middle and outer layers of the myometrium.
- **Vascular Changes:** The local blood vessels exhibit acute degenerative changes accompanied by thrombosis.

**Clinical Significance and Management**

- **Uterine Contractility:** Despite the profound myometrial hematoma, necrosis, and muscular dissociation, the condition rarely interferes with the uterus's ability to contract effectively following the delivery of the fetus and placenta.
- **Role of Hysterectomy:** Because normal uterine contractions are usually preserved, the mere visual diagnosis of a Couvelaire uterus during a cesarean section is _not_ an indication per se for performing a hysterectomy. The uterus can typically be conserved if bleeding is otherwise controlled.
- **Complications:** While rare, a Couvelaire uterus can weaken the uterine wall enough to be a predisposing cause of spontaneous rupture of the uninjured uterus during pregnancy.

4. Transverse lie
**TRANSVERSE LIE (SHOULDER PRESENTATION)**

**DEFINITION** When the long axis of the fetus lies perpendicularly to the maternal spine or centralized uterine axis, it is called a transverse lie. However, more commonly, the fetal axis is placed at an acute angle or oblique to the maternal spine (oblique lie). In either condition, the shoulder usually presents over the cervical opening during labor, and both are collectively called shoulder presentations.

**POSITIONS** The position is determined by the direction of the fetal back, which is the denominator.

- **Dorsoanterior (60%):** Most common. The flexor surface of the fetus is better adapted to the convexity of the maternal spine.
- **Dorsoposterior:** Carries an increased risk of fetal extension and arm prolapse.
- **Dorsosuperior and Dorsoinferior:** These are rare.
- Depending on the position of the fetal head, the position is termed right or left (left is more common).

**INCIDENCE**

- Overall incidence is about 1 in 200 births.
- It is 5 times more common in multiparae than primigravidae.
- It is common in premature and macerated fetuses.
- Transverse lie in twin pregnancy is found in 40% of cases.

**ETIOLOGY (CAUSES)**

1. **Multiparity (Most common):** Lax and pendulous abdomen, imperfect uterine tone, and extreme uterine obliquity.
2. **Prematurity:** The center of gravity lies almost in the middle of the fetal body.
3. **Multiple Pregnancy (Twins):** More common in the second baby.
4. **Polyhydramnios (Hydramnios)**.
5. **Placenta Previa**.
6. **Contracted Pelvis**.
7. **Pelvic Tumors**.
8. **Uterine Malformations:** Arcuate or subseptate uterus.
9. **Intrauterine Fetal Death (IUFD)**.

---

**DIAGNOSIS**

**I. Abdominal Examination**

- **Inspection:** The uterus looks broader and asymmetrical, losing its normal pyriform shape.
- **Palpation:**
    - _Fundal Height:_ Less than the period of amenorrhea.
    - _Fundal Grip:_ Empty (fetal pole is not palpable).
    - _Lateral Grip:_ A soft, broad, irregular breech is felt on one side, and a hard, smooth, globular head is felt on the other side (usually at a lower level in the iliac fossa). The back is felt anteriorly in dorsoanterior, or irregular small parts are felt anteriorly in dorsoposterior positions.
    - _Pelvic Grip:_ The lower pole of the uterus is empty during pregnancy (may be occupied by the shoulder during labor).
- **Auscultation:** Fetal heart sounds (FHS) are easily heard much below the umbilicus in dorsoanterior positions, but are located at a higher level and are indistinct in dorsoposterior positions.

**II. Vaginal Examination**

- **During pregnancy:** The presenting part is too high to be properly identified.
- **During labor:**
    - An elongated bag of membranes can be felt if it hasn't ruptured.
    - The shoulder is identified by palpating the acromion process, scapula, clavicle, and axilla.
    - A characteristic diagnostic sign is the feeling of the ribs and intercostal spaces (**"grid iron feel"**).
    - Prolapse of the arm/hand with or without a loop of cord is frequent. To determine the side of the prolapsed arm, one can "shake hands" with the fetus (e.g., if the obstetrician uses their right hand to correctly shake the fetal hand, it is the right arm).

**III. Ultrasonography (USG)** Confirms the diagnosis when clinical examination is difficult (e.g., in obese women) and assesses the liquor volume, placental location, and fetal anomalies.

---

**CLINICAL COURSE OF LABOR** There is no mechanism of labor in transverse lie, and an average-sized baby fails to pass through an average-sized pelvis.

- **Favorable Events (Extremely Rare):** May occur in multiparae with small/macerated fetuses and adequate liquor. Include _Spontaneous rectification_ (converts to vertex), _Spontaneous version_ (converts to breech), _Spontaneous evolution_ (breech delivers first, then trunk, then head), or _Spontaneous expulsion_.
- **Unfavorable Events (Common if left uncared for):**
    - Absence of a well-fitting presenting part leads to early/premature rupture of membranes with a sudden escape of liquor.
    - High risk of cord prolapse and hand prolapse.
    - The upper uterine segment strongly contracts while the lower segment stretches, leading to a neglected shoulder presentation, tonic contraction, and eventually **uterine rupture**.
    - **Maternal risks:** Dehydration, ketoacidosis, septicemia, hemorrhage, shock, peritonitis, and death.
    - **Fetal risks:** Perinatal mortality is immensely high (25–50%) due to cord prolapse, asphyxia, and neonatal sepsis.

---

**MANAGEMENT PROTOCOL**

**1. Antenatal Management**

- **External Cephalic Version (ECV):** Attempted in all cases beyond 36 weeks provided there are no contraindications.
- **Hospitalization:** If lie fails to stabilize or ECV is contraindicated, admit the patient at 36 weeks due to the high risk of PROM and cord prolapse.
- **Elective Cesarean Delivery:** Preferred method of delivery after 37 completed weeks if ECV fails or is contraindicated.

**2. Patient Seen in Early Labor**

- **ECV:** May be attempted under careful monitoring and tocolysis in selected cases if membranes are intact and liquor is adequate.
- **Cesarean Section:** The preferred and safest method of delivery if version fails or is contraindicated. A low transverse incision is commonly used, but a vertical (classical) incision may be required if the lower segment is poorly developed (e.g., 26-28 weeks) or in dorsoinferior positions.
- **Internal Podalic Version:** Strictly NOT recommended in modern obstetrics for a singleton pregnancy due to high maternal/fetal risks. Its only indication is for delivering the transverse second baby of twins.

**3. Patient Seen in Late/Neglected Labor**

- **Live Baby:** Immediate Cesarean delivery.
- **Dead Baby:** Cesarean delivery is still the treatment of choice as it is much safer than destructive procedures like fetal evisceration.

5. Postterm pregnancy
**DEFINITION AND CLASSIFICATION** A pregnancy continuing beyond 2 weeks of the expected date of delivery (>294 days) is clinically termed a prolonged or post-term pregnancy. According to the ACOG classification of term pregnancy:

- **Late term:** 41°17 weeks through 41 6/7 weeks.
- **Post-term:** 42°17 weeks and beyond.

**INCIDENCE** The incidence of pregnancies continuing beyond 42 completed weeks ranges between 4% and 10%, varying based on the dating criteria used (clinical versus sonography).

**ETIOLOGY** The exact cause of the prolongation of pregnancy remains obscure, but several associated factors include:

- **Wrong dates:** Due to an inaccurate Last Menstrual Period (LMP), which is the most common cause.
- **Biological variability:** A hereditary tendency may be seen in families.
- **Maternal factors:** Primiparity, previous history of prolonged pregnancy, sedentary lifestyle, and elderly multiparae.
- **Fetal factors:** Congenital anomalies such as anencephaly, which causes an abnormal fetal HPA axis and adrenal hypoplasia, leading to a diminished fetal cortisol response.
- **Placental factors:** Sulfatase deficiency leading to low estriol levels.
- **Genetic:** A mother who was herself born post-term has a high risk of a post-term pregnancy.

**DIAGNOSIS** Diagnosis relies on accurate gestational age dating using the LMP, timing of intercourse, early pregnancy ultrasound dating (first-trimester CRL is the most accurate), and the date of embryo transfer.

- **Clinical Findings (when pregnancy overruns EDD by 2 weeks):**
    - _Weight:_ Maternal weight may remain stationary or fall.
    - _Abdominal Girth:_ Gradually diminishes due to a decrease in liquor.
    - _Palpation:_ The uterus feels 'full of fetus' due to scanty liquor amnii.
    - _Internal Examination:_ The presence of an unripe cervix does not exclude fetal maturity.
- **Investigations and Fetal Surveillance:**
    - Daily Fetal Movement Counting (DFMCR); a decrease warrants immediate Biophysical Profile (BPP) evaluation.
    - Non-Stress Test (NST) and BPP should be performed twice a week starting at 41 weeks.
    - Amniotic fluid volume assessment twice a week; diminished amniotic fluid index (pocket <2 cm and AFI <=5 cm) indicates the need for induction or delivery.
    - Doppler velocimetry of the umbilical and middle cerebral arteries; an absence of umbilical artery end-diastolic velocity indicates fetal jeopardy.

**POSTMATURITY SYNDROME (Retrospective Clinical Diagnosis)** This syndrome is diagnosed after birth based on specific characteristics:

- **Baby:** Looks thin and old with wrinkled skin on the palms and soles, muscle wasting, and less subcutaneous fat. Vernix caseosa is absent, and the baby's body and cord are stained a greenish-yellow color. The head is hard with poor molding, and nails protrude beyond the nail beds.
- **Liquor and Placenta:** Liquor is scanty and meconium-stained. The placenta exhibits signs of aging, such as excessive infarction, apoptosis, and calcification.
- **Umbilical Cord:** Shows a diminished quantity of Wharton's jelly, increasing the risk of cord compression.

**COMPLICATIONS** **1. Fetal and Neonatal Risks:** The risk of stillbirth increases three-fold from 37 weeks (0.4 per 1,000) to 43 weeks (11.5 per 1,000).

- _During Pregnancy:_ Diminished placental function and oligohydramnios lead to fetal hypoxia, distress, and intrauterine fetal death (IUFD).
- _During Labor:_ Fetal hypoxia and acidosis, labor dysfunction, cord compression (due to oligohydramnios), meconium aspiration, and shoulder dystocia. There is an increased incidence of birth trauma due to fetal macrosomia and a non-molding, hard skull.
- _Following Birth:_ Neonates may develop chemical pneumonitis, atelectasis, and pulmonary hypertension due to meconium aspiration. Hypoglycemia, polycythemia, respiratory failure, and neonatal convulsions due to Hypoxic Ischemic Encephalopathy (HIE) are common, leading to increased NICU admissions.

**2. Maternal Risks:** Postmaturity per se does not directly put the mother at risk, but complications arise from carrying a large fetus. Maternal morbidity is high due to dysfunctional and prolonged labor, fetopelvic disproportion, birth injuries (like shoulder dystocia), and an increased rate of operative vaginal or cesarean delivery.

**MANAGEMENT** **1. Prevention:** The most common cause of prolonged pregnancy is an error in dating, which can be prevented by a first-trimester ultrasound to accurately confirm the expected date of delivery.

**2. Uncomplicated Cases:** If there is no demonstrable fetal malformation and fetal surveillance remains normal, induction of labor is considered at or beyond 41 weeks to reduce the rate of cesarean delivery and perinatal mortality.

- _Favorable (Ripe) Cervix:_ Induction is performed by stripping the membranes or by artificial rupture of the membranes (ARM/amniotomy). If the liquor is clear, oxytocin infusion may be added.
- _Unfavorable (Unripe) Cervix (Bishop score <6):_ Cervical ripening is achieved using vaginal PGE2 gel. This is followed by low rupture of the membranes and oxytocin augmentation if required.
- Continuous fetal surveillance with NST, BPP, and DFMCR is required while awaiting induction or spontaneous labor. Any evidence of fetal compromise mandates immediate induction and delivery.

**3. Complicated Cases:** Elective cesarean section is recommended when postmaturity is associated with high-risk factors such as elderly primigravidae, pre-eclampsia, Rh-incompatibility, oligohydramnios, or fetal compromise.

**4. Care During Labor:** Whether spontaneous or induced, labor may be prolonged due to fetal macrosomia and poor head molding, requiring adequate analgesia. Continuous electronic fetal monitoring (EFM) is mandatory. The obstetrician must anticipate and be prepared to manage shoulder dystocia. If fetal distress occurs, prompt delivery by cesarean section or operative vaginal delivery (forceps/ventouse) is indicated.


6. Puerperium
**DEFINITION AND DURATION** **Puerperium** is the period following childbirth during which the body tissues, especially the pelvic organs, revert back approximately to the pre-pregnant state both anatomically and physiologically.

- **Duration:** It begins as soon as the placenta is expelled and lasts for approximately 6 weeks.
- **Phases:** It is arbitrarily divided into: (a) Immediate (within 24 hours), (b) Early (up to 7 days), and (c) Remote (up to 6 weeks).

**INVOLUTION OF THE UTERUS** Involution is the process whereby the genital organs revert back nearly to the state as they were before pregnancy. The physiological process is most marked in the body of the uterus.

- **Anatomical Changes:** Immediately following delivery, the uterus is firm and retracted, measuring about 20 x 12 x 7.5 cm and weighing about 1,000 g. By the end of 6 weeks, it regresses to almost its nonpregnant size and weighs about 60 g.
- **Physiological Changes:**
    - _Muscles:_ There is a maximum reduction in the myometrial cell size, rather than a decrease in the cell number.
    - _Blood Vessels:_ Arteries undergo thrombosis, hyalinization, and fibrinoid endarteritis. New blood vessels grow (angiogenesis) inside the thrombi.
    - _Endometrium:_ Regeneration starts by the 7th day and is completed by the 16th day, except at the placental site which takes about 6 weeks for complete regeneration.
- **Clinical Assessment:** Immediately after delivery, the fundus lies about 13.5 cm above the symphysis pubis. It decreases steadily by 1.25 cm per day and becomes a pelvic organ by the end of the 2nd week.

**LOCHIA** Lochia is the vaginal discharge for the first fortnight during puerperium, originating from the uterine body, cervix, and vagina.

- **Characteristics:** It has a peculiar offensive fishy smell and an alkaline reaction. The average amount for the first 5-6 days is 250 mL, and the normal duration may extend up to 3 weeks.
- **Types:**
    1. **Lochia rubra (1-4 days):** Red in color. Consists of blood, shreds of fetal membranes, decidua, vernix caseosa, lanugo, and meconium.
    2. **Lochia serosa (5-9 days):** Yellowish, pink, or pale brownish. Contains fewer RBCs, more leukocytes, wound exudate, cervical mucus, and microorganisms.
    3. **Lochia alba (10-15 days):** Pale white. Contains plenty of decidual cells, leukocytes, mucus, and cholesterin crystals.
- **Clinical Importance:** Malodorous discharge indicates infection. Scanty or absent lochia signifies infection or lochiometra. Excessive amount or persistence of red color beyond the normal limit suggests subinvolution or retained bits of conceptus.

**GENERAL PHYSIOLOGICAL CHANGES**

- **Vitals:** Pulse rate is transiently raised but settles down to normal during the 2nd day. Temperature should not exceed 38°C (100.4°F) after the first 24 hours.
- **Urinary Tract:** Pronounced diuresis occurs on the 2nd or 3rd day. The bladder capacity increases, and it may become overdistended without the desire to pass urine, increasing the risk of urinary stasis and infection.
- **Weight Loss:** In addition to the 5-6 kg lost during delivery, a further loss of about 2 kg occurs during puerperium chiefly due to diuresis.
- **Hematological:** Blood volume decreases. Leukocytosis (up to 25,000/mm³) occurs in response to the stress of labor. A hypercoagulable state persists, with an increase in fibrinogen and platelets in the 1st week increasing the risk for puerperal venous thrombosis.
- **Ovarian Function (Menstruation & Ovulation):** In nonlactating mothers, menstruation returns by 12 weeks in 80% of cases. In exclusively breastfeeding women, lactational amenorrhea occurs because high prolactin levels suppress ovarian response to FSH and inhibit LH release.

**LACTATION** Colostrum (secreted for the first few days) is deep yellow, rich in protein, IgA, IgG, and IgM, and provides immunological defense and a laxative action for the newborn. Prolactin is the major hormone for milk synthesis, while oxytocin causes milk ejection.

**MANAGEMENT OF NORMAL PUERPERIUM**

- **Principles:** (1) Restore the health of the mother, (2) Prevent infection, (3) Take care of the breasts and promote exclusive breastfeeding, (4) Motivate for contraception.
- **Rest and Early Ambulation:** Early ambulation is highly beneficial as it provides a sense of well-being, facilitates normal bladder and bowel voiding, hastens uterine involution, and lessens the risk of puerperal venous thrombosis and embolism.
- **Diet:** A lactating mother requires high calories (+500 kcal/day), adequate protein, fat, plenty of fluids, minerals, and vitamins.
- **Care of Genitalia:** The vulva and episiotomy wound should be dressed with spirit and antiseptic powder after each micturition/defecation. Cold (ice) sitz baths can relieve pain and edema.
- **Ailments:** "After-pains" (spasmodic lower abdominal pain due to uterine contractions expelling clots) are common, especially in multiparae, and are treated with uterine massage and ibuprofen.
- **Contraception:** Exclusive breastfeeding on demand provides 98% contraceptive protection for up to 6 months (Lactational Amenorrhea Method). Nonlactating women should start contraception at 3 weeks postpartum; lactating women can use progestin-only pills or an IUCD.

**COMMON ABNORMALITIES (HIGH YIELD)** While monitoring the puerperium, one must be vigilant for complications:

1. **Puerperal Pyrexia & Sepsis:** Temperature ≥ 38°C (100.4°F) on two separate occasions 24 hours apart (excluding the first 24 hours). Sepsis involves endometritis, endomyometritis, or wound infection.
2. **Subinvolution:** Delayed involution often caused by retained bits, infection, or uterine overdistension.
3. **Breast Complications:** Breast engorgement (days 3-5), cracked nipples, mastitis, or breast abscess.
4. **Psychiatric Disorders:** Postpartum blues, depression, or psychosis.

5. Polyhydramnios
**DEFINITION AND INCIDENCE** Polyhydramnios (or hydramnios) is anatomically defined as a state where the liquor amnii exceeds 2,000 mL. Clinically, it is defined as an excessive accumulation of liquor amnii that causes discomfort to the patient and/or requires imaging to substantiate the clinical diagnosis of the fetal lie and presentation. Sonographically, it is diagnosed when the Amniotic Fluid Index (AFI) is >25 cm (above the 95th centile for gestational age) or the Maximum Vertical Pocket (MVP) is >8 cm. The incidence is 1–2% of pregnancies, and it is more common in multiparae than in primigravidae.

**CLINICAL TYPES** Depending on the rapidity of onset, polyhydramnios is classified into:

1. **Chronic (Most Common):** The onset is insidious, taking a few weeks to develop. It is further graded based on the MVP:
    - Mild: MVP 8–11 cm (79%).
    - Moderate: MVP 12–15 cm (16%).
    - Severe: MVP >16 cm (5%).
2. **Acute (Extremely Rare):** Sudden onset within a few days, typically occurring before 20 weeks. It is primarily associated with monozygotic twins (Twin-Twin Transfusion Syndrome) or chorioangioma of the placenta.

**ETIOLOGY** The exact cause is often speculative but results from either excessive production or deficient absorption of amniotic fluid.

- **Idiopathic (50–60%):** No identifiable cause is found.
- **Fetal Anomalies (20%):**
    - _Anencephaly:_ Accounts for 50% of these cases. The excess fluid is due to transudation from exposed meninges, absence of fetal swallowing, and suppression of fetal antidiuretic hormone (ADH) leading to excessive urination.
    - _Gastrointestinal:_ Esophageal or duodenal atresia (prevents swallowing).
    - _Others:_ Open spina bifida, facial clefts, neck masses, muscular dystrophy, aneuploidy, and genetic syndromes.
    - _Hydrops Fetalis:_ Due to Rh-isoimmunization, cardiothoracic anomalies, fetal cirrhosis, or fetal infections (TORCH, parvovirus B19).
- **Maternal Factors:**
    - _Diabetes Mellitus:_ Seen in 30% of cases. Maternal hyperglycemia leads to fetal hyperglycemia and subsequent fetal osmotic diuresis.
    - _Cardiac or Renal Disease:_ Leads to placental edema and increased transudation.
- **Multiple Pregnancy:** Hydramnios is about 10 times more common in multiple pregnancies, primarily affecting monozygotic twins (often the second sac or the recipient twin in Twin-Twin Transfusion Syndrome).
- **Placental Factors:** Chorioangioma of the placenta increases transudation.

**CLINICAL FEATURES** **Symptoms:** Mainly mechanical due to gross abdominal distension.

- Respiratory distress and dyspnea (patient may need to sit upright to breathe).
- Palpitations.
- Edema of the legs and vulva, varicosities, and hemorrhoids.
- In acute cases, features of an acute abdomen (pain, nausea, vomiting) predominate.

**Signs:**

- _General:_ Dyspnea in the supine position; features of pre-eclampsia (edema, hypertension, proteinuria) may be present.
- _Abdominal Inspection:_ Markedly enlarged, globular abdomen with flank fullness. Skin is tense, shiny, with large striae.
- _Abdominal Palpation:_ Fundal height is greater than the period of amenorrhea. Abdominal girth at the umbilicus is significantly increased. A fluid thrill can be elicited in all directions. Fetal parts are ill-defined, and external ballottement is exaggerated.
- _Auscultation:_ Fetal heart sounds are indistinct or muffled but can be picked up by a Doppler.
- _Internal Examination:_ Cervix is pulled up and may be partially taken up or dilated, revealing tense, bulging membranes.

**INVESTIGATIONS**

- **Ultrasonography (USG):** Essential to confirm the diagnosis (MVP >8 cm or AFI >25 cm). It excludes multiple gestations, assesses fetal lie and presentation, and diagnoses structural anomalies (e.g., central nervous system or gastrointestinal defects).
- **Blood Tests:** ABO and Rh grouping (to rule out isoimmunization causing hydrops), postprandial blood sugar, or Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT) to rule out maternal diabetes.
- **Amniotic Fluid:** Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) estimation, which is markedly elevated in open neural tube defects. Amniocentesis for karyotyping if genetic abnormalities are suspected.

**DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS**

1. **Multiple Pregnancy (Twins):** Often confused due to associated hydramnios. Distinctly reveals multiple fetal parts, absent fluid thrill, and is confirmed by USG.
2. **Pregnancy with a Huge Ovarian Cyst:** The gravid uterus can be palpated separately from the cyst. Internal examination shows the cervix pushed down, whereas in hydramnios, it is drawn up.
3. **Maternal Ascites:** Shifting dullness is present, and the midline is resonant (due to floating gut), whereas it is dull in hydramnios.

**COMPLICATIONS** **Maternal Complications:**

- _During Pregnancy:_ Increased incidence of pre-eclampsia (25%), malpresentations, premature rupture of membranes (PROM), preterm labor, and accidental hemorrhage (abruption) upon sudden escape of liquor.
- _During Labor:_ Early rupture of membranes, cord prolapse, uterine inertia, increased need for operative delivery, retained placenta, and postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) due to severe uterine atony.
- _Puerperium:_ Subinvolution and increased puerperal sepsis.

**Fetal Complications:**

- High perinatal mortality (approx. 50%), primarily due to prematurity and congenital anomalies (40%).
- Other factors include cord prolapse, hydrops fetalis, placental abruption, and birth trauma from operative deliveries.

**MANAGEMENT** The principles are to relieve symptoms, identify the cause, and avoid complications. **1. Mild Polyhydramnios (MVP 8–11 cm):** Common in the mid-trimester and usually transient. Requires no active treatment except extra bed rest.

**2. Severe Polyhydramnios (MVP > 16 cm):** Requires hospitalization as it is a high-risk pregnancy.

- _Supportive Therapy:_ Bed rest with a backrest. Treat associated causes like diabetes or pre-eclampsia. Diuretics are of no value. Indomethacin (COX-2 inhibitor) may be used short-term to reduce fetal urine output in severe cases with preterm labor, but prolonged use risks premature closure of the ductus arteriosus.
- _Management with No Fetal Anomaly (Uncomplicated):_
    - If responsive to treatment: Continue pregnancy and await spontaneous delivery at term.
    - If unresponsive and <37 weeks (with maternal distress): Therapeutic amniocentesis (amnioreduction) is done. Slow decompression (~500 mL/hour) is performed until AFI is <25 cm. It relieves distress and reduces the risk of abruption.
    - If unresponsive and >37 weeks: Induction of labor. Amniocentesis is first done to drain liquor and stabilize the fetal lie, followed by a stabilizing oxytocin infusion and low rupture of membranes.
- _Management with Fetal Anomaly:_
    - Delivery is indicated irrespective of gestational age. Amniocentesis is done to drain liquor, followed by vaginal PGE2 gel and low rupture of membranes to allow controlled, slow leakage of amniotic fluid (preventing abruption).

**3. Management during Labor:**

- Perform an internal examination immediately after membrane rupture to exclude cord prolapse.
- If uterine contractions are sluggish, augment with an oxytocin infusion.
- Strict vigilance during the third stage for PPH. Intravenous methergine 0.2 mg should be administered with the delivery of the anterior shoulder to prevent atonic PPH.

**4. Acute Polyhydramnios:** Usually ends in spontaneous abortion. In cases of severe TTTS, repetitive amnioreduction until AFI normalizes or laser photocoagulation of anastomotic vessels can be performed.


8. Maternal mortality
**DEFINITION** Maternal death is defined as the death of a woman while pregnant or within 42 days of the termination of pregnancy, irrespective of the duration and the site of pregnancy, from any cause related to or aggravated by the pregnancy or its management, but not from accidental or incidental causes.

**MATERNAL MORTALITY INDICES**

- **Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR):** The number of maternal deaths per 100,000 live births. In India, the MMR has significantly declined to 99 per 100,000 live births (2020).
- **Maternal Mortality Rate:** The number of maternal deaths divided by the number of women of reproductive age (15-49 years), expressed per 100,000 women of reproductive age per year.
- **Lifetime Risk:** The probability of a woman dying in her reproductive age (15-49 years) due to causes in pregnancy, childbirth, or within 6 weeks of childbirth. In India, it is currently 0.3%.

**CLASSIFICATION OF CAUSES OF MATERNAL MORTALITY** The causes of maternal death are broadly classified into three categories:

**1. Direct Obstetric Deaths (75%)** These result from obstetric complications of the pregnant state, delivery, or their management.

- **Hemorrhage (20-27%):** Mostly Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH), but also includes Antepartum Hemorrhage (abruptio placentae, placenta previa), retained placenta, ectopic pregnancy, and abortion complications,.
- **Infection/Puerperal Sepsis (11-15%):** Associated with labor and puerperium, often due to premature rupture of membranes, prolonged/obstructed labor, and unclean delivery practices,.
- **Hypertensive Disorders (12-15%):** Severe pre-eclampsia and eclampsia,.
- **Unsafe Abortion (8-13%):** Complications arising from unregulated fertility and unsafe termination procedures,,.
- **Obstructed Labor (8-9%):** Due to cephalopelvic disproportion, abnormal lie, or malpresentation,.
- **Embolism (3%):** Pulmonary or amniotic fluid embolism.

**2. Indirect Obstetric Deaths (25%)** These result from pre-existing diseases or diseases that developed during pregnancy, which were aggravated by the physiological effects of pregnancy and labor.

- **Anemia (15-20%):** The most important single indirect cause in developing countries, mostly due to nutritional deficiencies (iron, folic acid) or infections like malaria and hookworm,,.
- **Viral Hepatitis (5-10%):** Endemic in India, causing death mostly in the third trimester due to hepatic coma and coagulation failure,.
- **Other Medical Disorders:** Cardiac disease, diabetes, and thyroid disease.

**3. Non-Obstetric (Fortuitous) Deaths** Deaths resulting from accidental or incidental causes, such as motor vehicle accidents or infectious diseases like typhoid, not related to pregnancy.

**HIGH-RISK FACTORS & SOCIAL DETERMINANTS**

- **Age and Parity:** The risk is 3-4 times higher in women aged 35 years or above, and 3 times greater in grand multiparae (Para 5 or above) due to higher risks of PPH, malpresentations, and ruptured uterus.
- **Socioeconomic Status:** Mortality ratios are higher in women from low socioeconomic strata, often due to poor nutrition and lack of antenatal care (ANC),.
- **The "Three Delays" Model:** The Government of India identifies three major delays leading to maternal mortality:
    1. **Delay I:** Delay in recognizing the problem and deciding to seek care (lack of birth preparedness or ignorance),.
    2. **Delay II:** Delay in reaching the health facility (due to lack of transport or referral facilities),.
    3. **Delay III:** Delay in receiving treatment at the center (due to unequipped health facilities, lack of blood, medicines, or trained personnel),.

**PREVENTIVE MEASURES AND INTERVENTIONS** Maternal mortality is largely preventable with timely and effective interventions.

- **Antenatal and Intranatal Care:**
    - Routine ANC for risk assessment, which must be a continuous procedure throughout pregnancy,.
    - Mandatory hospital delivery for high-risk patients and ensuring a Skilled Birth Attendant (SBA) is present at every birth,.
    - Careful monitoring of labor using a partograph to detect and refer cases of prolonged or obstructed labor early.
- **Emergency Obstetric Care (EmOC):**
    - Upgrading First Referral Units (FRUs) to provide comprehensive EmOC, including facilities for blood transfusion, cesarean section, and manual removal of placenta,,.
- **Specific Clinical Interventions:**
    - _Hemorrhage:_ Active management of the third stage of labor (using oxytocics) and replacing fluid/blood loss.
    - _Eclampsia:_ Early detection, appropriate referral, and antiseizure prophylaxis/treatment with Magnesium Sulfate (MgSO4).
    - _Sepsis:_ Maintaining the "three cleans" during delivery and prompt use of broad-spectrum antibiotics.
    - _Anemia:_ Routine Iron-Folic Acid (IFA) supplementation and treatment of underlying infections,.
- **Family Planning and Safe Abortion:** Prevention of unwanted pregnancies through confidential family planning services and providing access to safe abortion services with post-abortion care,.
- **Audits and Surveillance:** Establishing Civil Registration and Vital Statistics (CRVS), Confidential Enquiry into Maternal Death (CEMD), and Maternal Deaths Surveillance and Response (MDSR) to evaluate the causes of death and avoidable factors,,,.

9. Methods of 2nd trimester pregnancy termination.
**METHODS OF SECOND TRIMESTER PREGNANCY TERMINATION (13–24 WEEKS)**

Pregnancy termination in the midtrimester is broadly classified into medical and surgical methods. Medical methods are generally safe, highly effective, noninvasive, and have fewer complications compared to surgical methods.

**I. MEDICAL METHODS** Prostaglandins and their analogs are extensively used and are highly effective in the second trimester. PGE analogs (such as misoprostol and dinoprostone) are preferred because they have a more selective action on the myometrium and produce fewer side effects.

- **Mifepristone and Misoprostol (Combination Regimen):**
    - **Dosage:** Mifepristone 200 mg orally, followed 36–48 hours later by Misoprostol 800 µg vaginally. Thereafter, Misoprostol 400 µg is given orally, vaginally, or buccally every 3 hours for up to 4-5 doses.
    - **Efficacy:** The success rate is 97%. Pretreatment with mifepristone significantly reduces the induction-abortion interval, bringing the median interval to 6.5 hours.
- **Misoprostol (PGE1 analog) Alone:**
    - **Dosage:** 600–800 µg vaginally, followed by 400 µg vaginally or buccally every 3–4 hours for up to 5 doses.
    - **Efficacy:** This regimen achieves a 100% success rate in the second trimester, with a mean induction-abortion interval of 11–12 hours.
- **Dinoprostone (PGE2 analog):**
    - **Dosage:** 20 mg vaginal suppository administered every 3–4 hours (maximum of 4–6 doses).
    - **Efficacy:** When used alongside osmotic dilators, the mean induction-to-abortion interval is 17 hours. Drawbacks are that it is expensive and thermolabile (requires refrigeration).
- **High-Dose Concentrated Oxytocin:**
    - **Dosage:** Used as a single agent through escalating sequential infusions. A standard regimen starts with 50 units of oxytocin in 500 mL normal saline infused over 3 hours, followed by 1 hour of diuresis without oxytocin. The dose is then escalated sequentially to 150, 200, 250, and 300 units in 500 mL normal saline.

**II. SURGICAL METHODS** Surgical termination in the midtrimester is technically more difficult and carries a higher complication rate than first-trimester procedures.

- **Dilatation and Evacuation (D&E):**
    - **Prerequisites:** Mandatory cervical preparation is required to prevent trauma; this is achieved using osmotic dilators (tents) or misoprostol 400 µg given vaginally/buccally 3–4 hours prior to surgery. Giving mifepristone 48 hours before misoprostol creates even better cervical dilatation.
    - **Procedure:** Following adequate dilatation, the amniotic sac is punctured, fluid is drawn using an 11 to 16 mm suction cannula, and the fetus is extracted in parts. The remaining placenta and tissue are removed with a large bore cannula. It may be performed under ultrasound guidance and alongside oxytocin infusion. Perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis (e.g., oral doxycycline) is mandatory.
    - **Complications:** Uterine perforation, cervical laceration, severe bleeding, postabortion infection, amniotic fluid embolism, and DIC.
- **Hysterotomy:**
    - **Definition:** Extraction of the products of conception through an abdominal and uterine incision. It is rarely performed in modern practice.
    - **Indications:** Prior failed medical termination, or when D&E cannot be safely performed (e.g., lower segment fibroids, uterine anomalies, repeated scarred uterus with placenta accreta). It is often combined with a sterilization operation.
    - **Complications:** Immediate risks include massive hemorrhage, shock, anesthetic complications, and peritonitis. Remote risks include scar endometriosis, incisional hernia, menstrual abnormalities, and future scar rupture.

10. Cardiovascular changes during pregnancy
**CARDIOVASCULAR CHANGES DURING PREGNANCY**

Profound anatomical, physiological, and hemodynamic changes occur in the cardiovascular system during pregnancy to meet the increased metabolic demands of the mother and the growing fetus.

**1. ANATOMICAL CHANGES**

- **Displacement:** Due to the elevation of the diaphragm by the enlarging gravid uterus, the heart is pushed upward and outward with a slight rotation to the left.
- **Apex Beat:** The apex beat is shifted to the 4th intercostal space, about 2.5 cm outside the midclavicular line.
- **Cardiac Size:** Doppler echocardiography shows an increase in the left ventricular end-diastolic diameters, as well as increased left and right atrial diameters.

**2. HEMODYNAMIC CHANGES**

- **Cardiac Output (CO):**
    - CO starts increasing from the 5th week of pregnancy and reaches its peak (40–50% above nonpregnant levels) at about 30–34 weeks.
    - The nonpregnant CO is roughly 4.5 L/min, which increases to about 6.26 L/min near term.
    - The increase in CO is chiefly due to an increase in stroke volume (increases by 27%, from 65 mL to 75 mL) and an increase in heart rate (increases by 17%, from 70 to 85 beats per minute).
    - CO is highly dependent on maternal posture: it is lowest in the sitting or supine position and highest in the right/left lateral or knee-chest position.
    - During labor, CO increases further (+50%) and peaks immediately following delivery (+70% over prelabor values) due to autotransfusion of blood squeezed from the contracting uterus into the maternal systemic circulation.
- **Blood Pressure (BP):**
    - Systemic Vascular Resistance (SVR) decreases by 21% due to the smooth muscle relaxing effects of progesterone, nitric oxide, prostaglandins, and Atrial Natriuretic Peptide (ANP).
    - Despite the large increase in cardiac output, maternal BP decreases due to the fall in SVR.
    - There is an overall decrease in diastolic BP and Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP) by 5–10 mm Hg in mid-pregnancy, followed by a gradual rise until term.
- **Venous Pressure:**
    - Antecubital venous pressure remains unaffected.
    - Femoral venous pressure is markedly raised in the later months, increasing from 8–10 cm H2O to about 20–25 cm H2O in the supine position, and up to 80–100 cm H2O in the standing position.
    - This rise is due to the mechanical pressure exerted by the gravid uterus on the common iliac veins and inferior vena cava, particularly on the right side due to uterine dextrorotation. This stagnation explains the development of physiological pedal edema, varicose veins, hemorrhoids, and deep vein thrombosis.

**3. SUPINE HYPOTENSION SYNDROME (POSTURAL HYPOTENSION)**

- In late pregnancy, the gravid uterus compresses the inferior vena cava when the patient is in the supine position.
- In most women, collateral circulation (paravertebral and azygos veins) opens up. However, in about 10% of cases, collateral circulation fails to compensate, severely curtailing venous return to the heart.
- This results in profound hypotension, tachycardia, and syncope. Normal BP is quickly restored by turning the patient to the lateral position.

**4. BLOOD VOLUME CHANGES (HYPERVOLEMIA)**

- **Total Blood Volume:** Increases progressively starting at the 6th week, expanding rapidly to a maximum of 40–50% (approx. 1500 mL increase, from 4000 mL to 5500 mL) above nonpregnant levels by 30–34 weeks.
- **Plasma vs. RBC Volume:** Plasma volume increases by 40–50% (1250 mL), while RBC mass increases by 20–30% (350 mL).
- **Hemodilution:** The disproportionate increase in plasma volume relative to RBC mass produces a state of hemodilution, leading to physiological anemia of pregnancy (fall in hematocrit and hemoglobin concentration).
- The diminished blood viscosity ensures optimum gaseous exchange, lowers SVR, and protects the mother against the adverse effects of blood loss during delivery.

**5. CLINICAL AND AUSCULTATORY FINDINGS**

- **Heart Sounds:** Splitting of the first heart sound is common; the second sound remains unchanged, and a loud third heart sound (S3) is frequently auscultated due to rapid diastolic filling.
- **Murmurs:**
    - A systolic ejection murmur is often audible in the apical or pulmonary area (due to decreased blood viscosity and increased blood flow across aortic and pulmonary valves).
    - A continuous hissing murmur ('mammary murmur' or 'mammary souffle') may be heard over the tricuspid area (left 2nd and 3rd intercostal spaces) due to increased flow through the internal mammary vessels.
- **Pulse Rate:** Raised by about 15 beats/minute, often accompanied by extrasystoles.

**6. ELECTROCARDIOGRAM (ECG) CHANGES**

- **Axis:** Left axis deviation due to the anatomical upward shift of the heart.
- **Waves:** Q waves in leads II, III, and aVF, and flat or inverted T-waves in leads III and V1–V3.
- Benign pericardial effusion may also be present.

**7. REGIONAL DISTRIBUTION OF BLOOD FLOW**

- **Uterus:** Uterine blood flow increases significantly from 50 mL/min in the nonpregnant state to about 750 mL/min near term (accounting for about 15% of total CO).
- **Kidneys:** Renal blood flow increases by 400 mL/min by the 16th week and is maintained until term.
- **Lungs:** Pulmonary blood flow increases by 2500 mL/min.
- **Skin & Mucous Membranes:** Blood flow reaches a maximum of 500 mL/min by the 36th week, explaining common complaints like heat sensation, sweating, and nasal stuffiness.

11. Screening for Down syndrome
**SCREENING FOR DOWN SYNDROME (TRISOMY 21)**

Noninvasive screening for chromosomal anomalies, including Down syndrome (Trisomy 21), should be offered as a routine to all pregnant women, irrespective of their age. The primary objective of screening is to identify high-risk pregnancies; women who screen positive must then be offered invasive fetal karyotyping (such as chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis) for definitive confirmation.

**1. FIRST TRIMESTER SCREENING (11–14 WEEKS)** This is considered equal or even superior to second-trimester screening and allows for earlier diagnostic testing if the screen is positive.

- **Biochemical Markers:** The maternal serum is tested for free $\beta$-hCG and Pregnancy-Associated Plasma Protein-A (PAPP-A). In Down syndrome, the level of PAPP-A is reduced and free $\beta$-hCG is increased.
- **Biophysical (Ultrasound) Markers:** Nuchal Translucency (NT) and Nasal Bone (NB) are measured. An increased NT thickness of $\ge$3 mm is an abnormal finding and a strong marker for Trisomy 21. The absence of the nasal bone on ultrasound at 10–12 weeks is also strongly associated with fetal Down syndrome.
- **Combined Test:** Combining the maternal age, NT measurement, PAPP-A, and free $\beta$-hCG achieves a detection rate of 90–92% with a false-positive rate (FPR) of 5%. When the absence of the nasal bone is added to the NT measurement, the detection rate rises to 92% with a lower FPR of 3.5%.

**2. SECOND TRIMESTER SCREENING (15–22 WEEKS)**

- **Quadruple (Quad) Test:** This screening is performed between 15 and 22 weeks of gestation. It evaluates four maternal serum biochemical analytes: Maternal Serum Alpha-Fetoprotein (MSAFP), Unconjugated Estriol (uE3), dimeric inhibin-A, and hCG.
- **Analyte Profile in Trisomy 21:** The levels of hCG and inhibin-A are elevated, whereas MSAFP and uE3 are reduced.
- **Efficacy:** The Quad screen can detect Trisomy 21 in 70–85% of cases with a false-positive rate ranging from 0.9% to 5%. Adjustments must be made for maternal variables such as age, weight, and ethnic group.
- **Ultrasound Soft Markers:** A detailed ultrasound anomaly scan can detect multiple "soft markers" associated with aneuploidy. These include absent nasal bone, choroid plexus cysts, echogenic intracardiac focus, pyelectasis, echogenic bowel, shortened femur or humerus, and an increased nuchal fold.

**3. INTEGRATED SCREENING** The best overall screening procedure recommended is the combination of first and second-trimester screening procedures. When second-trimester quad screening is combined with first-trimester screening, the detection rate for Trisomy 21 increases to 95%.

**4. NONINVASIVE PRENATAL TESTING (NIPT) / CELL-FREE FETAL DNA (cf-DNA)**

- **Principle:** Cell-free fetal DNA (cf-DNA) crosses into the maternal circulation through the placenta and can be detected in maternal plasma and whole blood.
- **Timing and Technique:** The test is generally performed from 10 weeks of pregnancy onwards. It utilizes Massively Parallel DNA Shotgun Sequencing (MPSS) to sequence millions of maternal and fetal DNA fragments.
- **Efficacy:** cf-DNA is the most sensitive and specific screening procedure for common fetal aneuploidies. The detection rate for Trisomy 21 using MPSS is 99–100%, with an exceptionally low false-positive rate of 0.1%.
- **Clinical Application:** Because of its high sensitivity and specificity, second-trimester ultrasound soft markers are not utilized if a patient has had a negative cell-free DNA test. However, cf-DNA remains a screening test, not a diagnostic one; women with a positive result must be referred for genetic counseling and offered invasive prenatal diagnosis (amniocentesis or CVS) for confirmation.

**5. SCREENING IN MULTIPLE PREGNANCIES** In twin pregnancies, the detection rate of Trisomy 21 using the first-trimester combined test is similar to that of singleton pregnancies. However, the false-positive rate in twin pregnancies is twice as high as in singletons.

12. Classical cesarean section
**CLASSICAL CESAREAN SECTION**

**DEFINITION** A classical cesarean section is an operative procedure where the baby is extracted through a vertical incision made in the upper segment (body) of the uterus.

**INDICATIONS** In present-day obstetrics, the indications for a classical cesarean section are very limited. It is usually performed only under forced circumstances or specific technical difficulties. **1. When the lower segment approach is difficult:**

- Dense adhesions limiting access to the lower uterine segment (LUS) due to previous abdominal operations.
- Severe contracted pelvis (osteomalacic or rachitic) with a pendulous abdomen. **2. When the lower segment approach is risky:**
- Presence of a big fibroid on the lower segment (higher blood loss and attempting myomectomy may end in hysterectomy).
- Invasive carcinoma of the cervix (to prevent dissemination of the growth and postoperative sepsis).
- Repair of a high vesicovaginal fistula (VVF).
- Complete anterior placenta previa with highly engorged vessels in the lower segment, where a lower segment incision would cause massive hemorrhage. **3. Other specific maternal or fetal conditions:**
- Transverse lie, particularly with a dorsoinferior position.
- Preterm delivery (e.g., 26–28 weeks) where the lower uterine segment is poorly developed and too narrow to allow safe extraction of the fetus.
- Planned cesarean hysterectomy or cases with Placenta Accreta Spectrum (PAS).
- Perimortem cesarean section: Done as an extreme emergency procedure to extract a live baby within 5 minutes of maternal cardiac arrest in the third trimester.

**OPERATIVE PROCEDURE (STEPS)**

- **Abdominal Incision:** A longitudinal (paramedian) incision about 15 cm (6 inches) in length is made, one-third of which extends above the umbilicus. A transverse (Pfannenstiel) incision is unsuitable for a classical operation.
- **Uterine Incision:** A longitudinal incision of about 12.5 cm (5 inches) is made in the midline of the anterior wall of the uterus, starting from below the fundus.
- **Delivery of the Fetus:** The incision is deepened until the membranes are exposed and punctured. If the placenta is encountered anteriorly (in about 40% of cases), the fingers are slipped between the placenta and the uterine wall to reach the membranes. The baby is commonly delivered by breech extraction.
- **Uterine Suture (Three Layers):**
    1. **First layer:** A continuous suture using No. '0' chromic catgut or vicryl taking the deep myometrium but excluding the decidua.
    2. **Second layer:** Interrupted sutures (1 cm apart) using No. '1' chromic catgut or vicryl taking the entire depth of the superficial muscles down to the first layer.
    3. **Third layer:** A continuous suture taking the serous coat along with adjacent muscles to peritonize the wound.

**DEMERITS AND COMPLICATIONS** While technically easy to perform, the classical cesarean section carries significant morbidity compared to the lower segment cesarean section (LSCS).

- **Intraoperative/Postoperative:** There is a higher risk of hemorrhage and shock. Because perfect peritonization is not possible, the chance of peritonitis is higher if uterine sepsis is present. Furthermore, extraction of the baby without disturbing the placenta is advantageous, but controlling bleeding from the thick upper segment can be problematic.
- **Impaired Wound Healing:** Healing is often imperfect. The thick muscle layer is difficult to properly appose, leading to the formation of pockets that fill with blood and are eventually replaced by fibrous tissue. A "gutter" often forms on the inner uterine surface if the decidua is excluded during suturing. During the puerperium, the wound remains in a state of tension due to active uterine contraction and retraction, causing sutures to loosen.
- **Risk of Scar Rupture:** The resulting scar is exceptionally weak. The physiological stretch of the growing uterus acts at right angles to the classical scar. The risk of rupture in subsequent pregnancies is very high (4–9%), which is 5 to 10 times higher than that of a lower segment scar.
- **Timing of Rupture:** Unlike an LSCS scar which predominantly ruptures during active labor, a classical scar is highly vulnerable to unpredictable, spontaneous rupture during the _late months of pregnancy_ as well as during labor.
- **Prognosis of Rupture:** Rupture of a classical scar is catastrophic. The maternal mortality ranges from 5–30%, and the perinatal mortality is extremely high (50–75%, or 6 in 8 fetuses) due to the expulsion of the fetus into the abdominal cavity. Additionally, in future pregnancies, the placenta is more likely to implant over the classical scar, risking trophoblastic penetration or amniotic sac herniation through the inner gutter.

Because of these profound risks, women with a previous classical cesarean section are absolutely contraindicated for a Trial of Labor After Cesarean (TOLAC) and must be delivered by elective repeat cesarean section at 36 to 38 weeks of gestation.


13. Discordant twins
**DISCORDANT TWINS / SELECTIVE GROWTH RESTRICTION (sGR)**

**DEFINITION** While some degree of discordant growth is considered normal in dizygotic twins, true pathological discordance is defined when the estimated fetal weight (EFW) difference is 25% or more between the fetuses. In the context of selective growth restriction (sGR), it is defined as an EFW difference of >20% between the fetuses.

**INCIDENCE**

- Discordant twin growth occurs in approximately 20% of twin pregnancies.
- It is seen in over 50% of monochorionic twins that are complicated by Twin-Twin Transfusion Syndrome (TTTS).

**ETIOLOGY (CAUSES)** Pathological discordance in twins can arise due to several factors:

1. Twin-Twin Transfusion Syndrome (TTTS).
2. Placental insufficiency affecting one fetus.
3. Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR).
4. Structural anomalies or chromosomal defects occurring in only one fetus.

**STAGING OF SELECTIVE GROWTH RESTRICTION (sGR)** In cases presenting with sGR, staging is based on the severity of the condition and Doppler ultrasound findings of the umbilical artery:

- **Stage I:** Growth discordance is present, but there are positive diastolic velocities in both fetal umbilical arteries.
- **Stage II:** Growth discordance is accompanied by Absent or Reversed End-Diastolic Velocities (AREDV) in one or both fetuses.
- **Stage III:** Growth discordance is present with cyclical umbilical artery diastolic waveforms over several minutes (known as intermittent AREDV or iAREDV).

**COMPLICATIONS**

- Intrauterine fetal death (IUFD) of one fetus.
- Complications related to TTTS (if present), such as polyhydramnios in the recipient and oligohydramnios in the donor.
- Twin Anemia-Polycythemia Sequence (TAPS), which causes chronic and severe hemoglobin discordance.
- Increased perinatal morbidity and mortality associated with preterm birth and low birth weight.

**MANAGEMENT**

- **Fetal Surveillance:** High-risk multifetal pregnancies (especially monochorionic twins) require serial sonography at 2-week intervals to monitor the fetal growth profile, anatomical survey, and Doppler velocimetry to detect discordance or TTTS early.
- **Timing of Delivery:**
    - _Stage I sGR:_ Preferably delivered between 34 and 36 weeks of gestation.
    - _Stage II and Stage III sGR:_ Delivery is indicated earlier, by 32 weeks of gestation.
- **Corticosteroid Therapy:** Antenatal corticosteroids must be administered prior to planned preterm delivery to accelerate fetal lung maturation.
- **Selective Termination / Reduction:** In cases with severe discordant growth, structural abnormalities, or genetic anomalies in one fetus, selective termination can be considered. This may involve intracardiac potassium chloride injection (in multichorionic twins) or cord occlusion/ablation techniques like Radiofrequency Ablation (RFA) or laser coagulation (in monochorionic twins).


14. Breast abscess
**BREAST ABSCESS**

**INTRODUCTION AND ETIOLOGY** A breast abscess is a severe complication of the puerperium, typically developing as a consequence of acute mastitis. The variable destruction of breast parenchymal tissues during the infective process leads to the formation of an abscess cavity.

**CLINICAL FEATURES** The diagnosis is primarily clinical, characterized by the following classic signs:

- Flushed breasts that do not respond promptly to antibiotic therapy.
- Brawny edema of the overlying skin.
- Marked local tenderness accompanied by the presence of **fluctuation**.
- A swinging temperature (high spiking fever).

**MANAGEMENT PROTOCOL**

**1. Surgical Management (Incision and Drainage)**

- **Anesthesia:** The procedure must be performed under general anesthesia.
- **Incision:** A **deep radial incision** is made, extending from near the areolar margin. This specific direction is crucial to prevent injury to the lactiferous ducts.
    - _Clinical Pearl:_ An incision made perpendicular to the lactiferous ducts significantly increases the risk of ductal occlusion and fistula formation.
- **Exploration:** Finger exploration of the cavity is mandatory to break up all the walls of the loculi (septations) within the abscess.
- **Packing:** The abscess cavity is loosely packed with gauze. This pack should be replaced after 24 hours with a smaller pack, and the procedure is repeated until the cavity heals by secondary intention.

**2. Alternative Drainage**

- In selected cases, the abscess can alternatively be managed by serial percutaneous needle aspiration performed under ultrasound guidance.

**3. Medical Management**

- Antibiotic therapy must be continued and tailored depending upon the culture and sensitivity report of the drained pus.

**4. Lactation Management** An acute breast complication such as a breast abscess is considered a temporary maternal contraindication to breastfeeding from the affected side. The management of lactation involves:

- **Uninvolved Breast:** Breastfeeding should be continued uninterrupted from the healthy, uninvolved side.
- **Involved Breast:** The infected breast must be mechanically pumped every 2 hours and with every let-down reflex to prevent engorgement and maintain milk production.
- **Resumption:** Once the cellulitis has completely resolved, normal breastfeeding from the involved side may be safely resumed.

**PROGNOSIS** With proper drainage and antibiotic coverage, healing is usually prompt, though the recurrence risk for a breast abscess is approximately 10%.


15. Folic acid supplementation in pregnancy
**FOLIC ACID SUPPLEMENTATION IN PREGNANCY**

**INTRODUCTION & PHYSIOLOGICAL ROLE** Folic acid is a water-soluble vitamin essential for the synthesis of DNA and nucleoproteins, particularly in erythropoietic cells. Folate stores are located in the liver and are usually sufficient for about 6 weeks. Vitamin C is essential for the conversion of folic acid to its active form, folinic acid.

**DAILY REQUIREMENT & DIETARY SOURCES**

- **Requirement:** The daily requirement of folic acid in a nonpregnant woman is 50–100 µg/day, which increases to 400–600 µg/day during pregnancy to meet the demands of the growing fetus, maternal tissue, and increased red cell volume.
- **Dietary Sources:** Green leafy vegetables, spinach, cauliflower, liver, and kidney. _Note:_ Excessive cooking destroys a significant amount of folate in food.

**CAUSES OF FOLIC ACID DEFICIENCY IN PREGNANCY** Megaloblastic anemia in pregnancy is almost always due to folic acid deficiency. Causes include:

- **Inadequate Intake:** Due to nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite, or dietary insufficiency.
- **Increased Demand:** Growing fetus, increased maternal tissue, and multiple pregnancy (increases demand eight-fold).
- **Diminished Absorption:** Intestinal malabsorption syndromes.
- **Abnormal Demand:** Hemolytic states (e.g., sickle cell anemia, chronic malaria, thalassemia) and hemorrhagic states (peptic ulcer, piles) lead to increased erythropoiesis and exhaustion of folate supplies.
- **Failure of Utilization:** Use of anticonvulsant drugs in epilepsy or the presence of infection.
- **Iron Deficiency Anemia Treatment:** Correcting anemia with simple iron therapy alone can stimulate a hyperplastic bone marrow, increasing the need for folic acid and unmasking an underlying folate deficiency.

**COMPLICATIONS OF FOLIC ACID DEFICIENCY**

- **Maternal:** Megaloblastic anemia, miscarriage, abruptio placentae, and prematurity.
- **Fetal:** Neural Tube Defects (NTDs) like anencephaly and spina bifida, cleft lip, cleft palate, and dysmaturity. Preconceptional folate deficiency is strongly linked to the development of NTDs.

**PROPHYLACTIC SUPPLEMENTATION GUIDELINES** Prophylactic supplementation is a cornerstone of preconceptional and antenatal care to prevent neural tube defects and maternal anemia.

**1. Routine Prophylaxis (Low-Risk Women):**

- **Dose:** 400 µg (0.4 mg) daily.
- **Timing:** Should ideally begin 4 weeks prior to conception and continue up to 12 weeks of pregnancy (or at least 3 months thereafter).

**2. High-Risk Prophylaxis:**

- **Dose:** 5 mg daily.
- **Timing:** Started 1 month before conception up to 12 weeks of pregnancy (or continued throughout pregnancy depending on the indication).
- **Indications for 5 mg dose:**
    - Previous infant born with a Neural Tube Defect (decreases recurrence risk of 4% by 85%).
    - Women on anticonvulsant therapy (e.g., epilepsy).
    - Hemoglobinopathies (e.g., Sickle cell disease, Thalassemia).
    - Multiple pregnancy.
    - Family history of congenital heart disease or neural tube defects.
    - Obese women with BMI > 30 or > 35 kg/m².

**THERAPEUTIC SUPPLEMENTATION (For Megaloblastic/Dimorphic Anemia)**

- **Dose:** Specific therapy includes oral administration of 5 mg folic acid daily.
- **Duration:** Continued throughout pregnancy and for at least 4 weeks following delivery.
- **Adjuncts:** Folic acid should _never_ be given without supplemental iron. Ascorbic acid (Vitamin C) 100 mg tablet thrice daily is added to enhance the action of folic acid by converting it to folinic acid.
- **Clinical Response:** Improvement is seen within 7-10 days, evidenced by a sense of well-being, increased appetite, reticulocytosis, and a rise in hemoglobin levels.


16. Inevitable abortion

**INEVITABLE MISCARRIAGE (ABORTION)**

**Definition** It is a clinical type of miscarriage where the process has progressed to a state from which recovery is impossible. The presence of vaginal bleeding, cervical dilatation, and abdominal pain indicates the impending expulsion of the products of conception.

**Clinical Features** The patient initially presents with features of a threatened miscarriage, which progressively worsen to develop the following manifestations:

- **Vaginal Bleeding:** Increased and often heavy vaginal bleeding.
- **Abdominal Pain:** Aggravation of pain in the lower abdomen, which typically becomes colicky in nature.
- **Uterine Size:** The size of the uterus is the same as or smaller than the expected period of amenorrhea.
- **Internal (Pelvic) Examination:** Reveals a dilated and open internal cervical os through which the products of conception can be palpated.

**Investigations / Ultrasonography**

- Ultrasound may reveal retroplacental hemorrhage, and the fetus is often found to be dead.

**Management** The primary aims of management are to accelerate the process of expulsion and to maintain strict asepsis.

**1. General Measures and Resuscitation**

- Excessive bleeding must be promptly controlled by administering Injection Methergine 0.2 mg or Oxytocin 10 IU IM (provided the cervix is dilated and the uterine size is less than 12 weeks).
- Blood loss should be vigorously corrected using Intravenous (IV) fluid therapy and blood transfusions.
- Resuscitation of the patient is prioritized, followed by uterine evacuation.

**2. Active Treatment (Before 12 weeks of gestation)**

- **Dilatation and Evacuation (D&E):** The primary treatment is D&E followed by curettage of the uterine cavity using a blunt curette under analgesia or general anesthesia.
- **Suction Evacuation:** Alternatively, suction evacuation followed by curettage can be safely performed.

**3. Active Treatment (After 12 weeks of gestation)**

- **Oxytocin Infusion:** Uterine contractions are accelerated by setting up an oxytocin drip (10 units of oxytocin in 500 mL of normal saline) running at 40–60 drops per minute.
- **Management of the Placenta:**
    - If the fetus is expelled but the placenta is retained and lying separated, it is removed using ovum forceps.
    - If the placenta remains unseparated, digital separation followed by its manual evacuation under general anesthesia is required.

17. Complications of preeclampsia
**COMPLICATIONS OF PRE-ECLAMPSIA**

The complications of pre-eclampsia are more likely to occur if the condition is left untreated. The complications are broadly categorized into immediate (affecting both the mother and the fetus) and remote (long-term health outcomes for the mother).

**I. IMMEDIATE MATERNAL COMPLICATIONS** These can occur during pregnancy, labor, or the puerperium.

- **During Pregnancy:**
    
    - _Neurological:_ Eclampsia occurs in about 2% of cases and is more common in acute fulminating types. Other severe cerebral complications include cerebral hemorrhage, cerebral edema, stroke, and Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome (PRES).
    - _Hematological:_ Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) occurs in about 15% of cases. HELLP syndrome (Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, and Low Platelet count) is a severe complication affecting 10–15% of cases.
    - _Renal:_ Oliguria, anuria, and acute renal failure.
    - _Hepatic:_ Subcapsular liver hematoma, hepatic infarction, and periportal necrosis.
    - _Respiratory/Cardiovascular:_ Pulmonary edema (8%), Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS), pleural effusions, severe ascites, and laryngeal edema.
    - _Ophthalmic:_ Visual disturbances such as dimness of vision, retinal detachment, and complete blindness.
    - _Obstetric:_ Abruptio placentae (accidental hemorrhage) and preterm labor.
    - _Systemic:_ Sepsis and maternal death (about 1%).
- **During Labor:**
    
    - Eclampsia.
    - Postpartum hemorrhage, which is often related to associated coagulation failure.
- **During Puerperium:**
    
    - Eclampsia, which usually occurs within the first 48 hours postpartum.
    - Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) and renal failure.
    - Shock, presenting as puerperal vasomotor collapse.
    - Puerperal sepsis.
- **Maternal Mortality:**
    
    - Increased maternal deaths are primarily related to eclampsia, accidental hemorrhage (abruptio placentae), acute renal failure, pulmonary edema, DIC, and HELLP syndrome.

**II. IMMEDIATE FETAL AND NEONATAL COMPLICATIONS** Fetal risks are directly related to the severity of pre-eclampsia, the duration of the pregnancy, and the degree of hypertension.

- **Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR):** Occurs due to chronic placental insufficiency.
- **Prematurity:** Results from either spontaneous preterm onset of labor or indicated (iatrogenic) preterm delivery.
- **Intrauterine Fetal Death (IUFD):** Can occur suddenly due to severe hypoxia or placental abruption.
- **Neonatal Morbidities:** High rates of birth asphyxia, Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS), neonatal sepsis, and an increased need for admission to the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU).
- **Perinatal Mortality:** The perinatal mortality rate remains significantly high at 7–15%, with prematurity and low birth weight being the primary causes.

**III. REMOTE (LONG-TERM) MATERNAL COMPLICATIONS** Women who experience severe pre-eclampsia are at an increased risk of several long-term health issues later in life.

- **Future Pregnancy Outcomes:** There is a 25% risk of recurrent pre-eclampsia in subsequent pregnancies. Other future pregnancy risks include placental abruption, fetal growth restriction (FGR), spontaneous or indicated preterm birth, and a higher risk of recurrent HELLP syndrome.
- **Cardiovascular Disease:** Increased long-term risk of chronic hypertension, cardiomyopathy, ischemic heart disease, and coronary artery disease.
- **Renal Disease:** Increased risk of persistent glomerular dysfunction and chronic renal disease.
- **Metabolic Disorders:** Higher incidence of developing metabolic syndrome, Type-2 diabetes mellitus, and obesity.
- **Other Risks:** Long-term ophthalmic complications such as retinal detachment.

18. Shoulder dystocia

**SHOULDER DYSTOCIA**

**DEFINITION** Shoulder dystocia is defined as a condition requiring a wide range of additional obstetric maneuvers to deliver the shoulder of the fetus after the head has been born and gentle traction has failed. It occurs when either the anterior or the posterior (rarely) fetal shoulder impacts on the maternal symphysis pubis or on the sacral promontory, respectively.

- **Incidence:** It occurs in about 0.2% to 1% of deliveries.

**RISK FACTORS** Risk factors are not always present, but can be broadly classified into prelabor and intrapartum factors.

- **Prelabor (Antenatal) Factors:**
    - History of previous shoulder dystocia (increases the risk of recurrence by 10 times).
    - Fetal macrosomia (estimated birth weight > 4.5 kg).
    - Maternal diabetes mellitus.
    - Maternal obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m²).
    - Multiparity and postmaturity.
- **Intrapartum Factors:**
    - Prolonged first or second stage of labor.
    - Secondary arrest of labor.
    - Induction of labor or oxytocin augmentation.
    - Mid-pelvic instrumental delivery (more common following ventouse extraction than forceps).
    - Fetal ascites or anencephaly.

**PREDICTION AND PREVENTION** Shoulder dystocia cannot be accurately predicted or entirely prevented, even with antenatal ultrasonographic assessment of fetal weight.

- Prolonged active labor, secondary arrest, and difficult mid-pelvic deliveries are important intrapartum predictors.
- **Prophylactic measures:** Elective cesarean delivery may be considered to prevent shoulder dystocia in highly selected cases, such as diabetic women with a macrosomic fetus, or women with a previous history of shoulder dystocia.

**DIAGNOSIS (CLINICAL SIGNS)** The diagnosis is made immediately following the delivery of the fetal head when the following signs are observed:

1. **Turtle Neck Sign:** Definite recoil of the fetal head back against the maternal perineum.
2. Failure of spontaneous restitution of the fetal head.
3. The fetal face becomes markedly plethoric.
4. Failure of the shoulders to descend despite routine traction.

**COMPLICATIONS** **1. Fetal/Neonatal (High Perinatal Morbidity and Mortality):**

- Birth asphyxia.
- Brachial plexus injury (plexopathy) due to excessive stretching of the neck, leading to Erb's palsy (C5, C6) or Klumpke's palsy (C8, T1).
- Fracture of the clavicle or humerus.
- Sternomastoid hematoma.

**2. Maternal:**

- Postpartum hemorrhage (11%).
- Severe perineal tears (3rd and 4th degree), vaginal tears, and cervical lacerations.
- Rupture of the uterus or bladder.
- Sacroiliac joint dislocation.

---

**MANAGEMENT**

**I. General Principles (Do's and Don'ts)**

- **Call for extra help:** Involve a senior obstetrician, an anesthetist (for analgesia), and a neonatologist (for infant resuscitation).
- Clear the infant's mouth and nose.
- **Perform a wide mediolateral episiotomy:** This does not release the bony impaction but provides essential space posteriorly for the operator's hands to perform internal maneuvers.
- **NEVER apply fundal pressure:** This is strictly contraindicated as it causes further impaction of the anterior shoulder against the symphysis pubis and increases the risk of uterine rupture.
- **Do NOT give strong traction over the baby's head or neck:** This increases the risk of brachial plexus injuries.

**II. Specific Obstetric Maneuvers** There is no strict evidence that one method is superior, but a stepwise approach is typically employed:

1. **Suprapubic Pressure (Rubin I Maneuver):**
    
    - The head and neck of the fetus are grasped and taken posteriorly.
    - An assistant applies firm suprapubic pressure slightly toward the side of the fetal chest.
    - This reduces the bisacromial diameter and rotates the anterior shoulder into the oblique diameter of the pelvis, allowing it to slip under the symphysis.
2. **McRoberts Maneuver:**
    
    - The maternal thighs are sharply abducted and hyperflexed onto her abdomen.
    - This rotates the symphysis pubis upward, straightens the lumbosacral angle, and increases the anteroposterior diameter of the pelvic inlet.
    - It is highly effective and successful in about 90% of cases, often used simultaneously with suprapubic pressure.
3. **Wood's Corkscrew Maneuver:**
    
    - Usually requires general anesthesia.
    - The operator inserts two fingers into the posterior vagina and applies pressure to the anterior aspect of the posterior shoulder.
    - The posterior shoulder is rotated 180 degrees to an anterior position via a corkscrew movement, helping the bisacromial diameter enter the pelvic inlet.
4. **Extraction of the Posterior Arm:**
    
    - The operator's hand is introduced into the vagina along the fetal posterior humerus in the sacral hollow.
    - The arm is swept across the fetal chest and delivered by gentle traction.
    - _Risk:_ This procedure frequently causes deliberate or accidental fracture of the clavicle or humerus.
5. **"All Fours" Position (Gaskin Maneuver):**
    
    - Changing the mother onto all fours (hands and knees) increases pelvic dimensions and allows the fetal position to shift.
    - Downward traction on the posterior shoulder then helps free the impacted shoulder.
6. **Last Resort Techniques (if all above fail):**
    
    - **Cleidotomy (Fracture of the clavicle):** Deliberate fracture of one or both clavicles by finger pressure or embryotomy scissors to reduce the shoulder girth. Usually applicable for a dead fetus or a living anencephalic baby.
    - **Zavanelli Maneuver:** Pushing the fetal head back into the uterine cavity followed by an emergency cesarean delivery.
    - **Symphysiotomy:** Dividing the symphysis pubis cartilage to physically widen the pelvic diameter (rarely performed).

**III. Post-Delivery Care**

- Active Management of the Third Stage of Labor (AMTSL) must be done as a routine to prevent PPH.
- Careful examination of the genital tract for lacerations.
- Meticulous documentation of all maneuvers employed and the timing of events to avoid litigation.

19. Causes of anemia during pregnancy
**CAUSES OF ANEMIA IN PREGNANCY**

Anemia during pregnancy is broadly classified into physiological and pathological causes. For practical and management purposes, it is classified as follows:

**I. PHYSIOLOGICAL ANEMIA OF PREGNANCY** This occurs due to a disproportionate increase in plasma volume (40–50%) relative to the red blood cell (RBC) mass (20–30%), producing a state of hemodilution. Along with a negative iron balance, this leads to an apparent fall in hemoglobin concentration during the second half of pregnancy.

**II. PATHOLOGICAL ANEMIA**

**1. Deficiency Anemias (Nutritional)**

- **Iron Deficiency Anemia:** Accounts for 95% of deficiency anemias and is the most common cause (about 75% of all anemias in pregnancy). In tropical and low-resource settings, the high prevalence is due to several factors:
    - _Pre-pregnancy factors:_ Faulty dietetic habits (high phosphate and phytic acid reducing iron absorption), faulty absorption mechanisms (intestinal infestation, hypochlorhydria), and chronic iron loss (heavy menstrual bleeding, hookworm infestation, malaria, dysentery, bleeding piles). Repeated pregnancies at short intervals along with prolonged lactation severely deplete iron stores.
    - _During pregnancy:_ Markedly increased demand for iron that cannot be met by diet alone, diminished intake (due to nausea, vomiting, or an iron-poor vegetarian diet), impaired absorption (due to antacids, H2 blockers, _H. pylori_), and associated infections (like asymptomatic bacteriuria) which impair erythropoiesis.
    - _Excess demand:_ Multiple pregnancy and rapidly recurring pregnancies.
- **Folic Acid Deficiency:** The primary cause of megaloblastic anemia in pregnancy. Causes include:
    - Inadequate dietary intake (e.g., overcooking vegetables) or nausea/vomiting.
    - Increased demand by the growing fetus, maternal tissues, or multiple pregnancy.
    - Diminished absorption (intestinal malabsorption syndromes).
    - Abnormal demand due to chronic hemolytic states (malaria, sickle cell anemia) or infections.
    - Failure of utilization (e.g., due to anticonvulsant drugs).
- **Vitamin B12 Deficiency:** Less common, as stores last for years. Causes include a strict vegetarian diet, gastritis, bariatric surgery, gastrectomy, Crohn's disease, Addisonian pernicious anemia, and the use of certain drugs (metformin, proton pump inhibitors, oral contraceptives).
- **Protein Deficiency:** Lack of adequate amino acids required for globin synthesis.

**2. Hemorrhagic Anemia**

- **Acute Blood Loss:** Bleeding in early pregnancy (miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, hydatidiform mole) or late pregnancy (antepartum hemorrhage like placenta previa or abruptio placentae).
- **Chronic Blood Loss:** Hookworm infestation, bleeding piles, or bleeding peptic ulcers.

**3. Hereditary / Hemoglobinopathies**

- **Thalassemia Syndromes:** Inherited defects in globin chain synthesis, primarily alpha or beta-thalassemia (major and minor).
- **Sickle Cell Hemoglobinopathies:** Homozygous sickle cell disease (Hb-SS) or heterozygous trait (Hb-AS) causing hemolysis.
- **Hereditary Hemolytic Anemias:** RBC membrane defects such as hereditary spherocytosis or elliptocytosis.

**4. Hemolytic Anemias (Acquired)**

- Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE).
- HELLP syndrome or severe pre-eclampsia.
- Autoimmune hemolysis.
- Drug-induced hemolysis (e.g., sulfonamides, nitrofurantoin, antimalarials) or associated Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency.
- Infections causing hemolysis (e.g., Malaria, _Clostridium welchii_).

**5. Bone Marrow Insufficiency (Hypoplasia/Aplasia)**

- Aplastic anemia resulting from bone marrow depression due to radiation, toxic drugs (aspirin, indomethacin, alkylating agents), or immunologically mediated damage.

**6. Anemia of Chronic Disease and Infection**

- Chronic systemic infections such as malaria, tuberculosis, or kala-azar.
- Chronic renal disease (causing erythropoietin deficiency) or systemic neoplasms.
- Hematologic malignancies like leukemias and lymphomas.


20. Follow up of a molar pregnancy
**FOLLOW-UP OF A MOLAR PREGNANCY**

Routine follow-up is absolutely mandatory following the evacuation of a molar pregnancy. The prime objective is to facilitate early diagnosis of persistent trophoblastic disease or choriocarcinoma, which develops in 15–20% of complete moles and predominantly occurs within the first 6 months.

**1. Duration and Schedule of Follow-Up**

- **Complete Mole:** Follow-up must be continued for at least 6 months. Initially, clinical checkups and serum $\beta$-hCG estimations are done at weekly intervals until the hCG level becomes negative. This normalization typically occurs within 4 to 8 weeks. Once negative within 56 days, the patient is evaluated at monthly intervals for the next 6 months.
- **Partial Mole:** The malignant potential is much lower; hence, follow-up is continued for 3 to 6 months after the hCG level returns to normal.

**2. Clinical Protocol at Each Visit**

- **History:** The patient must be specifically asked about symptoms such as irregular vaginal bleeding, continuing amenorrhea, persistent cough, breathlessness, or hemoptysis, which could indicate metastasis or persistent disease.
- **Abdominovaginal Examination:** Performed to assess the involution of the uterus, the regression of theca lutein cysts (which usually regress within 2 months), and to look for any malignant deposits in the anterior vaginal wall.
- **Imaging:** A pelvic examination or USG evaluation is typically done one week after molar evacuation. If the pre-evacuation chest radiograph was normal, it only needs to be repeated if the serum hCG titer plateaus or rises.
- **Biochemical Monitoring:** Serum $\beta$-hCG assay must be carried out at every visit, ensuring the test includes assays for all isoforms of hCG.

**3. Contraception Advice**

- The patient must strictly avoid becoming pregnant during the entire follow-up period.
- **Intrauterine Devices (IUD):** Strictly contraindicated because they can cause irregular vaginal bleeding (which confuses the diagnosis of choriocarcinoma) and carry a high risk of uterine perforation.
- **Oral Contraceptive Pills (COCs):** Can be safely prescribed only after the serum $\beta$-hCG values have become negative.
- **Other Methods:** Barrier methods, injectable DMPA, or progestin implants are safe and effective alternatives. Surgical sterilization may be considered if the patient has completed her family.

**4. Warning Signs of Persistent Gestational Trophoblastic Neoplasia (GTN)** The following unfavorable manifestations indicate a progression to persistent GTN and the potential need for chemotherapy:

- hCG titers that plateau, remain elevated, or re-elevate after an initial negative report.
- Persistent irregular vaginal bleeding, subinvolution, or persistently soft, enlarged uterus.
- Appearance of secondary metastases in the vagina.
- Respiratory symptoms or chest radiograph revealing a positive 'cannon ball' shadow.
- Persistent theca lutein cysts.

**5. Advice on Future Pregnancies**

- Once serum $\beta$-hCG remains undetectable for 6 months following the initial negative result, routine monitoring is discontinued and the patient is allowed to conceive.
- Pregnancy must be delayed for at least 1 year if Gestational Trophoblastic Neoplasia (GTN) developed, and up to 2 years if metastasis was present.
- In any subsequent pregnancy following a molar one, hCG levels should be routinely checked 6 weeks after the end of that pregnancy to ensure no recurrence or persistent disease.

21. Enumerate the steps of mechanism of labour.

**DEFINITION** The mechanism of labour encompasses the series of movements that occur on the fetal head in the process of adaptation during its journey through the pelvis.

**PRE-REQUISITES AND ENGAGING DIAMETERS** In normal labour, the head enters the brim most commonly through the available transverse diameter (70%) or the oblique diameter (left occipitoanterior is slightly more common).

- **Engaging Anteroposterior Diameter:** Suboccipitobregmatic (9.5 cm) or suboccipitofrontal in slight deflexion (10 cm).
- **Engaging Transverse Diameter:** Biparietal (9.5 cm).

**STEPS OF MECHANISM OF LABOUR** The principal movements in an occipitolateral position (the most common) occur in the following sequence, though some may occur simultaneously:

**1. Engagement** The head enters the pelvic brim. Due to lateral inclination of the fetal head, the sagittal suture often does not strictly correspond with the available transverse diameter of the inlet. Instead, it is deflected either anteriorly toward the symphysis pubis or posteriorly toward the sacral promontory, a condition known as asynclitism.

**2. Descent** Descent is a continuous process that is slow in the first stage of labour but becomes pronounced in the second stage, completing with the expulsion of the fetus. The factors facilitating descent include:

- Uterine contraction and retraction.
- Maternal bearing-down efforts.
- Straightening of the fetal ovoid after the rupture of membranes.

**3. Flexion** While some degree of flexion is present at the beginning of labour, complete flexion is achieved as the head encounters resistance during descent. This mechanism is explained by the lever principle, where the occipitoallantoid joint acts as the fulcrum. Flexion is crucial as it reduces the engaging diameter of the head from the longer suboccipitofrontal (10 cm) to the shorter suboccipitobregmatic (9.5 cm).

**4. Internal Rotation** This is a movement of great importance without which further descent cannot occur. The well-flexed head stretches the levator ani muscles, and their elastic recoil brings the occiput forward towards the midline (Hart's rule). The forward inclination of the pelvic side walls also aids in this process. In an occipitolateral position, the occiput rotates anteriorly by two-eighths of a circle, while the shoulders simultaneously rotate one-eighth of a circle.

**5. Crowning** After internal rotation, the head descends further until the subocciput lies underneath the pubic arch. The maximum diameter of the head (biparietal diameter) stretches the vulval outlet without any recession of the head even after the uterine contraction passes off. This phenomenon is called crowning.

**6. Extension** Delivery of the head takes place by extension through the 'couple of force' theory. The downward driving force of the uterus neutralizes with the upward and forward resistance of the pelvic floor, resulting in a forward thrust that facilitates extension. The successive parts of the fetal head to be born through the stretched vulval outlet are the vertex, brow, and face. Immediately following the release of the chin, the head drops down, bringing the chin close to the maternal anal opening.

**7. Restitution** Restitution is the visible passive movement of the head caused by the untwisting of the fetal neck. The head rotates through one-eighth of a circle in the opposite direction of internal rotation, turning to the side it originally directed at the time of engagement.

**8. External Rotation** This is the movement of the head that occurs simultaneously with the internal rotation of the shoulders. The head rotates a further one-eighth of a circle in the same direction as restitution. This aligns the shoulders in the anteroposterior diameter of the pelvis.

**9. Birth of Shoulders and Trunk (Expulsion)** After the shoulders are positioned in the anteroposterior diameter of the outlet, further descent allows the anterior shoulder to escape below the symphysis pubis first. By a movement of lateral flexion of the fetal spine, the posterior shoulder sweeps over the perineum. The rest of the trunk is then swiftly expelled by lateral flexion.



22. Complications of placental abruption

**COMPLICATIONS OF PLACENTAL ABRUPTION (ABRUPTIO PLACENTAE)**

The complications of abruptio placentae depend on the clinical type (revealed, concealed, or mixed) and the degree of placental separation. The concealed variety is associated with far more devastating outcomes for both the mother and the fetus compared to the revealed variety.

**I. MATERNAL COMPLICATIONS**

**A. Immediate Complications**

- **Hemorrhage and Shock:**
    - _Revealed type:_ Maternal risk is proportionate to the visible blood loss, and maternal death is rare.
    - _Concealed type:_ Shock is often profound and out of proportion to the visible blood loss. Hemorrhage remains concealed inside the uterus (retroplacental hematoma) or may extend into the intraperitoneal space or broad ligament. Hypovolemia requires urgent correction via blood transfusion.
- **Blood Coagulation Disorders (DIC):** Consumptive coagulopathy (Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation) is a hallmark of severe abruption. The massive retroplacental clot consumes fibrinogen and other procoagulants. Additionally, thromboplastin liberated from the damaged decidua and uterine muscle enters the maternal circulation, triggering widespread intravascular coagulation.
- **Acute Kidney Injury (Renal Failure):** A life-threatening complication characterized by oliguria or anuria. The pathogenesis involves:
    - Hypovolemia and shock leading to decreased renal perfusion.
    - Intrarenal vasospasm caused by serotonin liberated from damaged uterine musculature.
    - Acute tubular necrosis and, in severe cases, bilateral acute cortical necrosis.
- **Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH):** High risk of PPH due to uterine atony (from myometrial damage and overdistension by clots) and blood coagulopathy. Elevated Fibrin Degradation Products (FDPs) resulting from secondary fibrinolysis also inhibit proper myometrial contraction.
- **Couvelaire Uterus (Uteroplacental Apoplexy):** Massive intravasation of blood into the uterine musculature up to the serous coat, causing the uterus to appear dark port-wine in color and potentially impairing its contractility.
- **Amniotic Fluid Embolism:** The risk is significantly increased in severe abruption.

**B. Remote (Late) Complications**

- **Puerperal Sepsis:** The presence of devitalized tissues, blood clots, and a debilitated maternal state strongly favors infection.
- **Ischemic Pituitary Necrosis (Sheehan's Syndrome):** Survivors of severe hemorrhagic shock may suffer from necrosis of the anterior pituitary gland, which presents later as failing lactation and secondary amenorrhea.
- **Recurrence:** There is a 5–20% risk of recurrence of placental abruption in a subsequent pregnancy, which carries a high perinatal mortality rate.
- **Maternal Death:** Mortality varies from 2–8%, primarily caused by hemorrhagic shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), or acute renal failure.

**II. FETAL AND NEONATAL COMPLICATIONS**

Placental abruption contributes to nearly 30% of all cases of antepartum hemorrhage and is a massive driver of perinatal morbidity and mortality. Perinatal death is increased ten-fold.

- **Fetal Death / Stillbirth:**
    - _Revealed type:_ Fetal death occurs in approximately 25–30% of cases.
    - _Concealed type:_ Fetal mortality is exceedingly high, ranging from 50–100%.
- **Hypoxia and Asphyxia:** Premature separation of the placenta severely curtails gaseous exchange, leading to intrauterine hypoxia, fetal distress, and acidosis.
- **Prematurity:** Due to indicated preterm delivery or spontaneous preterm labor, neonates frequently suffer from prematurity and its associated complications, such as Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS).
- **Neurological Morbidity:** Severe fetal hypoxia can lead to periventricular leukomalacia and increases the risk of Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS).
- **Chronic Complications (in Chronic Abruption):** Small, recurrent, or chronic abruptions can cause chronic placental inflammation and dysfunction, leading to Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR) and oligohydramnios.
