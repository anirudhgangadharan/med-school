1. Define endometriosis. Discuss the clinical features and medical management of endometriosis.
**DEFINITION** Endometriosis is defined as the presence of functioning endometrium (glands and stroma) in sites other than the uterine mucosa.

- When ectopic endometrial tissues are found in the myometrium, it is called endometriosis interna or adenomyosis.
- When found at sites other than the uterus, it is called endometriosis externa (generally referred to as endometriosis).
- It is an estrogen-dependent disease and is not a neoplastic condition, although malignant transformation is possible.

**CLINICAL FEATURES** **Patient Profile:** Typically seen in women aged 30 to 45 years. Patients are mostly nulliparous or have had 1-2 children long back. It is associated with voluntary postponement of first conception, higher social status, and often has a familial predisposition.

**Symptoms:** About 25% of patients are asymptomatic, and the disease is accidentally discovered during laparoscopy or laparotomy. The degree of pain is not related to the severity or extent of the endometriosis; rather, the depth of penetration (>5 mm) is closely related to pain symptoms.

- **Dysmenorrhea (70%):** Characterized by progressively increasing secondary dysmenorrhea. The pain starts a few days prior to menstruation, worsens during menstruation, and takes time to relieve even after the period ceases (co-menstrual dysmenorrhea). The pain is deep-seated, often felt in the back or rectum, and is caused by the increased secretion of Prostaglandin F2α (PGF2α) and thromboxane β2 from endometriotic tissues.
- **Infertility (40–60%):** Endometriosis is found in 30–45% of infertile women. Factors include altered peritoneal fluid, ovulation disorders, distorted pelvic anatomy, tubal and ovarian damage, and implantation failure.
- **Dyspareunia (20–40%):** Usually deep dyspareunia, caused by stretching of the pouch of Douglas, fixed retroverted uterus, or nodules in the rectovaginal septum.
- **Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (AUB) (15–20%):** Menorrhagia is the predominant abnormality. Polymenorrhea or epimenorrhagia may occur if the ovaries are involved, and premenstrual spotting is common.
- **Chronic Pelvic Pain:** Non-cyclic pelvic discomfort, lower abdominal pain, or backache. Causes include peritoneal inflammation, tissue necrosis, adhesion formation, and nerve irritation due to deep penetration.
- **Organ-Specific Symptoms:**
    - _Urinary:_ Frequency, dysuria, hematuria, or back pain (hydronephrosis).
    - _Bowel:_ Painful defecation (dyschezia), diarrhea, constipation, melena, or bleeding per rectum during menses.
    - _Rare Sites:_ Catamenial hemoptysis (lung), catamenial seizures (brain), or bleeding from surgical scars.

**Signs:**

- **Abdominal Examination:** May be normal, or reveal a tender mass in the lower abdomen with restricted mobility (chocolate cyst/endometrioma or tubo-ovarian mass).
- **Pelvic (Bimanual) Examination:** Positive findings include pelvic tenderness, nodularity in the pouch of Douglas, nodular feel of the uterosacral ligaments, fixed retroverted uterus, and unilateral or bilateral adnexal masses (chocolate cysts).
- **Speculum Examination:** May reveal characteristic bluish spots or "powder-burn" lesions in the posterior vaginal fornix or cervix, which are tender and may bleed on touch.

**MEDICAL MANAGEMENT** The aim of medical treatment is to relieve pain, prevent disease progression, and induce atrophy of the endometriotic implants. Medical therapy is suppressive rather than curative, and recurrence rates are high (up to 40-50% in 5 years) following cessation of therapy.

**Principles of Medical Therapy:** Endometrial atrophy is induced by creating specific hormonal states:

1. _Pseudopregnancy:_ Using combined oral contraceptives (COCs) or progestogens.
2. _Pseudomenopause:_ Using Danazol or Gestrinone.
3. _Medical Oophorectomy:_ Using GnRH agonists/antagonists.

**Drugs Used:**

- **Nonsteroidal Anti-inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs):** Used as first-line therapy for symptomatic pain relief (e.g., Ibuprofen 800–1200 mg/day or Mefenamic acid 150–600 mg/day).
- **Combined Estrogen and Progestogen (Oral Pills):** Prescribed continuously or cyclically for 6-12 months. It causes endometrial decidualization and atrophy, improving symptoms in about 80% of women.
- **Progestogens:** They cause decidualization followed by atrophy of endometriotic tissues. They also reduce nerve fiber density and nerve growth factor expression in the lesions.
    - _Oral:_ Medroxyprogesterone acetate (10 mg thrice daily) or Norethisterone (10-30 mg daily) for 6-9 months.
    - _Dienogest:_ A 19-nortestosterone derivative given 2 mg/day orally. It inhibits cytokine secretion and cell proliferation, and is as effective as GnRH agonists for pain.
    - _Injectable:_ Depot medroxyprogesterone 150 mg IM every 3 months.
- **Levonorgestrel-Releasing Intrauterine System (LNG-IUS):** Releases 20 µg LNG/day and is effective for 5 years. It significantly reduces dysmenorrhea, pelvic pain, dyspareunia, and menorrhagia, and is highly effective for rectovaginal endometriosis.
- **Danazol:** An isoxazole derivative of 17-alpha ethinyl testosterone. It is given in doses of 400–600 mg/day orally in divided doses for 6-9 months. It creates a pseudomenopause. Due to marked androgenic and anabolic side effects (weight gain, acne, hirsutism), tolerance is low.
- **Gestrinone:** Dose is 1.25 or 2.5 mg twice a week for 6-9 months. It induces pseudomenopause but is better tolerated than Danazol.
- **GnRH Analogs (Agonists):** They cause sustained pituitary down-regulation, establishing a hypoestrogenic state ("medical oophorectomy"). They are the most effective for quick symptom relief and reduction of lesion volume.
    - _Preparations:_ Leuprolide (3.75 mg monthly or 11.5 mg IM every 3 months), Goserelin (3.6 mg implant monthly), Triptorelin (3 mg IM monthly) for a maximum of 6 months.
    - _Side effects:_ Hot flashes, vaginal dryness, and bone demineralization (osteoporosis).
    - _Add-back therapy:_ Low-dose combined estrogen (e.g., conjugated estrogen 0.625 mg) and progestin are co-administered to minimize bone loss and vasomotor symptoms if therapy exceeds 6 months.
- **GnRH Antagonists:** Elagolix (150–200 mg/day orally) is as effective as Leuprolide with less bone demineralization and no initial "flare" effect.
- **Aromatase Inhibitors:** Anastrozole (1 mg) or Letrozole (2.5 mg) inhibit the local aromatase activity in endometriotic tissues, reducing estrogen production and alleviating pain symptoms.
- **Selective Progesterone Receptor Modulators (SPRMs):** Mifepristone (50 mg/day) or Asoprisnil induce endometrial atrophy and amenorrhea, effectively reducing pelvic pain and lesion spread.

2. Discuss the etiology, clinical features and management of carcinoma cervix.
**CARCINOMA CERVIX: ETIOLOGY, CLINICAL FEATURES, AND MANAGEMENT**

**INTRODUCTION** Carcinoma of the cervix is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths in women worldwide and ranks first among female genital malignancies in developing countries like India.

**ETIOLOGY AND RISK FACTORS** The exact cause is unknown, but HPV infection is the most critical risk factor. The disease pathogenesis typically starts at the active 'transformation zone' of the cervix.

- **Human Papillomavirus (HPV):** Over 99.7% of patients with cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) and invasive cancer test positive for high-risk HPV-DNA. High-risk oncogenic types include HPV 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, 58, and 68.
- **Sexual Behavior:** Early sexual intercourse (≤16 years), having multiple sexual partners, and having a partner with multiple sexual contacts strongly predispose women to the disease.
- **Obstetric Factors:** Early age of first pregnancy, multiparity, and too many/frequent births are significant risk factors.
- **Other Infections:** Co-infections with HSV-2, HIV, Chlamydia, and other sexually transmitted infections.
- **Immunosuppression:** HIV-positive individuals and other immunocompromised states increase the risk.
- **Lifestyle & Environmental:** Poor genital hygiene, smoking habit, and long-term use of oral contraceptive pills.
- **Nutritional Deficiencies:** Dietary deficiency of vitamins A, C, E, and folic acid.
- **Lack of Screening:** Inadequate cervical cytology screening allows precancerous lesions to progress into invasive cancer.

**PATHOLOGY**

- **Site:** The lesion arises predominantly from the ectocervix (80%), with the remaining 20% arising from the endocervix.
- **Gross Appearance:**
    - _Exophytic:_ Cauliflower-like, friable mass that may fill the upper vagina.
    - _Ulcerative:_ Excavates the cervix and can involve vaginal fornices.
    - _Infiltrative (Endophytic):_ Typically endocervical, causing the cervix to expand and become barrel-shaped.
- **Histopathology:** Squamous cell carcinoma accounts for 80-85% of cases (including large cell and small cell varieties), while adenocarcinomas (endocervical, endometrioid, clear cell) make up 15-20%.

**CLINICAL FEATURES** **Patient Profile:** Patients are usually multiparous and in the premenopausal or perimenopausal age group, often with a history of ignored abnormal bleeding and no prior Pap smear screening.

**Symptoms:**

- **Early Stages:** May be asymptomatic or present with intermenstrual bleeding, postcoital (contact) bleeding, and excessive white discharge which may be offensive.
- **Advanced Stages:**
    - Irregular or continuous vaginal bleeding, which can be brisk, accompanied by highly offensive and malodorous purulent discharge.
    - _Pelvic Pain:_ Backache due to uterosacral ligament involvement, or deep-seated pain due to sacral plexus involvement.
    - _Pressure/Infiltrative Symptoms:_ Unilateral or bilateral leg edema from lymphatic or iliofemoral vein obstruction.
    - _Urinary Symptoms:_ Frequency, dysuria, hematuria, or true incontinence due to vesicovaginal fistula formation. Frequent attacks of pyelonephritis and uremia occur due to ureteric obstruction.
    - _Bowel Symptoms:_ Diarrhea, rectal pain, bleeding per rectum, or rectovaginal fistula.
    - Cachexia, anemia, and profound weakness develop as the disease progresses.

**Signs:**

- **Speculum Examination:** Reveals a red granular area in early stages, or an ulcerative/fungating exophytic mass in later stages that is highly friable and bleeds on touch.
- **Bimanual Examination:** The cervix feels indurated and friable, with restricted mobility as the disease extends to the vagina and parametrium.
- **Rectal Examination:** Invaluable for assessing parametrial involvement and determining if the nodular induration extends to the lateral pelvic wall.

**MANAGEMENT** Management involves a multidisciplinary team approach combining surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, heavily dependent on the clinical stage (FIGO staging). Pretreatment workup includes correcting anemia and evaluating the spread via MRI, CT, PET-CT, and cystoscopy.

**1. Preventive Management:**

- **Primary Prevention:** Prophylactic HPV vaccination using bivalent, quadrivalent, or nonavalent (Gardasil-9) vaccines for girls and young women (9-26 years) can prevent up to 90% of cervical cancers. Safe sexual practices, condom use, limiting parity, and treating STIs are also key.
- **Secondary Prevention:** Organized screening programs utilizing Pap smears (liquid-based cytology), HPV co-testing, and Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid (VIA) to detect and treat premalignant lesions (CIN), which can reduce cancer incidence by 80%.

**2. Curative Management (Stage-Specific):**

- **Microinvasive Carcinoma (Stage IA1):**
    - _Fertility Desired:_ Cervical conization or modified radical trachelectomy with close follow-up.
    - _Family Completed:_ Total extrafascial hysterectomy. If lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI) is present, modified radical hysterectomy with pelvic lymphadenectomy is indicated.
- **Stage IA2:**
    - Modified radical hysterectomy with pelvic lymphadenectomy, or radical trachelectomy with pelvic lymph node dissection for patients desiring to preserve fertility.
- **Early Invasive Carcinoma (Stage IB1, IB2, IIA1):**
    - _Primary Modality:_ Surgery (Type III Radical Hysterectomy with Pelvic Lymphadenectomy) or Primary Radiotherapy, as both offer a similar survival rate of about 85%.
    - _Fertility Sparing:_ Radical trachelectomy can be performed for tumors ≤2 cm (Stage IA2 to IB1).
- **Locally Advanced Carcinoma (Stage IB3, IIA2, IIB, III, IVA):**
    - Surgery is discouraged due to high morbidity.
    - _Standard of Care:_ Concurrent platinum-based chemoradiation therapy (CCRT). This involves external beam radiation therapy (EBRT) to the pelvis along with intracavitary brachytherapy, while weekly Cisplatin is given to sensitize cancer cells to radiation. The targeted cancerolytic radiation dose is approximately 7000–7500 cGy at Point A.
    - _Alternative:_ Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) may be used to shrink bulky tumors prior to surgery or in areas where radiotherapy is scarce.
- **Stage IVA (Central disease only):**
    - If disease is strictly central without pelvic sidewall involvement, a Pelvic Exenteration may be performed.
- **Stage IVB (Distant Metastasis):**
    - Palliative care including CCRT, palliative chemotherapy (e.g., Carboplatin-paclitaxel, Cisplatin-topotecan, Gemcitabine), and symptomatic treatment for pain and hemorrhage.

3. Dilatation and curettage.
**DEFINITION** Dilatation and curettage (D&C) is an operative procedure whereby dilatation of the cervical canal is followed by uterine curettage. It is the most common gynecological operation performed.

**INDICATIONS** The indications for D&C are broadly classified into diagnostic, therapeutic, and combined.

- **Diagnostic:**
    - Infertility (to assess ovulatory status via endometrial secretory changes).
    - Abnormal uterine bleeding (AUB).
    - Pathologic amenorrhea.
    - Endometrial tuberculosis.
    - Endometrial carcinoma.
    - Postmenopausal bleeding.
    - Chorionepithelioma.
- **Therapeutic:**
    - Dysfunctional uterine bleeding (DUB) – can stop the acute episode of excess bleeding when medical treatment fails.
    - Endometrial polyp removal.
    - Removal of retained intrauterine device (IUD).
    - Incomplete abortion.
- **Combined:**
    - DUB and Endometrial polyp.

**TIMING OF UTERINE CURETTAGE** The timing depends on the specific indication and menstrual pattern:

- _Menorrhagia:_ 5–6 days prior to the period or 5–6 days after the period starts.
- _Irregular shedding:_ 5–6 days after the period starts.
- _Irregular ripening:_ Soon after the period starts.
- _Acyclic bleeding:_ Done within 24 hours of menstruation or soon after the period starts.
- _Continuous bleeding:_ Can be done at any time.

**PRINCIPAL STEPS OF THE OPERATION**

1. **Preparation:** The patient is asked to empty the bladder prior to the operation.
2. **Anesthesia & Position:** The procedure is performed under general anesthesia or diazepam sedation with/without a paracervical block. The patient is placed in the lithotomy position.
3. **Asepsis:** Local antiseptic cleaning and draping are performed.
4. **Bimanual Examination:** Done to ascertain the size and position of the uterus.
5. **Exposure:** A posterior vaginal speculum is introduced, and the anterior lip of the cervix is grasped with an Allis tissue forceps (vulsellum).
6. **Uterine Sounding:** A uterine sound is introduced to confirm the position of the uterus and measure the length of the uterocervical canal.
7. **Dilatation:** The cervical canal is dilated using graduated dilators (e.g., Hawkin-Ambler or Hegar's). The dilator is held such that the index finger rests on the body of the instrument about 3 cm from the tip to act as a guard, preventing deep traumatic insertion. The tip must pass beyond the internal os. The cervix is kept steady by traction on the vulsellum.
8. **Curettage:** The uterine cavity is systematically curetted in a clockwise or anticlockwise direction, starting from the fundus down to the internal os.
    - A _sharp curette_ is used for benign lesions, whereas a _blunt curette_ is utilized for suspected malignancies.
    - The curettage must be gentle but thorough; vigorous scraping can damage the basal layer of the endometrium and the uterine muscle.
9. **Completion & Specimen Collection:** The vulsellum and speculum are removed. The curetted material is preserved in 10% formol-saline (or normal saline if tubercular endometritis is suspected), properly labeled, and sent for histological evaluation along with a short clinical history and the first day of the last menstrual period (LMP).
10. **Discharge:** Following a 3–4 hour observation period to allow anesthetic effects to wear off, the patient may be discharged.

**COMPLICATIONS** Though a minor surgery, complications can occur. D&C is a blind procedure and may miss the diagnosis of focal uterine lesions in 10–20% of women.

**Immediate Complications:**

- **Injury to the Cervix:** Often caused by a vulsellum bite or a lateral tear from the dilator. Slight tears are managed by gauze plugging or hemostatic sutures. Brisk hemorrhage from a descending cervical artery injury requires deep hemostatic sutures; if it extends to the uterine artery, an urgent laparotomy is required.
- **Uterine Perforation:** Most commonly occurs with the sound, dilator, or curette, especially in a pregnant uterus. It is diagnosed by a sudden loss of resistance, the instrument passing beyond the measured cavity length, undue mobility of the instrument, and vaginal bleeding.
    - _Management:_ Stop the procedure immediately, monitor vital signs (pulse, BP), and administer antibiotics. A small perforation may only need 24–48 hours of observation. Large perforations, or perforations in an infective/malignant uterus, require prompt laparoscopy or laparotomy.
- **Hemorrhage**.
- **Injury to the Gut**.
- **Infection**.

**Remote Complications:**

- **Cervical Incompetence:** Caused by forceful injury to the internal os, which can lead to recurrent midtrimester abortions.
- **Uterine Synechiae (Asherman's Syndrome):** Caused by overzealous curettage injuring the basal layer of the endometrium and uterine muscle, leading to secondary amenorrhea.


4. Hysterectomy - steps and complications.
**HYSTERECTOMY**

**DEFINITION AND TYPES** Hysterectomy is the surgical removal of the uterus.

- **Total Hysterectomy:** Removal of the entire uterus (body and cervix).
- **Subtotal (Supracervical) Hysterectomy:** Removal of the uterine body, leaving behind the cervix.
- **Panhysterectomy:** Removal of the uterus along with bilateral tubes and ovaries.
- **Radical Hysterectomy (Wertheim’s):** Removal of the uterus, bilateral tubes and ovaries, upper one-third of the vagina, adjacent parametrium, and draining pelvic lymph nodes.
- **Routes:** Abdominal, Vaginal, Laparoscopic, or Laparoscopic-assisted vaginal (LAVH), Robotic.

**PRINCIPAL STEPS OF TOTAL ABDOMINAL HYSTERECTOMY (TAH)** _Preoperative Work-up:_ Continuous bladder drainage by Foley catheter and vaginal packing under anesthesia.

- **Step 1: Incision and Exploration:** Abdomen is opened by a low transverse, intraumbilical paramedian, or midline incision. The uterus is drawn out, Doyen’s retractor is placed, and the patient is put in the Trendelenburg position. Bowels and omentum are packed off, and pelvic organs are examined.
- **Step 2: Uterine Traction:** Traction is applied to the uterus using a vulsellum or long artery forceps placed on either side of the uterine cornu.
- **Step 3: Management of Ovaries and Round Ligament:**
    - _If ovaries are removed:_ Paired clamps are placed on the infundibulopelvic (IP) ligament after creating a peritoneal window. The tissues are cut and transfixed with sutures.
    - _If ovaries are preserved:_ Paired clamps are placed near the uterine cornu to include the fallopian tube, mesosalpinx, and ovarian ligament, then cut and transfixed.
    - Paired clamps are placed on the round ligament, cut, and replaced by sutures.
- **Step 4: Dissection of Bladder:** The loose peritoneum of the uterovesical fold is cut transversely between the divided round ligaments. The bladder is pushed down and out with a gauze and scissors stripping to expose the anterior vaginal wall, minimizing injury to the bladder and ureters.
- **Step 5: Securing Uterine Vessels:** Paired clamps are placed on the parametrium containing the ascending branch of the uterine artery, close to the uterus at the level of the internal os. Tissues are cut and replaced by ligature.
- **Step 6: Securing Uterosacral and Mackenrodt’s Ligaments:**
    - The uterus is pulled forward to make uterosacral ligaments prominent; they are clamped close to the cervix, cut, and ligated.
    - Clamps are placed close to the cervix on the paracervical tissue (Mackenrodt’s ligament) containing the descending cervical artery, cut, and replaced by ligatures.
- **Step 7: Vault Opening and Uterine Removal:** The vaginal vault is opened by a stab incision at the cervicovaginal junction. The remaining vault is cut while applying traction on the cervix, and the uterus is removed.
- **Step 8: Vault Closure:** The lateral vaginal angles are closed by transfixation sutures to secure angle vessels. The vault is closed by interrupted sutures or reefed with a continuous locking suture.
- **Step 9: Closure:** Pelvic peritonization is optional. Abdominal packs are removed, peritoneal toileting is done, hemostasis is meticulously checked, and the abdomen is closed in layers.

**COMPLICATIONS OF HYSTERECTOMY** Complications are grouped into Intraoperative and Postoperative (Immediate, Late, and Remote).

**A. Intraoperative Complications:**

- **Hemorrhage:** From slipped ligatures or vascular injury.
- **Visceral Injury:** Injury to the urinary bladder, ureters, or intestines (especially common with cervical or broad ligament myomas, endometriosis, or pelvic adhesions).
- **Anesthetic Hazards:** Atelectasis, pulmonary edema, Mendelson's syndrome (aspiration pneumonitis), and embolism.

**B. Postoperative Complications (Immediate - within days to weeks):**

- **Hemorrhage:**
    - _Primary:_ Due to slipping of a ligature (usually at the vaginal angle), requiring immediate hemostasis under anesthesia.
    - _Secondary:_ Occurs between 7–14 days postoperatively due to sepsis or sloughing of the uterine/ovarian artery.
- **Shock:** Usually related to blood loss, hypovolemia, or prolonged procedure.
- **Infections and Febrile Morbidity:** Includes urinary tract infection (cystitis), abdominal wound infection, vault cellulitis, pelvic abscess, peritonitis, pneumonia, and septic pelvic thrombophlebitis.
- **Thromboembolism:** Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism.
- **Bowel Dysfunction:** Paralytic ileus or mechanical intestinal obstruction.
- **Wound Complications:** Hematoma in the pelvis or rectus sheath, seroma collection, wound dehiscence, or burst abdomen.
- **Necrotizing Fasciitis:** A rare but highly fatal infection of the superficial and subcutaneous tissues.

**C. Remote (Late) Complications:**

- **Vault Complications:** Vault granulation tissue, vault prolapse, or rare prolapse of the fallopian tube through the vault.
- **General Surgical:** Incisional hernia (more common with midline vertical incisions).
- **Fistula Formation:** Vesicovaginal fistula, ureterovaginal fistula, or rectovaginal fistula typically presenting weeks after the ischemic injury during surgery.
- **Ovarian Complications (If preserved):** Residual/trapped ovarian syndrome presenting with chronic pelvic pain, dyspareunia, or cyst formation requiring relaparotomy (1-3%).
- **Psychosexual:** Depression, psychiatric symptoms, and sexual dysfunction/dyspareunia.

5. Hormonal contraception.

**HORMONAL CONTRACEPTION**

Hormonal contraception comprises steroidal preparations designed to prevent pregnancy. These are among the most effective and widely used reversible methods of fertility control.

**CLASSIFICATION OF HORMONAL CONTRACEPTIVES**

1. **Oral Preparations:**
    - Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs / Pills)
    - Progestin-Only Pills (POPs / Minipill)
    - Centchroman (Saheli)
    - Emergency (Postcoital) Pills
2. **Parenteral (Injectables):** DMPA, NET-EN
3. **Implants:** Implanon, Norplant-II
4. **Intrauterine Devices:** LNG-IUS (Mirena)
5. **Transdermal Patch & Vaginal Ring**.

---

**1. COMBINED ORAL CONTRACEPTIVES (COCs)** COCs contain synthetic estrogen and progestin compounds and are the most effective reversible method of contraception.

- **Composition:** Estrogen (usually Ethinyl Estradiol 20–30 µg) and a Progestin (e.g., Levonorgestrel 0.15 mg, Desogestrel, or Drospirenone). Examples include Mala-N, Mala-D, Loette, and Yasmin.
- **Mechanism of Action:**
    - _Inhibition of Ovulation:_ Estrogen suppresses Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH), preventing follicular growth. Progestin suppresses the Luteinizing Hormone (LH) surge, thereby preventing ovulation.
    - _Cervical Mucus Alteration:_ Makes mucus thick, viscid, and scanty, which prevents sperm penetration.
    - _Endometrial Changes:_ Produces static endometrial hypoplasia, making it nonreceptive for blastocyst implantation.
    - _Tubal Motility:_ Alters tubal motility and transport.
- **Dosage & Administration:** One tablet daily, started on day 1 of the menstrual cycle, continued for 21 days followed by a 7-day hormone-free interval (or a 28-day pack with 7 dummy/iron pills).
- **Management of Missed Pills:** If 1 pill is missed (up to 24 hours late), it should be taken immediately and the rest continued as scheduled. If 2 or more pills are missed, the most recent missed pill is taken immediately, and extra barrier precautions must be used for the next 7 days.
- **Health Benefits:**
    - _Contraceptive:_ Highly effective with a failure rate of 0.1 per 100 Women Years (HWY).
    - _Non-contraceptive:_ Regulates the menstrual cycle, reduces dysmenorrhea (40%), menorrhagia (50%), and Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS). Protects against iron-deficiency anemia, Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID), ectopic pregnancy, benign breast disease, endometriosis, and functional ovarian cysts. Reduces the risk of endometrial and epithelial ovarian cancers by 50% and colorectal cancer by 40%.
- **Adverse Effects:**
    - _Minor:_ Nausea, vomiting, headache, mastalgia, breakthrough bleeding, weight gain, and chloasma.
    - _Major:_ Venous thromboembolism (VTE), arterial thrombosis (stroke, myocardial infarction), hypertension, and cholestatic jaundice. Prolonged use slightly increases the risk of breast cancer (RR 1.24) and cervical cancer.
- **Absolute Contraindications (WHO Category 4):** Circulatory diseases (arterial or venous thrombosis, severe hypertension, history of stroke/ischemic heart disease), active liver disease/tumors, estrogen-dependent neoplasia (breast cancer), migraine with focal aura, and heavy smokers over 35 years of age (>20 cigarettes/day).
- **Drug Interactions:** Enzyme-inducing drugs (e.g., Rifampicin, Barbiturates, Phenytoin, Griseofulvin) reduce the effectiveness of COCs. Additional barrier contraception is required during their use.

**2. PROGESTOGEN-ONLY CONTRACEPTIVES** These methods are devoid of estrogen and are highly suitable for lactating women and those with contraindications to estrogen.

- **A. Progestin-Only Pill (POP / Minipill):**
    - Contains very low doses of progestin (e.g., Levonorgestrel 75 µg, Desogestrel 75 µg) taken daily without any pill-free interval.
    - _Mechanism:_ Thickens cervical mucus preventing sperm penetration, causes endometrial atrophy, and inhibits ovulation in varying degrees (from 2% to 97% depending on the specific progestin).
    - _Advantages:_ Often called the "Lactation Pill" because it does not suppress breast milk. Ideal for hypertensives, diabetics, and smokers.
    - _Disadvantages:_ Irregular vaginal bleeding, spotting, amenorrhea, simple ovarian cysts, and a slightly higher failure rate (0.3–2 HWY).
- **B. Injectable Progestins:**
    - _Preparations:_ Depot Medroxyprogesterone Acetate (DMPA) 150 mg IM every 3 months; or Norethisterone Enanthate (NET-EN) 200 mg IM every 2 months.
    - _Mechanism:_ Suppresses the midcycle LH peak inhibiting ovulation, thickens cervical mucus, and causes endometrial atrophy.
    - _Advantages:_ Can be safely used during lactation, reduces menorrhagia, dysmenorrhea, and sickle cell crises. Protects against endometrial cancer.
    - _Disadvantages:_ Irregular bleeding, amenorrhea, weight gain, delayed return to fertility (6–12 months), and a reversible decrease in Bone Mineral Density (BMD).
- **C. Subdermal Implants (LARC):**
    - _Implanon/Nexplanon:_ A single rod containing 68 mg of Etonogestrel. Effective for 3 years. Very low failure rate (0.01 HWY).
    - _Norplant-II (Jadelle):_ Two rods containing Levonorgestrel, effective for 3 years.
    - _Benefits:_ Long-acting, immediately reversible, considered "reversible sterilization". Main drawback is frequent irregular bleeding and amenorrhea.
- **D. Levonorgestrel-Releasing Intrauterine System (LNG-IUS):**
    - Releases 20 µg of Levonorgestrel daily. Effective for 5–7 years. Acts locally by causing strong suppression of the endometrium and thickening cervical mucus.

**3. NONSTEROIDAL ORAL CONTRACEPTIVE**

- **Centchroman (Saheli / Chhaya):** A nonsteroidal compound with potent antiestrogenic properties. Dose is 30 mg twice a week for the first 3 months, then once a week.
- _Mechanism:_ Prevents implantation by creating asynchrony between the developing zygote and endometrium. It does not inhibit ovulation.
- _Benefits:_ Devoid of significant metabolic and hormonal adverse effects, safe during lactation. Failure rate is 1–4 HWY.

**4. TRANSDERMAL PATCH AND VAGINAL RING**

- **Transdermal Patch:** Delivers 150 µg norelgestromin and 20 µg ethinyl estradiol daily. Applied weekly for 3 weeks followed by a 1-week patch-free interval. Avoids hepatic first-pass metabolism.
- **Vaginal Ring (NuvaRing):** Releases ethinyl estradiol (15 µg) and etonogestrel (120 µg) daily. Inserted in the vagina for 21 days.

**5. EMERGENCY HORMONAL CONTRACEPTION (Postcoital Contraception)** Indicated after unprotected intercourse, condom rupture, or sexual assault, primarily acting to prevent or delay ovulation, or interfere with fertilization/implantation. Must not be used as a regular contraceptive method.

- **Levonorgestrel (LNG):** 1.50 mg as a single dose (or 0.75 mg two doses 12 hours apart) taken within 72 hours (up to 120 hours). Failure rate 0–1%.
- **Ulipristal Acetate (SPRM):** 30 mg single dose orally up to 120 hours. Superior to LNG. Delays ovulation and inhibits implantation.
- **Yuzpe Regimen:** Combined hormonal regimen (0.25 mg LNG + 50 µg Ethinyl estradiol) - 2 tablets stat and 2 tablets after 12 hours (within 72 hrs).
- **Mifepristone (RU 486):** 100 mg single dose within 5 days of intercourse. Acts as an antiprogesterone.


6. Vaginal candidiasis.

**VAGINAL CANDIDIASIS (MONILIASIS)**

**INTRODUCTION** Vaginal candidiasis (moniliasis) is a common fungal infection of the lower female genital tract. It is primarily caused by _Candida albicans_, which is a gram-positive, yeast-like fungus exhibiting dimorphic features (both yeast buds and hyphal forms).

**RISK FACTORS** The normal vaginal defense mechanisms are often impaired by specific factors that favor fungal overgrowth:

- **Pregnancy:** Causes increased vaginal acidity, glycosuria, and elevated glycogen in the vaginal epithelial cells.
- **Diabetes Mellitus:** Results in increased cellular glycogen and glycosuria, creating an excellent culture medium for the fungus.
- **Broad-Spectrum Antibiotics:** Destroy the normal, protective acid-forming _Lactobacilli_ flora.
- **Hormonal/Medication:** Use of combined oral contraceptive pills and prolonged use of corticosteroids.
- **Immunosuppression:** Seen in conditions like HIV or other debilitating diseases.
- **Others:** Obesity, and thyroid or parathyroid diseases.

**CLINICAL FEATURES** **Symptoms:**

- **Pruritus:** The hallmark symptom is intense vulvovaginal pruritus, which is typically out of proportion to the amount of vaginal discharge.
- **Vaginal Discharge:** Patients complain of a distinct, thick white discharge.
- **Dyspareunia:** Deep or superficial pain during intercourse due to local soreness and inflammation.

**Signs:**

- **Discharge Characteristics:** The discharge is thick, curdy white, and presents in flakes (resembling cottage cheese). It is characteristically firmly adherent to the vaginal walls.
- **Vulvovaginal Inflammation:** The vulva may appear red and markedly swollen with evidence of scratching (excoriation). Vaginal examination is often tender.
- **Oozing Spots:** When the adherent white flakes are wiped off the vaginal mucosa, multiple raw, oozing spots are revealed.

**DIAGNOSIS AND INVESTIGATIONS**

- **Wet Mount Microscopy:** A smear of the vaginal discharge is mixed with a drop of 10% potassium hydroxide (KOH) solution, which lyses red blood cells and clears cellular debris. Under the microscope, filamentous forms of mycelia, pseudohyphae, buds, and spores are visible.
- **Culture:** If wet mount is negative or in recurrent cases, culture on Nickerson’s or Sabouraud’s media confirms the diagnosis by showing positive growth in 24–72 hours.
- **Vaginal pH:** The pH remains acidic, typically <4.5 (unlike Trichomoniasis or Bacterial Vaginosis where it is elevated).
- **Whiff Test:** Negative (no fishy or amine odor upon adding KOH).

**CLASSIFICATION (CDC GUIDELINES)**

- **Uncomplicated:** Sporadic or infrequent episodes, mild to moderate in severity, likely caused by _C. albicans_, and occurring in non-immunocompromised women.
- **Complicated:** Recurrent or severe episodes, non-albicans candidiasis, or infections presenting in women with uncontrolled diabetes, immunosuppression, or debilitation.

**MANAGEMENT** The primary aim is to correct any underlying predisposing factors (e.g., tight glycemic control in diabetics, discontinuing broad-spectrum antibiotics if possible).

**1. Treatment of Uncomplicated Candidiasis:**

- **Topical Therapy:** Intravaginal fungicidal preparations of the polyene or azole group (e.g., clotrimazole, miconazole, or terconazole) in the form of creams or pessaries for 1–3 days.
- **Oral Therapy:** A single oral dose of Fluconazole (150 mg) or Itraconazole is highly effective.

**2. Treatment of Complicated or Recurrent Candidiasis:**

- **Topical Azoles:** Extended regimen for 7–14 days.
- **Oral Therapy:** A second dose of oral Fluconazole 150 mg is administered 72 hours after the first dose. Alternatively, Fluconazole 150 mg PO can be given weekly for 6 weeks.
- **Boric Acid:** Vaginal boric acid capsules (600 mg in a gelatin capsule) are highly effective for recurrent or resistant cases, as boric acid inhibits fungal cell wall growth.

**3. Additional Instructions:**

- Treatment should be continued without interruption even during menstruation.
- If there is associated intestinal moniliasis, systemic treatment with oral Fluconazole 50 mg daily for 7 days is indicated.
- **Partner Treatment:** Routine treatment of the male partner is not mandatory unless symptomatic, but he may be treated with local Nystatin ointment for a few days following coitus. The use of a condom is preferred during the treatment period.

7. Endometrial changes in menstrual cycle.
**ENDOMETRIAL CHANGES IN THE MENSTRUAL CYCLE**

The endometrium is the lining epithelium of the uterine cavity, consisting of surface epithelium, glands, stroma, and blood vessels. It is divided into two distinct zones:

- **Basal Zone (Stratum Basalis):** Forms about one-third of the total depth (approx. 1 mm thick) and lies in contact with the myometrium. It is uninfluenced by cyclic hormones and is responsible for the regeneration of all endometrial components after menstrual shedding.
- **Functional Zone:** The superficial layer that undergoes profound cyclic changes under the influence of fluctuating ovarian hormones (estrogen and progesterone).

The cyclic changes in the functional zone are traditionally divided into four phases:

**1. Regenerative Phase**

- **Timing:** Starts even before menstruation ceases and is completed 2–3 days after the end of menstruation.
- **Histology:** The glands and stromal cells regenerate from the remnants in the basal zone. The surface is lined by cubical epithelium, and new blood vessels grow from the stumps of old ones.
- **Thickness:** The endometrial thickness averages 2 mm.

**2. Proliferative Phase (Days 6–14)**

- **Hormonal Control:** Driven by the rising levels of ovarian estrogens.
- **Glands:** Become tubular, straight, or slightly coiled, and lie perpendicular to the surface. The lining epithelium becomes columnar with abundant cellular mitosis and preovulatory pseudostratification.
- **Stroma:** The stromal cells become spindle-shaped, compact, and show abundant mitosis and cell proliferation.
- **Blood Vessels:** The spiral vessels extend unbranched to a region just below the epithelium, forming a loose capillary network.
- **Thickness:** Reaches about 10–12 mm at the time of ovulation.

**3. Secretory Phase (Days 15–28)**

- **Hormonal Control:** Mediated by the combined effects of estrogen and progesterone liberated from the corpus luteum following ovulation. Progesterone acts on the estrogen-primed endometrium.
- **Glands:** They increase in size, undergo marked dilatation, and become corkscrew-shaped.
    - _Subnuclear vacuolation:_ The appearance of glycogen vacuoles between the nuclei and the basement membrane is the earliest histological evidence of progesterone effect (ovulation), appearing 36–48 hours post-ovulation.
    - _Saw-tooth appearance:_ Later, the secretions enter the gland lumina, pushing the nuclei back toward the basement membrane and creating a characteristic "saw-toothed" glandular epithelium.
- **Stroma:** Stromal edema and decidual changes occur. Stromal cells become large, swollen, and polyhedral.
- **Blood Vessels:** There is marked spiraling and coiling of the arterioles.
- **Thickness:** The endometrium reaches its maximum thickness of 12–14 mm. Growth ceases 5–6 days prior to menstruation due to dehydration of the glands.

**4. Menstrual (Bleeding) Phase (Days 1–5)**

- **Hormonal Control:** Triggered by the withdrawal of estrogen and progesterone due to the regression of the corpus luteum.
- **Pathophysiology:** Hormone withdrawal causes intense spasm of the spiral arterioles at the basal part, leading to stasis and tissue anoxemia.
- **Endometrial Breakdown:** There is intense breakdown and enzymatic autodigestion of the endometrial tissues by proteolytic enzymes, specifically matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which are stimulated by the inflammatory process.
- **Histology:** The glands crumble, and the stroma undergoes hemorrhage and shedding. The superficial functional layer is shed into the uterine cavity along with blood from broken arteries, veins, and capillaries.
- **Thickness:** Reduces drastically to 1–2 mm.
- **Hemostasis:** Menstrual flow eventually stops due to prolonged vasoconstriction, myometrial contraction, and local aggregation of platelets with fibrin deposition.

8. Medical management of AUB.
**MEDICAL MANAGEMENT OF ABNORMAL UTERINE BLEEDING (AUB / DUB)**

**1. GENERAL MANAGEMENT**

- **Rest and Assurance:** Rest is advised during the bleeding phase. Assurance and sympathetic handling are helpful, particularly in adolescents.
- **Correction of Anemia:** Anemia must be corrected appropriately by diet, hematinics (iron supplementation), and blood transfusion if severe.
- **Systemic Care:** Any underlying systemic or endocrinal abnormality (e.g., thyroid dysfunction) should be investigated and treated accordingly.

**2. NON-HORMONAL MANAGEMENT** Used predominantly as second-line therapy or for ovulatory DUB.

- **Prostaglandin Synthetase Inhibitors (NSAIDs):**
    - _Mechanism:_ Inhibits cyclooxygenase, blocking the synthesis of thromboxane and prostacyclin.
    - _Effect:_ Reduces menstrual blood loss in ovulatory DUB by 25–50% and improves dysmenorrhea.
    - _Drugs & Dose:_ Mefenamic acid 500 mg TID for 5 days during the bleeding phase. Highly effective in women over 35 years.
- **Antifibrinolytic Agents:**
    - _Mechanism:_ Counteracts the endometrial fibrinolytic system.
    - _Effect:_ Reduces menstrual blood loss by 50% and is particularly helpful in IUCD-induced menorrhagia.
    - _Drugs & Dose:_ Tranexamic acid 500 mg–1 g every 6 hours for 5 days.
    - _Contraindication:_ History of thromboembolism.
- **Ethamsylate:** 500 mg QID can be used to control bleeding.

**3. HORMONAL MANAGEMENT** **A. Progestogens (Mainstay of Therapy)**

- _Mechanism:_ Converts estradiol to less potent estrone, inhibits estrogen receptors, possesses antimitotic effects, halts endometrial growth, and promotes organized endometrial shedding.
- _Regimens:_
    - _To stop acute bleeding:_ Norethisterone 5 mg TID until bleeding stops (usually takes 3–7 days).
    - _Cyclic Therapy:_ Norethisterone (5 mg TID), Medroxyprogesterone acetate (MPA) (10 mg TID), or Dydrogesterone (10 mg TID) given from the 5th to 25th day of the menstrual cycle.
    - _Continuous Therapy:_ MPA 10 mg TID for at least 90 days, or injectable Depot Medroxyprogesterone Acetate (DMPA).

**B. Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs)**

- _Drugs & Dose:_ Low-dose pills (e.g., Ethinyl estradiol 20–30 µg + Levonorgestrel 0.15 mg) taken once daily for 21 days or as an extended use regimen.
- _Effect:_ Causes endometrial atrophy and strongly suppresses the hypothalamo-pituitary axis.

**C. Estrogens (For Acute & Severe Bleeding)**

- _Drugs & Dose:_ Conjugated equine estrogen 25 mg intravenously (IV) or 10 mg/day orally in 4 divided doses.
- _Mechanism:_ Rapidly grows the denuded endometrium and promotes platelet adhesiveness.
- _Follow-up:_ Once bleeding is controlled, progestin (e.g., MPA 10 mg/day) must be added.

**D. Levonorgestrel-Releasing Intrauterine System (LNG-IUS / Mirena)**

- _Dose:_ Releases 20 µg of Levonorgestrel daily and is effective for 5 years.
- _Mechanism:_ Induces profound endometrial glandular atrophy and stromal decidualization with minimal systemic absorption.
- _Effect:_ Reduces blood loss by 80–100% (considered a "medical hysterectomy"). Recommended as first-line therapy for heavy menstrual bleeding in the absence of structural abnormalities or in women with coagulopathies.

**E. Danazol & Gestrinone**

- _Danazol:_ A 19-nor steroid given in doses of 200–400 mg/day continuously for 3 months. Reduces blood loss by 60% or produces amenorrhea. Suitable for recurrent symptoms or patients awaiting hysterectomy.
- _Gestrinone:_ Dose is 1.25–2.5 mg twice a week.

**F. GnRH Agonists**

- _Mechanism:_ Subtherapeutic doses reduce blood loss, while therapeutic doses produce medical oophorectomy and amenorrhea.
- _Indication:_ Valuable for short-term use in severe DUB, prior to endometrial ablation, or if the woman is infertile and desires pregnancy.

**G. Progesterone Antagonists and Modulators**

- _Mifepristone (RU 486):_ An antiprogesterone that inhibits ovulation and induces amenorrhea.
- _Selective Progesterone Receptor Modulators (SPRMs):_ Ulipristal acetate significantly reduces blood loss (90%) and induces amenorrhea (70%) within 10 days of treatment without causing endometrial hyperplasia.

**4. OTHER SPECIFIC MEDICATIONS**

- **Ormeloxifene:** An estrogen receptor modulator given 60 mg twice a week for 3 months.
- **Desmopressin:** A synthetic analog of arginine-vasopressin. Specifically indicated for AUB associated with von Willebrand’s disease or factor VIII deficiency. Administered IV (0.3 µg/kg) or intranasally.

9. Female sterilization.
**FEMALE STERILIZATION**

**INTRODUCTION** Female sterilization is a highly effective, safe, and permanent method of terminal contraception. The underlying principle is the occlusion or resection of a segment of both fallopian tubes to prevent the meeting of sperm and ovum. It is the most popular method of terminal contraception globally.

**INDICATIONS**

- **Family Planning Purposes:** This is the primary indication, widely adopted for socioeconomic reasons once a couple has completed their desired family size.
- **Medicosurgical Indications:** Recommended for women in whom repeated pregnancies would worsen pre-existing medical conditions, such as heart disease, diabetes, severe hypertension, or chronic renal disease. It is also indicated during a third repeat cesarean section or following surgical repair of genital prolapse.
- **Risk-Reducing Salpingectomy:** Bilateral total salpingectomy is increasingly recommended as a preventive measure against serous ovarian and peritoneal cancers, particularly for high-risk women (e.g., BRCA mutations).

**TIMING OF SURGERY**

- **Puerperal (Postpartum):** Performed 24–48 hours following delivery. Its chief advantage is technical simplicity due to the enlarged, high uterus, and it allows the patient to recover from delivery and surgery during a single hospital stay.
- **Interval:** Performed at least 3 months following a delivery or abortion. The ideal time is in the proliferative phase, immediately following the menstrual period.
- **Concurrent:** Can be safely performed concurrently with Medical Termination of Pregnancy (MTP) or during a cesarean delivery.

**SURGICAL APPROACHES** The procedure can be approached abdominally, vaginally, or transcervically.

**1. Abdominal Tubectomy** This approach includes conventional laparotomy and minilaparotomy.

- **Pomeroy's Technique:** A loop is created by holding the fallopian tube with an Allis forceps at the isthmic-ampullary junction. The base of this loop is firmly tied with No. '0' chromic catgut through an avascular area in the mesosalpinx. A 1–1.5 cm segment of the loop distal to the ligature is then excised. Because the absorbable catgut ligature eventually dissolves, the cut ends seal independently and retract widely from each other. This is a highly effective, safe, and simple method with a low failure rate of 0.1–0.5%.
- **Irving Method:** The tube is ligated and divided; the free medial cut end is then buried into a newly created myometrial tunnel in the posterior uterine wall.
- **Uchida Technique:** A subserosal saline injection creates a bleb; the muscular tube is exposed, ligated, and a 3–5 cm segment is resected. The proximal stump retracts beneath the serosa and is buried, while the distal stump is left open to the peritoneal cavity.
- **Madlener Technique:** The tubal loop is simply crushed and tied with non-absorbable silk without any excision. Due to a high failure rate of up to 7%, this method has been abandoned.
- **Kroener Method:** Involves fimbriectomy (ligation and resection of the ampullary end), though it is not commonly performed.
- **Minilaparotomy (Mini-Lap):** A widely utilized method in National Family Planning Programmes. It is performed under local anesthesia via a small abdominal incision (1/2" to 3/4"). The uterus is elevated transvaginally using a manipulator to bring the fallopian tubes close to the incision, facilitating simple tubal resection. Hospital stay is short (24–48 hours).

**2. Vaginal Ligation** Tubectomy can be performed vaginally, either isolated through a posterior colpotomy or alongside vaginal plastic surgery. It is suitable for interval cases with a uterus smaller than 12 weeks in size. While it requires specific vaginal surgical skill and regional/general anesthesia, it offers the advantage of a short hospital stay and is convenient for obese women.

**3. Laparoscopic Sterilization** A highly popular method, especially for interval sterilization and concurrent with early MTP. It should generally not be done within 6 weeks of delivery.

- **Procedure:** Requires pneumoperitoneum (using CO2, N2O, or room air) established via a Veress needle, followed by trocar and laparoscope insertion.
- **Occlusion Devices:** The tubes can be occluded using a silastic Fallope ring or a Filshie clip (made of titanium and silicone rubber, which destroys only 4 mm of the tube).
- **Electrosurgical:** Tubes may also be coagulated using unipolar or bipolar electrosurgery. Bipolar is safer regarding bowel injury but carries a slightly higher failure rate (2.1%).
- **Advantages:** It has a very short hospital stay (3-4 hours) and is easy to perform, but requires specialized training.

**4. Transcervical Sterilization**

- **Essure:** A microcoil (nickel-titanium alloy) inserted hysteroscopically into the proximal fallopian tubes. Scar tissue grows into the device over 3 months, permanently blocking the tube. Temporary contraception is needed until occlusion is confirmed by hysterosalpingogram.

**COMPLICATIONS**

- **Immediate:** Complications relate to the surgical approach and anesthesia. These include primary hemorrhage, wound infection, broad ligament hematoma, peritonitis, or injury to adjacent viscera (like the rectum).
- **Remote (Late):**
    - **Post-Ligation Syndrome:** Characterized by chronic pelvic pain, congestive dysmenorrhea, menorrhagia, and cystic ovaries.
    - **Menstrual Abnormalities:** Irregular periods or hypomenorrhea.
    - **Ectopic Pregnancy:** If failure occurs and the patient conceives, there is a significantly high chance (15–50%) that the pregnancy will be ectopic.
    - **Other:** Dyspareunia, incisional hernia, and psychological upset.

**FAILURE RATE AND REVERSIBILITY**

- **Failure:** The overall failure rate of tubal sterilization is about 0.7%. Pomeroy's technique carries a very low failure rate (0.1–0.5%). In laparoscopy, the Filshie clip has a failure rate of 0.1%, Fallope ring 1.7%, and bipolar coagulation 2.1%. Failure may result from recanalization or fistula formation.
- **Reversibility:** Prior to surgery, the couple must be counseled that the procedure is intended to be permanent, though regret is not uncommon. If desired, reversal via microsurgical tubal reanastomosis offers an 80% pregnancy success rate, particularly if clips or rings were originally used. Alternatively, In Vitro Fertilization (IVF) is an option for achieving pregnancy after tubal occlusion.


10. Male factors in infertility.
**MALE FACTORS IN INFERTILITY**

**INTRODUCTION & INCIDENCE** Infertility is defined as the inability to conceive within one year of regular unprotected coitus. The male partner is directly responsible in about 30–40% of infertile couples, and contributes to a combined factor in another 10% of cases. The causes of male infertility are broadly classified into defective spermatogenesis, obstruction of the efferent duct system, failure to deposit sperm high in the vagina, and errors in the seminal fluid.

**ETIOLOGY**

**1. Defective Spermatogenesis (Primary Testicular Disorders & Pre-testicular Disorders)** This is the most common category, responsible for poor sperm count, motility, or morphology.

- **Genetic Factors:** Chromosomal abnormalities, such as Klinefelter's syndrome (47, XXY), are the most common cause of azoospermia. Microdeletions in the long arm of the Y chromosome (Yq) lead to severe oligospermia or azoospermia. Single gene mutations are also implicated.
- **Endocrine Disorders:** Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (e.g., Kallmann’s syndrome, characterized by isolated gonadotropin deficiency) leads to testicular failure. Other causes include hyperprolactinemia, which causes impotence, and thyroid dysfunction.
- **Congenital Defects:** Undescended testes (cryptorchidism) depresses spermatogenesis despite normal hormone secretion. Kartagener syndrome (immotile cilia syndrome) leads to loss of sperm motility.
- **Infections:** Post-pubertal mumps orchitis can permanently damage spermatogenesis. Chronic systemic illnesses (e.g., bronchiectasis) and genital tract infections with _Chlamydia trachomatis_ or _T. mycoplasma_ adversely affect sperm quality and count.
- **Thermal Factors:** Conditions that raise scrotal temperature, such as varicocele, may interfere with the cooling mechanism and depress spermatogenesis, although the exact association remains debated.
- **Toxins and Iatrogenic Factors:** Heavy smoking, alcohol consumption, malnutrition, radiation, and cytotoxic drugs reduce spermatogenesis. Drugs such as beta-blockers, antihypertensives, nitrofurantoin, cimetidine, and antipsychotics are also known culprits.
- **Immunological Factors:** Presence of antisperm antibodies (sperm agglutinating or immobilizing) can cause clumping of spermatozoa and hinder motility.

**2. Obstruction of the Efferent Duct System (Post-testicular)**

- **Congenital:** Bilateral congenital absence of the vas deferens (CBAVD), which is highly associated with cystic fibrosis gene mutations (seen in 66% of such cases). Young's syndrome involves epididymal obstruction.
- **Acquired Infections:** Tuberculosis and Gonorrhea can lead to scarring and obstruction of the ducts.
- **Surgical Trauma:** Iatrogenic injury during herniorrhaphy, or deliberate ligation via vasectomy.

**3. Failure to Deposit Sperm High in the Vagina (Coital Problems)**

- Erectile dysfunction or psychological impotence.
- Ejaculatory defects, including premature ejaculation, retrograde ejaculation, or failure of emission (aspermia).
- Anatomical defects of the penis, such as hypospadias.
- Bladder neck surgery.

**4. Errors in the Seminal Fluid**

- Unusually high or low ejaculate volume (low volume may indicate absent seminal vesicles or ductal obstruction).
- Abnormal viscosity of the seminal fluid.

**NOMENCLATURE OF SEMEN ABNORMALITIES**

- **Aspermia:** Failure of emission of semen (no ejaculate).
- **Hypospermia:** Low semen volume (<2 mL).
- **Oligozoospermia:** Sperm concentration less than 15-20 million/mL.
- **Azoospermia:** Complete absence of spermatozoa in the semen.
- **Asthenozoospermia:** Reduced progressive sperm motility.
- **Teratozoospermia:** >70% spermatozoa with abnormal morphology.
- **Leukocytospermia:** Increased white blood cells (pus cells) in the semen.

**INVESTIGATIONS**

- **History & Physical Examination:** Detailed medical, surgical, and sexual history. Assessment of Body Mass Index (BMI), secondary sex characters, gynecomastia, and thorough genital exam to evaluate testicular volume (normal >20 mL via orchidometer), presence of varicocele, and hypospadias.
- **Semen Analysis (Cornerstone of Diagnosis):** Should be collected by masturbation after 2-3 days of abstinence and examined within 2 hours. Two samples at least 4 weeks apart are required if the first is abnormal.
    - _WHO (2010) Normal Reference Values:_ Volume: $\ge$ 1.5 mL; Concentration: $\ge$ 15 million/mL; Total Motility: $\ge$ 32% progressive forward motility; Morphology: $\ge$ 4% normal forms; Viability: $\ge$ 58% living.
- **Endocrine Profile:** Serum FSH, LH, testosterone, prolactin, and TSH. Elevated FSH and LH suggest primary testicular failure. Low FSH, LH, and testosterone suggest hypogonadotropic hypogonadism. High prolactin can cause impotence.
- **Testicular Biopsy:** Performed in cases of azoospermia to differentiate primary testicular failure (spermatogenesis absent) from obstructive causes (spermatogenesis normal).
- **Karyotyping and Genetic Testing:** Indicated for severe oligospermia or azoospermia to rule out Klinefelter's syndrome (47, XXY) or Y chromosome microdeletions.
- **Immunological Tests & Prostatic Massage:** Sperm immobilizing tests for antisperm antibodies, and culture of prostatic fluid if plenty of pus cells are present in semen.

**MANAGEMENT** Management of male infertility depends heavily on the underlying etiology, though many cases remain idiopathic and are managed empirically or via Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART).

**Medical & General Management**

- **General:** Smoking cessation, avoiding alcohol, weight optimization, and avoiding gonadotoxic drugs.
- **Endocrine Therapy:**
    - Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism is treated with hCG (5000 IU IM) to stimulate testosterone, often combined with hMG or pure FSH to stimulate spermatogenesis.
    - Pulsatile GnRH therapy is effective for Kallmann’s syndrome.
    - Hyperprolactinemia is treated with dopamine agonists (e.g., Cabergoline, Bromocriptine) to restore libido and fertility.
- **Retrograde Ejaculation:** Alpha-adrenergic agonists (e.g., Phenylephrine) to improve internal urethral sphincter tone, followed by sperm recovery from neutralized urine for IUI.
- **Infections (Leukocytospermia):** Prolonged broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g., Doxycycline or Erythromycin for 4-6 weeks).
- **Erectile Dysfunction:** Psychosexual counseling and phosphodiesterase inhibitors (e.g., Sildenafil or Tadalafil).

**Surgical Management**

- **Microsurgery:** Vasovasostomy or vasoepididymostomy for post-testicular obstruction.
- **Orchidopexy:** For undescended testis (ideally should be done between 2-3 years of age to preserve spermatogenesis).
- Surgery for varicocele may be done, but its definitive benefit for improving fertility remains highly debated.

**Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART)** ART has revolutionized the management of male infertility.

- **Intrauterine Insemination (IUI):** Indicated for mild-to-moderate oligo/asthenospermia, hypospadias, retrograde ejaculation, or immunological factors. Processed, highly motile sperm are injected directly into the uterine cavity around the time of ovulation.
- **In Vitro Fertilization (IVF) with Intracytoplasmic Sperm Injection (ICSI):** The gold standard for severe oligoasthenoteratozoospermia, obstructive azoospermia, or failed IUI. A single sperm is injected directly into the oocyte.
- **Sperm Retrieval Techniques:** Testicular Sperm Extraction (TESE) or Percutaneous Epididymal Sperm Aspiration (PESA) are used to retrieve sperm directly from the testes/epididymis in men with obstructive azoospermia or testicular failure.
- **Artificial Insemination Donor (AID):** Used as a last resort for untreatable azoospermia, primary testicular failure, or known transmissible genetic diseases.

11. Role of laparoscopy in infertility.

**ROLE OF LAPAROSCOPY IN INFERTILITY**

Laparoscopy is considered the "gold standard" (definitive method) for the evaluation of tubal and peritoneal factors of infertility. It is most commonly performed in the proliferative phase, typically between the 7th and 10th day of the menstrual cycle. A major advantage of laparoscopy is that it allows for both accurate diagnosis and therapeutic intervention in the same sitting (a "one-stop" procedure).

**A. DIAGNOSTIC ROLE OF LAPAROSCOPY** Laparoscopy provides a direct, panoramic view of the pelvic organs.

**Indications for Diagnostic Laparoscopy in Infertility:**

- Age >35 years.
- History of pelvic pain, dyspareunia, pelvic surgery, or adhesions.
- Abnormal findings on Hysterosalpingography (HSG).
- Failure to conceive after a reasonable period (e.g., 6 months) despite a normal HSG.
- Women with suspected comorbid pelvic pathology, such as Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID) or endometriosis.
- Before attempting reversal of a sterilization operation.

**Diagnostic Benefits and Visualized Structures:**

- **Tubes (Chromopertubation):** Methylene blue dye is injected transcervically to check for tubal patency and spillage into the pouch of Douglas. It detects the exact site and side of a tubal block, tubal motility, hydrosalpinx changes, fimbrial agglutination, and peritubal adhesions.
- **Ovaries:** Evaluates morphological changes indicative of Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS), ovarian endometriosis (chocolate cysts), adhesions, and the presence of an ovulation stigma (evidence of recent ovulation).
- **Uterus:** Helps in the detection and assessment of uterine anomalies (e.g., bicornuate or septate uterus) and uterine fibroids.
- **Peritoneal Factors:** It is invaluable for diagnosing minimal pelvic endometriosis, adhesions, PID, and pelvic tuberculosis. A completely negative finding provides strong reassurance and helps rule out organic peritoneal causes of unexplained infertility.

**B. THERAPEUTIC (OPERATIVE) ROLE OF LAPAROSCOPY** Laparoscopic microsurgical techniques cause minimal tissue handling and damage, ensuring perfect hemostasis and minimal postoperative adhesion formation.

**Indications and Procedures:**

- **Tubal Reconstructive Surgery:** Procedures like adhesiolysis (salpingo-ovariolysis), fimbrioplasty (releasing agglutinated fimbriae), and salpingostomy can be effectively performed laparoscopically to restore tubal anatomy.
- **Management of Endometriosis:** Allows for the fulguration, ablation (by electrodiathermy or laser vaporization), or excision of endometriotic implants. For ovarian endometriomas, laparoscopic cyst aspiration, cyst wall vaporization, or complete cystectomy (for cysts $\ge$4 cm) can be performed to improve fertility.
- **Management of PCOS:** Laparoscopic Ovarian Drilling (LOD) using a diathermy needle or laser beam is performed for clomiphene-resistant, hyperandrogenic anovulatory women. Multiple punctures (4-6 sites) are made to destroy the stroma, which reduces intraovarian androgen levels and induces spontaneous ovulation.
- **Management of Uterine Fibroids:** Subserous and intramural fibroids that distort pelvic anatomy or contribute to infertility can be removed via laparoscopic myomectomy.
- **Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART):** Laparoscopy is utilized for transferring gametes or zygotes directly into the fallopian tubes in procedures such as Gamete Intrafallopian Transfer (GIFT) and Zygote Intrafallopian Transfer (ZIFT). It is also used for fallopian tube sperm perfusion.
- **Unexplained Infertility:** Unexplained cases may benefit therapeutically from laparoscopy due to the clearance of flimsy intraluminal tubal plugs or minor adhesions during chromopertubation or adhesiolysis.

**LIMITATIONS AND COMPARISON WITH HSG**

- Laparoscopy is more invasive than HSG, requires anesthesia (usually general), and carries surgical risks.
- While laparoscopy is excellent for evaluating external pelvic anatomy and peritoneal factors, it cannot detect abnormalities within the uterine cavity (like synechiae) or the internal tubal lumen.
- Therefore, HSG and laparoscopy are not substitutes for one another but should be regarded as complementary investigations in the infertility workup.
- In cases of suspected severe pelvic tuberculosis where dense adhesions are anticipated, laparoscopy is relatively contraindicated, and a diagnostic laparotomy is a safer alternative.

12. Diagnosis of urinary fistulas.
**DIAGNOSIS OF URINARY FISTULAS**

A genitourinary fistula is an abnormal communication between the urinary and genital tracts, with the involuntary escape of urine into the vagina. The vesicovaginal fistula (VVF) is the most common type. The diagnosis is established through a meticulous history, clinical examination, and special investigations.

**CLINICAL FEATURES (SYMPTOMS)**

- **True Incontinence:** The classic symptom is the continuous escape of urine per vaginam, without any urge to pass urine.
- **Positional Incontinence:** If the fistula is small, urine may escape only in certain positions, and the patient may also pass urine normally.
- **Timing of Leakage:**
    - _Post-surgical:_ Leakage following a direct surgical injury typically occurs from the first postoperative day.
    - _Obstetric:_ Symptoms of an obstetric (ischemic) fistula usually take 7–14 days to appear after delivery.
- **High Urethral Fistula:** Often presents with features mimicking stress incontinence.
- **Menouria (Youssef’s Syndrome):** Women with vesicocervical or vesicouterine fistulae may remain continent (holding urine at the uterine isthmus) but complain of cyclical hematuria at the time of menstruation, or intermittent leakage.
- **Associated Symptoms:** Pruritus vulvae due to continuous wetness, secondary amenorrhea (which resumes after repair), or features of complete perineal tear.

**CLINICAL SIGNS AND LOCAL EXAMINATION**

- **Vulvar Inspection:** Reveals an ammonia-smelling watery discharge and sodden, excoriated vulvar skin.
- **Speculum Examination:** A Sims' speculum introduced with the patient in Sims' position balloons the vagina and gives an excellent view of the anterior vaginal wall.
    - A _large fistula_ may show prolapsed bladder mucosa.
    - A _tiny fistula_ is evidenced by a puckered area of vaginal mucosa.

**CONFIRMATION OF DIAGNOSIS (CLINICAL TESTS)** When the fistula is tiny or the diagnosis is confusing (e.g., differentiating from stress incontinence), the following tests are high-yield:

- **Knee-Chest Position Test:** When the patient is placed in the knee-chest position and asked to cough, bubbles of air may be seen escaping through a tiny fistula.
- **Metal Catheter Test:** A metal catheter passed through the external urethral meatus into the bladder that comes out through the fistula confirms the VVF and concurrently ensures the patency of the urethra.
- **Methylene Blue Dye Test:** The anterior vaginal wall is swabbed dry. Methylene blue solution is introduced into the bladder via a catheter. The dye will be seen coming out through the fistulous opening into the vagina.
- **Three-Swab (Tampon) Test:** This is the gold standard clinical test to confirm and differentiate between Vesicovaginal (VVF), Ureterovaginal, and Urethrovaginal fistulas.
    - _Procedure:_ Three cotton swabs are placed in the vagina—one at the vault (upper), one at the middle, and one just above the introitus (lower). Methylene blue or indigo carmine is instilled into the bladder. The patient walks for 5 minutes and then lies down for swab inspection.
    - _Interpretation:_
        - **Ureterovaginal Fistula:** Upper-most swab is soaked with urine but _unstained_ with dye. The lower two swabs remain dry.
        - **Vesicovaginal Fistula:** Upper and lower swabs remain dry, but the _middle swab is stained_ with dye.
        - **Urethrovaginal Fistula:** Upper two swabs remain dry, but the _lower swab is stained_ with dye.

**SPECIFIC DIAGNOSIS OF URETEROVAGINAL FISTULA**

- **Intravenous Indigo Carmine Test:** If the three-swab test leaves the vaginal urine unstained (confirming urine is bypassing the bladder), indigo carmine is injected intravenously. The urine in the vagina turning blue within 4–5 minutes establishes the diagnosis of a ureterovaginal fistula.
- **Cystoscopy:** Reveals no spurt of urine from the affected ureteric orifice. Passing a ureteric catheter under cystoscopic guidance will meet an obstruction at the site of the injury.

**IMAGING AND SPECIAL INVESTIGATIONS**

- **Examination Under Anesthesia (EUA):** Helpful for identifying small fistulae and allowing exploration with a metallic probe.
- **Intravenous Urography (IVU) or Contrast-Enhanced CT (CECT):** Crucial for diagnosing associated ureterovaginal fistulas (present in 10% of VVF cases) and confirming the exact side and site of the ureteric injury or extravasation.
- **Endoscopy (Cystourethroscopy):** Performed in selective cases to define the exact level, number, and location of the fistula, as well as its precise relationship to the ureteric orifices and the bladder neck.
- **Retrograde Pyelography:** Used to diagnose the exact site of a ureterovaginal fistula.
- **Voiding Cystourethrography (VCUG):** Can detect leakage of contrast into the vagina.
- **Hysterosalpingography (Lateral View):** Used specifically for diagnosing vesicouterine fistulas (Youssef’s syndrome).
- **USG and MRI:** Non-invasive evaluation tools for complex fistulae involving the ureters or intestines, and for identifying retroperitoneal urinomas or hydronephrosis.

13. Decubitus ulcer.
**DECUBITUS ULCER**

**DEFINITION** A decubitus ulcer is a trophic ulcer that is characteristically found at the dependent part of a prolapsed genital mass lying outside the vaginal introitus.

**PATHOGENESIS** The development of a decubitus ulcer is primarily due to vascular compromise and chronic exposure.

- **Vascular Factor:** There is diminished circulation to the prolapsed tissue due to the constriction of the prolapsed mass by the vaginal opening, combined with the narrowing of the uterine vessels caused by the stretching effect of the prolapse.
- **Sequence of Events:** Prolonged exposure of the vaginal mucosa to the air leads to initial surface keratinization $\rightarrow$ development of cracks $\rightarrow$ superimposed infection $\rightarrow$ sloughing $\rightarrow$ frank ulceration. This ultimately results in the complete denudation of the surface epithelium.

**CLINICAL FEATURES**

- **Symptoms:** Patients commonly present with excessive white or blood-stained vaginal discharge due to the presence of the decubitus ulcer or associated vaginitis.
- **Signs:** The ulcer is visibly detected on the dependent part of the prolapsed mass during inspection of the perineum, especially when the cervix or uterus remains outside the introitus.

**COMPLICATIONS** While it may mimic malignancy, the development of carcinoma on a pre-existing decubitus ulcer is a rare association.

**MANAGEMENT** The treatment aims at relieving edema, treating the infection, promoting epithelial healing, and ruling out neoplastic changes before definitive surgery for the prolapse is undertaken. The steps include:

1. **Exclusion of Malignancy:** Cervical cytology must be done to exclude malignancy. If there is any clinical suspicion, colposcopy and a directed biopsy are performed.
2. **Manual Reduction:** The prolapsed mass should be manually reduced into the vagina.
3. **Local Packing:** A vaginal pack utilizing a roller bandage soaked with an antiseptic lotion containing glycerin and acriflavine is applied. The glycerin acts as a hygroscopic agent to reduce edema and congestion, while acriflavine treats the local infection.
4. **Hormonal Therapy:** In postmenopausal women, the application of local estrogen cream is highly beneficial to promote maturation and healing of the vaginal epithelium.


14.HPV vaccines.
**HPV VACCINES**

**INTRODUCTION** Human Papillomavirus (HPV) vaccines are highly immunogenic, prophylactic vaccines developed from the capsid coat of the virus. They are utilized for the primary prevention of cervical cancer, vulvar cancer, vaginal cancer, and condyloma acuminata (genital warts). The vaccines are purely prophylactic and do not have therapeutic effects on pre-existing HPV infections or dysplastic lesions.

**TYPES OF VACCINES** Currently, three main types of HPV vaccines are available:

- **Bivalent Vaccine (Cervarix):** Protects against the high-risk oncogenic HPV types 16 and 18.
- **Quadrivalent Vaccine (Gardasil):** Protects against HPV types 6, 11, 16, and 18.
- **Nonavalent Vaccine (Gardasil-9):** Offers the broadest protection against nine HPV types: 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58.

**MECHANISM OF ACTION** The vaccines induce strong local and humoral immunity by stimulating the production of neutralizing antibodies (IgG and IgA). These antibodies work locally at the cervix by preventing the attachment and entry of the HPV virus into the cervical squamous epithelium. While the immune defense is primarily type-specific, both bivalent and quadrivalent vaccines offer some cross-protection against other high-risk HPV types, such as 31, 33, and 45.

**EFFICACY**

- HPV vaccines are highly effective, capable of preventing 90–100% of cervical cancers.
- The nonavalent vaccine (Gardasil-9) can prevent Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia (CIN II) lesions by up to 96%.
- They are also effective in preventing about 90% of condyloma acuminata.
- The protective effect of the vaccines lasts for at least 7.5 years.

**TARGET POPULATION AND TIMING**

- The ideal target population is young girls and women aged 9 to 26 years.
- The clinical impact of the vaccine is greatest when it is administered prior to the onset of sexual activity (typically targeted at adolescent girls aged 9–13 or 12–18 years) so that immunity is established before any exposure to the virus.

**DOSAGE AND ADMINISTRATION (WHO SCHEDULE)** The vaccine is administered as an intramuscular (IM) injection over the deltoid muscle.

- **Females < 15 years of age:** A 2-dose schedule is recommended, given at 0 and 6 months.
- **Females $\ge$ 15 years of age:** A 3-dose schedule is recommended, given at 0, 1–2, and 6 months.
- **Immunocompromised women:** Should receive the 3-dose schedule regardless of age.

**SAFETY AND SPECIAL SITUATIONS**

- **General Safety:** The vaccines are extremely safe and generally well-tolerated by patients.
- **Pregnancy:** HPV vaccination is strictly not recommended during pregnancy.
- **Lactation:** The vaccine is completely safe to be administered to lactating mothers.

**POST-VACCINATION SURVEILLANCE** A critical clinical point is that routine cervical cytology screening (Pap smear) must be continued even after a woman is fully vaccinated. This is mandatory because the currently available vaccines are type-specific and do not offer absolute protection against all existing oncogenic strains of HPV that can cause cervical cancer.


15. Transformation zone.

**TRANSFORMATION ZONE**

**DEFINITION** The transformation zone (TZ) is the area of metaplastic squamous epithelium situated between the original squamocolumnar junction (OSCJ) and the active squamocolumnar junction (ASCJ) of the cervix. The OSCJ is the initial meeting point between the stratified squamous epithelium of the vagina/ectocervix and the columnar epithelium of the endocervical canal.

**ANATOMY AND DYNAMIC NATURE**

- **Width and Histology:** The transitional zone may vary from 1 to 10 mm in width and presents with variable histological features. It is composed of endocervical stroma and glands covered by squamous epithelium.
- **Dynamic Shift:** The squamocolumnar junction is not static; it is a dynamic point that moves up and down the cervix in response to ovarian hormones (estrogen) and environmental influences throughout different phases of life.
    - _Childhood:_ Before puberty, the ectocervix is entirely covered with mature squamous epithelium.
    - _Reproductive Age & Pregnancy:_ Following menarche and particularly during pregnancy, heightened estrogenic activity exposes the columnar epithelium onto the ectocervix (eversion), moving the new SCJ to or slightly outside the external os.
    - _Postmenopause:_ Due to estrogen deficiency, the SCJ and the transformation zone recede and are drawn high up into the endocervical canal.

**PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF METAPLASIA** The process of replacing the endocervical columnar epithelium with squamous epithelium in the transformation zone occurs via two mechanisms:

1. **Squamous Metaplasia:** Transformation of the subcolumnar reserve cells into squamous cells.
2. **Squamous Epidermalization:** The direct ingrowth of mature squamous epithelium from the ectocervix extending under the columnar epithelium.

- **Trigger Mechanism:** Under the influence of estrogen, the vaginal epithelium accumulates glycogen. Vaginal lactobacilli act on this glycogen to produce lactic acid, resulting in a low (acidic) vaginal pH. This acidic environment acts as an important physiological trigger for the metaplastic process.

**CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE (HIGH YIELD)**

- **Site of Carcinogenesis:** Cervical neoplasia (Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia - CIN, and invasive carcinoma) almost always originates from the transformation zone. The zone is constantly irritated by hormones, infection, and trauma, which increases the likelihood of severe dysplasia and malignant transformation.
- **Vulnerability to HPV:** The cervical reserve cells and the immature metaplastic cells in the TZ are highly unstable and extremely vulnerable to the oncogenic effects of the Human Papillomavirus (HPV) and other carcinogens.
- **Age Susceptibility:** Metaplastic change is most active during adolescence and pregnancy. Consequently, early age of sexual activity exposes these immature cells to HPV, making it a critical high-risk factor for the development of cervical cancer.
- **Pap Smear Screening:** For exfoliative cytology (Pap smear) to be diagnostically accurate, the sample must contain cells from the entire transformation zone, the SCJ, and the endocervix. This is optimally achieved using an Ayre’s spatula along with an endocervical brush.
- **Colposcopy:** During colposcopic evaluation, the examination is only deemed "satisfactory" if the entire transformation zone and the original SCJ are fully visualized. If the TZ extends into the endocervical canal and cannot be completely seen (unsatisfactory colposcopy), an endocervical curettage (ECC) becomes mandatory.
- **Surgical Management:** Excisional treatment modalities for high-grade CIN, such as Large Loop Excision of the Transformation Zone (LLETZ) or Loop Electrosurgical Excision Procedure (LEEP), are specifically designed to entirely remove the TZ along with a portion of the endocervical canal for comprehensive histopathological evaluation.

16. MRI in gynecology.

**MAGNETIC RESONANCE IMAGING (MRI) IN GYNECOLOGY**

**BASIC PRINCIPLES AND TECHNIQUE**

- **Mechanism:** MRI utilizes a combination of radiowaves (nonionizing radiation) and powerful magnetic fields to create high-resolution images. Biologic tissue nuclei (protons) with magnetic properties are temporarily altered by a strong magnet. When a pulse of radiowaves is applied, resonance occurs, and the tissues absorb energy. Upon turning off the radiofrequency, the protons emit signals that are detected by a receiver coil to construct detailed images.
- **Signal Intensity:** The strength of the emitted signal varies directly with the proton density. For example, on T1-weighted images, urine in the bladder appears dark (low signal intensity), whereas on T2-weighted images, the same urine appears bright (high signal intensity).
- **Imaging Planes:** Standard techniques involve acquiring T1- and T2-weighted sequences in axial, sagittal, and coronal planes without the need to move the patient.
- **Resolution:** MRI provides exceptional soft-tissue contrast with a resolution of 0.5–1 mm in gynecology.

**CLINICAL INDICATIONS AND USES IN GYNECOLOGY**

**1. Evaluation of Uterine Pathologies (Benign)**

- **Normal Anatomy:** MRI clearly differentiates the zones of the uterus: the high-signal inner endometrium, the very-low-signal junctional zone (normally up to 8 mm thick), and the intermediate-signal myometrium.
- **Fibroids (Leiomyomas):** MRI is superior to ultrasonography (USG) for diagnosing myomas. It accurately determines the size, number, and location (especially submucous extension), and identifies secondary degenerative changes or rare sarcomatous transformations.
- **Adenomyosis:** MRI is the most accurate imaging modality to differentiate adenomyosis from uterine fibroids. A junctional zone thickness of >12 mm is strongly diagnostic of adenomyosis.
- **Therapeutic adjunct:** MR-guided high-intensity focused ultrasound (MRgHIFU) is a safe and minimally invasive procedure used for the thermal ablation of leiomyomas.

**2. Congenital Müllerian Anomalies**

- MRI is the gold standard for diagnosing and classifying Müllerian duct anomalies.
- It accurately differentiates a septate uterus from a bicornuate uterus using T2-weighted coronal images.
- It identifies conditions like uterine agenesis, cervicovaginal agenesis, and uterus didelphys.
- It can detect functioning endometrial tissue within a noncommunicating rudimentary horn, which can cause hematometra and cyclic pain.

**3. Endometriosis**

- MRI is a highly sensitive (91–95%) and noninvasive tool for the diagnosis of deep infiltrating endometriosis (DIE) and deep-seated ovarian endometriomas.
- Endometriomas show a characteristic hyperintensity on T1-weighted images and hypointensity on T2-weighted images.

**4. Gynecologic Oncology**

- **Carcinoma Cervix:** MRI is superior to CT for assessing tumor size, the depth of stromal invasion, and the extension of the tumor to the parametrium, vagina, bladder, and rectum. It is a crucial preoperative step before performing fertility-sparing surgery (radical trachelectomy) to ensure the cancer margin is at least 1 cm below the internal os. MRI is also highly reliable in distinguishing post-treatment pelvic fibrosis from tumor recurrence.
- **Endometrial Carcinoma:** MRI excellently demonstrates the depth of myometrial invasion, which is a critical prognostic factor. It is also helpful in distinguishing primary endometrial cancer with cervical extension from primary endocervical adenocarcinoma.
- **Ovarian Carcinoma:** MRI offers superior contrast resolution to differentiate benign from malignant ovarian masses. It is valuable for detecting peritoneal deposits, retroperitoneal lymph node metastases, and tumor extension to the omentum, bowel, and vessels.

**5. Urogynecology and Pelvic Floor Disorders**

- MRI provides detailed soft-tissue imaging of the female urethra, levator ani muscles, and the genital hiatus.
- MR defecography is highly useful for women presenting with pelvic organ prolapse (POP), urinary incontinence, and defecatory dysfunction.

**6. Breast Imaging**

- MRI has a high sensitivity (77%) for detecting breast cancer compared to mammography (40%) but suffers from low specificity (37–97%).
- It is used as an adjunct to mammography and clinical breast examination, particularly for evaluating complex solid masses or guiding deep-seated biopsies.

**ADVANTAGES OF MRI**

- **Superior Tissue Resolution:** Offers the best soft-tissue contrast among all imaging modalities, multiplanar imaging capabilities, and detailed anatomical mapping.
- **No Radiation:** It is devoid of any ionizing radiation, making it biologically safe.
- **Safety in Pregnancy:** MRI is safe in pregnancy regardless of the trimester and is not mutagenic. It is highly useful in obstetrics to assess morbidly adherent placenta (placenta accreta/increta/percreta), posterior placenta previa, and complex fetal neuroanatomy.

**LIMITATIONS AND DISADVANTAGES**

- **Cost and Availability:** MRI is expensive (twice the cost of a CT scan) and is not as widely available.
- **Time-Consuming:** The procedure takes a longer time to complete compared to ultrasound or CT.
- **Microscopic Disease:** While excellent for macroscopic tumors, MRI cannot detect microscopic malignant disease (micrometastases) or fully replace histological staging.
- **Artifacts:** Image quality can be degraded by respiratory movements (e.g., in breast MRI).
- **Psychological Distress:** Patient claustrophobia (feeling "trapped" in the machine) reduces patient acceptance.

**CONTRAINDICATIONS AND SAFETY**

- **Absolute Contraindications:** Presence of mechanically, electrically, or magnetically activated implants. This includes cardiac pacemakers, cardiac defibrillators, cochlear implants, and electronic infusion pumps.
- **Safe Implants:** Intrauterine devices (e.g., Mirena), Essure, Filshie clips, contraceptive implants (Implanon), and silicone breast implants are safe during MRI.
- **Medical Precautions:** Caution is advised in epileptic patients or those with a recent myocardial infarction due to the risk of electroconvulsions or atrial fibrillation triggered by the rapidly changing magnetic field.
- **Contrast Agents:** Intravenous Gadolinium contrast is contraindicated in pregnancy because it crosses the placenta, and it should only be used if absolutely essential.


17. Complication of vaginal hysterectomy.
**COMPLICATIONS OF VAGINAL HYSTERECTOMY**

Vaginal hysterectomy, often performed along with pelvic floor repair (PFR), carries risks of surgical and anatomical complications. These are broadly classified into intraoperative, immediate postoperative, and late (remote) complications.

**1. INTRAOPERATIVE COMPLICATIONS**

- **Hemorrhage:** Bleeding can be brisk during the procedure, potentially leading to a hypovolemic state requiring infusion and blood transfusion.
- **Visceral Trauma:** The bladder or rectum may be inadvertently injured during dissection. If these injuries are not effectively repaired at the time of surgery, they can lead to fistula formation later.

**2. POSTOPERATIVE COMPLICATIONS (IMMEDIATE & EARLY)**

- **Hemorrhage:**
    - _Primary Hemorrhage:_ Occurs within the first 24 hours. It is usually caused by imperfect hemostasis at the time of operation or the slipping of a ligature.
    - _Secondary Hemorrhage:_ Usually occurs between the 5th and 10th postoperative day, but may occur up to the 3rd week. It is primarily due to sloughing secondary to wound sepsis.
- **Urinary Complications:**
    - _Retention of Urine:_ This is a very common complication. It results from spasm, edema, and tenderness of the pubococcygeus muscle and urethral wall, reflex spasm from the wound, or the pressure effect of a tight postoperative vaginal pack.
    - _Urinary Tract Infection:_ Often leading to cystitis.
- **Infection and Sepsis:**
    - _Vault Cellulitis and Pelvic Abscess:_ The vaginal cuff may develop an infected hematoma progressing to cellulitis and pelvic abscess. This typically presents with fever, lower abdominal pain, and offensive purulent discharge.
    - _Wound Sepsis:_ Infection of the vaginal or perineal wounds, which may rarely lead to complete disruption of the perineal wound.
- **Thromboembolic Events:** Patients are at risk of developing thrombophlebitis and pulmonary embolism.

**3. LATE (REMOTE) COMPLICATIONS**

- **Vault Prolapse:** Secondary prolapse of the vaginal vault is a distinct late complication following the removal of the uterus.
- **Fistula Formation:**
    - _Vesicovaginal Fistula (VVF):_ Develops as a sequela to unrecognized or improperly repaired bladder injury.
    - _Rectovaginal Fistula (RVF):_ Follows rectal injury.
- **Dyspareunia:** May occur due to vaginal scarring, narrowing, or a tender perineal/vaginal wound.


18. Classification of uterovaginal prolapse.

**CLASSIFICATION OF UTEROVAGINAL PROLAPSE**

Genital prolapse is broadly grouped into vaginal prolapse and uterine prolapse. While vaginal prolapse can occur independently, uterine prolapse is usually associated with variable degrees of vaginal descent.

**1. ANATOMICAL / CLINICAL TYPES OF PELVIC ORGAN PROLAPSE** **A. Vaginal Prolapse**

- **Anterior Wall:**
    - _Cystocele:_ Formed by laxity and descent of the upper two-thirds of the anterior vaginal wall, leading to herniation of the bladder.
    - _Urethrocele:_ Laxity of the lower-third of the anterior vaginal wall with herniation of the urethra.
    - _Cystourethrocele:_ Combined occurrence of cystocele and urethrocele.
- **Posterior Wall:**
    - _Rectocele:_ Laxity of the middle-third of the posterior vaginal wall and the adjacent rectovaginal septum, resulting in herniation of the rectum.
    - _Relaxed perineum:_ Torn perineal body producing a gaping introitus with a bulge of the lower part of the posterior vaginal wall.
- **Vault Prolapse:**
    - _Enterocele:_ Laxity of the upper-third of the posterior vaginal wall resulting in herniation of the pouch of Douglas, which may contain omentum or small bowel.
    - _Secondary vault prolapse:_ Occurs following a vaginal or abdominal hysterectomy.

**B. Uterine Prolapse**

- _Uterovaginal Prolapse:_ Prolapse of the uterus, cervix, and upper vagina. Cystocele usually occurs first, followed by a traction effect on the cervix.
- _Congenital (Nulliparous) Prolapse:_ The uterus herniates down along with an inverted upper vagina. There is usually no cystocele.
- _Complex Prolapse:_ Prolapse associated with other specific defects, such as urinary or fecal incontinence.

---

**2. CLINICAL DEGREES OF UTERINE PROLAPSE** Based on the descent of the uterus relative to the introitus:

- **Normal:** The external os lies at the level of the ischial spines; no prolapse.
- **First Degree:** The uterus descends down from its normal anatomical position, but the external os still remains above the vaginal introitus.
- **Second Degree:** The external os protrudes outside the vaginal introitus, but the uterine body still remains inside the vagina.
- **Third Degree (Procidentia / Complete Prolapse):** The uterine cervix, body, and fundus descend to lie outside the introitus. Procidentia involves prolapse of the uterus with eversion of the entire vagina.

---

**3. BADEN-WALKER HALFWAY SYSTEM** Evaluates the descent of the anterior vaginal wall, posterior vaginal wall, or apical prolapse relative to the hymen:

- **Grade 0:** Normal position for each respective site.
- **Grade 1:** Descent halfway to the hymen.
- **Grade 2:** Descent to the hymen.
- **Grade 3:** Descent halfway past the hymen.
- **Grade 4:** Maximum possible descent for each site.

---

**4. PELVIC ORGAN PROLAPSE QUANTIFICATION (POP-Q) SYSTEM** Recommended by the International Continence Society (ICS), this is an objective, site-specific, and anatomical system that uses the plane of the hymen as the fixed zero level.

- **Stage 0:** No prolapse is demonstrated.
- **Stage I:** The most distal portion of the prolapse is >1 cm above the level of the hymen.
- **Stage II:** The most distal portion of the prolapse is $\le$1 cm proximal or distal to the plane of the hymen.
- **Stage III:** The most distal portion of the prolapse is >1 cm below the plane of the hymen but protrudes no farther than 2 cm less than the total vaginal length (TVL).
- **Stage IV:** Essentially complete eversion of the total length of the lower genital tract.


19. Syndromic approach in the management of STI/RTI.
**SYNDROMIC MANAGEMENT OF STI/RTI (WHO – 1991)**

**PRINCIPLE**

- Treatment of Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs) should be initiated at the patient’s first visit to a clinic.
- Syndromic management is based on epidemiological studies worldwide; its accuracy is considered similar to laboratory-assisted diagnosis.
- At the first visit, the couple is counseled regarding the importance of condom use and the prevention of STIs.

**METHODOLOGY AND PROTOCOL** Healthcare providers in developing countries are trained to follow standardized protocols (flowcharts) for treatment. The management includes:

- **Detailed History:** Medical history including a detailed sexual history.
- **Clinical Examination:** Physical and pelvic examinations (speculum and bimanual) are performed to detect vaginal discharge, abdominal pain, cervical motion tenderness, and adnexal tenderness, as well as to exclude pregnancy and cervical cancer.
- **Voluntary Counseling and Testing (VCT):** Promoted for HIV.
- **Counseling & Education:** Patient education regarding STI preventive issues.
- **Partner Management & Follow-up:** Promotion of condom use, referring all patients to Integrated Counseling and Testing Centers (ICTC), treating the partner, advising follow-up after 7 days, and advising immunization against Hepatitis B.

**COMMON SYNDROMES IDENTIFIED**

- **Vaginal Discharge:** Vaginitis (Trichomoniasis, Bacterial vaginosis, Candidiasis).
- **Cervicitis:** Chlamydia, Gonorrhea.
- **Lower Abdominal Pain:** Chlamydia, Gonorrhea, other pyogenic organisms.
- **Genital Ulcers:** Genital herpes, Chancroid, Syphilis.

**SYNDROMIC CASE MANAGEMENT (NACO KITS)** Specific color-coded kits are utilized for the syndromic management of STIs/RTIs:

- **KIT 1 (Grey) - Urethral/Cervical Discharge or Painful Scrotal Swelling:**
    - _Drugs:_ Tab Azithromycin 1 gm OD stat + Tab Cefixime 400 mg OD stat.
    - _Partner Management:_ Treat male partners.
- **KIT 2 (Green) - Vaginal Discharge:**
    - _Drugs:_ Tab Secnidazole 2 gm OD stat + Cap Fluconazole 150 mg OD stat.
    - _Partner Management:_ Treat partners only when symptomatic.
- **KIT 5 (Red) - Genital Ulcer (Herpetic):**
    - _Drugs:_ Tab Acyclovir 400 mg TDS for 7 days.
- **KIT 6 (Yellow) - Lower Abdominal Pain (PID):**
    - _Drugs:_ Tab Cefixime 400 mg OD stat + Tab Metronidazole 400 mg BD for 14 days + Tab Doxycycline 100 mg BD for 14 days.

**ADVANTAGES OF SYNDROMIC MANAGEMENT**

- Provides an opportunity for simultaneous counseling and treatment.
- Avoids delay in treatment in settings where laboratory facilities are limited.
- Prevents loss to patient follow-up, which is common when referral systems are poorly structured.
- Reduces the continued transmission of infection.
- Utilizes standardized protocols (flowcharts) allowing for better diagnosis, treatment, and disease surveillance.
- It is a simple, inexpensive, cost-effective, and rapid method of management.
- Results in a high cure rate once treated appropriately.
- Patient education is integrated as standard care.

**LIMITATIONS OF SYNDROMIC MANAGEMENT**

- Poor prediction accuracy because it relies on symptoms rather than definitive laboratory investigations.
- Leads to over-diagnosis and over-treatment.
- Not useful for asymptomatic patients.
- The syndromic approach for vaginal discharge is a poor predictor for specific cervical infections like Chlamydia and Gonorrhea.
- Results in over-treatment of women's partners who may not actually harbor an STI.



20. Complication of ovarian cysts.
**COMPLICATIONS OF OVARIAN CYSTS (TUMORS)**

The complications of benign ovarian cysts can manifest acutely or develop chronically. The major complications include torsion of the pedicle, intracystic hemorrhage, infection, rupture, pseudomyxoma peritonei, and malignant transformation.

**1. TORSION OF THE PEDICLE (AXIAL ROTATION)** This is the most common complication of benign cystic ovarian tumors, occurring in about 10–15% of cases.

- **Tumor Characteristics:** It is particularly common in tumors having a moderate size, round contour, free mobility, and a long pedicle. It is highly associated with moderate-weight cysts, such as dermoid cysts (due to high fat content) and simple serous cystadenomas.
- **Laterality:** Torsion of the right ovary is 2–3 times more common than the left, as the mobility of the left ovary is limited by the sigmoid colon.
- **Predisposing Factors:** Trauma, violent physical movements, intestinal peristalsis, pulsation of major pelvic vessels, and contractions of a pregnant uterus.
- **Pathophysiology & Clinical Features:** Partial axial rotation is usually followed by complete torsion. It results in venous congestion, arterial occlusion, and ultimately gangrenous changes. The patient classically presents with an acute hypogastric pain and a lump. Abdominal examination reveals a tense, cystic, and tender mass with restricted mobility arising from the pelvis.
- **Diagnosis & Management:** Doppler sonography may show the disruption of normal adnexal blood flow. Management involves laparotomy or laparoscopy with detorsion of the adnexa (if the ovary can be salvaged) and cystectomy, or ovariotomy if gangrenous changes have occurred.

**2. INTRACYSTIC HEMORRHAGE**

- **Incidence:** It is more common in serous cystadenomas, particularly the papillary varieties.
- **Causes:** Hemorrhage inside the cyst can occur secondary to venous congestion caused by axial torsion of the pedicle, or it may be due to malignant changes within the cyst.

**3. INFECTION**

- Infection of the cyst is common following torsion.
- The infecting microorganisms are usually derived from the adjacent intestines or the uterine tubes when these structures become adherent to the ovarian cyst.

**4. RUPTURE OF THE CYST**

- Rupture typically occurs in large and tense cysts where a part of the cyst wall has undergone degeneration.
- **Precipitating Factors:** It can follow an intracystic hemorrhage, direct trauma, or occur spontaneously in cysts with papillary projections or malignant changes.

**5. PSEUDOMYXOMA PERITONEI**

- **Pathology:** This is a condition characterized by mucinous ascites, often associated with a mucinous cystadenoma of the ovary, as well as mucocele of the appendix or gallbladder.
- **Mechanism:** Spontaneous leakage from a mucinous cyst can lead to the implantation of cells of low-grade malignancy onto the peritoneal surfaces. These implanted cells (mesothelium converted to high columnar secretory epithelium) continuously secrete mucin into the peritoneal cavity.
- **Prognosis:** The condition has a poor prognosis due to severe complications like inanition, recurrent infection, and intestinal obstruction, and it tends to recur even after the primary ovarian tumor is removed.

**6. MALIGNANCY (MALIGNANT TRANSFORMATION)**

- A clinically benign tumor may turn into a malignant one, though the malignancy rate varies based on the type of cyst.
- **Serous Cystadenoma:** The malignant potential is maximum in serous cystadenomas, especially the papillary variety, which can transform into adenocarcinoma.
- **Dermoid Cyst:** The risk of malignancy is the least (about 1–2%), and when it occurs, it typically gives rise to squamous cell carcinoma.

**7. COMPLICATIONS DURING PREGNANCY AND LABOR** Ovarian cysts can cause specific mechanical and anatomical complications during pregnancy, labor, and the puerperium:

- **During Pregnancy:** Torsion of the pedicle is common, usually occurring between 8–10 weeks of pregnancy (when the tumor is pushed out of the pelvis) or in the early puerperium due to the lax abdominal wall. Intracystic hemorrhage and rupture are also increased. Furthermore, impaction of the tumor in the pelvis can lead to retention of urine.
- **During Labor:** The presence of a cyst can cause malpresentation, non-engagement of the fetal head at term, and obstructed labor if the tumor becomes impacted in the pelvis.


21. Hydrosalpinx.
**DEFINITION** Hydrosalpinx is the collection of mucus secretion into the fallopian tube, resulting in its distension. It is the end result of repeated attacks of mild endosalpingitis, or it may present as the end stage of a pyosalpinx when the pent-up pus becomes liquefied and the fluid turns clear.

**ETIOPATHOGENESIS**

- The organisms commonly responsible are pyogenic and sexually transmitted bacteria, including _Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Escherichia coli, Gonococcus_, and _Chlamydia trachomatis_.
- During the initial infection, the fimbriae become edematous and indrawn, and the serous surfaces adhere together to close the abdominal ostium.
- Concurrently, the uterine ostium gets closed by inflammatory congestion.
- The continued secretion from the tubal mucosa is pent up inside, distending the tubal lumen.

**PATHOLOGY & MACROSCOPIC APPEARANCE**

- The distension is more marked at the ampullary region compared to the more rigid isthmus.
- Because the mesosalpinx is fixed, the resultant marked distension causes the tube to curl, giving it a characteristic "retort shape".
- The tubal wall becomes smooth and shiny, containing clear fluid that is usually sterile.
- In some cases, the uterine ostium is not completely closed anatomically, which allows for the intermittent discharge of the fluid into the uterine cavity, a condition termed intermittent hydrosalpinx or hydrops tubae profluens.

**CLASSIFICATION OF DISTAL TUBAL DISEASE** Hydrosalpinx is classified based on tubal diameter and the extent of fimbrial/pelvic damage:

- **Mild:** Small hydrosalpinx <15 mm in diameter, inverted fimbriae, tubal patency is present, there are no significant peritubal/periovarian adhesions, and the tubal rugae pattern is preserved on Hysterosalpingography (HSG).
- **Moderate:** Hydrosalpinx 15–30 mm in diameter, fimbriae are not readily identified, periovarian or tubal adhesions are present without fixation, and the tubal rugae pattern is absent on HSG.
- **Severe:** Hydrosalpinx >30 mm in diameter, absence of fimbriae, dense pelvic and adnexal adhesions with fixation of the ovary and tube, and obliteration of the cul-de-sac.

**DIAGNOSIS AND INVESTIGATIONS**

- **Ultrasonography (TVS):** Hydrosalpinx appears as a sausage-shaped, complex, cystic, hypoechoic adnexal mass. It may show incomplete septa and multiple small hyperechoic mural nodules representing fibrotic endosalpingeal folds, giving a "beads on a string" appearance. A cross-section of the distended tube may give a characteristic "cogwheel sign". Color and power Doppler evaluate increased blood flow and hyperemia of the inflamed tubes.
- **Hysterosalpingography (HSG):** Reveals a markedly dilated tube with retention of dye and an absence of peritoneal spillage due to fimbrial block. However, if hydrosalpinges are known to be present beforehand, HSG is contraindicated due to the risk of flaring up the pelvic infection.
- **Laparoscopy:** Useful for the direct evaluation of tubal patency, block site, hydrosalpinx changes, peritubal adhesions, and fimbrial agglutination.

**COMPLICATIONS**

- Formation of a tubo-ovarian cyst.
- Torsion of the distended tube.
- Infection tracking from the gut.
- Spontaneous rupture of the hydrosalpinx.

**IMPACT ON FERTILITY**

- Hydrosalpinx drastically impairs fertility by causing distal tubal obstruction and by damaging the ciliary function of the endosalpinx.
- The presence of a hydrosalpinx reduces the pregnancy rates of In Vitro Fertilization (IVF) by about 50%.
- The hydrosalpinx fluid reduces endometrial receptivity, resulting in embryonic implantation failure.

**MANAGEMENT**

- **Surgery for Fertility Optimization:** Large hydrosalpinges causing distal tubal disease should be treated by salpingectomy.
- Bilateral salpingectomy must be performed prior to IVF, as this pretreatment improves the IVF pregnancy outcome by eliminating the toxic effects of the fluid on endometrial receptivity.
- IVF is considered the best treatment option for conception in any complicated tubal occlusive disease.
- Reconstructive procedures like salpingostomy (creating a neo-ostium in the completely occluded tube) may be performed, but the prospect of fertility remains very poor if the tubal cilia are irreparably damaged.

22. Corpus cancer syndrome.
**CORPUS CANCER SYNDROME**

**DEFINITION** Corpus cancer syndrome refers to a specific constellation of metabolic and clinical disorders that strongly predispose a woman to develop endometrial cancer (carcinoma of the uterine corpus).

**CLASSICAL TRIAD (COMPONENTS)** The syndrome typically encompasses the following three major conditions:

- **Obesity:** Specifically, a Body Mass Index (BMI) greater than 30. Obesity leads to high levels of free circulating estradiol because the levels of sex hormone-binding globulin are low, creating a state of prolonged, unopposed estrogen stimulation.
- **Diabetes Mellitus**.
- **Hypertension**.

**ASSOCIATED PATHOLOGIES** Women presenting with corpus cancer syndrome often have coexisting gynecological conditions driven by the same hyperestrogenic state:

- **Uterine Fibroids:** A fibroid uterus is associated in about 30% of these cases.
- **Endometrial Hyperplasia:** This is a well-documented precursor lesion that precedes the development of actual endometrial carcinoma in 25% of cases.

**CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE**

- **High-Risk Identification:** The presence of this syndrome categorizes a woman as being at "high-risk" for endometrial cancer.
- **Co-existing Risk Factors:** Patients with corpus cancer syndrome often share other high-risk factors for endometrial cancer, such as nulliparity and late menopause.
- **Clinical Vigilance:** Recognizing this syndrome is crucial in clinical practice. Any abnormal perimenopausal or postmenopausal bleeding in a patient with these features must be promptly and thoroughly investigated to rule out endometrial carcinoma.
