1. Discuss etiology, clinical features and different modalities of treatment of Fibroid uterus.
**INTRODUCTION** Leiomyomas (fibroids, myomas, or fibromyomas) are the most common benign solid tumors of the uterus in females, composed of smooth muscle and fibrous connective tissue. They are observed in at least 20% of women by age 30, with the highest prevalence between 35 and 45 years.

**ETIOLOGY AND PATHOGENESIS** The exact etiology is unclear, but they are monoclonal tumors arising from a neoplastic single smooth muscle cell (myocyte) of the myometrium. The following factors influence their development and growth:

- **Chromosomal Abnormalities:** Present in about 40% of cases, commonly involving rearrangements or deletions in chromosomes 7 or 12.
- **Polypeptide Growth Factors:** Epidermal growth factor (EGF), insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-b) stimulate growth either directly or via estrogen.
- **Hormonal Influence:** Fibroids are predominantly estrogen-dependent tumors. Myoma cells contain higher concentrations of estrogen and progesterone receptors, as well as higher levels of the aromatase enzyme, leading to high local estrogen levels. Fibroids are rare before menarche, enlarge rapidly during pregnancy, and undergo atrophy following menopause or with the use of GnRH agonists.
- **Risk Factors:** Increasing age, nulliparity, obesity (increased BMI), polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), hyperestrogenic states, high fat diet, family history, and being of African descent.
- **Protective Factors:** Late menarche, multiparity, menopause, combined oral contraceptive (COC) use, and smoking.

**CLINICAL FEATURES** **Patient Profile:** Commonly seen in nulliparous women or those with a long period of secondary infertility, typically between 35 and 45 years of age.

**Symptoms:**

- **Asymptomatic:** The majority (75%) of fibroids remain completely asymptomatic and are discovered incidentally.
- **Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (AUB) (30%):** Menorrhagia is the classic and most common symptom, typically conspicuous in submucous or interstitial fibroids. It occurs due to an increase in the endometrial surface area, congestion of venous plexuses, associated endometrial hyperplasia, and an imbalance of prostanoids. Metrorrhagia (irregular bleeding) can occur if a submucous fibroid or polyp ulcerates.
- **Dysmenorrhea:** May be congestive (due to associated endometriosis or pelvic congestion) or spasmodic (due to uterine contractions attempting to expel a submucous polyp).
- **Infertility (30%):** Caused by elongation/distortion of the uterine cavity impairing sperm transport, defective nidation/implantation, or cornual block from the tumor's position.
- **Pregnancy Complications:** Associated with a higher risk of recurrent miscarriage, preterm labor, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), red degeneration, torsion, labor dystocia, and postpartum hemorrhage.
- **Pain:** Generally painless. Pain arises due to complications like torsion of a subserous pedunculated fibroid, degeneration (e.g., red degeneration), extrusion of a polyp, or associated pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)/endometriosis.
- **Pressure Symptoms:** A posterior fibroid can cause constipation or dysuria, while a broad ligament fibroid can compress the ureter leading to hydroureter and hydronephrosis. Heaviness or a lump in the lower abdomen may be the only complaint.

**Signs:**

- **General Examination:** Varying degrees of pallor corresponding to menstrual blood loss.
- **Abdominal Examination:** A firm-to-hard, nodular pelvic mass dull on percussion. Mobility is restricted from above downwards but can be moved side to side.
- **Bimanual Pelvic Examination:** The uterus is irregularly enlarged and indistinguishable from the swelling. A distinct groove between the uterus and mass is absent, and the cervix moves with the movement of the tumor (except in subserous pedunculated fibroids).

**MODALITIES OF TREATMENT**

**1. Expectant Management (Observation)**

- **Indications:** Asymptomatic fibroids sized <12 weeks of pregnancy, when the diagnosis is certain, and regular follow-up is possible.
- **Protocol:** Periodic clinical examination every 6 months and an annual ultrasound.

**2. Medical Management** Aims to minimize blood loss, correct anemia before surgery, reduce tumor size/vascularity to facilitate surgery, or act as an alternative to surgery in perimenopausal or high-risk women.

- **Nonhormonal Therapy:** Prostaglandin synthetase inhibitors (NSAIDs like mefenamic acid) to relieve pain, and antifibrinolytic agents (Tranexamic acid) to reduce heavy menstrual bleeding.
- **Hormonal Therapy:**
    - _Antiprogesterones (Mifepristone/RU486):_ A daily dose of 5-10 mg reduces tumor size by up to 50% and significantly decreases bleeding.
    - _Selective Progesterone Receptor Modulators (SPRMs):_ Ulipristal acetate significantly reduces blood loss (90%) and myoma size (53%) before surgery without causing endometrial hyperplasia.
    - _Levonorgestrel-Intrauterine System (LNG-IUS):_ Reduces blood loss and uterine size, provided the cavity is not severely distorted and uterine size is <12 weeks.
    - _Antigonadotropins (Danazol):_ Given for 3-6 months, it induces amenorrhea and shrinks the tumor via antigonadotropic and androgenic actions.
    - _GnRH Agonists (Goserelin, Leuprorelin):_ Given for 3 months to cause sustained pituitary down-regulation, inducing hypoestrogenism and marked tumor shrinkage. Add-back therapy may be required for vasomotor symptoms.
    - _Aromatase Inhibitors (Letrozole):_ Decreases local estrogen synthesis in myomas, reducing volume (10–70%) and bleeding.

**3. Surgical Management**

- **Myomectomy:** Enucleation of the myomata preserving the uterus. Indicated in young women desirous of pregnancy, recurrent pregnancy loss, or symptomatic fibroids >12 weeks size. Pretreatment with GnRH analogs reduces size and vascularity, minimizing intraoperative blood loss.
    - _Laparotomy (Open Myomectomy):_ A single midline anterior uterine incision is preferred to avoid tubal injury and adhesions.
    - _Endoscopic Surgery:_ Laparoscopy is suited for subserous and intramural fibroids. Hysteroscopic resection is the gold standard for submucous fibroids and polyps <3-4 cm in diameter.
    - _Vaginal Myomectomy:_ For pedunculated submucous myomas protruding through the cervix.
- **Hysterectomy:** The definitive operation of choice for symptomatic women >40 years of age who have completed their family. Can be done via abdominal, vaginal, laparoscopic, or robotic routes. Vaginal hysterectomy is ideal for 10-12 week sized fibroids associated with uterine prolapse.

**4. Interventional Radiology and Minimally Invasive Modalities**

- **Uterine Artery Embolization (UAE):** Percutaneous femoral catheterization is used to inject polyvinyl alcohol particles into the uterine arteries, causing avascular necrosis and shrinkage of the fibroids. Results in 60% size reduction and 80-90% improvement in menorrhagia.
- **MR-Guided Focused Ultrasound (MRgFUS):** High-energy focused ultrasound waves induce targeted coagulative necrosis and localized thermal ablation of the fibroid.
- **Myolysis:** Laparoscopic destruction of fibroids (<10 cm) using high-frequency ultrasound, laser, cryotherapy, or bipolar diathermy.
- **Laparoscopic Uterine Artery Occlusion (LUAO):** Devascularizes myomas by surgically sealing the uterine arteries near their origin.

2. Enumerate the causes of Uterovaginal prolapse. Discuss the management of Uterovaginal prolapse in 30 year old multi parous woman.
**CAUSES OF UTEROVAGINAL PROLAPSE**

Pelvic organ prolapse occurs due to the weakness of the anatomical structures supporting the pelvic organs. The etiology is multifactorial and is broadly classified into predisposing and aggravating factors.

**I. Predisposing Factors** **A. Acquired Factors (Most Common):** Vaginal delivery trauma is the single most important acquired factor causing injury (tear or break) to the ligaments, endopelvic fascia, levator ani muscles, perineal body, and pudendal nerve.

- **Overstretching of Mackenrodt’s and uterosacral ligaments due to:**
    - Premature bearing down efforts prior to full cervical dilatation.
    - Prolonged second stage of labor.
    - Forceful traction during instrumental delivery (forceps or ventouse).
    - Downward fundal pressure applied in an attempt to deliver the placenta.
    - Precipitate labor.
- **Perineal and Fascial Damage:** Overstretching of the perineum, breaks in the endopelvic fascial sheath, and imperfect repair of perineal injuries.
- **Subinvolution of supporting structures:** Seen in asthenic/ill-nourished women, following repeated childbirths at frequent intervals, or due to early resumption of strenuous activities before tissues regain their tone postpartum.

**B. Congenital/Genetic Factors:**

- Inherent genetic weakness of the supporting connective tissues (decreased ratio of type I collagen).
- Connective tissue disorders like Marfan syndrome or Ehlers-Danlos syndrome.
- Occult spina bifida and associated neurological abnormalities.

**II. Aggravating Factors** These factors exert their effect on already weakened pelvic supports.

- **Increased intra-abdominal pressure:** Due to chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), chronic cough, chronic constipation, or heavy weight lifting (occupation).
- **Increased uterine weight:** Conditions such as uterine fibroids or myohyperplasia.
- **General factors:** Multiparity, obesity, smoking, asthenia, and undernutrition.
- **Anatomical stress:** Gravitational stress due to human bipedal posture and anterior inclination of the pelvis directing the force anteriorly.
- **Postmenopausal atrophy:** Estrogen deficiency leading to poor collagen tissue repair and atrophy (though not applicable to a young patient).

**MANAGEMENT OF UTEROVAGINAL PROLAPSE IN A 30-YEAR-OLD MULTIPAROUS WOMAN**

In a 30-year-old multiparous woman, the primary goal of management is to relieve symptoms, restore pelvic anatomy, and crucially, **preserve the uterus and reproductive function** if desired.

**I. Preventive & General Management**

- **Lifestyle modifications:** Avoidance of strenuous activities, heavy weight lifting, and proper treatment of chronic cough or constipation to prevent increased intra-abdominal pressure.
- **Spacing pregnancies:** Contraceptive practice to avoid future pregnancies too soon.
- **Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT):** Kegel exercises to actively strengthen the levator ani and pelvic floor muscles.

**II. Conservative Management** Indicated if the prolapse is of a mild degree, if she desires another pregnancy soon, or wishes to delay surgery.

- **Pessary Treatment:** Use of a vaginal pessary (e.g., Hodge-Smith or ring pessary) acts by stretching the hiatus urogenitalis and supporting the structures, preventing descent. It relieves symptoms but does not permanently cure the prolapse.

**III. Surgical Management** Surgery is the definitive treatment for symptomatic prolapse when conservative measures fail or are not preferred. For a 30-year-old woman, **restorative surgery (preservation of the uterus)** is the standard of care.

**1. Fothergill’s or Manchester Operation:** This is the classic and preferred operation to correct uterine descent associated with cystocele and rectocele while preserving the uterus.

- **Component steps include:**
    - Dilatation and Curettage (D&C).
    - Amputation of the cervix. _Note: In a young woman where future reproduction is desired, a strict **low amputation** of the cervix must be performed to avoid subsequent cervical incompetence, infertility, or cervical dystocia in future labor_.
    - Plication of Mackenrodt’s (cardinal) ligaments in front of the cervix to elevate the uterus.
    - Pelvic Floor Repair (PFR): Anterior colporrhaphy (to repair cystocele) and colpoperineorrhaphy (to repair rectocele and restore the perineal body).

**2. Vaginal Hysterectomy with Pelvic Floor Repair (Ward Mayo's Operation):**

- Generally **avoided** in a 30-year-old woman as it is an extirpative procedure typically reserved for elderly women who have completed their families.
- _Exception:_ It may only be considered if the patient has definitively completed her family, requests sterilization, and has a coexisting diseased uterus (e.g., intractable Dysfunctional Uterine Bleeding or small submucous fibroid).

**Future Obstetric Advice:** If the patient conceives following a prolapse repair (such as Fothergill's), she must have mandatory regular antenatal check-ups and hospital delivery. A liberal mediolateral episiotomy or an elective cesarean section may be required to protect the repaired pelvic floor during childbirth.


3. Semen analysis.
**SEMEN ANALYSIS**

Semen analysis is the first and most crucial step in the investigation of male infertility.

**I. PREREQUISITES AND COLLECTION OF SEMEN**

- **Abstinence:** Coitus should be avoided for 2–3 days prior to the test.
- **Method of Collection:** The collection is best done by masturbation; failing which, it can be collected by coitus interruptus.
- **Container:** Semen is collected in a clean, wide-mouthed, dry glass jar.
- **Timing:** The sample should be sent to the laboratory as soon as possible and the examination must be performed within 2 hours of ejaculation, because sperm motility begins to decline after 2 hours.

**II. NORMAL REFERENCE VALUES (WHO - 2010)** Normal male fertility requires a count of over 15 million spermatozoa per mL and a progressive motility of over 32%. The normal reference values (with lower reference limits) are as follows:

- **Volume:** 2.0 mL or more (lower limit: 1.5 mL).
- **pH:** 7.2–7.8.
- **Viscosity:** <3 (on a scale of 0–4).
- **Sperm Concentration:** 20 million/mL (lower limit: 15 million/mL).
- **Total Sperm Count:** >40 million/ejaculate (lower limit: 39 million/ejaculate).
- **Motility:** >50% progressive forward motility (Progressive motility = 32%).
- **Morphology:** >14% normal form (lower limit: 4%).
- **Viability:** 75% or more living (lower limit: 58%).
- **Leukocytes:** Less than 1 million/mL.
- **Round Cells:** <5 million/mL.
- **Sperm Agglutination:** <10% spermatozoa with adherent particles.

**III. NOMENCLATURE FOR ABNORMAL SEMEN PARAMETERS**

- **Aspermia:** Failure of emission of semen (no ejaculate).
- **Hypospermia:** Low semen volume (<2 mL).
- **Oligospermia (Oligozoospermia):** Sperm count is less than 20 million per mL.
- **Polyzoospermia:** Sperm count is more than 350 million/mL.
- **Azoospermia:** No spermatozoan in the semen.
- **Asthenozoospermia:** Reduced sperm motility.
- **Teratozoospermia:** >70% spermatozoa with abnormal morphology.
- **Oligoasthenoteratozoospermia:** Disturbance of all 3 variables (count, motility, and morphology).
- **Necrozoospermia:** Spermatozoa are dead or motionless.
- **Leukocytospermia:** Increased white cells in the semen.

**IV. CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE AND FURTHER EVALUATION**

- **Repeat Testing:** Semen values normally vary widely. Therefore, if one report is abnormal, two properly performed semen analyses should be done at least 4 weeks apart.
- **Sperm Function Tests:** In selected cases, biochemical tests evaluating creatine phosphokinase (which helps sperm transport) and reactive oxygen species (which interfere with sperm function) are performed.
- **In-Depth Evaluation:** If gross abnormalities (such as azoospermia, severe oligospermia, or low ejaculate volume) are detected, in-depth investigations are warranted. This may include hormonal assays (FSH, LH, testosterone, prolactin, TSH), testicular biopsy, karyotyping, and genetic testing.
- **Counseling:** When severe defects are confirmed, the couple should be counseled for the need of Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART).


4. Cryptomenorrhoea.

**CRYPTOMENORRHEA**

**DEFINITION** Cryptomenorrhea (or concealed menstruation) is a condition where there is periodic shedding of the endometrium and bleeding, but the menstrual blood fails to come out from the genital tract due to an obstruction in the passage.

**ETIOLOGY** The causes are broadly classified into congenital and acquired:

- **Congenital (Most Common):**
    - Imperforate hymen (the single most common cause).
    - Transverse vaginal septum.
    - Atresia of the upper-third of the vagina and cervix.
- **Acquired (Rare):**
    - Cervical stenosis following surgical procedures like cervical amputation, conization, or deep cauterization.
    - Secondary vaginal atresia resulting from neglected and difficult vaginal deliveries.

**PATHOPHYSIOLOGY**

- **Sequence of Accumulation:** In cases of low obstruction (e.g., imperforate hymen), the accumulated menstrual blood first distends the vagina (**hematocolpos**). It then progressively dilates the uterine cavity (**hematometra**) and eventually distends the fallopian tubes (**hematosalpinx**).
- If the obstruction is at the level of the cervix, it directly produces hematometra followed by hematosalpinx.
- **Pressure Effects:** The massive distension of the vagina (hematocolpos) produces marked elongation and stretching of the urethra, which frequently leads to acute retention of urine.
- **Complications:** In neglected cases, the fimbrial ends of the fallopian tubes get blocked by adhesions, and retrograde spillage of blood can lead to the development of pelvic endometriosis.

**CLINICAL FEATURES** **Symptoms:**

- **Age of Presentation:** Usually presents in young adolescent girls aged 13 to 16 years (in congenital varieties).
- **Primary Amenorrhea:** Absence of visible menses despite attaining the age of menarche. (In acquired cases, it presents as secondary amenorrhea dated back to the inciting event).
- **Pain:** The classic symptom is periodic lower abdominal pain that corresponds to the menstrual cycle. In later stages, this pain may become continuous.
- **Urinary Symptoms:** Frequency of micturition, dysuria, or retention of urine. Often, acute retention of urine is the primary presenting feature.

**Signs:**

- **Abdominal Examination:** A uniform, globular suprapubic mass is palpable in the hypogastrium. Prior catheterization of the bladder is mandatory to differentiate the mass from a full bladder.
- **Vulvar Inspection:** Classically reveals a tense, bulging hymen with a characteristic bluish coloration due to the pent-up blood behind it.
- **Rectal Examination:** Confirms the diagnosis by revealing a bulging, full vagina and a palpably enlarged uterine mass.

**INVESTIGATIONS**

- **Ultrasonography (USG):** Helps confirm the diagnosis of hematometra and hematocolpos. It is also highly useful to rule out any associated urinary tract malformations.

**MANAGEMENT** The treatment is surgical and depends on the level of obstruction:

- **Imperforate Hymen:** The definitive treatment is a **cruciate incision** made on the hymen. The four quadrants of the hymen are partially excised (carefully, not too close to the vaginal mucosa) to allow the spontaneous escape of the accumulated dark, tarry-colored blood.
- **Transverse Vaginal Septum:** Excision or incision of the septum is performed, which is technically easier when the upper vagina is distended with blood.
- **Cervical Stenosis:** Simple dilatation of the cervical canal is sufficient to drain the collected blood.
- **Secondary Vaginal Atresia:** Requires reconstructive surgery (vaginoplasty) to recreate and maintain the patency of the vaginal canal.

5. Syndromic management of sexually transmitted diseases.

**SYNDROMIC MANAGEMENT OF SEXUALLY TRANSMITTED INFECTIONS (STIs)**

**INTRODUCTION** The syndromic management of Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs) and Reproductive Tract Infections (RTIs) was introduced by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 1991. It is a management strategy based on epidemiological studies conducted worldwide, wherein diagnosis and treatment are guided by the identification of consistent groups of clinical symptoms and easily recognized signs (syndromes) rather than relying on laboratory-based etiological diagnosis. Studies have shown that syndromic diagnosis and laboratory-assisted diagnosis are similar in terms of accuracy.

**PRINCIPLES OF MANAGEMENT**

- **Immediate Treatment:** Treatment for STIs should be initiated at the patient's very first visit to the clinic.
- **Simultaneous Counseling:** At the time of the initial visit, the couple must be counseled about the importance of condom use and the prevention of STIs.

**METHODOLOGY AND CLINICAL APPROACH** Healthcare providers are trained to follow standardized protocols and flowcharts for treating patients, making this approach particularly suitable for healthcare setups in developing countries. The management steps include:

- **Medical History:** A detailed medical history must be elicited, which crucially includes a detailed sexual history and risk assessment.
- **Physical and Pelvic Examination:** A thorough physical examination is mandatory, including a speculum and bimanual pelvic examination. This aims to detect vaginal discharge, abdominal pain, cervical motion tenderness, and adnexal tenderness. It is also essential to exclude cervical cancer and pregnancy during this examination.
- **Counseling and Education:** Voluntary Counseling and Testing (VCT) for HIV is actively promoted, and patients are referred to Integrated Counseling and Testing Centers (ICTC). Patient education on preventive issues and the promotion of condom use are standard parts of care.
- **Partner Management:** Tracing and treating the sexual partner is a vital component of the protocol.
- **Follow-up and Immunization:** Follow-up is advised after 7 days for all STI patients, and immunization against Hepatitis B is recommended.

**COMMON SYNDROMES AND TREATMENT KITS (WHO)** Syndromic case management utilizes specific color-coded kits tailored to the presenting clinical syndrome.

**1. Vaginal Discharge Syndrome** This syndrome can be caused by vaginitis (due to Trichomoniasis, Bacterial vaginosis, or Candidiasis) or cervicitis (due to _Chlamydia_ or _Gonorrhea_).

- **KIT 1 (Grey):** Used for cervical discharge (Chlamydia/Gonorrhea). Contains Tab Azithromycin 1 gm OD stat + Tab Cefixime 400 mg OD stat. Male partners are also treated with Kit 1.
- **KIT 2 (Green):** Used for vaginal discharge (Vaginitis). Contains Tab Secnidazole 2 gm OD stat + Cap Fluconazole 150 mg OD stat. No partner treatment is routinely required unless symptomatic.

**2. Genital Ulcer Syndrome** Common etiologies include Genital herpes, Chancroid, and Syphilis.

- **KIT 5 (Red):** Specifically indicated for herpetic genital ulcers. Contains Tab Acyclovir 400 mg TDS for 7 days. Partners are treated only when symptomatic.

**3. Lower Abdominal Pain Syndrome** Typically caused by _Chlamydia_, _Gonorrhea_, or other pyogenic organisms leading to Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID).

- **KIT 6 (Yellow):** Contains Tab Cefixime 400 mg OD stat + Tab Metronidazole 400 mg BD for 14 days + Tab Doxycycline 100 mg BD for 14 days. It is mandatory to treat all recent partners for this syndrome.

**ADVANTAGES OF SYNDROMIC MANAGEMENT**

- **Immediate Care:** It provides an opportunity for simultaneously counseling and treating the patient without waiting for lab results.
- **Standardized Approach:** The use of standardized protocols and flowcharts allows for better diagnosis, treatment, and disease surveillance. Patient education is integrated as a standard of care for all types of STIs.
- **Resource Efficiency:** It avoids the delay of treatment in settings where laboratory facilities are limited or unavailable.
- **Better Compliance:** It avoids the loss of patient follow-up, which is a major issue where referral systems are poorly structured.
- **Public Health Impact:** It effectively prevents the continued transmission of the infection in the community.
- **Cost-Effective:** It is a simple, inexpensive, rapid, and highly cost-effective management strategy that yields a high cure rate once appropriate treatment is instituted.

**LIMITATIONS AND DRAWBACKS**

- **Poor Predictive Value:** The predictive accuracy is poor because the diagnosis relies entirely on symptoms rather than microbiological investigations.
- **Over-treatment:** There is a well-known hazard of over-diagnosis and over-treatment. This includes the unnecessary overtreatment of women's sexual partners who may not actually be infected with an STI.
- **Misses Asymptomatic Carriers:** The syndromic approach is completely ineffective for patients who are asymptomatic, thereby leaving a reservoir of infection untreated.
- **Lack of Specificity:** The syndromic approach for vaginal discharge has poor predictive value for specifically identifying and isolating _Chlamydia_ and _Gonorrhea_ infections.

6. Tubal patency tests.
**TUBAL PATENCY TESTS**

**Introduction:** Evaluation of the anatomical patency and functional integrity of the fallopian tubes is a fundamental step in the investigation of female infertility. It is also performed to verify success following tuboplasty operations.

**Timing of the Tests:** All tubal patency tests are ideally performed in the **proliferative phase** of the menstrual cycle, typically between **Day 7 and Day 10** (2 days after the cessation of menstrual bleeding).

**General Contraindications:** Tubal patency tests are strictly contraindicated in the presence of active pelvic infection, suspected pelvic tuberculosis, unexplained abnormal uterine bleeding, or suspected pregnancy.

**Classification of Tubal Patency Tests:**

1. Dilatation and Insufflation test (Rubin’s test)
2. Hysterosalpingography (HSG)
3. Laparoscopy and Chromopertubation (Gold Standard)
4. Sonohysterosalpingography (Saline Infusion Sonography - SIS) / HyCoSy
5. Tubal Endoscopy (Falloposcopy and Salpingoscopy)

---

**1. DILATATION AND INSUFFLATION TEST (RUBIN'S TEST)**

- **Principle:** Air or CO2 is pushed transcervically under pressure. If the tubes are patent, the gas enters the peritoneal cavity.
- **Positive Evidences of Patency:**
    - Drop in manometer pressure when raised beyond 120 mm Hg.
    - Hissing sound heard on auscultation over either iliac fossa.
    - Shoulder pain experienced by the patient on sitting up (due to diaphragmatic irritation by gas, mediated by the phrenic nerve).
- **Drawbacks:** It is rarely performed nowadays because it yields a high false-negative rate (about 1/3rd of cases) due to cornual spasm, and it cannot pinpoint the exact side or site of the block.

**2. HYSTEROSALPINGOGRAPHY (HSG)**

- **Principle:** A radiographic study where a radio-opaque contrast dye (water-soluble like meglumine diatrizoate or oil-based like ethiodized oil) is instilled transcervically into the uterine cavity and tubes.
- **Findings:** Tubal patency is confirmed by the visualization of free peritoneal spillage of the dye on the X-ray.
- **Advantages over Rubin's Test:**
    - Precisely identifies the exact side and site of the tubal obstruction.
    - Can detect uterine cavity abnormalities (e.g., submucous fibroids, polyps, synechiae/Asherman's syndrome, bicornuate or septate uterus).
    - Has an incidental therapeutic benefit by flushing out mucous debris or breaking flimsy intraluminal adhesions, which can improve conception rates in subsequent months.
- **Complications:** Pelvic pain, vasovagal attack, risk of flaring up pelvic infection (1-3%), and rare dye intravasation into venous/lymphatic channels.

**3. LAPAROSCOPY AND CHROMOPERTUBATION (DYE TEST)**

- **Status:** Considered the **Gold Standard** (definitive method) for evaluating tubal factors in infertility.
- **Procedure:** Methylene blue dye is injected through the cervix via a cannula, and the spillage of dye from the fimbrial ends into the pouch of Douglas is directly visualized using a laparoscope.
- **Advantages:**
    - Directly detects tubal patency, motility, fimbrial agglutination, and hydrosalpinx.
    - Evaluates associated pelvic/peritoneal pathology that HSG misses (e.g., pelvic endometriosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, peritubal adhesions, polycystic ovaries).
    - Provides the opportunity for simultaneous therapeutic operative procedures (e.g., adhesiolysis, fulguration of endometriotic implants).

**4. SONOHYSTEROSALPINGOGRAPHY (SIS) / HyCoSy**

- **Principle:** Normal saline is infused into the uterine cavity via a pediatric Foley catheter (the balloon occludes the internal os to prevent leak). Transvaginal ultrasonography tracks the fluid flowing through the tubes and spilling into the pouch of Douglas.
- **Advantages:** It is non-invasive, completely avoids radiation exposure, and is superior to HSG for the detection of intrauterine pathology (like polyps or synechiae).

**5. TUBAL ENDOSCOPY**

- **Falloposcopy:** A fine, flexible fiberoptic device is passed transcervically through a hysteroscope to study the entire length of the tubal lumen, directly visualizing tubal ostia, mucosal patterns, intratubal polyps, or debris.
- **Salpingoscopy:** A rigid endoscope is introduced through the abdominal (fimbrial) ostium via the operating channel of a laparoscope. It evaluates the tubal mucosa and helps in selecting patients for In Vitro Fertilization (IVF) versus tubal reconstructive surgery.

7. Germ cell tumours of the ovary
**GERM CELL TUMORS OF THE OVARY**

**INTRODUCTION AND INCIDENCE**

- Germ cell tumors (GCTs) arise from embryonic germ cells and constitute about 15–20% (up to 25%) of all primary ovarian neoplasms.
- They are the second most common group of ovarian tumors.
- They predominantly occur in children and young adults (mean age often <20 years).
- About 95% of GCTs are benign (predominantly mature cystic teratomas), while about 5% are malignant.
- Malignant GCTs are usually diagnosed at an early stage, are highly chemoresponsive, and have an excellent prognosis, allowing for fertility-sparing surgery.

**WHO CLASSIFICATION OF GERM CELL TUMORS**

1. **Dysgerminoma**
2. **Endodermal sinus tumor (Yolk sac tumor)**
3. **Embryonal cell carcinoma**
4. **Polyembryoma**
5. **Choriocarcinoma (Nongestational)**
6. **Teratoma:**
    - Immature
    - Mature (Dermoid cyst)
    - Monodermal (Struma ovarii, Carcinoid)
7. **Mixed germ cell tumors**

---

**HIGH-YIELD SPECIFIC TUMORS**

**1. Mature Cystic Teratoma (Dermoid Cyst)**

- **Incidence:** Most common germ cell tumor (95%) and constitutes about 30-40% of all ovarian tumors. Bilateral in 15–20% of cases.
- **Origin:** Arises from a single germ cell arrested after the first meiotic division (Karyotype is usually 46,XX).
- **Pathology:** Contains mature elements from all three germ cell layers.
    - _Ectoderm:_ Skin, hair, sebaceous glands, teeth.
    - _Mesoderm:_ Bone, cartilage, muscle.
    - _Endoderm:_ Thyroid, respiratory/intestinal epithelium.
- **Special Features:** The solid area of the cyst is called _Rokitansky’s protuberance_. _Struma ovarii_ is a specialized teratoma composed predominantly of thyroid tissue.
- **Complications:** Torsion is the most common complication (15–20%) due to its moderate weight (high fat content) and long pedicle. Rupture is rare (1%). Malignant transformation occurs in 1–2% of cases, with squamous cell carcinoma being the most common.

**2. Dysgerminoma**

- **Incidence:** Second most common malignant GCT (33%).
- **Pathology:** Arises from undifferentiated germ cells and is the female counterpart of male seminoma.
- **Clinical Features:** 75% occur before age 30. It is the most common malignant tumor found in patients with dysgenetic gonads (presence of Y chromosome).
- **Tumor Markers:** Often positive for hCG and LDH.
- **Prognosis:** Highly sensitive to both radiation and chemotherapy.

**3. Immature Teratoma**

- **Incidence:** Constitutes 35% of ovarian teratomas and is the most common malignant GCT. Mostly seen in females aged 10–20 years.
- **Pathology:** Contains undifferentiated tissue elements from the 3 germ layers.
- **Prognosis:** Depends heavily on the quantity of immature neural tissue elements and the histologic grade (Grade 3 has a poor prognosis).

**4. Endodermal Sinus Tumor (Yolk Sac Tumor)**

- **Incidence:** Third most common malignant GCT (15-20%). Mostly seen between 15 and 20 years of age.
- **Pathology:** Characteristic histological feature is the _Schiller-Duval body_ (a tuft of vascular tissue within a cystic space lined by flattened epithelium).
- **Clinical Features:** Highly malignant, friable, and rapidly growing. Patients often present with acute abdomen due to necrosis and hemorrhage.
- **Tumor Marker:** Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) is highly elevated and is used for diagnosis and monitoring.

**5. Embryonal Carcinoma**

- **Features:** Highly aggressive tumor occurring at a mean age of 15 years.
- **Markers:** Secretes AFP, hCG, and estrogens.

**6. Nongestational Choriocarcinoma**

- **Features:** Extremely rare, pure nongestational forms arise from a solid teratoma.
- **Clinical Features:** Isosexual precocious puberty is common (50%) due to hCG production. Differentiated from gestational choriocarcinoma by the _absence of paternal DNA_.
- **Prognosis:** Poor. Unlike gestational choriocarcinoma, it is relatively resistant to methotrexate.

**7. Gonadoblastoma & Mixed Germ Cell Tumors**

- _Gonadoblastoma:_ Contains germ cells and gonadal stromal cells. Strongly associated with dysgenetic gonads (Karyotype 45,X or 46,XY). Prophylactic gonadectomy is recommended due to high malignancy risk.
- _Mixed GCTs:_ Contain at least two germ cell elements; dysgerminoma is the most common component (70-80%).

---

**TUMOR MARKERS IN GERM CELL TUMORS** Estimation of serum tumor markers is high-yield for diagnosis, assessing response to therapy, and detecting recurrence:

- **LDH (Lactate Dehydrogenase):** Dysgerminoma.
- **AFP (Alpha-Fetoprotein):** Yolk sac tumor, Immature teratoma, Embryonal carcinoma.
- **hCG (Human Chorionic Gonadotropin):** Nongestational Choriocarcinoma, Embryonal carcinoma, Dysgerminoma.

**MANAGEMENT OF MALIGNANT GERM CELL TUMORS**

**1. Surgical Management**

- **Fertility-Sparing Surgery:** As these tumors predominantly affect young females and are usually unilateral, the gold standard treatment for early-stage disease (Stage IA) is **unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy** with comprehensive surgical staging.
- **Contraindications for Biopsy:** Routine biopsy of the contralateral normal-looking ovary is avoided to prevent future infertility and adhesion formation.
- **Advanced Age/Completed Family:** Total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy is performed in older women.
- **Dysgenetic Gonads:** If a Y chromosome is present (e.g., Swyer syndrome, Androgen Insensitivity Syndrome), bilateral gonadectomy is strictly indicated due to the high risk of dysgerminoma and gonadoblastoma.

**2. Chemotherapy**

- Malignant GCTs are highly chemosensitive. The routine use of combination chemotherapy has dramatically improved survival rates (often >90% even in advanced disease).
- **Regimens Used:**
    - **BEP:** Bleomycin, Etoposide, and Cisplatin (Most common and effective).
    - **VAC:** Vincristine, Actinomycin-D, Cyclophosphamide.
    - **VBP:** Vinblastine, Bleomycin, Cisplatin.
- **Post-Chemotherapy Function:** Menstrual function, fertility, and endocrine functions generally return to normal following the completion of these chemotherapeutic regimens.

8. Puberty menorrhagia.
**PUBERTY MENORRHAGIA**

**INTRODUCTION** Menstrual abnormalities in adolescents are common, with periods initially presenting as heavy, irregular, or scanty. Eventually, the majority of these teenage girls establish a normal cycle and are fertile. Puberty menorrhagia refers to excessively heavy menstrual bleeding occurring at or shortly after the onset of menarche.

**ETIOLOGY / CAUSES** The causes of puberty menorrhagia can be broadly classified into the following categories:

- **Dysfunctional Uterine Bleeding (DUB) (95%):** This is the most common cause. It is primarily due to anovulatory cycles where there is unopposed estrogen stimulation. This leads to endometrial hyperplasia, which subsequently results in prolonged and heavy menstrual periods.
- **Endocrine Dysfunction:**
    - Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS).
    - Thyroid disorders (Hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism).
- **Hematological Disorders:** Bleeding diatheses must be excluded in a young girl presenting with severe menorrhagia. Common hematological causes include:
    - Idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP).
    - Von-Willebrand’s disease.
    - Leukemia.
- **Pelvic Tumors:** Though rare in this age group, they must be considered:
    - Fibroid uterus.
    - Sarcoma botryoides (a type of embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma).
    - Estrogen-producing ovarian tumors (e.g., Granulosa cell tumor).
- **Pregnancy Complications:** Complications of pregnancy, such as abortion, must always be ruled out.

**DIAGNOSIS AND INDICATIONS FOR EVALUATION** Diagnosis is made by careful history taking and a thorough clinical examination. Clinical evaluation is strictly indicated if:

- The menstrual interval is less than 22 days or greater than 44 days.
- The menstrual bleeding lasts for longer than one week.
- The bleeding is excessively heavy, leading to the development of clinical anemia.

**INVESTIGATIONS** Investigations are tailored according to the suspected clinical diagnosis.

- **Routine Hematology:** Hemoglobin (Hb%) estimation, peripheral blood film, bleeding time (BT), clotting time (CT), and platelet count.
- **Coagulation Profile:** Prothrombin time (PT), Partial thromboplastin time (PTT), Factor VIII levels, and von Willebrand factor (vWF).
- **Endocrine Profile:** Thyroid profile (TSH, T3, T4).
- **Imaging:** Pelvic ultrasonography (USG) or MRI to exclude organic pelvic pathology such as tumors or polyps.
- **Surgical Evaluation:** Examination under anesthesia (EUA) and uterine curettage (D&C) may be needed to exclude pelvic pathology (like pregnancy complications), and the curetted material is sent for histopathological study.

**MANAGEMENT** The primary goals of management are to control the bleeding, correct anemia, and restore a regular menstrual cycle.

**1. General Measures & Supportive Therapy:**

- The young girl needs adequate explanation, reassurance, and psychological support.
- Rest and correction of anemia with hematinics are helpful in the majority of cases.
- Blood transfusion may be required if the bleeding is severe and the patient is hemodynamically compromised or severely anemic.

**2. Medical Management:**

- **Progestin Therapy:** For primary DUB (which forms the majority), progestogens such as medroxyprogesterone acetate (10-20 mg/day) or norethisterone 5 mg thrice daily are given until the bleeding stops. The bleeding is usually controlled within 3–7 days. This medication is continued for 21 days (for 2-3 cycles). Following 2–3 courses, normal cycles typically resume.
- **Emergency Hemostasis:** In cases of severe, unresponsive acute bleeding, conjugated equine estrogen 20–40 mg is administered intravenously (IV) every 6–8 hours.
- **Maintenance Therapy:** Once the acute bleeding is controlled, therapy is replaced with combined oral contraceptive (COC) pills containing 50 µg of estrogen.
- **GnRH Analogs:** These can be used for short-term management in severe, refractory cases.

**3. Surgical Management:**

- If the bleeding remains unresponsive to both progestins and IV estrogen therapy, an Examination Under Anesthesia (EUA) and Dilatation & Curettage (D&C) with endometrial biopsy is indicated.

**4. Treatment of Secondary Causes:**

- If investigations reveal a secondary cause (e.g., thyroid dysfunction, ITP, leukemia, Von-Willebrand’s disease, anatomical disorders, neoplasms, or pregnancy complications), appropriate specific therapy directed at the underlying cause must be instituted.

_Note: Regular menstrual cycles will be established naturally once the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis fully matures._

9. Emergency contraception.
**EMERGENCY CONTRACEPTION (POSTCOITAL CONTRACEPTION)**

**INTRODUCTION** Emergency contraception (also called "interception" or morning-after pill) is used to prevent conception following an accidental, unprotected exposure around the time of ovulation. It is strictly an emergency measure and should not be employed as a regular method of contraception.

**INDICATIONS** Emergency contraception is indicated in situations where the risk of pregnancy is high (estimated up to 30% following a single episode of unprotected intercourse near ovulation).

- Unprotected sexual intercourse (UPSI).
- Condom rupture or slippage.
- Missed oral contraceptive pills.
- Delay in taking a progestin-only pill (POP) for more than 3 hours (or 12 hours with desogestrel POP).
- Sexual assault or rape.
- First-time intercourse (often unplanned).
- UPSI from day 21 postpartum (if lactational amenorrhea criteria are not met).
- UPSI from day 5 after an abortion, miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, or uterine evacuation of gestational trophoblastic disease (GTD).

**METHODS OF EMERGENCY CONTRACEPTION** The available methods are broadly classified into non-hormonal (Intrauterine devices) and hormonal methods.

**1. Copper Intrauterine Contraceptive Device (Cu-IUCD) – _Gold Standard & Most Effective_**

- **Timing:** Inserted as soon as possible, up to 120 hours (5 days) of UPSI, or within 5 days of ovulation (whichever is later).
- **Mechanism:** Toxic to sperms and ova, inhibits fertilization, and prevents implantation (possesses both pre- and post-fertilization inhibition).
- **Efficacy:** Failure rate is less than 0.1% to 1%.
- **Advantage:** It can be retained for 10 years to serve as a highly effective regular method of contraception.

**2. Ulipristal Acetate (UPA-EC) – _Second Most Effective_**

- **Type:** Selective Progesterone Receptor Modulator (SPRM).
- **Dose:** 30 mg orally as a single dose.
- **Timing:** To be taken as soon as possible or within 120 hours (5 days) of UPSI.
- **Mechanism:** Suppresses follicular and endometrial growth, delays ovulation, and inhibits implantation.
- **Efficacy:** Pregnancy/failure rate is 0–2%.
- **Contraindications:** Severe hepatic dysfunction and severe asthma.

**3. Levonorgestrel (LNG / E-Pills) – _Third Most Effective_**

- **Dose:** 1.50 mg taken as a single dose, OR 0.75 mg taken in two doses at a 12-hour interval.
- **Timing:** First dose should ideally be taken within 72 hours (most effective) but can be taken up to 120 hours post-coitus.
- **Mechanism:** Ineffective once the LH surge has already happened.
- **Efficacy:** Highly successful in 85% of cases; failure rate is 0–1%.

**4. Mifepristone (RU 486)**

- **Type:** Antiprogesterone (progesterone receptor antagonist).
- **Dose:** A single dose of 10 mg (given on 27th day of the cycle) or 100 mg.
- **Timing:** Taken within 120 hours (5 days) of intercourse.
- **Mechanism:** Prevents implantation due to its potent antiprogesterone effect.
- **Efficacy:** Pregnancy rate is 0–0.6%.

**5. Yuzpe Method (Combined Hormonal Regimen)**

- **Dose:** 2 tablets of Ovral (0.25 mg levonorgestrel + 50 µg ethinyl estradiol) stat, followed by 2 more tablets 12 hours later.
- **Timing:** Should be taken as early as possible within 72 hours of coitus.
- **Efficacy:** Failure rate is 0–2%.

**6. Centchroman (Saheli)**

- A nonsteroidal compound that can also be used safely as an emergency contraceptive.

**MECHANISM OF ACTION OF HORMONAL EC** While the exact mechanism remains slightly unclear, the possibilities include:

1. **Delay or prevention of ovulation**, especially when the drug is taken in the early part of the cycle.
2. **Interference with fertilization**.
3. **Prevention of implantation** by rendering the endometrium unfavorable (except for LNG pills which mainly delay ovulation).
4. **Luteolysis** or interference with corpus luteum function.

**DRAWBACKS AND COUNSELING**

- **Side Effects:** Nausea and vomiting are the most common side effects, particularly intense with estrogen-containing regimens (like the Yuzpe method). Pre-medication with an oral antiemetic (e.g., 10 mg metoclopramide or meclizine) 1 hour before the dose is recommended to reduce vomiting.
- **Failure of EC:** If the emergency contraception fails and pregnancy continues, no significant fetal adverse effects or teratogenicity have been observed. However, the patient must be counseled appropriately, and induced abortion should be offered as an option.

10. Cervical cancer screening.

**CERVICAL CANCER SCREENING**

**INTRODUCTION** Cervical cancer screening aims to detect precancerous changes (Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia - CIN) and early invasive cancer of the cervix. It is an entirely preventable disease if diagnosed and treated in its preinvasive phase.

- **Impact:** Routine Pap smear testing is the most effective cancer screening procedure. It has reduced the incidence of invasive cervical cancer by approximately 80% and mortality by 70%.

**SCREENING MODALITIES**

1. **Conventional Pap Smear:** Exfoliative cytology is the gold standard for screening.
2. **Liquid-Based Cytology (LBC):** Cells are placed in a liquid medium. It improves cell collection quality, evenly distributes abnormal cells, allows for concurrent HPV DNA testing, and reduces the risk of false-negative or unsatisfactory smears.
3. **HPV-DNA Testing:** A highly sensitive test to detect high-risk oncogenic HPV types (e.g., HPV 16, 18) using techniques like Hybrid Capture 2. Used as a primary screen or in co-testing.
4. **Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid (VIA):** A "downstaging" screening method used in low-resource settings (LMICs). 5% acetic acid is applied to the cervix, and acetowhite areas are suspected for neoplasia and managed with a "see and treat" approach (e.g., cryotherapy).

**SCREENING GUIDELINES (ACOG, ACS, FOGSI, USPSTF)**

- **Initiation:** Screening should begin at **25 years** (or **30 years** as per the Government of India) regardless of the onset of sexual activity.
- **Age 25–29 years:** Pap testing alone every **3 years**.
- **Age 30–65 years:**
    - **Co-testing** (Pap smear + High-risk HPV testing) every **5 years** (Preferred method).
    - _Alternatively:_ Pap testing alone every **3 years**.
- **Age > 65 years:** Discontinue screening if there have been normal test results over the past 10 years and no history of high-grade dysplasia (CIN 2 or 3) in the past 20 years.
- **High-Risk Exceptions:** Women with HIV seropositivity, immunosuppression, or in-utero DES exposure require **annual screening**.
- **Post-Hysterectomy:** No follow-up screening is needed if a total hysterectomy was performed for benign conditions. If a subtotal hysterectomy was done, usual screening protocols apply.

**TECHNIQUE OF CELL COLLECTION**

- **Preparation:** The cervix is exposed using a Cusco’s bivalve speculum **without lubricant**, as lubricants distort cell morphology.
- **Tools:**
    - _Ectocervix:_ Sampled using a plastic **Ayre’s spatula**. The projected end is placed within the external os and rotated 360° to scrape the entire squamocolumnar junction (transformation zone).
    - _Endocervix:_ Sampled using an **endocervical brush (cytobrush)** rotated one-quarter to one-half turn.
    - _LBC:_ A **plastic broom** with long central bristles and shorter flanking bristles can sample both the ecto- and endocervix simultaneously with five rotations.
- **Fixation (Conventional):** The material is immediately spread evenly over a glass slide and rapidly fixed with 95% ethyl alcohol before drying to prevent artifacts. It is then stained using the Papanicolaou method.

**CYTOLOGY REPORTING: THE BETHESDA SYSTEM (2014)** Smears are reported using the Bethesda System, classifying epithelial abnormalities: **1. Squamous Cell Abnormalities:**

- **ASC:** Atypical Squamous Cells (ASC-US: of undetermined significance; ASC-H: cannot exclude high-grade lesion).
- **LSIL:** Low-grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion (correlates with HPV infection/CIN 1).
- **HSIL:** High-grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion (correlates with CIN 2 / CIN 3 / CIS).
- **SCC:** Squamous Cell Carcinoma. **2. Glandular Cell Abnormalities:**
- **AGC:** Atypical Glandular Cells (Endocervical, endometrial, or not specified).
- **AIS:** Adenocarcinoma in situ.
- **Adenocarcinoma.**

**TRIAGE AND MANAGEMENT OF ABNORMAL SMEARS**

- **ASC-US:** Reflex HPV-DNA testing is performed. If HPV positive $\rightarrow$ Colposcopy. If HPV negative $\rightarrow$ Routine screening in 5 years.
- **LSIL:** If age >30, HPV testing is done. If HPV negative $\rightarrow$ Repeat testing in 1 year. If both HPV positive and cytology positive $\rightarrow$ Colposcopy and biopsy.
- **ASC-H & HSIL:** High risk of underlying CIN 2/3 or invasive carcinoma. All such women require immediate **Colposcopy and directed biopsy**.
- **AGC:** High risk of underlying endometrial/cervical neoplasia. Requires Colposcopy along with endocervical and endometrial sampling.

**SCREENING IN PREGNANCY** Cervical cytology is a routine part of antenatal care unless already done recently. Cone biopsy is generally avoided during pregnancy due to the risk of hemorrhage and preterm labor. Abnormalities like CIN and CIS are followed up with periodic cytology and colposcopy, and re-evaluated 6 weeks postpartum.

11. Role of laparoscopy in Gynaecology.

**DEFINITION** Laparoscopy is a minimally invasive surgical (MIS) technique used to visualize the peritoneal cavity and pelvic organs using a fiberoptic endoscope introduced through the abdominal wall, typically following the creation of a pneumoperitoneum with carbon dioxide or air.

**DIAGNOSTIC INDICATIONS** Laparoscopy is considered the "gold standard" for the diagnosis of many gynecological conditions.

- **Infertility Workup:** To assess peritubal adhesions, perform chromopertubation (dye test) to check tubal patency, detect minimal endometriosis, and look for an ovulation stigma on the ovary.
- **Acute Pelvic Pain / Acute Abdomen:** To accurately diagnose conditions like unruptured or disturbed ectopic pregnancy, acute salpingitis/pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), torsion of an ovarian cyst, and acute appendicitis.
- **Chronic Pelvic Pain:** To evaluate undiagnosed pain and identify minimal endometriosis or pelvic adhesions.
- **Pelvic Mass Evaluation:** To differentiate a pedunculated uterine fibroid from an ovarian cyst when clinical examination and ultrasonography are inconclusive.
- **Primary/Secondary Amenorrhea:** To visualize suspected Müllerian abnormalities or perform an ovarian gonadal biopsy.
- **Uterine Perforation:** To assess the extent of injury and internal hemorrhage if the uterus is perforated by a sound, dilator, or curette during procedures like Dilatation and Curettage (D&C) or Medical Termination of Pregnancy (MTP).
- **'Second Look' Evaluation:** Used for follow-up of pelvic surgeries, such as after tuboplasty, therapy for endometriosis, or monitoring ovarian malignancy.

**OPERATIVE (THERAPEUTIC) INDICATIONS** Virtually all gynecological surgical procedures can now be performed via laparoscopy. They are classified by complexity:

- **Minor Procedures:** Tubal sterilization (using clips, rings, or electrocoagulation), mild adhesiolysis (without bowel involvement), aspiration of simple ovarian cysts, and ovarian biopsy.
- **Moderate Procedures:**
    - _Ovary:_ Laparoscopic ovarian drilling (diathermy/laser) for Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome (PCOS), ovarian cystectomy, and drainage of endometriomas.
    - _Ectopic Pregnancy:_ Linear salpingostomy, segmental resection, salpingectomy, or salpingo-oophorectomy.
    - _Endometriosis:_ Ablation or excision of implants by diathermy or laser vaporization.
- **Extensive/Major Procedures:**
    - _Uterus:_ Myomectomy, Laparoscopic Assisted Vaginal Hysterectomy (LAVH), and Total Laparoscopic Hysterectomy (TLH).
    - _Malignancy:_ Radical hysterectomy and retroperitoneal (pelvic and para-aortic) lymphadenectomy for cervical and endometrial cancers.
    - _Urogynecology/Prolapse:_ Sacrocolpopexy for vault prolapse and surgeries for urinary incontinence (e.g., Burch colposuspension).

**ADVANTAGES OF LAPAROSCOPY**

- Excellent visualization of organs and tissues due to magnified images.
- Minimal abdominal scars, yielding high cosmetic value.
- Reduced blood loss and less adhesion formation compared to open surgery.
- Less postoperative pain, reducing the need for postoperative analgesia.
- Shorter hospital stay and quicker resumption of day-to-day activities.
- Less risk of incisional hernia and overall decreased surgical morbidity.

**CONTRAINDICATIONS**

- **Absolute:** Severe cardiopulmonary disease, hemodynamic instability, significant hemoperitoneum, and intestinal obstruction.
- **Relative:** Tubercular peritonitis, extensive peritoneal adhesions, large pelvic tumors, morbid obesity, advanced malignancy, generalized peritonitis, large hiatal hernia, and ongoing anticoagulation therapy.

**COMPLICATIONS**

- **Procedure-Specific:**
    - _Extraperitoneal insufflation:_ Leading to surgical or omental emphysema.
    - _Vascular Injury:_ Damage to mesenteric, omental, inferior epigastric, or major pelvic/abdominal vessels (iliac artery, aorta, vena cava) during trocar insertion.
    - _Visceral Injury:_ Mechanical or thermal (electrosurgical/laser) injury to the bowel, bladder, or ureters.
    - _Gas Embolism:_ Carbon dioxide embolism resulting in hypotension and cardiac arrhythmias.
- **Anesthesia-Related:** Hypoventilation (due to pneumoperitoneum and Trendelenburg position), hypercarbia, metabolic acidosis, tachycardia, basal lung atelectasis, and potential cardiac arrest.
- **General Surgical:** Hemorrhage, pelvic infection, wound dehiscence, and port-site hernias.

12. Medical management of AUB.
**MEDICAL MANAGEMENT OF ABNORMAL UTERINE BLEEDING (AUB)**

The medical management of AUB depends on the patient's age, desire for childbearing, severity of bleeding, and any associated pathology. The primary goals are to arrest acute bleeding, prevent recurrences, and correct complications like anemia.

**I. GENERAL MEASURES**

- **Rest and Assurance:** Bed rest is advised during the bleeding phase. Sympathetic handling and assurance are particularly helpful in adolescent girls.
- **Correction of Anemia:** Anemia must be appropriately corrected through diet, oral or parenteral hematinics, and blood transfusion in severe cases.
- **Systemic Care:** Any underlying systemic or endocrinal abnormality (e.g., thyroid dysfunction) should be investigated and treated accordingly.

**II. NONHORMONAL MANAGEMENT** Nonhormonal agents are typically used as first- or second-line therapy, especially in ovulatory AUB.

- **Prostaglandin Synthetase Inhibitors (NSAIDs):**
    - _Examples & Dose:_ Mefenamic acid 500 mg TID for 5 days during menstruation.
    - _Mechanism & Effect:_ They inhibit the cyclooxygenase enzyme, blocking thromboxane and prostacyclin synthesis. They reduce menstrual blood loss by 25% to 50% and provide the added benefit of relieving dysmenorrhea.
- **Antifibrinolytic Agents:**
    - _Examples & Dose:_ Tranexamic acid 500 mg to 1 g, 6-hourly for 5 days.
    - _Mechanism & Effect:_ Counteracts the endometrial fibrinolytic system, reducing menstrual blood loss by up to 50%.
    - _Contraindication:_ Avoid in women with a history of thromboembolism.
- **Ethamsylate:** Given in a dose of 500 mg QID to reduce bleeding.

**III. HORMONAL MANAGEMENT** Hormonal therapy is the mainstay of treatment for Dysfunctional Uterine Bleeding (DUB), particularly in anovulatory cycles.

**1. Progestogens** They are highly effective in anovulatory AUB and have largely replaced the isolated use of androgens and estrogens.

- _Mechanism:_ They exert an anti-estrogenic effect by stimulating the conversion of estradiol to less potent estrone, inhibiting estrogen receptors, halting endometrial growth, and causing organized endometrial shedding up to the basal layer.
- _Regimens:_
    - **To stop acute bleeding:** Norethisterone acetate 5 mg TID until bleeding stops (usually 3–7 days).
    - **Cyclic therapy (Day 5 to 25):** Medroxyprogesterone acetate (MPA) 10 mg TID or Norethisterone 5 mg TID for 3 cycles.
    - **Luteal phase therapy (Day 15 to 25):** Dydrogesterone 10 mg TID. Useful if the patient desires pregnancy, but less effective than the Day 5-25 regimen.
    - **Continuous therapy:** MPA 10 mg TID for at least 90 days. Depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA) can also be used, producing oligomenorrhea or amenorrhea.

**2. Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs)**

- _Dose:_ Formulations containing Ethinyl Estradiol (20–30 µg) + Levonorgestrel (0.15 mg) given once a day for 21 days.
- _Effect:_ Highly effective in ovular bleeding. They cause endometrial atrophy and efficiently suppress the hypothalamo-pituitary axis.

**3. Levonorgestrel Intrauterine System (LNG-IUS - Mirena)**

- _Mechanism:_ Releases 20 µg of LNG daily directly into the uterine cavity, inducing profound endometrial glandular atrophy, stromal decidualization, and cell inactivation with minimal systemic absorption.
- _Effect:_ Reduces blood loss significantly (80–100%) and is effective for 5 years. It is considered a "medical hysterectomy" and is the recommended first-line therapy for heavy menstrual bleeding without structural abnormality, as well as for women with coagulopathies.

**4. Estrogens**

- _Indication:_ Used for acute, severe, life-threatening bleeding.
- _Dose:_ Conjugated equine estrogen 25 mg intravenously every 4 hours, or 10 mg/day orally in 4 divided doses.
- _Mechanism:_ Promotes rapid growth of the denuded endometrium, promotes platelet adhesiveness, and increases fibrinogen and clotting factors.

**5. Danazol**

- _Dose:_ 200 mg/day.
- _Effect:_ Reduces blood loss by 60% or produces amenorrhea. It is suitable for recurrent symptoms or for patients waiting for hysterectomy.

**6. GnRH Analogs (Agonists)**

- _Effect:_ Subtherapeutic doses reduce blood loss, while therapeutic doses produce amenorrhea.
- _Indication:_ Valuable for short-term use in severe AUB, to improve anemia before surgery, prior to endometrial ablation, or in infertile women. Prolonged use causes hypoestrogenic features.

**7. Antiprogesterones**

- _Mifepristone (RU 486):_ Inhibits ovulation, induces amenorrhea, and reduces the size of any associated myomas.

**IV. OTHER PHARMACOLOGICAL AGENTS**

- **Ormeloxifene:** An estrogen receptor modulator given 60 mg twice a week for 3 months to reduce blood loss.
- **Desmopressin:** A synthetic analog of arginine-vasopressin. It is given IV (0.3 µg/kg) or intranasally, specifically indicated for AUB in patients with von Willebrand’s disease or factor VIII deficiency.
13. Indications for Hysteroscopy.

**DEFINITION** Hysteroscopy is an endoscopic operative procedure that allows for the direct visualization of the endometrial cavity and tubal ostia using a fiberoptic telescope. It serves as the "gold standard" for evaluating the uterine cavity.

The indications for hysteroscopy are broadly classified into diagnostic and operative (therapeutic) purposes.

**I. DIAGNOSTIC INDICATIONS** Diagnostic hysteroscopy is typically performed as an outpatient procedure to evaluate the interior of the uterus. The indications include:

- **Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (AUB):** It is highly indicated in cases of menorrhagia, postmenopausal bleeding, or unresponsive irregular uterine bleeding to definitively exclude or diagnose endometrial polyps, submucous fibroids, or retained products of conception.
- **Infertility Workup:** Indicated especially when a hysterosalpingogram (HSG) reveals abnormalities such as filling defects or uterine synechiae.
- **Recurrent Miscarriage:** To directly diagnose underlying intrauterine pathology that may cause recurrent pregnancy loss, such as submucous fibroids, polyps, or Asherman's syndrome.
- **Müllerian Anomalies:** For the precise diagnosis and differentiation of congenital uterine malformations like arcuate, subseptate, septate, bicornuate, or uterus didelphys.
- **Missing IUCD Threads / Misplaced IUD:** To locate and verify the position of an intrauterine contraceptive device when its strings are not visible through the external cervical os.
- **Intrauterine Adhesions:** To diagnose the presence and extent of uterine synechiae (Asherman's syndrome).
- **Chronic Pelvic Pain:** To identify obstructive uterine anomalies, bicornuate uterus, or submucous fibroids contributing to pain.
- **Evaluation of the Transformation Zone:** Microcolpohysteroscopy is utilized to visualize the transformation zone of the cervix when routine colposcopic findings are unsatisfactory.
- **Vascular Abnormalities:** To diagnose endometrial hemangiomas and arteriovenous (AV) malformations.

**II. OPERATIVE (THERAPEUTIC) INDICATIONS** Operative hysteroscopy uses specialized instruments passed through the hysteroscope to treat diagnosed intrauterine conditions, often preventing the need for more invasive surgeries like laparotomy or hysterectomy.

- **Polypectomy and Myomectomy:** The targeted removal of endometrial polyps and the transcervical resection of submucous leiomyomas.
- **Adhesiolysis (Synaecholysis):** The division and lysis of intrauterine adhesions in patients with Asherman’s syndrome to restore normal cavity anatomy.
- **Endometrial Ablation and Resection:** Used as an alternative to hysterectomy in patients with intractable dysfunctional uterine bleeding (DUB). Techniques include laser ablation, roller-ball endometrial ablation, and transcervical resection of the endometrium (TCRE).
- **Metroplasty:** The hysteroscopic resection of a congenital uterine septum, generally performed under concurrent laparoscopic guidance.
- **Removal of Foreign Bodies:** Extraction of a retained or misplaced IUCD or other foreign bodies when the threads are missing.
- **Targeted Endometrial Biopsy:** Obtaining an endometrial biopsy under direct vision from a specific, suspected offending site.
- **Proximal Tubal Cannulation:** Passing a special catheter through the tubal ostium to release proximal fallopian tube obstructions caused by mucus plugs or tubal spasm.
- **Transcervical Sterilization:** Achieving permanent tubal occlusion by inserting microcoils (e.g., Essure) into the proximal part of the fallopian tubes, or by destroying the interstitial portion of the tubes using Nd:YAG laser or electrocoagulation.
- **Vascular Coagulation:** Laser coagulation to treat endometrial hemangiomas or AV malformations causing unresponsive uterine bleeding.

**III. LEVELS OF HYSTEROSCOPIC PROCEDURES (RCOG Classification)** The Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RCOG) grades hysteroscopic procedures based on surgical complexity:

- **Level 1 (Diagnostic and Simple Procedures):** Diagnostic hysteroscopy with targeted biopsy, removal of a simple endometrial polyp, and removal of an IUCD.
- **Level 2 (Minor Operative Procedures):** Proximal fallopian tube cannulation, minor adhesiolysis for mild Asherman's syndrome, and the removal of a pedunculated fibroid or a large polyp.
- **Level 3 (Complex Operative Procedures):** Resection of a uterine septum (metroplasty), major synaecholysis for severe Asherman's syndrome, transcervical resection of the endometrium (TCRE), and the resection of submucous leiomyomas.

14. Transformation zone.
**TRANSFORMATION ZONE**

**DEFINITION AND ANATOMY**

- **Squamocolumnar Junction (SCJ):** It is the meeting point of the columnar epithelium (which lines the endocervical canal) with the stratified squamous epithelium (which lines the ectocervix).
- **Transformation Zone (TZ):** It is defined as the area of metaplastic squamous epithelium situated between the original squamocolumnar junction (OSCJ) and the active squamocolumnar junction (ASCJ).

**DYNAMICS OF SQUAMOCOLUMNAR JUNCTION** The SCJ is not a static anatomical landmark but a highly dynamic point that moves up and down the cervical canal in response to different phases of life, driven by ovarian hormonal and environmental influences.

- **Childhood/Before Puberty:** The ectocervix is covered predominantly with squamous epithelium.
- **Reproductive Period (Puberty & Pregnancy):** Heightened estrogenic activity causes the columnar epithelium to evert onto the ectocervix, placing the OSCJ on the vaginal portion of the cervix. During pregnancy, there is further eversion.
- **Postmenopausal Period:** Due to a lack of estrogen, the SCJ and the transformation zone recede (are drawn inward) high up into the endocervical canal.

**PATHOGENESIS AND METAPLASIA** The transformation zone is in a continuous dynamic state. The replacement of endocervical columnar epithelium by squamous epithelium to form the TZ occurs via two mechanisms:

1. **Squamous metaplasia** of the subcolumnar reserve cells.
2. **Squamous epidermalization**, which is the ingrowth of the mature squamous epithelium of the ectocervix underneath the columnar epithelium.

- **Trigger Mechanism:** In the presence of estrogen, the vaginal epithelium accumulates glycogen. Vaginal lactobacilli act on this glycogen to produce lactic acid. The resulting acidic vaginal pH acts as a crucial trigger for the metaplastic process.

**CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE (HIGH YIELD)**

- **Site of Oncogenesis:** Cervical neoplasia, including Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia (CIN) and invasive squamous cell carcinoma, almost always originates from the transformation zone. The process of carcinogenesis starts specifically at this zone.
- **Vulnerability:** The cervical reserve cells and the immature, unstable metaplastic cells within the TZ are highly vulnerable to the oncogenic effects of the Human Papillomavirus (HPV) and other carcinogens.
- **Period of Maximum Risk:** Metaplastic change is most active during adolescence and pregnancy. Consequently, early age of sexual activity and coital microtrauma (which facilitates viral entry into the basal or parabasal cells of the TZ) are high-risk factors for the development of cervical cancer.

**EVALUATION AND MANAGEMENT**

- **Cervical Cytology (Pap Smear):** For a cervical smear to be considered adequate, it must contain cells from the SCJ, the transformation zone, and the endocervix. Instruments like the Ayre’s spatula, cytobrush, or cervical plastic broom are specifically designed to effectively sample the TZ at the SCJ.
- **Colposcopy:** Colposcopy evaluates the morphological and vascular changes in the TZ under magnification (6-16 times) after the application of 3-5% acetic acid. A colposcopic examination is classified as "satisfactory" only if the entire transformation zone and the original SCJ are completely visualized. If the TZ extends high into the endocervical canal and is not fully seen, the colposcopy is termed "unsatisfactory," mandating an endocervical curettage (ECC).
- **Surgical Excision:** Premalignant lesions (such as CIN II and III) located within the TZ are effectively treated by excisional methods like Large Loop Excision of the Transformation Zone (LLETZ) or Loop Electrosurgical Excision Procedure (LEEP). These procedures excise the entire TZ for both therapeutic clearance and detailed histopathological evaluation.


15. Decubitus ulcer.

**DEFINITION** A decubitus ulcer is a trophic ulcer that is characteristically found at the most dependent part of a prolapsed genital mass lying outside the vaginal introitus.

**PATHOGENESIS AND MECHANISM**

- **Mechanism:** The ulcer develops due to diminished local blood circulation. This impaired circulation is caused by the constriction of the prolapsed mass by the tight vaginal opening and the narrowing of the uterine vessels secondary to the severe stretching effect of the prolapse.
- **Sequence of Events:** The continuously exposed and stretched vaginal mucosa undergoes initial surface keratinization $\rightarrow$ formation of cracks $\rightarrow$ superimposed infection $\rightarrow$ sloughing of the devitalized tissues $\rightarrow$ frank ulceration with complete denudation of the surface epithelium.

**CLINICAL FEATURES**

- **Discharge:** Patients often present with excessive white or blood-stained vaginal discharge.
- **Abnormal Uterine Bleeding:** It is a recognized cause of contact bleeding, intermenstrual bleeding, and postmenopausal bleeding.

**COMPLICATIONS**

- Malignant transformation (carcinoma) developing on a pre-existing decubitus ulcer is possible but is a very rare association.

**MANAGEMENT** The primary goal of management is to promote healing of the ulcer prior to any definitive surgical repair of the pelvic organ prolapse.

- **Evaluation to Rule out Malignancy:** Cervical cytology (Pap smear) must be performed routinely to exclude malignancy. If there is any clinical suspicion, colposcopy and a directed biopsy of the ulcer edge should be performed.
- **Reduction of Prolapse:** Manual reduction of the prolapsed mass is essential to relieve the venous congestion and restore local blood circulation.
- **Vaginal Packing:** The vagina is packed with a roller bandage soaked in an antiseptic lotion containing glycerin and acriflavine. Glycerin acts by its hygroscopic action to reduce local edema, while acriflavine controls the infection.
- **Hormonal Therapy:** Local application of estrogen cream is highly beneficial, especially in postmenopausal women, to improve the trophicity of the vaginal epithelium and accelerate healing.

16. Causes for Azoospermia.
**DEFINITION** Azoospermia is defined as the complete absence of spermatozoa in the ejaculated semen.

**CLASSIFICATION OF CAUSES** The causes of azoospermia are broadly classified into three main pathophysiological categories: Pre-testicular, Testicular (Non-obstructive), and Post-testicular (Obstructive).

**I. PRE-TESTICULAR CAUSES (Endocrine / Hormonal)** These conditions occur due to a failure of the hypothalamic-pituitary axis to adequately stimulate the testes, typically presenting with low levels of Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH) and Luteinizing Hormone (LH).

- **Hypogonadotropic Hypogonadism:** Lack of adequate gonadotropin stimulation to the testes.
- **Kallmann’s Syndrome:** A genetic disorder causing isolated gonadotropin deficiency.

**II. TESTICULAR CAUSES (Primary Testicular Failure / Non-obstructive)** In this category, there is an intrinsic defect within the testes leading to defective or absent spermatogenesis, typically characterized by elevated serum FSH levels and small testicular volume.

- **Chromosomal and Genetic Abnormalities:** Abnormal karyotypes are observed in 15% of men suffering from azoospermia.
    - _Klinefelter's Syndrome (47, XXY):_ A very common chromosomal cause.
    - _Y Chromosome Microdeletions:_ Deletions involving the long arm of the Y chromosome (Yq11) are seen in nearly 15% of men presenting with azoospermia or severe oligospermia.
- **Congenital/Developmental Defects:**
    - _Sertoli-Cell-Only Syndrome:_ Absence of germ cells within the seminiferous tubules.
    - _Agenesis of Seminiferous Tubules_.
- **Acquired Testicular Damage:**
    - Severe testicular atrophy or destruction of the tubular epithelium (e.g., secondary to severe infections, radiation, or toxins).

**III. POST-TESTICULAR CAUSES (Obstructive Azoospermia)** In these cases, testicular biopsy confirms normal spermatogenesis, but there is a physical blockage in the efferent duct system preventing the transport of spermatozoa into the ejaculate.

- **Congenital Obstruction:**
    - _Congenital Bilateral Absence of Vas Deferens (CBAVD):_ This is highly associated with genetic mutations; about 66% of men with cystic fibrosis exhibit CBAVD.
    - _Young's Syndrome:_ A condition characterized by epididymal obstruction associated with bronchiectasis.
- **Acquired Infection:** Obstruction of the efferent ducts following severe genital tract infections such as Tuberculosis or Gonorrhea.
- **Surgical / Iatrogenic Trauma:** Ligation or injury to the vas deferens following procedures like bilateral herniorrhaphy or an intentional vasectomy.


17. Non contraceptive benefits of OC Pills.
**NON-CONTRACEPTIVE HEALTH BENEFITS OF COMBINED ORAL CONTRACEPTIVE (COC) PILLS**

Combined oral contraceptives (COCs) are the most effective reversible method of contraception, and apart from preventing pregnancy, they offer a wide range of non-contraceptive health benefits. These benefits are broadly categorized into three groups: improvement of menstrual abnormalities, protection against health disorders, and prevention of malignancies.

**I. Improvement of Menstrual Abnormalities**

- **Regulation of Menstrual Cycle:** COCs are highly effective in providing good cycle control and regulating the menstrual cycle.
- **Reduction of Menorrhagia:** They reduce menstrual blood loss by about 50%, making them an effective treatment for heavy menstrual bleeding or functional menorrhagia.
- **Protection against Anemia:** By significantly reducing menstrual blood loss, COCs offer protection against iron-deficiency anemia.
- **Relief from Dysmenorrhea:** They reduce the severity of dysmenorrhea by 40% and are considered a reasonable first-line treatment, especially when contraception is desired.
- **Reduction of Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS):** COCs help in the reduction and relief of premenstrual tension and PMS symptoms, with newer formulations containing drospirenone being particularly useful for PMS and premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD).
- **Reduction of Mittelschmerz Syndrome:** By inhibiting ovulation, they relieve mid-cycle ovular pain (Mittelschmerz syndrome).

**II. Protection Against Health Disorders**

- **Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID):** COCs reduce the risk of PID by producing a thick, viscid cervical mucus plug that prevents the ascent of sperm and bacterial penetration. Additionally, they decrease the duration of menstruation, which creates a shorter interval for bacterial colonization in the upper genital tract.
- **Ectopic Pregnancy:** Because they prevent follicular growth and ovulation, the chance of ectopic pregnancy is significantly reduced.
- **Endometriosis:** COCs provide protection against endometriosis and are used therapeutically to cause endometrial decidualization and atrophy, thereby relieving pain.
- **Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome (PCOS) & Hyperandrogenism:** COCs are highly effective in treating hirsutism, acne, and alopecia associated with PCOS. The progestin component suppresses Luteinizing Hormone (LH), while the estrogen component increases Sex Hormone-Binding Globulin (SHBG), thereby reducing free active testosterone levels.
- **Functional Ovarian Cysts:** They suppress ovarian activity and gonadotropin levels, which protects against the development of functional ovarian cysts.
- **Benign Breast Disease:** COCs provide protection against benign cystic breast disease.
- **Fibroid Uterus:** Their use provides protection against the development of uterine fibroids.
- **Bone Health:** They increase bone mineral density and offer protection against osteopenia and postmenopausal osteoporotic fractures.
- **Other Systemic Conditions:** They offer protection against autoimmune disorders of the thyroid and rheumatoid arthritis.

**III. Prevention of Malignancies**

- **Endometrial Carcinoma:** Use of combined oral contraceptives reduces the risk of endometrial cancer by 50%. This chemoprotective effect persists for 10–15 years even after stopping the method following a use of 6 months to 1 year. COCs also prevent endometrial hyperplasia.
- **Epithelial Ovarian Carcinoma:** COCs reduce the risk of epithelial ovarian cancer by 50%, serving as an important primary chemopreventive measure by suppressing incessant ovulation (repeated ovulatory trauma to the ovarian epithelial lining).
- **Colorectal Cancer:** The use of COCs is associated with a 20% to 40% reduction in the incidence of colorectal carcinoma.


18. Missing threads of IUCD diagnosis and management.
**MISSING THREADS OF IUCD: DIAGNOSIS AND MANAGEMENT**

**CAUSES OF MISSING THREADS** Failure to visualize the strings of an Intrauterine Contraceptive Device (IUCD) through the external cervical os can occur due to several reasons:

- **Thread coiled inside:** The thread may be curled up within the cervical canal or the uterine cavity.
- **Thread torn through:** The thread might have snapped or broken off.
- **Expulsion:** The device may have been spontaneously expelled completely unnoticed by the patient.
- **Uterine Perforation:** The device may have perforated the uterine wall and migrated into the peritoneal cavity.
- **Pregnancy:** The thread may be pulled up into the uterine cavity by the enlarging growing uterus if pregnancy occurs.

**DIAGNOSIS AND INVESTIGATIONS** The primary step is to **exclude pregnancy**. The patient must be advised to use an alternative contraceptive method (like barrier methods) during the evaluation period.

**1. Ultrasonography (USG):**

- Transvaginal Sonography (TVS) and Transabdominal Sonography (TAS) are the preferred initial imaging modalities to detect the exact location of the IUD, whether it is inside the uterine cavity or displaced into the peritoneal cavity.
- 3-D TVS is highly informative for precise localization.

**2. Hysteroscopy:**

- Provides direct visualization of the uterine cavity to locate the missing thread or device, and concurrently allows for its removal.

**3. Uterine Sounding:**

- A uterine probe or sound can be gently passed into the uterine cavity to feel the device directly.

**4. Radiography (Plain X-ray):**

- If the USG is inconclusive or the uterine cavity sounding is negative, an anteroposterior (AP) and lateral view X-ray of the abdomen and pelvis is performed.
- A radiopaque probe (uterine sound) is introduced into the uterine cavity before taking the X-ray. If the device has perforated the uterus, its shadow will be seen distinctly separate and away from the radiopaque shadow of the uterine sound.

**5. Advanced Imaging:**

- Computed Tomography (CT) or Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is rarely needed but can be used in complex cases.

**MANAGEMENT AND PROTOCOLS FOR REMOVAL**

**A. If the Device is INSIDE the Uterine Cavity** The cervical canal is dilated if needed, and the IUCD can be removed as an outpatient procedure using any of the following methods:

- **Specially designed blunt hook** (e.g., Dutta's hook or Loop hook): Introduced into the cavity to blindly catch and pull the device out.
- **Hysteroscopic retrieval:** Removal under direct hysteroscopic vision is highly effective and minimizes trauma.
- **Uterine curette** or **Artery forceps**.

**B. If the Device is OUTSIDE the Uterus (Perforated into the Peritoneal Cavity)**

- **Copper-bearing devices:** Must be removed immediately as ionized copper induces an intense local inflammatory reaction, leading to dense pelvic adhesions and possible bowel injury.
- **Method of removal:** Operative **laparoscopy** is the standard approach. **Laparotomy** is rarely required, mostly reserved for severe adhesions or visceral complications.
- _Note:_ Inert devices (like the historic Lippes Loop) do not typically cause adhesions, but surgical removal by laparoscopy is generally advised for psychological reassurance and safety.

**C. If Associated with PREGNANCY** When a patient conceives with an IUCD in situ and the threads are missing/not visible:

- **If the patient desires to continue the pregnancy:** She must be thoroughly counseled regarding the high risks of continuing the pregnancy with the device inside (e.g., miscarriage, preterm labor, sepsis, placenta previa, abruption, and low birth weight). Pelvic USG is done to locate the sac and the device. The device is generally left alone and is expected to be expelled spontaneously along with the placenta during delivery.
- **If the pregnancy is undesired:** Manual Vacuum Aspiration (MVA) is performed to simultaneously terminate the pregnancy and extract the device.

19. Causes of Heavy Menstrual Bleeding.

**CAUSES OF HEAVY MENSTRUAL BLEEDING (MENORRHAGIA)**

**DEFINITION** Heavy Menstrual Bleeding (Menorrhagia or Hypermenorrhea) is defined as cyclic bleeding at normal intervals where the bleeding is either excessive in amount (> 80 mL) or prolonged in duration (> 7 days), or both.

**FIGO CLASSIFICATION (PALM-COEIN)** The International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) introduced the PALM-COEIN acronym to classify the causes of Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (AUB) into structural and nonstructural categories.

- **Structural Causes (PALM):**
    - **P**olyp (AUB-P).
    - **A**denomyosis (AUB-A).
    - **L**eiomyoma (AUB-L) - including submucosal and other myomas.
    - **M**alignancy and hyperplasia (AUB-M).
- **Nonstructural Causes (COEIN):**
    - **C**oagulopathy (AUB-C).
    - **O**vulatory dysfunction (AUB-O).
    - **E**ndometrial (AUB-E).
    - **I**atrogenic (AUB-I).
    - **N**ot yet identified (AUB-N).

**DETAILED ETIOLOGICAL CLASSIFICATION** Menorrhagia is a symptom of an underlying organic or functional pathology. The causes can be systematically grouped as follows:

**1. Organic Pelvic Causes**

- **Uterine Fibroid (Leiomyoma):** The most common benign solid tumor and the most common cause of heavy menstrual bleeding worldwide.
- **Adenomyosis:** Ingrowth of endometrium into the myometrium, causing an enlarged uterus and heavy bleeding.
- **Pelvic Endometriosis:** Ectopic functioning endometrial tissue in the pelvic cavity.
- **Intrauterine Contraceptive Device (IUCD) in utero:** A common iatrogenic local cause.
- **Chronic Tubo-Ovarian Mass / Chronic Pelvic Infection:** Inflammatory changes leading to pelvic congestion.
- **Tubercular Endometritis:** Typically in the early stages of the disease.
- **Retroverted Uterus:** Causes bleeding secondary to pelvic congestion.
- **Ovarian Tumors:** Estrogen-producing tumors, such as Granulosa cell tumors of the ovary.
- **Malignancies:** Carcinoma of the cervix and endometrial carcinoma.
- **Pregnancy Complications:** Conditions such as abortion or miscarriage.

**2. Systemic Causes**

- **Liver Dysfunction (e.g., Cirrhosis):** Leads to the failure of the liver to conjugate and inactivate circulating estrogens, resulting in hyperestrogenism.
- **Congestive Cardiac Failure**.
- **Severe Hypertension**.

**3. Endocrinal Causes**

- **Hypothyroidism:** Commonly associated with menorrhagia or oligomenorrhea.
- **Hyperthyroidism**.
- **Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS):** Results in chronic anovulation and unopposed estrogenic stimulation of the endometrium.

**4. Hematological Causes (Coagulopathies)**

- **Idiopathic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (ITP)**.
- **Von Willebrand's Disease:** A bleeding disorder affecting platelet adhesion and coagulation.
- **Leukemia**.
- **Platelet Deficiency (Thrombocytopenia)**.
- **Deficiency of Clotting Factors:** Specifically factors V, VII, X, XI, and XIII.
- **Anticoagulation Therapy:** Women actively taking anticoagulants like Heparin or Low Molecular Weight Heparin (LMWH).

**5. Functional Causes (Dysfunctional Uterine Bleeding - DUB)**

- DUB is defined as abnormal bleeding without any clinically detectable organic, systemic, or iatrogenic cause, resulting from a disturbed hypothalamo-pituitary-ovarian-endometrial axis.
- It is the most common cause (95%) of puberty menorrhagia due to anovulatory cycles leading to unopposed estrogen and endometrial hyperplasia.
- It is predominantly seen during adolescence, following childbirth and abortion, and in the premenopausal period.

**6. Emotional Upset**

- Psychological stress and emotional upset can occasionally cause functional menstrual disturbances manifesting as heavy bleeding.

20. Causes of Chronic pelvic pain.
**DEFINITION** Chronic pelvic pain (CPP) is defined as noncyclic (nonmenstrual) pain of 6 months duration or more, localized to the pelvis, anterior abdominal wall below the pelvis, or lower back, which is severe enough to cause functional disability requiring medical or surgical treatment.

**CAUSES OF CHRONIC PELVIC PAIN** The causes of chronic pelvic pain are broadly classified into gynecological and non-gynecological etiologies.

**I. GYNECOLOGICAL CAUSES** These are further subdivided into cyclic and acyclic causes.

**A. Cyclic Causes:**

- Intermenstrual pain (Mittelschmerz).
- Dysmenorrhea (Spasmodic or Congestive).
- Premenstrual syndrome.
- Pelvic congestion syndrome (Taylor syndrome).
- Endometriosis and Adenomyosis.
- Ovarian remnant syndrome.
- Uterine fibroids.
- Intrauterine Contraceptive Device (IUCD).

**B. Acyclic Causes:**

- Endometriosis and Adenomyosis.
- Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID) and Tubo-ovarian abscess.
- Uterine displacement (Retroversion, Prolapse).
- Uterine fibroid.
- Ovarian cyst (Functional or Neoplastic).
- Pelvic adhesions disease (secondary to PID, endometriosis, or postsurgical).
- Intrauterine device (IUD).
- Trapped or residual ovarian syndrome.
- Idiopathic causes (Pelvic varicosities, Psychosomatic).

**II. NON-GYNECOLOGICAL CAUSES**

- **Gastrointestinal:** Irritable bowel syndrome, Appendicitis, Constipation, Diverticulitis, and Adhesions.
- **Urological:** Interstitial cystitis, Urethral syndrome, Calculi, Chronic urinary tract infection (UTI), and Urinary tract stones.
- **Orthopedic:** Diseases of the lumbar bones, ligaments, and muscles of the lumbosacral region, and coccydynia.
- **Neurological:** Nerve entrapment or compression (differentiated by Cornett sign), Neuralgia (Pudendal, ilioinguinal, or genito-femoral nerve), and Pyriformis syndrome (due to the compression of the sciatic nerve by the pyriformis).
- **Musculoskeletal:** Muscular strain, myofascial pain, levator ani syndrome, fibromyositis, and Trigger points (hyperirritable areas within a muscle causing neuromuscular end plate dysfunction).
- **Hernias:** Inguinal, femoral, and obturator hernias.
- **Miscellaneous:** Physical, sexual, or psychiatric factors.

**SPECIAL HIGH-YIELD SYNDROMES ASSOCIATED WITH CPP**

- **Pelvic Congestion Syndrome (Taylor Syndrome):** Characterized by CPP, dyspareunia, and abnormal uterine bleeding along with pelvic venous congestion.
- **Trapped Ovarian Syndrome:** Due to the ovary intentionally left at the time of hysterectomy, causing pain due to tension within the developing follicle and periovarian adhesions.
- **Ovarian Remnant Syndrome:** Persistence of ovarian function and pain due to the remnant of ovarian cortical tissue left behind unintendedly following a difficult bilateral oophorectomy.

21. Human Papilloma Virus (HPV) Vaccine.
**HUMAN PAPILLOMA VIRUS (HPV) VACCINE**

**INTRODUCTION AND PURPOSE**

- HPV vaccines are developed from the capsid coat of the Human Papilloma Virus and possess high immunogenicity.
- They are prophylactic, not therapeutic, and are designed to produce local and humoral immunity against HPV infection.
- They are highly effective, preventing about 90% of cervical cancers and reducing the overall incidence of cervical cancer by up to 90–100%.
- They also offer protection against vaginal carcinoma, vulvar carcinoma, and genital warts (condyloma acuminata).

**TYPES OF VACCINES** Currently, three main types of vaccines are available:

1. **Bivalent Vaccine (Cervarix):** Protects against highly oncogenic HPV types 16 and 18.
2. **Quadrivalent Vaccine (Gardasil):** Protects against HPV types 6, 11, 16, and 18. It is highly effective in preventing both cervical cancer and genital warts (types 6 and 11 prevent 90% of condyloma).
3. **Nonavalent Vaccine (Gardasil-9):** Protects against nine HPV types: 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58. This vaccine can prevent Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia (CIN) II by 96%.

**MECHANISM OF ACTION**

- The vaccines induce the production of neutralizing antibodies (IgG, IgA) which work locally at the cervix by preventing the attachment of the virus to the cervical epithelium.
- The immune defense is type-specific and is effective only when given prophylactically.
- However, both the bivalent and quadrivalent vaccines offer some cross-protection against other high-risk HPV types (e.g., types 31, 33, and 45).

**TARGET POPULATION AND AGE**

- The vaccines are ideally given to girls aged 9–26 years (approved for school girls 12–18 years and women 16–25 years).
- The impact of the vaccine is greatest when administered to females who are not already infected with the virus. Therefore, it is highly recommended to be given to adolescent girls (9–13 years) before the onset of sexual activity.

**DOSAGE AND ADMINISTRATION SCHEDULE (WHO)**

- **Route:** Administered as an intramuscular (IM) injection over the deltoid muscle.
- **Females <15 years:** A 2-dose schedule is recommended at 0 and 6 months.
- **Females $\ge$15 years:** A 3-dose schedule is recommended at 0, 1 to 2, and 6 months.
- **Immunocompromised Women:** Should receive the full 3-dose schedule.

**SAFETY AND SPECIAL SITUATIONS**

- **General Safety:** The vaccines are safe, well-tolerated, and effective for at least 7.5 years.
- **Pregnancy:** HPV vaccination is not recommended during pregnancy. However, if administered inadvertently, it is not considered harmful in pregnancy.
- **Lactation:** The vaccine is completely safe to be administered during lactation.

**POST-VACCINATION SURVEILLANCE**

- Because the vaccines are type-specific and do not protect against all oncogenic types of HPV, regular screening with Pap tests (cervical cytology) must be continued even in vaccinated women.

22. Mention drugs used in induction of Ovulation.
**DRUGS USED IN INDUCTION OF OVULATION**

The pharmacological agents used for the induction of ovulation in females with dysovulatory infertility (such as anovulation, oligo-ovulation, or luteal phase defects) are broadly classified into three main categories based on their mechanism of action: drugs for direct stimulation of ovulation, drugs for the correction of biochemical abnormalities, and substitution therapy for underlying medical disorders.

**I. DRUGS FOR STIMULATION OF OVULATION**

**1. Selective Estrogen Receptor Modulators (SERMs)**

- **Clomiphene Citrate (CC):** It is a nonsteroidal triphenylethylene compound that acts as a weak estrogen and a potent anti-estrogen. It blocks estrogen receptors in the hypothalamus, thereby removing the negative feedback of endogenous estrogen. This increases the GnRH pulse amplitude, leading to increased secretion of FSH and LH from the pituitary, which stimulates follicular growth. It is typically given at a dose of 50–250 mg daily for 5 days.

**2. Aromatase Inhibitors**

- **Letrozole:** It is an aromatase inhibitor that prevents the conversion of androgens to estrogens in the ovarian follicles. The resulting hypoestrogenic state reduces negative feedback on the pituitary, leading to increased FSH secretion. The buildup of intra-ovarian androgens also enhances the sensitivity of the ovarian follicles to FSH. It is used as a primary drug or in clomiphene-resistant cases.

**3. Gonadotropins** Gonadotropins are used in hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, clomiphene-resistant cases, or in Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART) for controlled ovarian stimulation.

- **Human Menopausal Gonadotropin (hMG):** Extracted from postmenopausal urine, it contains a mixture of 75 IU FSH and 75 IU LH per ampoule.
- **FSH Preparations:** Include Purified urinary FSH (uFSH), Highly purified urinary FSH (HP), and Recombinant FSH (rFSH) which is administered subcutaneously.
- **Recombinant LH (rLH):** Administered subcutaneously to supplement FSH when LH levels are profoundly low.
- **Human Chorionic Gonadotropin (hCG) / Recombinant hCG:** Acts as a surrogate for the midcycle LH surge due to its structural similarity to LH. A dose of 5,000–10,000 IU is administered to trigger final follicular maturation and ovulation once the dominant follicle reaches 18–20 mm in diameter.

**4. Gonadotropin-Releasing Hormone (GnRH) and Analogs**

- **Pulsatile GnRH:** Administered via an intravenous or subcutaneous infusion pump in a pulsatile manner (e.g., every 90 minutes) to mimic physiological GnRH release. It is highly effective for women with hypothalamic amenorrhea or hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (e.g., Kallmann’s syndrome).
- **GnRH Analogs (Agonists and Antagonists):** Drugs like Leuprolide, Goserelin (agonists), or Cetrorelix (antagonist) are used in ART protocols to cause pituitary down-regulation. This prevents a premature LH surge, allowing exogenous gonadotropins to safely induce superovulation.

---

**II. DRUGS FOR CORRECTION OF BIOCHEMICAL ABNORMALITIES (ADJUVANT THERAPY)**

When anovulation is secondary to a specific endocrine or metabolic derangement, targeted adjuvant medications are used, often in combination with primary ovulation-inducing agents.

- **Insulin Sensitizers (For Hyperinsulinemia):** Women with Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) frequently exhibit insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia. **Metformin** (500 mg thrice daily) reduces hyperinsulinemia and hyperandrogenemia. When combined with clomiphene, it significantly increases the ovulation rate. **Pioglitazone** and **rosiglitazone** are also used in cases resistant to metformin.
- **Dopamine Agonists (For Hyperprolactinemia):** Elevated prolactin disrupts the normal pulsatility of GnRH secretion. **Bromocriptine** or **Cabergoline** normalise prolactin levels, restoring cyclic menses and ovulation in the majority (70-80%) of affected women.
- **Corticosteroids (For Androgen Excess):** **Dexamethasone** (0.25–0.5 mg daily) is used to suppress adrenal androgen production. It can be given as an adjunct to clomiphene citrate in cases of elevated androgens or elevated LH.
- **Growth Hormone:** Used as an adjunct in poor responders (especially elderly women) during gonadotropin therapy, as it mediates its action via Insulin-like Growth Factor-I (IGF-I) to improve follicular response.
- **Estrogen:** Conjugated estrogen (1.25 mg daily) may be added to clomiphene therapy to counteract its anti-estrogenic effects on the cervical mucus, which can hinder sperm penetration.

---

**III. SUBSTITUTION THERAPY**

Used to correct underlying systemic or endocrinological medical diseases that indirectly cause anovulation.

- **Hypothyroidism:** **Thyroxine (Eltroxin)** substitution is given for clinical or subclinical hypothyroidism.
- **Diabetes Mellitus:** Appropriate **antidiabetic drugs** to maintain euglycemia.
- **Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia (CAH):** **Corticosteroids** are administered to suppress endogenous ACTH and reduce adrenal androgen excess.
