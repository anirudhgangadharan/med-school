1. Define Abortion. Enumerate the causes of Abortion. Discuss the diagnosis and management of Septic Abortion.

**DEFINITION OF ABORTION** Abortion, or miscarriage, is defined as the termination of pregnancy resulting in the expulsion or extraction of an embryo or fetus weighing less than 500 g, or occurring before 22 weeks (154 days) of gestation. The expelled embryo or fetus is referred to as an abortus.

**CAUSES OF ABORTION** The aetiological factors of abortion are broadly categorised by the trimester in which they predominantly occur.

**First Trimester Miscarriage:**

- **Genetic Factors (50%):** Chromosomal abnormalities are the most common cause. The majority are numerical, such as autosomal trisomies, Monosomy X (45,X), and triploidy. Structural anomalies like translocations, deletions, and inversions are also seen.
- **Endocrine and Metabolic Disorders (10-15%):** Includes Luteal Phase Defect (LPD) leading to deficient progesterone, overt thyroid abnormalities (hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism), and poorly controlled diabetes mellitus.
- **Immunological Disorders (5-10%):** Autoimmune conditions, predominantly Antiphospholipid Antibody Syndrome (APAS), and alloimmune factors.
- **Infections (5%):** Transplacental infections which can be viral (Rubella, Cytomegalovirus, HIV), parasitic (Toxoplasma, malaria), or bacterial (Ureaplasma, Chlamydia, Brucella).
- **Unexplained (40-60%):** In a vast majority of early miscarriages, the exact cause remains obscure despite investigations.

**Second Trimester Miscarriage:**

- **Anatomical Abnormalities (3-38%):** Cervico-uterine factors such as congenital or acquired cervical incompetence, Müllerian fusion defects (bicornuate or septate uterus), uterine fibroids (especially submucous), and intrauterine adhesions (synechiae or Asherman's syndrome).
- **Maternal Medical Illness:** Chronic conditions like cyanotic heart disease and haemoglobinopathies.
- **Thrombophilias:** Inherited conditions (such as factor V Leiden mutation, Protein C or S deficiency, hyperhomocysteinemia) and acquired thrombophilias cause intravascular coagulation and thrombosis, leading to fetal loss.
- **Environmental Factors:** Cigarette smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, exposure to X-irradiation, and noxious agents or chemicals.
- **Paternal Factors:** Sperm chromosomal anomalies, such as translocations.

---

**DIAGNOSIS AND MANAGEMENT OF SEPTIC ABORTION**

**Definition** Septic abortion is defined as any abortion associated with clinical evidence of infection of the uterus and its contents. Clinical criteria for diagnosis include a rise in temperature of at least 100.4°F (38°C) for 24 hours or more, offensive or purulent vaginal discharge, and signs of pelvic infection such as lower abdominal pain and tenderness.

**Clinical Grading**

- **Grade I:** The infection is localised in the uterus (most common).
- **Grade II:** The infection spreads beyond the uterus to the parametrium, tubes, ovaries, or pelvic peritoneum.
- **Grade III:** Generalised peritonitis, endotoxic shock, jaundice, or acute renal failure.

**Clinical Features (Diagnosis)**

- **Symptoms:** The woman looks sick and anxious, experiencing high fever (>38°C) often accompanied by chills and rigors indicating bacteremia. Symptoms also include lower abdominal or chest pain, diarrhoea, vomiting, and an impaired mental state.
- **General Signs:** Persistent tachycardia (≥90 bpm), tachypnea (respiratory rate >20/min), and in cases of endotoxic shock, hypothermia (<36°C).
- **Pelvic Examination:** Reveals an offensive, purulent vaginal discharge, marked uterine tenderness, and occasionally a boggy feel in the pouch of Douglas indicating a pelvic abscess.

**Investigations**

- **Microbiology:** High vaginal or cervical swab taken prior to internal examination for aerobic/anaerobic culture, Gram stain, and antibiotic sensitivity. Blood and urine cultures are also mandatory.
- **Blood Tests:** Full blood count (leukocytosis), haemoglobin estimation, haematocrit, ABO and Rh grouping, coagulation profile, serum electrolytes, C-Reactive Protein (CRP), and serum lactate (≥4 mmol/L indicates tissue hypoperfusion).
- **Imaging:** Pelvic and abdominal ultrasonography to detect retained products of conception, physometra, free fluid, pelvic abscess, or foreign bodies. Plain X-ray of the abdomen and chest to rule out bowel injury or pulmonary complications.

**Management Protocol**

- **General Measures:** Mandatory hospitalisation and isolation. A thorough assessment to level the patient according to the clinical grading, alongside strict monitoring of vital signs and fluid-electrolyte balance.
- **Grade I Management:**
    - Initiate broad-spectrum antibiotics, analgesics, and sedatives. Administer blood transfusions if anaemia is present.
    - Evacuation of the Retained Products of Conception (ERPC) should be performed gently within 24 hours following the initial antibiotic therapy to remove the nidus of infection and control any bleeding.
- **Grade II Management:**
    - Intravenous broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy combining piperacillin-tazobactam or carbapenem with clindamycin to cover aerobic, anaerobic, and gram-positive/gram-negative organisms.
    - Early evacuation of the uterus following the initial dose of antibiotics.
    - Posterior colpotomy is indicated if the infection is localised in the pouch of Douglas and a pelvic abscess has formed.
- **Grade III Management:**
    - Intensive Care Unit (ICU) admission for supportive therapy targeting generalised peritonitis, endotoxic shock, or acute renal failure.
    - **Laparotomy** is indicated for: (1) Injury to the uterus or bowels, (2) Presence of an intra-abdominal foreign body, (3) Unresponsive peritonitis or suspected pus collection, (4) Septic shock or oliguria not responding to conservative measures, or (5) A uterus too large to be safely evacuated vaginally.
    - During laparotomy, removal of the uterus (hysterectomy) regardless of parity may be required, alongside adnexa removal based on the pathology, and thorough inspection of the gut for injuries. Simple drainage of pus can also be highly effective.


2. Enumerate the causes of breech presentation at term. Discuss the diagnosis, management of multi Gravida with breech in labour.

**BREECH PRESENTATION: CAUSES, DIAGNOSIS, AND MANAGEMENT IN LABOR**

**I. CAUSES OF BREECH PRESENTATION AT TERM** While prematurity is the most common cause of breech presentation overall, its persistence at term is often due to factors that either prevent spontaneous cephalic version, cause undue fetal mobility, or provide favorable adaptation for the breech pole.

**1. Factors preventing spontaneous version:**

- **Fetal:** Breech with extended legs (frank breech), multiple pregnancy (twins), short umbilical cord (relative or absolute), intrauterine fetal death.
- **Maternal/Uterine:** Congenital malformations of the uterus (e.g., septate or bicornuate uterus), oligohydramnios.

**2. Factors favoring adaptation:**

- **Fetal:** Hydrocephalus (the abnormally large head accommodates better in the wider uterine fundus).
- **Placental:** Placenta previa, cornu-fundal attachment of the placenta (minimizes fundal space, forcing the smaller cephalic pole to occupy it).
- **Maternal:** Contracted pelvis.

**3. Factors causing undue mobility of the fetus:**

- **Maternal:** Multiparity with a lax, pendulous abdominal wall.
- **Fetal:** Polyhydramnios, fetal abnormalities altering muscle tone and mobility (e.g., Trisomy 13, 18, 21, anencephaly, myotonic dystrophy).

**4. Recurrent (Habitual) Breech:**

- Recurs in 3 or more consecutive pregnancies (8-10% recurrence rate), usually due to uterine anomalies or repeated cornu-fundal placental implantation.

---

**II. DIAGNOSIS OF BREECH PRESENTATION IN LABOR**

**1. Abdominal Examination:**

- **Fundal Grip:** A hard, globular, and ballotable mass (the fetal head) is felt at the uterine fundus.
- **Lateral Grip:** The smooth, curved fetal back is felt on one side, and irregular small parts (limbs) are on the other.
- **Pelvic Grip:** A soft, broad, and irregular mass (the breech) is palpated at the lower pole. In a frank breech, it may be engaged; in a complete breech, it is usually not engaged.
- **Auscultation:** Fetal Heart Sounds (FHS) are typically heard at a higher level, around the umbilicus.

**2. Vaginal Examination (During Labor):**

- **Complete Breech:** Soft, irregular parts are felt through the fornix. The ischial tuberosities, sacrum, and feet can be palpated alongside the buttocks. The chance of cord prolapse is higher (5%).
- **Frank Breech:** A small, hard, and conical mass is felt, and the hard sacrum may be mistaken for the fetal head. The ischial tuberosities, anal opening, and sacrum are felt in a straight line. The chance of cord prolapse is lower (0.5%).
- _Differential Diagnosis:_ Breech must be differentiated from a face presentation. In breech, the anus and ischial tuberosities form a straight line, there is no sucking reflex, and hard alveolar margins are absent.

**3. Ultrasonography (Gold Standard):**

- Confirms the clinical diagnosis, determines the exact type of breech (complete vs. incomplete), estimates fetal weight, assesses the attitude of the head (flexed vs. hyperextended), localizes the placenta, evaluates liquor volume, and rules out congenital anomalies.

---

**III. MANAGEMENT OF MULTI-GRAVIDA WITH BREECH IN LABOR**

**A. Prerequisites and Pre-labor Assessment:**

- Vaginal delivery (Trial of Labor) is favored in a multipara if the following criteria are met: average estimated fetal weight (2.0–3.5 kg), adequate pelvis, flexed or neutral head, frank or complete breech, and absence of other obstetric/medical complications.
- Must be conducted by a skilled obstetrician in a center equipped with emergency cesarean facilities, an anesthetist, and a neonatologist.

**B. Management of the First Stage of Labor:**

- **Expectant observation:** Spontaneous onset of labor is preferred.
- **Vaginal Examination:** Essential at the onset of labor for pelvic assessment and _immediately after the rupture of membranes_ to exclude cord prolapse.
- **Supportive Care:** Establish an IV line with Ringer's solution, withhold oral intake, and send blood for grouping and cross-matching.
- **Analgesia:** Adequate pain relief is given; epidural analgesia is helpful for ease of maneuvers but may slightly increase the CS rate.
- **Monitoring:** Continuous electronic fetal monitoring (EFM) is preferred. Progress of labor is plotted on a partograph.
- **Oxytocin:** May be used carefully for labor augmentation if contractions are inadequate.
- _Indications for Emergency CS in the First Stage:_ Arrest of progress, fetal distress (non-reassuring FHR), or cord prolapse.

**C. Management of the Second Stage of Labor (Assisted Breech Delivery):**

- **Principle:** "Assisted breech delivery" is the method of choice. The cardinal rules are: **Never rush**, **never pull from below but push from above** (with fundal pressure), and **always keep the fetal back anterior**. Premature traction can cause deflexion and entrapment of the aftercoming head.
- **Position & Preparation:** Place the patient in the lithotomy position with a slight lateral tilt (15°) to avoid aortocaval compression once the posterior buttock distends the perineum. Empty the bladder using a catheter.
- **Episiotomy:** A liberal mediolateral episiotomy is performed when the perineum is distended to straighten the birth canal and prevent perinea! trauma and head compression.
- **Delivery of the Breech and Trunk:** Use a "no touch" policy while the mother bears down during contractions, allowing spontaneous expulsion until the umbilicus is born.
- **Cord Handling:** Gently pull down a loop of the umbilical cord and mobilize it to the sacral bay to minimize compression.
- **Delivery of the Legs:** If extended (frank breech), decompose the legs by applying pressure on the popliteal fossa (Pinard's maneuver principle) to cause flexion and abduction of the knee.
- **Delivery of the Shoulders and Arms:** Usually delivers spontaneously with the next contraction. If arms are extended or nuchal, use **Lovset’s maneuver** (rotating the fetal trunk keeping the back anterior to deliver the posterior arm first under the symphysis pubis, then reversing rotation for the other arm) or the classical method.
- **Delivery of the Aftercoming Head:**
    1. **Burns-Marshall Method:** The baby's trunk is allowed to hang by its own weight to promote head flexion, while an assistant applies suprapubic pressure. The trunk is then swept up and over the mother's abdomen.
    2. **Forceps Delivery (Piper Forceps):** Specially designed forceps applied to the aftercoming head to provide controlled traction and protect the head from compression-decompression injuries.
    3. **Malar Flexion and Shoulder Traction (Mauriceau-Smellie-Veit maneuver):** The baby rests on the operator's forearm. The index and middle fingers of one hand are placed on the fetal maxilla (malar bones) to maintain flexion, while the other hand hooks over the fetal shoulders for downward and backward traction until the nape of the neck appears, followed by upward elevation.

**D. Management of the Third Stage of Labor:**

- Active Management of the Third Stage of Labor (AMTSL) is mandatory due to the increased risk of postpartum hemorrhage from prolonged labor or manipulation.
- Administer Injection Oxytocin 10 IU IM or Methergine 0.2 mg IV immediately following the delivery of the baby.
- Carefully inspect the genital tract and repair the episiotomy or any lacerations.
- Prompt neonatal resuscitation by the pediatrician if birth asphyxia is present.


3. Fetal circulation.
**FETAL CIRCULATION**

**Introduction** The fetal circulation differs significantly from the adult circulation because the lungs are non-functional and the placenta serves as the organ for gaseous exchange and nutrition. The fetal cardiovascular system is designed to distribute the most highly oxygenated blood to the vital organs (heart and brain) while bypassing the non-functional lungs and liver. The mean cardiac output in the fetus is comparatively high, estimated to be 350 mL/kg/min.

**Course of Fetal Blood Circulation**

**1. From Placenta to the Fetal Liver and IVC:**

- Oxygenated blood (80% saturated) from the placenta is carried by a single **umbilical vein** which enters the fetus at the umbilicus and runs along the free margin of the falciform ligament of the liver.
- In the liver, it gives off branches to the left lobe and receives deoxygenated blood from the portal vein.
- However, the greater portion of this oxygenated blood short-circuits the liver by passing through the **ductus venosus** directly into the Inferior Vena Cava (IVC).
- In the IVC, this blood mixes with the deoxygenated blood returning from the lower half of the fetal body, slightly reducing its oxygen content.

**2. Flow within the Heart:**

- **Right Atrium:** The mixed blood from the IVC enters the right atrium. Here, the majority of the well-oxygenated blood (75%) is preferentially directed by the valve of the IVC and the _crista dividens_ through the **foramen ovale** into the left atrium.
- **Left Atrium to Left Ventricle:** In the left atrium, it mixes with a small amount of venous blood returning from the non-functional lungs via the pulmonary veins. This blood passes through the mitral valve into the left ventricle.
- **Aortic Distribution:** During ventricular systole, the left ventricle pumps this highly oxygenated blood into the ascending aorta and aortic arch, distributing it to the heart, head, neck, brain, and arms.
- **Right Ventricle:** A smaller portion of blood (25%) in the right atrium, consisting largely of deoxygenated blood returning from the upper body via the Superior Vena Cava (SVC), passes through the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle. The right ventricular blood is 15–20% less saturated with oxygen compared to the left ventricle.

**3. Pulmonary Bypass and Return to Placenta:**

- **Ductus Arteriosus:** The right ventricle pumps its blood into the pulmonary trunk. Because the fetal pulmonary vascular resistance is very high, about 90% of this blood bypasses the lungs and passes directly through the **ductus arteriosus** into the descending aorta. The ductus arteriosus carries about 70% of the total cardiac output (60% from the right ventricle and 10% from the left) to the descending aorta.
- **Descending Aorta to Placenta:** The descending aorta supplies the lower half of the body. Ultimately, about 40% of the combined cardiac output is carried away by the two **umbilical arteries** (branches of the internal iliac arteries) back to the placenta for re-oxygenation and clearance of waste products.

---

**CHANGES IN FETAL CIRCULATION AT BIRTH**

The hemodynamics of the fetal circulation undergo profound and rapid changes immediately after birth. These changes are triggered by two main events: (1) cessation of the placental blood flow, and (2) initiation of pulmonary respiration.

The following anatomical and functional closures occur:

- **Closure of the Umbilical Arteries:**
    - Functional closure is almost instantaneous to prevent even a slight amount of fetal blood loss.
    - Actual anatomical obliteration takes about 2–3 months.
    - _Remnants:_ The distal parts form the **lateral umbilical ligaments**, while the proximal parts remain patent as the **superior vesical arteries**.
- **Closure of the Umbilical Vein:**
    - Obliteration occurs slightly later than the arteries, which allows a small extra volume of blood (80–100 mL) to be transferred from the placenta to the neonate (if clamping is delayed).
    - _Remnant:_ It completely obliterates to form the **ligamentum teres**.
- **Closure of the Ductus Venosus:**
    - Following the clamping of the umbilical vein, the ductus venosus collapses, leading to a fall in IVC and right atrial venous pressure.
    - _Remnant:_ It forms the **ligamentum venosum**.
- **Closure of the Ductus Arteriosus:**
    - Functional closure occurs within a few hours of respiration. The smooth muscle wall contracts in response to the rising oxygen tension in the blood flowing through it. This response is also mediated by prostaglandin levels.
    - Anatomical obliteration takes about 1–3 months.
    - _Remnant:_ It forms the **ligamentum arteriosum**.
- **Closure of the Foramen Ovale:**
    - Initiation of respiration causes a dramatic fall in pulmonary vascular resistance and a consequent increase in pulmonary blood flow returning to the left atrium.
    - This causes increased pressure in the left atrium, combined with decreased pressure in the right atrium (due to cessation of umbilical venous return).
    - This pressure gradient functionally closes the flap-like valve of the foramen ovale soon after birth.
    - Anatomical fusion occurs in about 1 year. During the first few days, this functional closure may be reversible, which is why a newborn may appear temporarily cyanotic while crying due to a transient right-to-left shunt.

4. Define fetal attitude, lie, presentation and position with example.
**ATTITUDE**

- **Definition:** The relation of the different parts of the fetus to one another is called the attitude of the fetus.
- **Universal Attitude:** The universal and most common attitude is that of flexion. During the later months of pregnancy, the head, trunk, and limbs of the fetus maintain an attitude of flexion on all joints, forming an ovoid mass that roughly corresponds to the shape of the uterine ovoid.
- **Examples/Variations:** Exceptions to the universal flexed attitude occur when there is an extension of the head (resulting in a deflexed vertex, brow, or face presentation, depending on the degree of extension) or when the legs become extended, as seen in a frank breech presentation.

**LIE**

- **Definition:** The lie refers to the relationship of the long axis of the fetus to the long axis of the centralized uterus or the maternal spine.
- **Examples/Types:**
    - **Longitudinal Lie:** This is the most common lie, occurring in 99.5% of cases, where the fetal long axis is parallel to the maternal long axis.
    - **Transverse Lie:** The long axis of the fetus lies perpendicularly to the maternal spine or centralized uterine axis.
    - **Oblique Lie:** The fetal axis is placed at an acute angle to the maternal spine.
    - **Unstable Lie:** The lie is unstable and constantly changes from one presentation to another even beyond 36 weeks of pregnancy, until labor sets in.

**PRESENTATION**

- **Definition:** The part of the fetus which occupies the lower pole of the uterus (pelvic brim) is called the presentation of the fetus.
- _Note on Presenting Part:_ Closely related is the "presenting part," defined as the part of the presentation that overlies the internal os and is felt by the examining finger through the cervical opening.
- **Examples/Types:**
    - **Cephalic Presentation:** The most common presentation (96.5%). Depending on the attitude of the head, the presenting part may be the vertex (well-flexed), brow (partially extended), or face (fully extended).
    - **Podalic (Breech) Presentation:** Occurs in about 3% of cases. The presenting part can be the buttocks and feet (complete breech) or just the buttocks with extended legs (frank breech).
    - **Shoulder Presentation:** Occurs in about 0.5% of cases, associated with a transverse or oblique lie.
    - **Compound Presentation:** Occurs when more than one part of the fetus presents together, such as the head presenting alongside a hand.

**POSITION**

- **Definition:** It is the relation of the denominator (an arbitrary bony fixed point on the presenting part) to the different quadrants of the maternal pelvis.
- **Explanation:** For descriptive purposes, the maternal pelvis is divided into equal segments of 45 degrees, creating theoretically 8 positions for each presenting part. Anterior, posterior, right, or left position is determined in relation to the maternal pelvis with the mother in the erect position.
- **Examples:**
    - In a vertex presentation, the denominator is the **occiput**.
    - **Left Occipitoanterior (LOA):** The occiput occupies the left anterior quadrant of the maternal pelvis. This is the most common position and is known as the first vertex position.
    - **Right Occipitoanterior (ROA):** The occiput is in the right anterior quadrant (second vertex position).
    - **Right Occipitoposterior (ROP):** The occiput is in the right posterior quadrant (third vertex position).
    - Other examples based on different denominators include the **mentum (chin)** in a face presentation (e.g., Left Mentoanterior), the **frontal eminence** in a brow presentation, and the **sacrum** in a breech presentation (e.g., Left Sacroanterior).


5. Diagnosis and management of Hyperemesis gravidarum.
**DIAGNOSIS OF HYPEREMESIS GRAVIDARUM (HG)**

**Definition** Hyperemesis gravidarum is a severe type of vomiting in pregnancy that has a deleterious effect on the mother's health and/or incapacitates her in day-to-day activities. The diagnosis is characterized by a triad of adverse effects: >5% loss of pre-pregnancy weight, dehydration, and electrolyte imbalance.

**Clinical Features (Symptoms & Signs)**

- **Onset:** Usually insidious, mostly limited to the first trimester (peak around 9 weeks) and predominantly seen in nulliparous women.
- **Symptoms:** Vomiting occurs throughout the day with increasing frequency and retching. Normal day-to-day activities are curtailed. The patient may also experience oliguria, epigastric pain, and constipation.
- **Signs of Dehydration & Ketoacidosis:** The patient looks ill with a dry coated tongue, sunken eyes, and an acetone smell in the breath.
- **Vitals:** Tachycardia, hypotension, and a rise in temperature are noted. Jaundice is a late and rare feature.

**Investigations** The diagnosis requires confirming the pregnancy and excluding other medical, surgical, or gynecological causes.

- **Urine Analysis:** Shows diminished quantity, high color, and the presence of ketonuria.
- **Blood Biochemistry:** Reveals hyponatremia, hypokalemia, low serum urea, raised hematocrit (due to hemoconcentration), and metabolic alkalosis (from loss of hydrochloric acid) or metabolic acidosis in severe cases.
- **Ultrasonography (USG):** Essential to confirm a viable intrauterine pregnancy and to rule out obstetric causes of severe vomiting such as multiple pregnancy and hydatidiform mole, as well as other pelvic pathologies.
- **Endocrine Tests:** Serum TSH, T3, and free T4 are evaluated, as 60% of women may suffer from a transient phase of clinical or subclinical hyperthyroidism.
- **Other Tests:** ECG is required if serum potassium is abnormal. Ophthalmoscopic examination is needed in seriously ill patients to rule out retinal hemorrhage or detachment.

---

**MANAGEMENT OF HYPEREMESIS GRAVIDARUM**

**Principles of Management** The core principles include maintaining hydration, controlling vomiting, correcting fluid and electrolyte imbalances, rectifying metabolic disturbances (acidosis or alkalosis), and preventing serious complications. Management requires a multidisciplinary team involving an obstetrician, physician, psychiatrist, nutritionist, and midwife.

**1. Indications for Hospitalization** Patients must be admitted if they exhibit protracted nausea and vomiting of pregnancy (NVP) despite oral antiemetics, continued NVP with ketonuria and/or weight loss >5%, or if any medical/surgical comorbidity is present.

**2. Fluid and Nutritional Therapy**

- **Intravenous Fluids:** Oral feeding is strictly withheld for at least 24 hours after the cessation of vomiting. Fast hydration IV therapy (1–1.5 L) with normal saline and potassium (if not contraindicated) is given. This rectifies dehydration, ketoacidosis, and electrolyte imbalance.
- **Enteral Nutrition:** Feeding through a nasogastric tube may be administered if necessary.
- **Dietary Resumption:** Before omitting IV fluids, oral feeding is gradually reintroduced starting with dry carbohydrate foods (like biscuits, bread, and toast) in small, frequent feeds. Gradually, a full diet is restored.

**3. Pharmacological Management (Antiemetics & Drugs)**

- **First-line therapy:** Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine 25 mg) and doxylamine (25 mg) is a safe and effective combination. Other first-line options include cyclizine, prochlorperazine, promethazine, and chlorpromazine.
- **Second-line therapy:** Metoclopramide (stimulates motility without stimulating secretions), domperidone, and ondansetron.
- **Third-line therapy:** Corticosteroids, such as Hydrocortisone (100 mg IV twice daily), used for intractable vomiting or cases with hypotension. Once clinical improvement occurs, it is converted to oral prednisolone (40–50 mg daily) and gradually tapered.

**4. Prevention and Management of Complications**

- **Neurological Complications:** To prevent Wernicke's encephalopathy (characterized by blurred vision, ataxia, confusion, and nystagmus), parenteral Vitamin B1 (Thiamine 100 mg IV daily) MUST be administered. Nutritional supplementation with Vitamins B6, C, and B12 is also given.
- **Stress Ulcers:** Managed with intravenous proton pump inhibitors.
- **Thromboembolism:** Women admitted with severe hyperemesis and hemoconcentration require thromboprophylaxis with Low Molecular Weight Heparin (LMWH).

**5. Nursing Care and Monitoring**

- Sympathetic but firm handling with social and psychological support is essential.
- A **Hyperemesis Progress Chart** should be maintained to assess daily improvement. Monitor daily pulse, temperature, blood pressure (at least twice daily), precise intake-output, urine for acetone and protein, and blood biochemistry.
- _Signs of improvement:_ Subsidence of vomiting, return of hunger, disappearance of acetone from breath and urine, and normalization of electrolytes, pulse, and blood pressure.
- Termination of pregnancy is rarely indicated with modern management.


6. Methods of Antepartum Fetal surveillance.

**ANTEPARTUM FETAL SURVEILLANCE**

**OBJECTIVES** The primary objectives of antepartum fetal surveillance are:

1. Assessment of fetal wellbeing and satisfactory growth.
2. Prevention of fetal death.
3. Avoidance of unnecessary interventions.

**METHODS OF FETAL SURVEILLANCE** The methods are broadly classified into Clinical, Biochemical, and Biophysical.

---

**I. CLINICAL EVALUATION** Clinical assessment is used as a primary screening tool at every antenatal visit.

- **Maternal Weight Gain:** Average weight gain is 1 kg a fortnight in the second half of pregnancy. Stationary or falling weight suggests intrauterine growth restriction (FGR), while excess weight gain may indicate pre-eclampsia.
- **Blood Pressure:** Hypertension impairs fetal growth due to reduced uteroplacental perfusion.
- **Symphysis Fundal Height (SFH):** Measured from the superior border of the symphysis pubis to the top of the fundus. A variation falling below the 10th centile or a lag of 3 cm or more suggests Fetal Growth Restriction (FGR).
- **Abdominal Girth:** Measured at the umbilicus; a diminishing girth beyond term or earlier arouses suspicion of placental insufficiency or oligohydramnios.

---

**II. BIOPHYSICAL METHODS** These tests screen for uteroplacental insufficiency. Fetal hypoxia leads to metabolic acidosis, causing CNS depression and altered fetal biophysical activities.

**1. Fetal Movement Count (FMC / DFMC)** It is an ideal first-line screening test started at 28 weeks. A healthy fetus should have a minimum of 10 movements in a 12-hour period.

- **Cardiff 'Count 10' Formula:** The mother counts movements starting at 9 AM and stops when 10 movements are perceived. Fetal compromise is suspected if there are <10 movements in 12 hours on two successive days, or no movement in 12 hours in a single day.
- **Daily Fetal Movement Count (DFMC):** Three counts of 1 hour each (morning, noon, evening) are taken. The total is multiplied by 4 to get the 12-hour count. Less than 10 kicks in 12 hours (or <3 per hour) indicates fetal compromise.

**2. Non-Stress Test (NST)** A continuous electronic monitoring of FHR along with recording of fetal movements (cardiotocography) to identify fetal wellness.

- **Reactive (Reassuring):** Presence of two or more FHR accelerations of >15 beats per minute above the baseline, lasting >15 seconds in a 20-minute observation window.
- **Non-reactive (Non-reassuring):** Absence of any fetal reactivity. It warrants further testing.

**3. Vibroacoustic Stimulation (VAS) Test** Uses an electronic larynx over the maternal abdomen to change the fetal sleep state from quiet (non-REM) to active (REM) sleep. A reactive NST following VAS indicates a healthy fetus.

**4. Fetal Biophysical Profile (BPP)** It considers 5 parameters (Manning's criteria) observed over 30 minutes using real-time ultrasound and CTG. Each normal parameter scores 2; abnormal scores 0.

- **Parameters:**
    1. **NST:** Reactive pattern.
    2. **Fetal breathing movements:** >=1 episode lasting >30 seconds.
    3. **Gross body movements:** >=3 discrete body/limb movements.
    4. **Fetal muscle tone:** >=1 episode of active extension with return to flexion.
    5. **Amniotic fluid volume:** >=1 pocket measuring 2x2 cm.
- **Interpretation:**
    - **8-10:** Normal, low risk of asphyxia.
    - **6:** Equivocal (suspect chronic asphyxia).
    - **<=4:** Abnormal, associated with fetal acidemia and requires delivery.

**5. Modified Biophysical Profile** Combines NST and the ultrasonographically determined Amniotic Fluid Index (AFI). It is abnormal (non-reassuring) if the NST is non-reactive and/or AFI is <5 cm.

**6. Ultrasonography (Fetal Growth & AFI)**

- **Fetal Growth Profile:** Serial measurement of Biparietal Diameter (BPD), Head Circumference (HC), Abdominal Circumference (AC), and Femur Length (FL). AC is the single best measurement reflecting fetal nutrition. An elevated HC/AC ratio (>1.0) after 34 weeks suggests asymmetric IUGR.
- **Amniotic Fluid Volume (AFV):** AFI <=5 cm or a Single Deepest Vertical Pocket (SDVP) <2 cm indicates oligohydramnios, associated with increased perinatal morbidity and placental insufficiency.

**7. Doppler Ultrasound Velocimetry** Evaluates downstream vascular resistance and fetal cardiac function.

- **Umbilical Artery (UA):** Normally, the Systolic/Diastolic (S/D) ratio, Resistance Index (RI), and Pulsatility Index (PI) decrease as pregnancy advances. Decreased, absent, or reversed end-diastolic velocity (AEDV/REDV) indicates increased placental resistance and fetal hypoxia.
- **Middle Cerebral Artery (MCA):** Hypoxemia causes cerebral vasodilatation (brain-sparing effect), seen as increased diastolic flow and a decreased PI.
- **Cerebroplacental Ratio (CPR):** Calculated as MCA PI / UA PI. A decreased CPR is a highly sensitive indicator of fetal compromise.
- **Ductus Venosus (DV):** Abnormal or reversed flow in the 'A' wave indicates impending cardiac failure and profound fetal acidemia.

**8. Contraction Stress Test (CST)** Based on observing the FHR response to uterine contractions (induced by oxytocin).

- **Negative (Normal):** No late or significant variable decelerations.
- **Positive (Abnormal):** Persistent late decelerations with 50% or more of contractions, indicating uteroplacental insufficiency.

---

**III. BIOCHEMICAL EVALUATION** Biochemical tests in late pregnancy are mainly done for the assessment of pulmonary maturity.

- **Amniocentesis:** Done to assess the Lecithin/Sphingomyelin (L/S) ratio (L/S >=2 indicates maturity), Lamellar body count (>30,000/µL indicates maturity), or the presence of Phosphatidylglycerol (PG) to rule out the risk of Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS).


7. Discuss causes and management of Polyhydramnios.

**POLYHYDRAMNIOS (HYDRAMNIOS)**

**DEFINITION** Anatomically, polyhydramnios is defined as a state where the liquor amnii exceeds 2,000 mL. Clinically, it is the excessive accumulation of liquor amnii causing maternal discomfort and/or requiring imaging to substantiate the fetal lie and presentation,. Sonographically, it is diagnosed when the Amniotic Fluid Index (AFI) is >25 cm or a single Maximum Vertical Pocket (MVP) is >8 cm.

**CAUSES OF POLYHYDRAMNIOS** The exact cause is often speculative, resulting from deficient absorption or excessive production of liquor amnii.

- **Fetal Anomalies (20%):**
    - _Central Nervous System:_ Anencephaly (seen in 50% of cases, causing excess fluid due to transudation from exposed meninges, absent swallowing reflex, and suppression of fetal antidiuretic hormone leading to excessive urination), open spina bifida.
    - _Gastrointestinal:_ Esophageal or duodenal atresia (prevents swallowing of liquor).
    - _Facial clefts and neck masses:_ Interferes with normal swallowing.
    - _Hydrops fetalis:_ Immune (Rh isoimmunization) and non-immune (cardiothoracic anomalies, fetal cirrhosis).
    - _Infections:_ TORCH and parvovirus B19 infections.
    - _Genetic/Chromosomal:_ Aneuploidy, genetic syndromes like muscular dystrophy.
- **Placental Causes:** Chorioangioma of the placenta (tumor of a single villus causing increased transudation),.
- **Multiple Pregnancy:** Up to 10 times more common, especially in monozygotic twins (usually affecting the second sac) and Twin-to-Twin Transfusion Syndrome (TTTS) where the recipient twin develops polyhydramnios.
- **Maternal Causes:**
    - _Diabetes Mellitus:_ Present in about 30% of cases. Maternal hyperglycemia leads to fetal hyperglycemia and fetal diuresis.
    - _Cardiac or renal disease:_ Causes placental edema leading to increased transudation.
- **Idiopathic:** No cause is found in 50-60% of cases.

**DIAGNOSTIC INVESTIGATIONS**

- **Ultrasonography (Gold Standard):** Identifies MVP >8 cm or AFI >25 cm, excludes multiple fetuses, identifies fetal lie/presentation, and screens for congenital malformations (CNS, gastrointestinal, skeletal),.
- **Blood Tests:** ABO and Rh grouping (to rule out Rh isoimmunization causing hydrops), Postprandial blood sugar/GTT (to rule out diabetes).
- **Amniotic Fluid Analysis:** Estimation of Alpha-Fetoprotein (markedly elevated in open neural tube defects) and genetic karyotyping if indicated,.

**MANAGEMENT OF POLYHYDRAMNIOS**

**Principles of Management** (1) Relieve symptoms, (2) Find out the cause, and (3) Avoid and manage complications.

**1. Mild Polyhydramnios (MVP: 8-11 cm)** Usually found in the mid-trimester and requires no active treatment except extra bed rest. It is generally transient and diminishes as pregnancy advances.

**2. Severe Polyhydramnios (MVP >16 cm)** Requires hospitalization in a high-risk center.

- **Supportive Therapy:** Bed rest with a back rest, and treatment of associated conditions (like diabetes or pre-eclampsia). Diuretics are of little value.
- **Indomethacin Therapy:** A COX-2 inhibitor that reduces fetal urine production. It is used for short periods for severe cases with preterm labor. Prolonged use is avoided as it can cause premature closure of the ductus arteriosus and neonatal renal anomalies,.

**Protocol for Uncomplicated Cases (No Fetal Malformation):**

- _Responsive to treatment:_ Pregnancy is continued awaiting spontaneous delivery at term.
- _Unresponsive with maternal distress (<37 weeks):_ Therapeutic amniocentesis (amnioreduction) is done to relieve distress and prolong pregnancy. Slow decompression (500 mL/hr) is performed until AFI is <25 cm. This can be repeated if fluid re-accumulates,.
- _Unresponsive with maternal distress (>37 weeks):_ Induction of labor is indicated. Amniocentesis is first done to drain liquor, check for a favorable lie, and establish a stabilizing oxytocin infusion. This is followed by a low rupture of the membranes (LRM),.

**Protocol for Complicated Cases (With Fetal Anomaly):**

- Referral to a maternal-fetal medicine unit is ideal. Termination is done irrespective of the period of gestation,.
- Amniocentesis is performed to drain fluid, followed by vaginal PGE2 gel insertion for cervical ripening, and low rupture of membranes.
- Sudden escape of liquor must be strictly controlled by placing a palm over the introitus to prevent placental abruption.

**Management During Labor:**

- Immediately following rupture of membranes, a careful internal examination is mandatory to exclude umbilical cord prolapse,.
- If uterine contractions become sluggish, an oxytocin infusion may be started.
- **Active Management of Third Stage:** To prevent postpartum hemorrhage (due to uterine atony from overdistension), intravenous methergine 0.2 mg or oxytocin is administered with the delivery of the anterior shoulder.
- Thorough neonatal examination to exclude structural malformations.

**3. Acute Polyhydramnios** Extremely rare, occurs acutely before 20 weeks, and presents with acute abdominal pain and vomiting. It is typically associated with monozygotic twins (TTTS) or chorioangioma,. Most often ends in spontaneous abortion. In TTTS, repetitive amnioreduction (until normal AFI) or laser ablation of placental anastomoses can improve perinatal outcome.



8. Medical nutritional therapy in Gestational Diabetes.
**MEDICAL NUTRITIONAL THERAPY (MNT) IN GESTATIONAL DIABETES MELLITUS (GDM)**

Medical Nutritional Therapy (MNT) is the cornerstone and first-line management for Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM). A joint review in the antenatal clinic with an obstetrician, diabetologist, and a dietician is essential for optimal care.

**Caloric Requirements** The daily caloric prescription is individualized based on the patient's pre-pregnancy weight and Body Mass Index (BMI) to ensure optimal fetal growth and prevent excessive maternal weight gain:

- **Normal weight women:** 2,000–2,500 kcal/day, which corresponds to 30 kcal/kg/day.
- **Overweight women:** Caloric restriction to 1,200–1,800 kcal/day, which corresponds to 24 kcal/kg/day.
- **Morbidly obese women:** Strictly limited to 12 kcal/kg/day.
- _Note on Lactation:_ Women who breastfeed postpartum should receive an additional 500 kcal daily in their diet.

**Macronutrient Distribution**

- **Carbohydrates (40–50% of total calories):** Complex carbohydrates (fiber-containing, low glycemic index foods) are strongly preferred. Simple carbohydrates must be avoided as they rapidly absorb and produce significant postprandial hyperglycemia.
- **Proteins (20% of total calories):** Adequate protein is required to meet the anabolic demands of pregnancy.
- **Fats (30–40% of total calories):** Saturated fat should be restricted to <10% of the total caloric intake. In obese patients, total fat consumption should be further curtailed.

**Meal Pattern and Distribution** To prevent starvation ketosis and avoid extreme fluctuations in blood glucose, the diet is divided into multiple smaller portions rather than a few large meals.

- **Schedule:** An average of three main meals and three snacks a day is highly recommended.
- **Calorie Allotment:** Usually, 25% of the total daily calorie intake is given at breakfast, 30% at lunch, and 30% at dinner. The remaining 15% is distributed evenly among the three snacks.

**Role of Exercise as an Adjuvant to MNT** MNT is significantly more effective when combined with physical activity.

- Aerobic exercises, such as brisk walking for 30 minutes daily (for at least 5 days a week), are safe and may obviate the need for insulin therapy.
- Specifically, walking for 10–15 minutes after each meal helps efficiently control postprandial glucose levels.

**Monitoring the Efficacy of MNT** Patients must be taught self-monitoring of capillary blood glucose using a reflectance meter (glucometer) 5 to 7 times a day to assess the success of the dietary regimen. The target glycemic values while on MNT are:

- **Fasting Plasma Glucose:** ≤95 mg/dL.
- **Pre-meal Glucose:** ≤100 mg/dL.
- **1-Hour Postprandial (PP):** <140 mg/dL.
- **2-Hour Postprandial (PP):** <120 mg/dL.
- **Night-time (2 am – 6 am):** ≥60 mg/dL.
- **Mean Average Glucose:** 100 mg/dL.
- **HbA1c Level:** ≤6%.

**Progression to Pharmacotherapy** If the capillary blood glucose levels frequently exceed the cut-off values (e.g., 1-hour PP >140 mg/dL or 2-hour PP >120 mg/dL) despite strict compliance with the prescribed diet and exercise, pharmacological intervention with Insulin or Metformin is recommended as the next step.



9. Expectant management in Placenta Praevia.
**EXPECTANT MANAGEMENT IN PLACENTA PRAEVIA (MacAfee and Johnson Regimen)**

**Aim and Objective** The expectant management policy was advocated by McAfee and Johnson in 1945. The primary aim is to safely continue the pregnancy for fetal maturity without compromising the maternal health, thereby improving fetal salvage and reducing undue maternal hazards.

**Vital Prerequisites** Before initiating expectant management, two strict prerequisites must be met:

1. Continuous availability of blood for transfusion whenever required.
2. Facilities for emergency Cesarean section must be available around the clock (24 hours), should it prove necessary.

**Selection of Cases (Criteria for Expectant Management)** A patient is considered suitable for expectant management if she fulfills the following criteria:

- **Gestational Age:** Pregnancy is less than 37 weeks.
- **Bleeding Status:** There is no active vaginal bleeding.
- **Maternal Status:** The mother is hemodynamically stable (Hemoglobin $\ge$ 10 g%, hematocrit >30%).
- **Fetal Status:** Fetal well-being is assured (reassuring FHR on CTG and normal USG).

**Conduct of Expectant Treatment**

- **Rest:** Complete bed rest (pelvic rest) is advised, with washroom privileges allowed.
- **Investigations:** Routine investigations like hemoglobin estimation, hematocrit, blood grouping, cross-matching, and urine for protein are conducted.
- **Monitoring:** Periodic inspection of the vulval pads is necessary to assess any ongoing blood loss. Fetal surveillance with ultrasonography (USG) should be repeated at intervals of 2-3 weeks.
- **Correction of Anemia:** Supplementary hematinics (oral or intravenous) should be given. Any significant blood loss must be replaced by adequate cross-matched blood transfusion if the patient is anemic.
- **Speculum Examination:** Once the patient is allowed out of bed (usually 2-3 days after the bleeding stops), a gentle Cusco's speculum examination is performed to rule out local cervical and vaginal lesions as the cause of bleeding.
- **Tocolysis:** The use of tocolytic agents (such as magnesium sulfate) can be considered if the vaginal bleeding is associated with uterine contractions.
- **Rh Prophylaxis:** Rh-immunoglobulin (anti-D) must be administered to all Rh-negative (unsensitized) women.
- **Cervical Cerclage:** The use of cervical cerclage to reduce bleeding or prolong pregnancy is not helpful and is not recommended (RCOG-2005).

**Hospital vs. Home Care**

- A hospital setting is the ideal environment for expectant management.
- However, considering the high cost of prolonged hospitalization and associated psychological morbidity, home care may be permitted in highly selected cases.
- **Criteria for Home Care:** The patient must be highly compliant, live close to the hospital, have 24-hour transportation available, assure strict activity restriction, and be well-motivated and fully understand the risks involved.

**Termination of Expectant Treatment** Expectant management is typically carried out until **37 weeks of pregnancy**, by which time the baby is considered sufficiently mature for delivery.

**Indications for Preterm Termination/Active Delivery:** Expectant management must be abandoned and delivery expedited (regardless of gestational age) under the following conditions:

1. Recurrence of brisk vaginal hemorrhage which is continuous.
2. Non-reassuring fetal status (fetal distress).
3. Intrauterine fetal death (IUFD). _Note: Repeated small bouts of hemorrhage are not an indication for termination; the blood loss can be replaced by transfusion, though the physician must monitor for the risk of Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR)_.

**Role of Corticosteroids:** If preterm delivery is anticipated and the duration of pregnancy is less than 34 weeks, steroid therapy (Betamethasone) is indicated to accelerate fetal lung maturation and reduce the risk of neonatal respiratory distress syndrome.


10. Complications of Eclampsia.

**COMPLICATIONS OF ECLAMPSIA**

The complications of eclampsia can be broadly categorised into maternal and fetal/neonatal complications. Maternal complications are notably higher in cases of antepartum eclampsia.

**I. MATERNAL COMPLICATIONS**

**A. Immediate / System-Specific Complications**

- **Injuries:** Tongue bite (usually occurring in the clonic stage), injuries due to falling from the bed, and bed sores.
- **Pulmonary:** Pulmonary edema (caused by leaky blood capillaries), pneumonia (aspiration, hypostatic, or infective), Adult Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS), and pulmonary embolism.
- **Cardiac:** Acute left ventricular failure, cardiomyopathy, and cardiopulmonary arrest.
- **Cerebral and Neurological:** Cerebral edema (vasogenic), cerebral hemorrhage, neurological deficits, and postpartum psychosis.
- **Renal:** Acute renal failure (AKI) and oliguria.
- **Hepatic:** Hepatic necrosis, subcapsular liver hematoma, and HELLP syndrome.
- **Hematological:** Thrombocytopenia and Disseminated Intravascular Coagulopathy (DIC).
- **Ophthalmological:** Disturbed vision or blindness due to retinal detachment or occipital lobe ischemia.
- **General/Others:** Hyperpyrexia.

**B. Postpartum / Obstetric Complications**

- Postpartum hemorrhage (PPH).
- Postpartum shock.
- Puerperal sepsis.

**C. Remote (Long-term) Complications**

- While the recurrence of eclampsia in subsequent pregnancies is uncommon, the chance of developing pre-eclampsia in future pregnancies is about 30%.

**II. FETAL AND NEONATAL COMPLICATIONS**

The perinatal mortality is significantly high, ranging from 10–20%. The primary causes and complications include:

- **Prematurity:** Either spontaneous preterm birth or iatrogenic (induced) due to the necessity of terminating the pregnancy.
- **Intrauterine Asphyxia:** Caused by placental insufficiency, placental infarction, or retroplacental hemorrhage (placental abruption).
- **Drug Toxicity:** Adverse effects on the neonate from the drugs used to control maternal convulsions.

11. Define Anemia and discuss the effects of Anemia on pregnancy.
**DEFINITION OF ANEMIA IN PREGNANCY**

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), anemia in pregnancy is defined as a hemoglobin (Hb) concentration in the peripheral blood of 11 g/100 mL or less, or a hematocrit of less than 33%.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) defines anemia as a hemoglobin concentration of <11 g/dL (hematocrit <33%) in the first or third trimester, or a hemoglobin concentration of <10.5 g/dL (hematocrit <32%) in the second trimester.

However, due to the physiological expansion of plasma volume resulting in hemodilution during pregnancy, a hemoglobin level below 10 g/dL at any time during pregnancy is generally considered anemia (WHO, CDC). A hemoglobin level at or below 9 g/dL mandates detailed investigations and appropriate treatment.

---

**EFFECTS OF ANEMIA ON PREGNANCY**

The effects and complications of anemia can be broadly classified into maternal and fetal/neonatal effects.

**I. MATERNAL EFFECTS**

**During Pregnancy:**

- **Pre-eclampsia:** Increased risk, potentially related to associated hypoproteinemia.
- **Infections:** Increased susceptibility to intercurrent infections, which in turn further impairs erythropoiesis by depressing the bone marrow.
- **Cardiovascular complications:** Angina pectoris and Heart Failure (especially at 30–32 weeks of gestation when plasma volume is at its peak).
- **Preterm Labor:** Increased incidence of premature onset of labor.
- **Other clinical manifestations:** Sideropenic dysphagia (Plummer-Vinson syndrome) and atrophic glossitis.

**During Labor:**

- **Uterine Inertia is UNCOMMON:** Contrarily, labor is often short due to a small baby and multiparity.
- **Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH):** A real and severe threat; even a minimal amount of blood loss can be disastrous for a severely anemic patient.
- **Congestive Cardiac Failure:** Very likely to occur during labor or immediately following delivery, as the blood from the uterine circulation is squeezed into the general circulation, putting an additional strain on an already hypoxic myocardium.
- **Shock:** A small amount of hemorrhage can precipitate profound shock or lethal hypoxia.

**During Puerperium:** There is a significantly increased risk of:

- Puerperal sepsis.
- Subinvolution of the uterus.
- Poor lactation / Failing lactation.
- Puerperal venous thrombosis and Pulmonary embolism.
- Poor wound healing.
- Postpartum depression.

_Note on Maternal Mortality (Risk Periods):_ An anemic pregnant woman is at a high risk of sudden death during specific periods:

1. At 30–32 weeks of pregnancy.
2. During labor.
3. Immediately following delivery.
4. Any time in the puerperium, especially 7–10 days following delivery (due to cardiac failure or pulmonary embolism).

**II. FETAL AND NEONATAL EFFECTS**

- **Iron Transfer:** The amount of iron transferred to the fetus remains largely unaffected unless the mother suffers from severe iron deficiency anemia.
- **Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR):** Significantly increased incidence.
- **Prematurity:** Increased rate of preterm delivery.
- **In Severe Anemia:** Leads to low fetal oxygenation, reduced amniotic fluid volume (oligohydramnios), and Intrauterine Death (IUD).
- **Neonatal Anemia:** Babies are at a higher risk of developing anemia in infancy due to reduced fetal iron stores.
- **Perinatal Loss:** The sum effect of prematurity, IUGR, and hypoxia leads to an overall increase in perinatal loss.
- **Effects specific to Megaloblastic (Folic Acid/B12) Anemia:** Increased risk of dysmaturity, abruptio placentae, and fetal congenital malformations such as neural tube defects, cleft lip, and cleft palate.


12. Discuss the management of post Cesarean pregnancy in labour.

**MANAGEMENT OF POST-CESAREAN PREGNANCY IN LABOR**

The management of a pregnancy with a prior cesarean delivery (CD) involves choosing between an Elective Repeat Cesarean Section (ERCS) and a Trial of Labor After Cesarean (TOLAC) for a Vaginal Birth After Cesarean (VBAC). The overall success rate of VBAC is 72-75%. The risk of a lower segment scar rupturing during labor is 0.5-1.0%, whereas a classical scar carries a 4-9% risk of rupture.

**I. HOSPITALIZATION GUIDELINES**

- **Elective Admission:** Women with a previous lower segment transverse scar should be admitted at 38 weeks of gestation for assessment and to formulate a delivery plan. Women with a previous classical or hysterotomy scar should be admitted at 36 weeks due to a higher risk of scar rupture in late pregnancy.
- **Emergency Admission:** Immediate admission is required if labor starts spontaneously, or if there are symptoms suggestive of scar rupture (acute abdominal pain, bleeding per vaginam, tenderness over the scar).

**II. SELECTION CRITERIA FOR TOLAC (TRIAL OF LABOR)** Careful selection is mandatory. Criteria for attempting TOLAC include:

- One or two previous lower segment transverse scars.
- Non-recurring indication for the prior cesarean section.
- Adequate pelvis for the fetus.
- Continuous labor monitoring is possible.
- Availability of resources (anesthesia, blood transfusion, and operation theater) to perform an emergency cesarean delivery within 30 minutes if needed.
- Informed consent of the woman.
- _Favorable predictors for success:_ Prior vaginal delivery, spontaneous onset of labor, admission cervical dilatation >4 cm, and estimated fetal weight of average size.

**III. CONTRAINDICATIONS FOR TOLAC (INDICATIONS FOR ERCS)**

- Previous classical, inverted T-shaped, or hysterotomy uterine incision.
- Previous more than two lower segment cesarean sections.
- Contracted pelvis or suspected cephalopelvic disproportion (CPD).
- Presence of obstetric complications (pre-eclampsia, malpresentation, placenta previa) or medical complications.
- History of prior uterine rupture.
- Patient refusal or limited resources for emergency CD.

**IV. INTRAPARTUM MANAGEMENT (TOLAC)**

**1. General Measures & First Stage:**

- **Onset of Labor:** Spontaneous onset of labor is desired.
- **Induction/Cervical Ripening:** The use of Prostaglandins (PGs) like PGE1 (misoprostol) for cervical ripening is strictly contraindicated as it significantly increases the risk of uterine scar rupture. Mechanical methods (e.g., Foley catheter) are considered safe.
- **Supportive Care:** An intravenous line is established with Ringer's solution. Blood is sent for grouping and cross-matching.
- **Analgesia:** Epidural analgesia is not contraindicated. It provides excellent pain relief, does not delay the course of labor, and does not mask the diagnosis of scar rupture.
- **Oxytocin Augmentation:** May be used selectively and judiciously if contractions are inadequate, but it increases the risk of uterine scar rupture and subsequent cesarean delivery.

**2. Monitoring of Labor:**

- **Progress:** Assessed by progressive descent of the head, rotation of the back/anterior shoulder, increasing flexion, and progressive cervical dilatation.
- **Fetal Monitoring:** Continuous Electronic Fetal Monitoring (EFM) is highly desirable. The most consistent finding of scar rupture (55-87%) is an abnormal CTG, notably variable decelerations, late decelerations, prolonged decelerations, or bradycardia.
- **Maternal Monitoring:** Watch for signs of scar dehiscence or rupture. These include: severe suprapubic pain (especially persisting between contractions), shoulder tip or chest pain, acute scar tenderness, abnormal vaginal bleeding or hematuria, cessation of previously adequate uterine contractions, maternal tachycardia/shock, and loss of station of the presenting part.

**3. Second Stage:**

- The second stage is typically cut short using prophylactic forceps or ventouse (operative vaginal delivery) to avoid undue bearing-down efforts and strain on the scar.

**4. Third Stage:**

- Routine exploration of the uterine scar after expulsion of the placenta is not mandatory for all. However, it should be done manually with two fingers in selected cases where there is continued excessive vaginal bleeding or maternal hypotension despite a well-contracted uterus, to detect asymptomatic scar rupture.

**V. INDICATIONS TO ABANDON TOLAC (EMERGENCY CESAREAN)** The trial of labor must be abandoned and an emergency cesarean section performed if any of the following occur:

- Arrest of labor or failure to progress.
- Incoordinate uterine action.
- Fetal distress (non-reassuring FHR).
- Clinical evidence of scar tenderness or overt scar rupture.

_Note: If overt scar rupture is diagnosed, urgent resuscitation (IV crystalloids, blood transfusion) and immediate laparotomy (repair or hysterectomy) are required._


13. Role of corticosteroid in preterm labour.

**ROLE OF CORTICOSTEROIDS IN PRETERM LABOUR**

**Indications and Timing**

- Maternal administration of corticosteroids is highly advocated in cases of preterm labour when the pregnancy is less than 34 weeks of gestation.
- Tocolytic agents are often utilized to delay delivery for at least 48 hours specifically to allow the glucocorticoid therapy to work and enhance fetal lung maturation.

**Mechanism of Action**

- Fetal cortisol is the natural trigger for augmented surfactant synthesis.
- Exogenous corticosteroids cross the placenta and act directly on the type II alveolar pneumocytes of the fetal lungs.
- They stimulate the synthesis of pulmonary surfactant (phospholipids), which lowers the surface tension of the lung fluid, allowing the alveoli to open easily and preventing alveolar collapse (atelectasis).
- Beyond the lungs, corticosteroids also accelerate the maturation of the fetal cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, and central nervous systems.

**Choice of Drug and Dosage Schedule** Betamethasone and dexamethasone are the preferred drugs as both cross the placenta and share identical, long-acting biological activity.

- **Betamethasone (Drug of choice):** 12 mg Intramuscularly (IM), given 24 hours apart for a total of two doses.
- **Dexamethasone:** 6 mg IM, given every 12 hours for a total of four doses.

**Therapeutic Benefits and Efficacy** The use of antenatal corticosteroids provides significant fetal and neonatal protection by reducing the incidence of several prematurity-related complications:

- **Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS):** Incidence is reduced significantly (by about 70%).
- **Neurological injuries:** Reduces the risk of Intraventricular Hemorrhage (IVH) and Periventricular Hemorrhage (PVH).
- **Gastrointestinal complications:** Reduces the risk of Necrotizing Enterocolitis (NEC).
- **Cardiopulmonary complications:** Decreases the risk of Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia (BPD) and Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA).
- **Overall Mortality:** Substantially decreases overall neonatal mortality.

**Duration of Action and Rescue Therapy**

- The clinical benefits are obtained 24 hours after the administration of the first dose.
- The optimal effect lasts for about 7 days, though some benefit persists for as long as 18 days.
- **Rescue Therapy:** A single rescue course of corticosteroids should be administered to a woman before 34 weeks if her first course was given more than two weeks earlier and preterm birth is again imminent.

**Risks and Side Effects of Antenatal Corticosteroids** While highly beneficial, corticosteroid therapy carries some maternal and fetal risks:

- **Infection:** May cause a flare-up of any pre-existing infection. It does not, however, increase the risk of maternal and neonatal sepsis in cases of Prelabor Rupture of Membranes (PROM).
- **Metabolic:** In women with insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus, it causes hyperglycemia requiring readjustment of the insulin dose.
- **Hematological:** Causes a transient increase in maternal White Blood Cell (WBC) count for about 48 hours.
- **Fetal:** Causes a transient reduction in fetal breathing and body movements.

14. Complications of caesarean section.

**COMPLICATIONS OF CESAREAN SECTION**

The complications of a cesarean section are related either to the operative procedure itself (inherent hazards) or to the anesthesia administered. They are broadly classified into maternal and fetal complications.

**I. MATERNAL COMPLICATIONS**

Maternal complications are further divided into intraoperative and postoperative (immediate and remote) complications.

**A. Intraoperative Complications**

- **Hemorrhage:** Can occur due to uterine atony, lacerations, or bleeding from the placental site.
- **Extension of Uterine Incision:** The incision may extend to one or both sides, potentially involving major uterine vessels and leading to severe hemorrhage or broad ligament hematoma.
- **Uterine Lacerations:** Lower uterine segment lacerations may extend laterally or inferiorly into the vagina.
- **Visceral Injuries:**
    - _Bladder Injury:_ Rare in primary cesarean sections but the risk increases significantly in repeat procedures. Requires a two-layer closure and continuous catheterization for 7-10 days.
    - _Ureteral Injury:_ Rare (1 in 1,000 procedures), usually occurring while attempting to control bleeding from lateral extensions.
    - _Gastrointestinal Tract Injury:_ Rare unless there are prior pelvic or abdominal adhesions.
- **Morbidly Adherent Placenta (Placenta Accreta):** Commonly encountered in patients with a history of prior cesarean deliveries presenting with placenta previa. It can cause massive, uncontrollable hemorrhage often necessitating an emergency peripartum (cesarean) hysterectomy.

**B. Postoperative Complications (Immediate)**

- **Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH) & Shock:** Blood loss in cesarean sections is often underestimated. Severe hemorrhage can lead to hypovolemic shock, particularly if the patient was dehydrated or in prolonged labor prior to surgery.
- **Anesthetic Hazards:**
    - _General Anesthesia:_ Risks include aspiration of gastric contents leading to aspiration pneumonitis (Mendelson's syndrome) or aspiration atelectasis.
    - _Regional (Neuraxial) Anesthesia:_ Risks include severe hypotension and post-dural puncture (spinal) headache.
- **Infections:** The risk of infection is higher in emergency operations or after prolonged labor/rupture of membranes. Common sites include the uterus (endomyometritis), urinary tract, abdominal wound, peritoneal cavity (peritonitis), and lungs.
- **Venous Thromboembolism (VTE):** Deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism are significantly more likely following a cesarean section compared to a vaginal delivery. Septic pelvic thrombophlebitis is also a known risk.
- **Gastrointestinal Complications:** Paralytic ileus following peritonitis, or mechanical intestinal obstruction due to adhesions and bands.
- **Wound Complications:** Abdominal wound sepsis is common. Manifestations include serosanguineous discharge or frank pus, hematoma, wound dehiscence (with intact peritoneum), burst abdomen, and rarely, life-threatening necrotizing fasciitis.
- **Secondary PPH:** Can occur in the late postoperative period.

**C. Postoperative Complications (Remote / Long-Term)**

- **Gynecological:** Menstrual excess or irregularities, chronic pelvic pain, and backache.
- **General Surgical:** Increased risk of incisional hernia and intestinal obstruction due to intraperitoneal adhesions and bands.
- **Future Pregnancy Risks:** Increased likelihood of scar dehiscence or uterine rupture during subsequent pregnancies or labor. There is also a significantly heightened risk of placenta previa, placenta accreta spectrum (PAS), cesarean scar ectopic pregnancy, and the need for repeat cesarean deliveries and potential peripartum hysterectomies.

**II. FETAL AND NEONATAL COMPLICATIONS**

- **Iatrogenic Prematurity:** Delivery before term when fetal maturity is miscalculated or uncertain.
- **Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS):** Higher incidence in neonates delivered via cesarean section, especially if performed before 39 weeks without prior labor.
- **Birth Trauma:** Accidental scalpel injuries (lacerations/cuts) to the fetus during the uterine incision, typically occurring on the scalp, buttocks, or thighs.
- **Intracranial Hemorrhage:** Can occur if there is difficulty or undue traction when attempting a breech extraction through a small or inadequate uterine incision.

**III. MATERNAL AND PERINATAL MORTALITY**

- **Maternal Mortality:** Though the overall rate is low, deaths are primarily caused by hemorrhage and shock, anesthetic hazards, severe infections, and thromboembolic disorders.
- **Perinatal Mortality:** Usually related to the emergency condition that necessitated the surgery. Causes include fetal asphyxia, RDS, prematurity, infection, and intracranial hemorrhage.


15. Causes of neonatal seizures.

**CAUSES OF NEONATAL SEIZURES**

A neonatal seizure is a paroxysmal manifestation of neurological dysfunction (affecting behaviors, motor, or autonomic function). It is usually a visible manifestation of an underlying pathology, primarily resulting from excessive depolarization of neurons within the central nervous system.

The etiological factors for neonatal seizures are broadly classified as follows:

**1. Traumatic and Hypoxic Causes (Most Common)**

- **Perinatal Asphyxia:** Impaired placental gaseous exchange leading to fetal hypoxemia, acidosis, and hypercarbia. Seizures typically appear within 12–24 hours of birth.
- **Hypoxic-Ischemic Encephalopathy (HIE):** Severe asphyxia leading to cerebral edema, infarction, and brain injury.
- **Intracranial Hemorrhage:** Includes subarachnoid, peri- or intraventricular, and subdural hemorrhages resulting from birth trauma or anoxia.

**2. Metabolic Abnormalities**

- **Hypoglycemia:** Blood glucose <40 mg/dL, often seen in premature infants, IUGR, or infants of diabetic mothers.
- **Electrolyte Imbalances:** Hypocalcemia, hypercalcemia, hyponatremia, and hypomagnesemia.
- **Kernicterus:** Bilirubin-induced neurologic dysfunction resulting from severe unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia (usually >20 mg/dL in a term infant) crossing the blood-brain barrier.
- **Pyridoxine (Vitamin B6) Dependence**.

**3. Infective Causes**

- **Central Nervous System (CNS) Infections:** Meningitis or encephalitis caused by organisms such as Group B _Streptococcus_ and _Escherichia coli_.
- **Intrauterine TORCH Infections:** Toxoplasmosis, Rubella, Cytomegalovirus, and Herpes simplex virus infections.
- **Tetanus Neonatorum:** Caused by the neurotoxin of _Clostridium tetani_ entering through the umbilical cord.
- **High Fever**.

**4. Inborn Errors of Metabolism**

- Pyridoxine-dependent seizures.
- Mitochondrial disorders.
- Aminoacidopathies.
- Organic acidurias.

**5. Iatrogenic and Toxic Causes (Drugs)**

- **Narcotic Withdrawal:** Seen in neonates born to narcotic-addicted mothers.
- **Drug Toxicity:** Such as accidental toxicity from theophylline.
- **Respiratory Stimulants**.

**6. Congenital and Other Causes**

- **Cerebral Malformations:** Structural brain defects developed in utero.
- **Chromosomal Syndromes:** Genetic anomalies predisposing to seizures.
- **Neonatal Epileptic Syndromes**.
- **Unknown/Idiopathic:** In rare instances, no underlying cause can be identified.


16. Twin to twin transfusion.
**TWIN-TO-TWIN TRANSFUSION SYNDROME (TTTS)**

**INTRODUCTION** Twin-to-Twin Transfusion Syndrome is a clinicopathological state exclusively met within Monochorionic Diamniotic (MCDA) twin gestations (complicating 10-15% of cases), and very rarely in Monochorionic Monoamniotic (MCMA) pregnancies. It occurs when one twin (the donor) bleeds into the other (the recipient) through placental vascular anastomoses.

**PATHOPHYSIOLOGY** The syndrome develops due to a hemodynamic imbalance caused by unidirectional deep arteriovenous (AV), arterioarterial (AA), and venovenous (VV) anastomoses in the shared placental vascular bed. This leads to a characteristic anemia-polycythemia sequence and "Poly-Oli syndrome" (polyhydramnios-oligohydramnios).

**CLINICAL FEATURES & FETAL EFFECTS** The diagnosis is suspected when the difference in hemoglobin concentration between the two twins exceeds 5 g% and the estimated fetal weight (EFW) discrepancy is 25% or more.

- **Recipient Twin:** Receives excess blood, becoming larger, polycythemic, hypertensive, polyuric, and hypervolemic. The excessive urination leads to severe polyhydramnios. This twin runs a high risk of developing congestive cardiac failure and hydrops.
- **Donor Twin:** Loses blood, becoming smaller (IUGR), anemic, hypotensive, oliguric, and hypovolemic. The reduced urine output causes severe oligohydramnios, which may cause the donor to appear "stuck" against the uterine wall.

**STAGING (QUINTERO'S STAGING)** Staging helps in assessing the severity and formulating a management plan:

- **Stage 1:** Polyhydramnios (Deepest Vertical Pocket [DVP] >8 cm) in the recipient and oligohydramnios (DVP <2 cm) in the donor, with a visible bladder in the donor.
- **Stage 2:** As in Stage 1, but with no visible bladder in the donor twin.
- **Stage 3:** As in Stage 2, plus abnormal Doppler indices (in the umbilical artery, umbilical vein, or ductus venosus).
- **Stage 4:** As in Stage 3, plus the presence of hydrops in either twin.
- **Stage 5:** Fetal demise of one or both twins.

**COMPLICATIONS & PROGNOSIS**

- **Perinatal Mortality:** Without intervention, perinatal mortality is extremely high (about 80%). The earlier the onset, the poorer the prognosis.
- **Congenital Anomalies:** The rate of congenital abnormalities, such as neural tube defects and holoprosencephaly, is 2-3 times higher.
- **Dead Fetus Syndrome:** If one twin dies, the surviving twin is at a 25% risk of poor outcomes, including cerebral palsy, multicystic encephalomalacia, microcephaly, renal cortical necrosis, and DIC, due to acute hypotension and thromboplastin liberation through the anastomoses.

**MANAGEMENT** Antenatal diagnosis is made via ultrasound combined with Doppler blood flow studies of the placental vascular bed. Management requires a specialized maternal-fetal medicine unit and includes:

- **Laser Photocoagulation:** The treatment of choice. It involves fetoscopic laser ablation to interrupt the anastomotic vessels on the chorionic plate, effectively treating the underlying cause.
- **Repetitive Amnioreduction (Amniocentesis):** Repeated drainage of excess amniotic fluid to control polyhydramnios in the recipient twin. This improves perinatal outcomes by treating the symptoms, although it does not cure the underlying vascular cause.
- **Septostomy:** Creating a deliberate hole in the dividing amniotic membrane to allow fluid equilibration between the two sacs.
- **Selective Reduction (Feticide):** Considered when the survival of both fetuses is severely at risk. The smaller twin generally has a better outcome, so the procedure may target the severely compromised twin to save the other.
- **Timing of Delivery:** Planned delivery is generally recommended between 32 and 34 weeks of gestation, following the administration of prophylactic antenatal corticosteroids.


17. Mention methods of medical termination of pregnancy.
**METHODS OF MEDICAL TERMINATION OF PREGNANCY (MTP)**

The methods of MTP are broadly classified based on the gestational age into first-trimester methods (up to 12 weeks) and second-trimester methods (13-24 weeks). Under each trimester, both medical (pharmacological) and surgical methods are employed.

**I. FIRST TRIMESTER TERMINATION (UP TO 12 WEEKS)**

**A. Medical Methods**

- **Mifepristone and Misoprostol (PGE1) combination:** This is a highly successful regimen. Mifepristone 200-600 mg orally is administered, followed in 48 hours by Misoprostol 200-600 mg orally or 400-800 µg buccally, sublingually, or vaginally. A commonly used combipack (approved up to 63 days) uses 200 mg oral Mifepristone followed by 800 µg vaginal Misoprostol after 6-48 hours.
- **Misoprostol (PGE1) alone:** 800 µg is given sublingually or vaginally every 3 hours for up to 3 doses.
- **Methotrexate and Misoprostol:** Methotrexate 50 mg/m² IM is given (used before 56 days of gestation), followed 7 days later by Misoprostol 800 µg vaginally.
- **Letrozole and Misoprostol:** Letrozole 10 mg PO daily for 3 days followed by Misoprostol 800 µg sublingually on the 4th day.

**B. Surgical Methods**

- **Vacuum Aspiration (MVA/EVA):** Manual (MVA) or Electric (EVA) Vacuum Aspiration is performed up to 12 weeks with minimal cervical dilatation. MVA uses a plastic Karman's cannula and a 60 mL double-valve syringe.
- **Suction Evacuation and/or Curettage:** Employs a suction machine fitted with a cannula (plastic or metal) to evacuate the products of conception.
- **Dilatation and Evacuation (D&E):**
    - _Rapid method:_ Dilatation and evacuation performed in one sitting, usually under diazepam sedation and paracervical block.
    - _Slow method:_ Gradual cervical dilatation is achieved by inserting laminaria tents (osmotic dilators) for 12 hours. Alternatively, vaginal Misoprostol 400 µg given 3 hours prior to surgery provides effective cervical ripening before evacuation.

---

**II. SECOND TRIMESTER TERMINATION (13-24 WEEKS)**

**A. Medical Methods**

- **Mifepristone and Misoprostol (PGE1):** Mifepristone 200 mg is given orally; followed 24-48 hours (or 36-48 hours) later by Misoprostol 400-800 µg vaginally or buccally every 3 hours up to 4-5 doses. This pretreatment significantly reduces the induction-abortion interval.
- **Misoprostol alone:** 600-800 µg vaginally followed by 400 µg vaginally or buccally every 3 hours up to 5 doses.
- **Dinoprostone (PGE2):** 20 mg vaginal suppository every 4 hours (for a maximum of 4-6 doses).
- **Letrozole and Misoprostol:** Similar to the first-trimester regimen, utilized for second-trimester terminations.
- **Concentrated Oxytocin:** 50 units of oxytocin in 500 mL of normal saline infused over 3 hours, followed by 1 hour of diuresis (no oxytocin). The dose is then sequentially escalated (150, 200, 250, and 300 units) in 500 mL normal saline.

**B. Surgical Methods**

- **Dilatation and Evacuation (D&E):** For mid-trimester terminations (14-24 weeks), wide mechanical cervical dilatation is necessary. Cervical priming must be done using medical methods (Mifepristone + Misoprostol) or osmotic dilators. Following adequate dilatation, the amniotic sac is punctured with a large bore cannula, and the fetus and placenta are extracted in parts.
- **Hysterotomy:** This involves extracting the products of conception by cutting through the anterior abdominal and uterine wall. It is rarely performed today, but is indicated in cases of failed medical MTP or when D&E cannot be safely performed (e.g., lower segment fibroids, uterine anomalies, or completely low-lying placenta).


18. Janani Surakha Yojana.


**JANANI SURAKSHA YOJANA (JSY) & JANANI SHISHU SURAKSHA KARYAKRAM (JSSK)**

**Introduction**

- Janani Suraksha Yojana (JSY) is a safe motherhood intervention scheme implemented under the umbrella of the National Rural Health Mission (NRHM), now National Health Mission (NHM), which has been instrumental since 2007.
- Building upon this, the Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram (JSSK) was introduced as a key health program in Maternal and Child Health Care by the Government of India in 2011.

**Primary Objectives**

- **Promote Institutional Deliveries:** The core aim of the scheme is to provide support to pregnant women to encourage institutional deliveries, which has led to a significant rise in hospital births and a reduction in maternal and perinatal mortality.
- **Targeted State Focus:** The scheme implements interventions across all states and Union Territories (UTs) with a special focus on Low-Performing States (LPS) that have historically low institutional delivery rates. These LPS include Uttar Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Bihar, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Assam, Rajasthan, Odisha, and Jammu and Kashmir.

**Implementation and Key Features**

- **Reduction of Maternal Deaths:** JSY and JSSK represent successful government initiatives, working alongside the Safe Motherhood Programme and Reproductive and Child Health (RCH) Programme, to effectively reduce maternal deaths.
- **Role of ASHA:** The Accredited Social Health Activist (ASHA) plays a pivotal role in the scheme's execution at the village level. She arranges escorts and transportation to ensure the pregnant woman or sick child reaches the First Referral Unit (FRU) or health facility safely.
- **Coordinated Grassroots Care:** Under the JSSK scheme, ASHAs work in close coordination with Anganwadi Workers (AWWs) and Traditional Birth Attendants (TRBAs) to provide comprehensive services and ensure beneficiaries receive the intended care.



19. Advantages and disadvantages of vacuum delivery.
**ADVANTAGES AND DISADVANTAGES OF VACUUM (VENTOUSE) DELIVERY**

**I. ADVANTAGES OF VACUUM DELIVERY (OVER FORCEPS)**

- **Facilitates Autorotation:** The ventouse can be applied to an unrotated or malrotated fetal head (such as occipitoposterior or occipitotransverse positions) and actively helps in autorotation during descent.
- **Non-Space-Occupying:** Unlike forceps blades, the vacuum cup is not a space-occupying device within the maternal pelvis.
- **Lesser Traction Force:** The traction force required to effect delivery is significantly less (approximately 10 kg) compared to forceps.
- **Reduced Maternal Trauma:** Vacuum delivery is highly advocated as the instrument of first choice because it causes significantly lower rates of maternal pelvic floor injuries, genital tract lacerations, and severe (3rd and 4th degree) perineal tears.
- **Minimal Anesthesia Required:** The need for analgesia is much less; a pudendal block supplemented by perineal infiltration is usually adequate, whereas forceps often require regional or general anesthesia.
- **Less Postpartum Morbidity:** Postpartum maternal discomfort and perineal pain are considerably less compared to forceps delivery.
- **Simplicity and Ease of Learning:** The technique is easier to learn and perform, making it highly convenient for operators, including trained midwives.

**II. DISADVANTAGES AND LIMITATIONS**

- **Unsuitable for Acute Fetal Distress:** Establishing an effective vacuum takes time (ranging from 2 minutes up to 10 minutes for gradual pressure increase), making the ventouse unsuitable when highly expeditious delivery is required for acute fetal distress.
- **Higher Failure Rate & "Pop-offs":** The ventouse has a higher failure rate than forceps and is prone to cup detachment (pop-offs) if the vacuum is poorly maintained, if traction is not synchronous with contractions, or if the traction force required is too high.
- **Less Effective for Strong Traction:** In cases where moderate to strong traction is required to overcome resistance, forceps are more effective than the ventouse.
- **Strict Presentation Limits:** Ventouse is strictly contraindicated in any presentation other than a vertex, meaning it cannot be used for face presentations, brow presentations, or the aftercoming head in a breech delivery.
- **Prematurity Restriction:** It is absolutely contraindicated in preterm fetuses (especially <32 weeks) due to the heightened risk of cephalhematoma, subgaleal hemorrhage, and intracranial hemorrhage.
- **Maternal Soft Tissue Entrapment:** There is a distinct risk of accidentally trapping maternal cervical or vaginal tissues inside the metal or silastic cup, which can cause maternal injury if not checked.

**III. FETAL AND NEONATAL COMPLICATIONS**

- **Scalp Trauma:** It inevitably produces an artificial caput succedaneum ("chignon"), though this typically disappears within a few hours. Superficial scalp abrasions and sloughing of the scalp may also occur.
- **Extracranial Hemorrhages:** There is a significantly higher risk of cephalhematoma (rupture of emissary veins beneath the periosteum) and potentially life-threatening subaponeurotic (subgaleal) hemorrhage compared to forceps.
- **Retinal Hemorrhage:** The incidence of neonatal retinal hemorrhage is twice as high as seen in forceps deliveries, although it usually resolves without long-term consequences.
- **Neonatal Jaundice:** There is an increased incidence of neonatal jaundice due to the breakdown of RBCs from scalp hemorrhages.
- **Intracranial Hemorrhage:** Though rare, severe intracranial bleeding can occur.


20. Complication of artificial rupture of membranes.
**COMPLICATIONS OF ARTIFICIAL RUPTURE OF MEMBRANES (ARM) / AMNIOTOMY**

Artificial Rupture of Membranes (ARM), also known as amniotomy, is a surgical method used for the induction or augmentation of labor. While it is a widely practiced and highly successful procedure, it is associated with several maternal and fetal hazards. The complications of ARM include the following:

**1. Irreversibility of the Decision**

- Once the procedure of artificial rupture of membranes is adopted, there is no scope of retreating from the decision of delivery. If labor does not progress, it mandates delivery by operative means (like cesarean section) to avoid prolonged rupture and subsequent infection.

**2. Umbilical Cord Prolapse**

- There is a significant chance of umbilical cord prolapse occurring immediately following the rupture of the membranes.
- However, this risk is relatively low if the procedure is performed when the fetal head is already well engaged in the maternal pelvis.

**3. Chorioamnionitis (Amnionitis)**

- ARM carries a direct risk of causing amnionitis, which is the infection of the chorion, amnion, and the amniotic fluid.
- The chance of ascending infection from the lower genital tract becomes much higher if labor fails to start within 24 hours of the rupture.
- This infected liquor can subsequently lead to fetal infection and neonatal sepsis. Adhering to strict aseptic procedures during the amniotomy reduces this risk.

**4. Placental Abruption (Premature Separation of Placenta)**

- The sudden escape of amniotic fluid causes rapid uterine decompression.
- This sudden decompression leads to a diminished surface area of the uterus adjacent to the placental attachment, which can result in the shearing and premature separation of the placenta (abruptio placentae).
- To prevent this complication, especially in conditions associated with excess fluid like polyhydramnios, a slow and controlled ARM must be performed.

**5. Accidental Fetal and Maternal Injuries**

- The use of instruments (like an amnihook or Kocher's forceps) can cause accidental traumatic injury to the cervix, uterus, placenta, or directly to the fetal parts.
- Adequate care and gentle technique taken during the rupture of the membranes minimize the occurrence of this problem.

**6. Rupture of Vasa Previa and Fetal Exsanguination**

- In cases of a velamentous insertion of the umbilical cord, the fetal vessels may lie below the presenting part and run directly over the internal cervical os (vasa previa).
- These unprotected vessels can be accidentally torn during the artificial rupture of membranes.
- Because the bleeding is entirely from the fetal circulation, it can rapidly lead to fetal exsanguination and death.

**7. Liquor Amnii Embolism (Amniotic Fluid Embolism)**

- Although extremely rare, the procedure can force amniotic fluid into the maternal circulation, precipitating a catastrophic and often fatal liquor amnii embolism.


21. Difference between constriction ring and retraction ring.


**DIFFERENCES BETWEEN CONSTRICTION RING AND RETRACTION RING**

**1. Nature and Definition**

- **Constriction Ring (Schroeder’s Ring):** It is a manifestation of localized incoordinate uterine action characterized by a localized myometrial spasm/contraction.
- **Retraction Ring (Pathological Retraction Ring / Bandl’s Ring):** It is the end result of generalized tonic uterine contraction and severe retraction.

**2. Etiology**

- **Constriction Ring:** Caused by undue irritability of the uterus, often triggered by the injudicious administration of oxytocics, premature rupture of membranes, or premature attempts at instrumental delivery.
- **Retraction Ring:** Always occurs as a direct consequence of neglected, obstructed labor.

**3. Anatomical Situation and Movement**

- **Constriction Ring:** Usually forms at the junction of the upper and lower segments (often around a constricted fetal part, like the neck), but it can occur at any place in the uterus. Once formed, its position remains stationary and does not alter.
- **Retraction Ring:** Always situated exactly at the junction of the active upper segment and the passive lower segment. As obstruction persists, the ring progressively moves upwards toward the umbilicus.

**4. State of the Uterus and Polarity**

- **Constriction Ring:** The upper segment contracts and retracts, but experiences relaxation in between contractions. The lower segment remains thick and loose. Uterine polarity is completely abnormal.
- **Retraction Ring:** The upper segment is tonically contracted with absolutely no phase of relaxation, becoming progressively thicker. The lower segment becomes dangerously distended and thinned out. Uterine polarity remains normal.

**5. Abdominal Examination Findings**

- **Constriction Ring:** The uterus feels normal and is non-tender. Fetal parts are easily palpable. The ring is _not_ felt per abdomen. Round ligaments are not taut. Fetal Heart Sounds (FHS) are usually present and normal initially.
- **Retraction Ring:** The uterus is extremely tense and tender. Fetal parts cannot be easily felt. The ring is distinctly felt per abdomen as an obliquely placed transverse groove. Round ligaments are taut and tender. FHS is usually absent due to severe hypoxia/fetal death.

**6. Vaginal Examination Findings**

- **Constriction Ring:** The lower segment is not pressed by the presenting part. The ring _can_ be felt vaginally, usually above the presenting fetal head. Features of obstructed labor are absent.
- **Retraction Ring:** The lower uterine segment is heavily pressed by the forcibly driven presenting part. The ring _cannot_ be felt vaginally. Classic features of obstructed labor (severe molding, massive caput, edematous vagina) are conspicuously present.

**7. Maternal and Fetal Effects**

- **Constriction Ring:** The maternal condition remains largely unaffected unless the labor is extensively prolonged. Maternal exhaustion is a late feature. Fetal hypoxia also typically appears late.
- **Retraction Ring:** Features of severe maternal exhaustion, dehydration, ketoacidosis, and sepsis appear very early. Fetal anoxia and intrauterine fetal death occur early.

**8. Risk of Uterine Rupture**

- **Constriction Ring:** The chance of uterine rupture is absolutely absent.
- **Retraction Ring:** There is a highly imminent threat of uterine rupture, and actual rupture of the thinned-out lower segment is very common, especially in multigravidae.

**9. Principles of Management**

- **Constriction Ring:** Treatment aims to relax the ring (e.g., using deep anesthesia) followed by delivery of the baby. If it fails to relax during a cesarean section, the ring may have to be cut vertically to extract the fetus.
- **Retraction Ring:** Requires immediate Cesarean delivery, simultaneously excluding or surgically managing a ruptured uterus.


22. Causes of intrauterine death (IUD).

**DEFINITION OF INTRAUTERINE FETAL DEATH (IUFD)** Intrauterine fetal death is defined as the antepartum death of a fetus beyond the period of viability (weighing 500 g or more, or occurring beyond 22 weeks of gestation), which usually results in the delivery of a macerated fetus. Deaths occurring during labor end in the delivery of a fresh stillborn, while deaths before 22 weeks are termed abortions.

**CAUSES OF INTRAUTERINE FETAL DEATH (IUFD)** The etiological factors responsible for IUFD are broadly classified into maternal, fetal, placental, iatrogenic, and idiopathic (unexplained) causes.

**I. MATERNAL CAUSES (5–10%)**

- **Hypertensive Disorders in Pregnancy:** Includes pre-eclampsia and eclampsia, which cause placental insufficiency.
- **Endocrine and Metabolic Disorders:** Diabetes in pregnancy (especially when poorly controlled) and thyroid dysfunction.
- **Maternal Infections:** Systemic infections crossing the placenta, such as malaria, viral hepatitis, influenza, toxoplasmosis, and syphilis.
- **Hyperpyrexia:** Maternal temperature exceeding 39.4°C.
- **Antiphospholipid Syndrome (APS):** Presence of Lupus Anticoagulant (LA) and Anticardiolipin Antibodies (ACA), which lead to decidual vasculopathy with fibrinoid necrosis, placental vascular atherosis, and intervillous thrombosis.
- **Thrombophilias:** Inherited coagulation defects including Factor V Leiden mutation, Protein C and Protein S deficiency, and hyperhomocysteinemia, which share a similar thrombotic mechanism to APS.
- **Autoimmune Disease:** Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE).
- **Prolonged/Post-term Pregnancy:** Placental aging and insufficiency beyond the expected date of delivery.
- **High-Risk Demographic Factors:** Advanced maternal age (>35 years) and obesity (BMI ≥25).
- **Abnormal Labor:** Neglected obstetric emergencies such as prolonged labor, obstructed labor, or ruptured uterus.

**II. FETAL CAUSES (25–40%)**

- **Genetic and Structural Anomalies:** Chromosomal abnormalities and major structural/congenital malformations.
- **Fetal Growth Restriction (FGR):** Severe growth restriction due to chronic hypoxia and nutrient deprivation.
- **Fetal Infections:** Viral or bacterial infections, including chorioamnionitis.
- **Hemolytic Disease:** Rh-incompatibility leading to severe fetal anemia, tissue anoxemia, and cardiac failure.
- **Non-immune Hydrops Fetalis:** Fluid accumulation in fetal compartments leading to cardiac failure.

**III. PLACENTAL AND CORD CAUSES (20–35%)**

- **Antepartum Hemorrhage (APH):** Conditions like abruptio placentae and placenta previa can precipitate acute placental insufficiency and massive fetal hypoxia.
- **Cord Accidents:** Umbilical cord prolapse, a true knot in the cord, or tight cord around the neck leading to sudden cord compression and asphyxia.
- **Multiple Pregnancy Complications:** Twin-to-Twin Transfusion Syndrome (TTTS) causing severe hemodynamic imbalance between fetuses.
- **Placental Insufficiency:** Chronic insufficiency due to infarction, thrombosis, or poor placentation.

**IV. IATROGENIC CAUSES**

- **Manipulative Procedures:** Complications following External Cephalic Version (ECV), such as cord entanglement or placental abruption.
- **Drug Toxicity:** Administration of certain drugs, such as quinine, beyond therapeutic doses.

**V. IDIOPATHIC / UNEXPLAINED CAUSES (25–35%)**

- In a significant proportion of cases (25–35%), the exact cause of fetal death remains entirely obscure, even after thorough maternal clinical examination, comprehensive investigations, and fetal autopsy.
