1. Define infertility. Discuss causes, investigations and treatment of tubal factor infertility.

**DEFINITION OF INFERTILITY** Infertility is defined as the inability to conceive within one or more years of regular unprotected coitus.

- **Primary Infertility:** Denotes patients who have never conceived.
- **Secondary Infertility:** Indicates previous pregnancy but failure to conceive subsequently.

**TUBAL FACTOR INFERTILITY** Tubal and peritoneal factors are responsible for about 25–35% to 30–40% of all cases of female infertility. The normal functioning of the fallopian tubes is essential for ovum pick-up, fertilization, and embryonic transport to the uterine cavity.

**CAUSES OF TUBAL AND PERITONEAL INFERTILITY** Tubal obstruction or dysfunction can occur due to the following factors:

- **Pelvic Infections:** This is a major cause leading to endosalpingeal damage and peritubal adhesions. Common causative organisms include _Chlamydia trachomatis_, _Neisseria gonorrhoeae_, and _Mycobacterium tuberculosis_ (especially following postabortal or puerperal sepsis).
- **Previous Tubal Surgery or Sterilization:** Iatrogenic causes include surgical trauma or previous voluntary tubal ligation.
- **Salpingitis Isthmica Nodosa:** A condition characterized by nodular thickening of the tube due to the proliferation of tubal epithelium within the hypertrophied myosalpinx, often causing bilateral blockage.
- **Pelvic Endometriosis:** Can distort pelvic anatomy, cause peritubal adhesions, and impair ovum pick-up by the fimbriae.
- **Intraluminal Obstruction:** Due to polyps, mucous debris within the tubal lumen, or tubal spasm.
- **Congenital Anomalies:** Elongated tubes, diverticula, or accessory ostia may lower fertility or predispose to ectopic pregnancy.

**INVESTIGATIONS FOR TUBAL PATENCY** Investigations to assess the anatomical patency and functional integrity of the tubes are typically performed in the postmenstrual (proliferative) phase, usually between Days 7 to 10 of the menstrual cycle.

**1. Hysterosalpingography (HSG)**

- **Principle:** A water-soluble or oil-based radio-opaque dye is instilled transcervically, and radiographic views are taken to observe the uterine cavity and tubal filling.
- **Findings:** Tubal patency is evidenced by peritoneal spillage of the dye. It precisely identifies the side and site of tubal obstruction and can detect intraluminal abnormalities or uterine malformations.
- **Contraindications:** Active pelvic infection, pelvic tenderness, pregnancy, suspected pelvic tuberculosis, and abnormal uterine bleeding.

**2. Laparoscopy and Chromopertubation (Gold Standard)**

- **Principle:** Direct visualization of the pelvic organs while a dye (methylene blue) is injected through the cervix. Patency is confirmed by visualizing the spillage of dye from the fimbrial ends into the peritoneal cavity.
- **Advantages:** It is the definitive method. It evaluates tubal patency, block site, tubal motility, hydrosalpinx, peritubal adhesions, and co-existing peritoneal pathologies like endometriosis or pelvic inflammatory disease (PID). Therapy (adhesiolysis, fulguration) can be performed in the same sitting.

**3. Sonohysterosalpingography (SIS) / HyCoSy**

- **Principle:** Normal saline is pushed into the uterine cavity using a pediatric Foley catheter, and transvaginal ultrasonography is used to follow the fluid through the tubes into the pouch of Douglas.
- **Advantages:** Noninvasive, lacks radiation exposure, and is highly superior for detecting intrauterine pathology alongside tubal patency.

**4. Falloposcopy and Salpingoscopy**

- **Falloposcopy:** Uses a fine, flexible fiberoptic device passed transcervically through a hysteroscope to study the entire tubal lumen, ostia, mucosal pattern, and intratubal polyps.
- **Salpingoscopy:** A rigid endoscope is introduced through the fimbrial end during laparoscopy to evaluate the tubal mucosa.

**5. Insufflation Test (Rubin’s Test)**

- **Principle:** Air or CO2 is pushed transcervically under pressure. A fall in pressure, hissing sound on auscultation, and shoulder pain (diaphragmatic irritation) indicate patency.
- _Note:_ Rarely done now due to high false-negative rates (e.g., from cornual spasm) and its inability to identify the site/side of the block.

**TREATMENT OF TUBAL FACTOR INFERTILITY**

Management depends on the site and extent of tubal damage, patient age, and the presence of other infertility factors.

**1. Surgical Management (Tuboplasty)** Tuboplasty involves microsurgical or laparoscopic techniques to restore tubal anatomy and physiology.

- **Proximal Tubal Block:** Managed by hysteroscopic proximal tubal cannulation with a guidewire/balloon tuboplasty. It restores tubal patency in about 85% of cases and yields a 45–60% pregnancy rate.
- **Distal Tubal Block / Fimbrial Disease:**
    - _Fimbrioplasty / Fimbriolysis:_ Separation of agglutinated fimbriae or dilation of fimbrial phimosis to open the abdominal ostium.
    - _Neosalpingostomy:_ Creation of a new opening (terminal or 'cuff') in a completely occluded tube.
- **Mid-Tubal Block / Reversal of Sterilization:** Managed by _Tubotubal anastomosis_ (resecting the diseased/ligated segment and performing end-to-end anastomosis). Pregnancy rates are highest here (50–82%), provided the final reconstructed tube length is >4 cm.
- **Peritubal Adhesions:** Managed by _Salpingo-ovariolysis_ (separation of adhesions) via laparoscopy.

_Factors associated with poor surgical outcomes:_ Dense pelvic adhesions, loss of fimbriae, bilateral hydrosalpinx >3 cm, and reconstructed tube length <4 cm. _Adjuvant Therapy:_ Prophylactic antibiotics, adhesion prevention devices (Interceed, Seprafilm), and postoperative hydrotubation (instilling gentamicin and dexamethasone) are used to improve success rates.

**2. Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART) / IVF-ET** In Vitro Fertilization and Embryo Transfer (IVF-ET) is considered the best treatment option for any complicated tubal occlusive disease.

- **Indications:** Bilateral tubal block, severe pelvic adhesions, failed tuboplasty, or advanced reproductive age.
- **Management of Hydrosalpinx:** A large hydrosalpinx significantly impairs IVF success (reducing pregnancy rates by ~50% due to reduced endometrial receptivity and implantation failure). Therefore, bilateral _Salpingectomy_ must be performed prior to IVF in cases of severe distal tubal disease with hydrosalpinx.
- **Genital Tuberculosis:** Tubal surgery is strictly withheld in tubercular salpingitis. Following adequate antitubercular therapy, IVF-ET is the treatment of choice once the endometrium is proven free from the disease.


2. Describe FIGO staging of cervical cancer. Discuss role of radiotherapy in the management of stage IIA cervical cancer.

**FIGO STAGING OF CARCINOMA OF THE CERVIX UTERI (2018)**

The clinical staging as recommended by the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) is based principally on clinical examination, imaging, and pathology. When in doubt, the lower staging should be assigned.

**STAGE I: The carcinoma is strictly confined to the cervix (extension to the uterine corpus should be disregarded).**

- **Stage IA:** Invasive carcinoma that can be diagnosed only by microscopy with a maximum depth of invasion < 5 mm.
    - **IA1:** Measured stromal invasion < 3 mm in depth.
    - **IA2:** Measured stromal invasion $\ge$ 3 mm and < 5 mm in depth.
- **Stage IB:** Invasive carcinoma with measured deepest invasion $\ge$ 5 mm (greater than stage IA), lesion limited to the cervix uteri.
    - **IB1:** Invasive carcinoma $\ge$ 5 mm depth of stromal invasion, and < 2 cm in greatest dimension.
    - **IB2:** Invasive carcinoma $\ge$ 2 cm and < 4 cm in greatest dimension.
    - **IB3:** Invasive carcinoma $\ge$ 4 cm in greatest dimension.

**STAGE II: The carcinoma invades beyond the uterus, but has not extended onto the lower third of the vagina or to the pelvic wall.**

- **Stage IIA:** Involvement limited to the upper two-thirds of the vagina without parametrial involvement.
    - **IIA1:** Invasive carcinoma < 4 cm in greatest dimension.
    - **IIA2:** Invasive carcinoma $\ge$ 4 cm in greatest dimension.
- **Stage IIB:** With parametrial involvement but not up to the pelvic wall.

**STAGE III: The carcinoma involves the lower third of the vagina and/or extends to the pelvic wall and/or causes hydronephrosis or nonfunctioning kidney and/or involves pelvic and/or para-aortic lymph nodes.**

- **Stage IIIA:** The carcinoma involves the lower third of the vagina, with no extension to the pelvic wall.
- **Stage IIIB:** Extension to the pelvic wall and/or hydronephrosis or nonfunctioning kidney (unless known to be due to another cause).
- **Stage IIIC:** Involvement of pelvic and/or para-aortic lymph nodes, irrespective of tumor size and extent (with r and p notations to indicate imaging or pathology findings).
    - **IIIC1:** Pelvic lymph node metastasis only.
    - **IIIC2:** Para-aortic lymph node metastasis.

**STAGE IV: The carcinoma has extended beyond the true pelvis or has involved (biopsy proven) the mucosa of the bladder or rectum.** _(Note: A bullous edema, as such, does not permit a case to be allotted to stage IV)._

- **Stage IVA:** Spread to adjacent pelvic organs.
- **Stage IVB:** Spread to distant organs.

**ROLE OF RADIOTHERAPY IN THE MANAGEMENT OF STAGE IIA CERVICAL CANCER**

Stage IIA cervical cancer indicates that the tumor involves the upper two-thirds of the vagina without parametrial involvement. The management heavily depends on the tumor size, dividing Stage IIA into IIA1 (< 4 cm) and IIA2 ($\ge$ 4 cm).

**1. Primary Modality Selection Based on Substage:**

- **Stage IIA1 (< 4 cm):** Primary modality can be either surgery (Type III radical hysterectomy with pelvic lymphadenectomy) or radiotherapy. Both modalities yield similar survival results (approximately 85% 5-year survival). Radiotherapy is highly preferred if the patient is unfit for surgery or has significant medical comorbidities.
- **Stage IIA2 ($\ge$ 4 cm / Bulky Tumor):** Primary surgery is discouraged due to high morbidity. The standard treatment of choice is **Concurrent Platinum-based Chemoradiation Therapy (CCRT)** using a pelvic field. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) followed by surgery or radiation can be used where radiotherapy facilities are scarce.

**2. Principles and Techniques of Radiotherapy:** Radiotherapy in Stage IIA employs a combination of External Beam Radiation Therapy (EBRT) and Brachytherapy (Intracavitary Radiation Therapy - ICRT).

- **External Beam Radiation Therapy (EBRT):**
    - Megavoltage photons (Cobalt 60 or Linear accelerators delivering X-rays of 4-8 MeV) are used to cover the entire pelvis, including the regional pelvic nodes, parametrium, and upper vagina.
    - It is generally delivered in 25 fractions over 5 weeks, providing a total dose of 40-50 Gy.
    - EBRT shrinks the bulky tumor, making subsequent brachytherapy more effective.
- **Brachytherapy (ICRT):**
    - Delivers a very high, localized dose of radiation directly to the cervix, vagina, and paravaginal tissues while sparing normal surrounding tissues due to a rapid fall-off of radiation (inverse square law).
    - A hollow stem (tandem) is placed in the uterine cavity, and ovoids or colpostats are placed in the vaginal vault under anesthesia.
    - Radioactive sources (e.g., Cesium 137, Cobalt 60, Iridium 192) are introduced using remote after-loading techniques to prevent radiation hazards to personnel.
    - High Dose Rate (HDR) or Low Dose Rate (LDR) brachytherapy can be used; both have similar survival rates and toxicity profiles.

**3. Dosimetry (Point A and Point B Calculation):**

- **Point A** (located 2 cm cephalic and 2 cm lateral to the external os; crossing of the uterine artery and ureter) receives the cancerolytic dose of approximately 7000–8000 cGy.
- **Point B** (located 2 cm cephalic and 5 cm lateral; representing the obturator node site) receives about 2000 cGy from brachytherapy. The remaining required dose is supplemented by the EBRT (4000 cGy).

**4. Concurrent Chemoradiation (CCRT):**

- Weekly Cisplatin (40 mg/m$^2$) is administered concurrently with radiotherapy.
- **Mechanism:** Chemotherapy acts as a radiosensitizer. It sensitizes the cancer cells to radiation, thereby significantly improving the overall survival rate.

**5. Postoperative Adjuvant Radiotherapy:** In cases where Stage IIA1 is managed primarily by radical hysterectomy, adjuvant radiotherapy (EBRT + Brachytherapy) is indicated if:

- Pelvic lymph nodes are found positive.
- Surgical margins are positive or close.
- Lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI) or deep stromal invasion is noted.
- This approach reduces locoregional recurrence.

**Advantages of Primary Radiotherapy:**

- Highly effective for bulky IIA2 tumors where surgery carries unacceptable morbidity.
- Less primary mortality and morbidity compared to extensive radical surgery.
- Wider applicability, especially in patients with medical comorbidities.

3. Causes and management of Hirsuitism.
**HIRSUTISM**

**Definition** Hirsutism is defined as the excessive growth of androgen-dependent sexual hair (terminal hair) in the facial and central parts of the body (following a male pattern distribution) that causes distress to a female. It must be differentiated from hypertrichosis, which is the excessive growth of nonsexual, fetal lanugo-type hair in normal locations. Hirsutism represents an early manifestation in the spectrum of hyperandrogenism and virilization, though virilism (clitoromegaly, deepening of voice, male muscle mass) is a more severe form of androgen excess.

**Pathophysiology** The pilosebaceous unit (PSU) serves as the target organ for androgens. The pathophysiology of hirsutism involves a combination of the following mechanisms:

- **Increased Serum Androgens:** Elevated production of testosterone or other androgens from the ovaries or adrenal glands.
- **Decreased Sex Hormone-Binding Globulin (SHBG):** Lower levels of SHBG lead to an increase in biologically active "free" testosterone in the circulation.
- **Increased 5α-Reductase Activity:** In the skin and hair follicles, the enzyme 5α-reductase converts testosterone into dihydrotestosterone (DHT), which is the most potent androgen responsible for stimulating the proliferation of terminal hair (anagen phase).
- **Target Organ Sensitivity:** Increased sensitivity of the hair follicles to normal circulating androgen levels, often related to ethnic and genetic backgrounds.

**Clinical Grading** The severity of hirsutism is objectively quantified using the **modified Ferriman-Gallwey scoring system**.

- It evaluates 11 androgen-sensitive body areas (e.g., upper lip, chin, chest, upper/lower back, upper/lower abdomen, arms, and thighs).
- Each area is graded from 1 to 4 depending on the extent of hair growth.
- A total score of >8 indicates hirsutism, though a lower threshold (>3) is considered abnormal in Asian women.

---

**CAUSES OF HIRSUTISM (ETIOLOGY)** The causes of hirsutism are broadly categorized based on the source of the androgen excess:

**1. Ovarian Causes**

- Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome (PCOS) (most common cause of hirsutism).
- Androgen-secreting tumors: Sertoli-Leydig cell tumor, Hilus cell tumor, Lipoid cell tumor.
- Stromal hyperthecosis and luteoma of pregnancy.

**2. Adrenal Causes**

- Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia (CAH) or late-onset CAH (most commonly 21-hydroxylase deficiency).
- Cushing’s syndrome.
- Adrenal androgen-secreting tumors.

**3. Obesity and Metabolic Factors**

- Android obesity leading to insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia, which stimulates excess ovarian androgen production.
- HAIR-AN syndrome (Hyperandrogenism, Insulin Resistance, and Acanthosis Nigricans).

**4. Pituitary Causes**

- Excess ACTH secretion (Cushing’s disease).
- Excess Growth Hormone (Acromegaly).

**5. Iatrogenic (Exogenous/Drug-induced)**

- Intake of androgens, anabolic steroids, danazol, phenytoin, diazoxide, cortisone, and certain synthetic progestogens or oral contraceptives.

**6. Idiopathic Hirsutism**

- Characterized by increased end-organ (pilosebaceous unit) sensitivity to normal circulating androgens, often associated with elevated local 5α-reductase activity.

---

**INVESTIGATIONS AND EVALUATION** A systematic approach is required to rule out life-threatening neoplastic causes of hyperandrogenism.

- **Clinical Evaluation:** A rapid and progressive onset of hirsutism, especially when associated with signs of virilization (e.g., clitoromegaly, deepening of voice), strongly indicates an androgen-producing ovarian or adrenal tumor. Insidious onset beginning around puberty is more characteristic of PCOS or late-onset CAH. Check for BMI, blood pressure, and acanthosis nigricans.
- **Serum Testosterone:** Levels >200 ng/dL (or >150 ng/dL in some contexts) are highly suspicious for an ovarian androgen-producing tumor. Levels <150 ng/dL are commonly seen in PCOS.
- **Serum DHEA-S:** DHEAS >700 µg/dL specifically indicates an adrenal source, strongly suggesting an adrenal tumor.
- **Serum 17-Hydroxyprogesterone (17-OHP):** Baseline or ACTH-stimulated levels >800 ng/dL confirm the diagnosis of 21-hydroxylase deficiency (CAH).
- **Dexamethasone Suppression Test:** Used to differentiate adrenal hyperplasia from an adrenal tumor; DHEAS levels are suppressed by dexamethasone in hyperplasia but not in tumors.
- **LH:FSH Ratio:** An elevated ratio (e.g., 3:1) is supportive of PCOS.
- **Imaging:** Transvaginal ultrasonography, CT scan, or MRI of the pelvis and abdomen are indicated if testosterone or DHEAS levels are markedly elevated, to detect ovarian or adrenal neoplasms.

---

**MANAGEMENT OF HIRSUTISM** The therapeutic principles include: (1) Removing the source of excess androgen, (2) Suppressing or neutralizing the action of androgens, and (3) Removing the already present excess hair. _Note: Medical therapies take at least 6 months to show clinical benefit because the life cycle of a hair follicle is approximately 6 months_.

**1. General Measures & Lifestyle Modification**

- **Weight Reduction:** In obese women and those with PCOS, reducing the Body Mass Index (BMI to <25 kg/m2) is the crucial first-line step. Weight loss reduces hyperinsulinemia, lowers androgen excess, and increases SHBG levels.

**2. Removal of the Source**

- **Surgical Management:** Excision (oophorectomy or adrenalectomy) is mandatory for ovarian and adrenal tumors. Pituitary tumors causing Cushing's disease require transsphenoidal resection or radiation.
- **Iatrogenic:** Discontinuation of the offending medications.

**3. Medical Management (Antiandrogens and Suppression)** Antiandrogens prevent the growth of new hair follicles but cannot remove terminal hair that is already present.

- **Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs):** First-line therapy for idiopathic hirsutism and PCOS. COCs suppress pituitary LH secretion, increase hepatic SHBG production (which binds free testosterone), and inhibit 5α-reductase activity. Pills containing newer progestins like drospirenone, desogestrel, or norgestimate are preferred due to their low androgenic profiles.
- **Cyproterone Acetate:** A potent antiandrogenic progestogen that competitively blocks androgen receptors and inhibits 5α-reductase. It is often prescribed in a combined pill format (2 mg cyproterone acetate + 35 µg ethinyl estradiol) for 21 days per cycle.
- **Spironolactone:** An aldosterone antagonist and potassium-sparing diuretic that acts by competitively binding androgen receptors and inhibiting 5α-reductase directly. Given at a dose of 50–200 mg/day; serum potassium must be monitored for hyperkalemia.
- **Finasteride:** A specific 5α-reductase inhibitor that blocks the peripheral conversion of testosterone to DHT. The effective dose is 5 mg daily.
- **Flutamide:** A nonsteroidal androgen receptor blocker (250–750 mg/day) with a notable risk of hepatotoxicity.
- **Dexamethasone / Glucocorticoids:** Used specifically for suppressing endogenous ACTH in adrenal hyperandrogenism and CAH. The dose is 0.25–1 mg daily at bedtime.
- **Insulin Sensitizing Agents:** Metformin or rosiglitazone decrease circulating insulin and subsequently lower ovarian androgen production in patients with PCOS and insulin resistance.
- **GnRH Agonists:** In severe, refractory cases, continuous GnRH analogs selectively suppress pituitary LH, inducing a state of medical oophorectomy.

**4. Local and Cosmetic Removal of Hair** Because medical therapy only prevents new hair growth, local measures are required for existing terminal hair.

- **Temporary/Physical Methods:** Bleaching, plucking/twitching, shaving, waxing, and depilatory creams.
- **Permanent Reduction:** Electrolysis (which destroys the individual hair follicle through electric current) and Laser/Pulsed light photo-epilation (which utilizes heat targeted at pigmented hair follicles).
- **Topical Medical Therapy:** **Eflornithine hydrochloride** (13.9% cream) applied topically retards hair growth by irreversibly inhibiting ornithine decarboxylase, an enzyme in the dermal papilla essential for hair growth.

4. Leucorrhoea.
**LEUCORRHOEA**

**DEFINITION AND CRITERIA** Leucorrhoea is strictly defined as an excessive amount of normal vaginal discharge. To be classified as leucorrhoea, the discharge must fulfill the following clinical criteria:

- It is evident from persistent vulvar moistness, staining of the undergarments (which turns brownish-yellow on drying), or necessitates the use of a vulvar pad.
- It is strictly nonpurulent and nonoffensive.
- It is nonirritant and never causes pruritus (itching).

**CHARACTERISTICS OF NORMAL VAGINAL FLUID**

- **Physical:** Watery, white in color, nonodorous, with an acidic pH around 4.0.
- **Microscopic:** Contains squamous epithelial cells, Lactobacilli (Doderlein bacilli), a few gram-negative bacteria, and anaerobes.
- **Absence of Pathology:** There are absolutely no white blood cells (pus cells) or red blood cells present in normal vaginal fluid.

**ETIOLOGY (CAUSES)** The causes of excessive vaginal discharge can be broadly divided into physiological excess and non-infective pathological excess.

**1. Physiological Excess** Normal secretion increases in conditions where endogenous or exogenous estrogen levels are high:

- **Puberty:** Increased levels of endogenous estrogen cause marked overgrowth of the endocervical epithelium. This may encroach onto the ectocervix, producing a congenital ectopy (erosion) and leading to increased secretion.
- **During Menstrual Cycle:**
    - _Around ovulation:_ A peak rise in estrogen leads to increased secretory activity of the cervical glands.
    - _Premenstrual phase:_ Pelvic congestion and increased mucus secretion from the hypertrophied endometrial glands.
- **Pregnancy:** A state of hyperestrinism with increased vascularity leads to an increase in vaginal transudate and cervical gland secretion.
- **Sexual Excitement:** Abundant secretion is produced from the Bartholin’s glands during sexual stimulation.
- **Neonatal Leucorrhoea:** Excessive secretion of cervical mucus from hypertrophied cervical glands under the influence of maternal estrogen.

**2. Pathological / Non-Infective Causes**

- **Cervical Causes:** Noninfective cervical lesions can produce excessive secretion that pours out at the vulva. These include cervical ectopy, chronic cervicitis, mucous polyps, and ectropion (where cervical glands are exposed to the vagina).
- **Vaginal Causes (Increased Transudation):** Conditions associated with increased pelvic congestion increase vaginal transudation. Examples include uterine prolapse, acquired retroverted uterus, chronic pelvic inflammation, oral contraceptive (‘pill’) use, and vaginal adenosis.
- **General Ill Health & Malnutrition:** Important causes of excessive discharge as they produce excess exfoliation of the superficial vaginal cells.

**CLINICAL EVALUATION AND DIAGNOSIS** Evaluation requires a detailed history, physical examination, and targeted investigations to rule out infective causes.

- **History:** Assess the duration of discharge, prior episodes, contraceptive practices, and rule out associated symptoms like dysuria, dyspareunia, and pelvic pain.
- **Physical Examination:**
    - _General:_ Health assessment and abdominal examination for any mass or tenderness.
    - _Local:_ Inspection of the vulva for discharge or ulcers, speculum examination to detect any cervical or vaginal pathology, and bimanual pelvic examination to check for adnexal tenderness, masses, or foreign bodies.
- **Investigations:** Microbiological study of the discharge, Pap smear, urine for routine examination and culture sensitivity (RE and CS), and Nucleic Acid Amplification Tests (NAAT) / PCR to rule out specific pathogens.

**MANAGEMENT** The management of leucorrhoea is largely conservative and targeted at the underlying cause if one is identified:

- **General Measures:** Improvement of general health, maintaining a sympathetic attitude towards the patient's ailments (assurance), and instructing the patient on meticulous local perineal hygiene.
- **Medical Management:** If the patient is an oral contraceptive pill user and the symptom is highly annoying, the ‘pill’ may have to be stopped temporarily. Any specific infection found during the workup must be treated with appropriate antimicrobial therapy.
- **Surgical Management:** Cervical factors (like persistent ectopy or chronic cervicitis) may require surgical treatments such as electrocautery, cryosurgery, or trachelorrhaphy. Pelvic lesions producing vaginal leucorrhoea require appropriate targeted therapy for the specific pathology.

5. Staging laparotomy for ovarian mass.
**STAGING LAPAROTOMY FOR OVARIAN MASS**

Staging laparotomy is the keystone in the primary treatment of ovarian malignancy. It should preferably be performed in a specialized gynecologic oncology center,.

**Aims of Staging Laparotomy**

- To accurately stage the disease (surgicopathological staging according to FIGO guidelines), which dictates the choice of adjuvant therapy and assessment of prognosis,.
- To perform maximum and effective surgical removal (cytoreduction).

**Prerequisites and Incision**

- **Incision:** A liberal vertical (midline or paramedian) incision is the incision of choice,,.
- **Rationale:** It allows adequate exposure of the upper abdomen, facilitates the delivery of large cysts intact, and minimizes the risk of tumor rupture and subsequent malignant peritoneal spillage,. Cyst aspiration or tapping to reduce tumor size is strictly avoided due to the high risk of spreading malignant cells,,.

**Principal Steps of the Procedure (The "Protocol")**

**1. Ascitic Fluid Collection / Peritoneal Washings**

- Immediately upon opening the abdomen, before manipulating the mass, inspect the peritoneal fluid.
- Collect the ascitic fluid for cytological examination.
- If no free fluid is present, perform peritoneal washings by instilling 100 mL of normal saline into the subdiaphragmatic areas and paracolic gutters and aspirating it for cytology,.

**2. Systematic Exploration**

- Perform a systematic visual and manual exploration in a clockwise fashion starting from the cecum.
- Thoroughly palpate the liver, gastrointestinal tract, the undersurface of the diaphragm, omentum, and para-aortic lymph nodes.
- Inspect the pelvis for the nature of the tumor, extent of adhesions, the condition of the contralateral ovary, uterus, and fallopian tubes, and palpate the pelvic lymph nodes.

**3. Primary Tumor Extirpation and Frozen Section**

- Deliver the tumor intact and carefully inspect its characteristics.
- The tumor should be cut open to inspect the inner surface for solid areas, hemorrhage, or necrosis, and suspicious lesions must be sent for microscopic frozen section biopsy to confirm malignancy,,.

**4. Definitive Surgery**

- **Standard Procedure:** For menopausal women or those whose family is complete, the definitive surgery is a Total Abdominal Hysterectomy (TAH) with Bilateral Salpingo-Oophorectomy (BSO),,.
- **Fertility-Sparing Surgery (FSS):** In young, highly motivated women desiring fertility, if the disease is early (Stage IA, Grade 1 or 2) with an intact capsule and a clinically normal contralateral ovary, a conservative Unilateral Salpingo-Oophorectomy (USO) is justified,,. Routine biopsy of the normal-looking contralateral ovary is avoided to preserve ovarian reserve,,.

**5. Omentectomy and Appendicectomy**

- **Omentectomy:** A total or infracolic omentectomy is a mandatory step of staging to remove occult metastases and improve the efficacy of subsequent chemotherapy,,,.
- **Appendicectomy:** This is routinely performed if a mucinous ovarian cancer is encountered.

**6. Lymph Node Sampling**

- Selective pelvic and para-aortic lymph node sampling (or total lymphadenectomy) must be done,,.

**7. Peritoneal Biopsies**

- Even in the absence of gross metastatic disease, multiple random peritoneal biopsies and scrapings from the undersurface of the diaphragm should be taken, as occult metastasis is present in 10–40% of early-stage (Stage I and II) epithelial ovarian cancers,.

**8. Cytoreductive (Debulking) Surgery in Advanced Disease**

- In advanced stages, maximum cytoreductive surgery is performed to remove as much tumor bulk as possible.
- The goal is optimal cytoreduction, aimed at leaving a residual tumor load of ≤1–2 cm in diameter (optimally <1 cm), as this confers a significant survival advantage,.
- To achieve this, the surgeon may need to perform bowel resection, diaphragmatic stripping, splenectomy, or hepatic resection for parenchymal metastatic disease.


6. Define AUB and its classification. Management of puberty menorrhagia.
**ABNORMAL UTERINE BLEEDING (AUB)**

**Definition:** Any uterine bleeding outside the normal volume, duration, regularity, or frequency is considered abnormal uterine bleeding (AUB).

**Classification of AUB (FIGO - 2011):** The acronym **PALM-COEIN** is used to subdivide all the causes of AUB into nine main categories, separating them into structural (PALM) and non-structural (COEIN) causes.

- **Structural Causes (PALM) - Diagnosed by imaging or histology:**
    
    - **P - Polyp (AUB-P):** Includes endometrial or endocervical polyps.
    - **A - Adenomyosis (AUB-A):** Ingrowth of the endometrium into the myometrium.
    - **L - Leiomyoma (AUB-L):** Subdivided into patients with submucosal myoma (AUB-LSM) and those with other myomas that do not affect the endometrial cavity (AUB-LO).
    - **M - Malignancy and hyperplasia (AUB-M):** Includes endometrial hyperplasia, and cancers of the genital tract.
- **Non-structural Systemic Causes (COEIN):**
    
    - **C - Coagulopathy (AUB-C):** Due to systemic bleeding disorders.
    - **O - Ovulatory dysfunction (AUB-O):** Often due to neuroendocrine abnormalities resulting in anovulation.
    - **E - Endometrial (AUB-E):** Abnormal endometrial production of prostaglandins.
    - **I - Iatrogenic (AUB-I):** Mostly due to medications, anticoagulants, or IUCDs.
    - **N - Not yet identified (AUB-N):** Unclassified causes.

---

**PUBERTY MENORRHAGIA**

**Etiopathology:** In 95% of cases, the underlying cause is Dysfunctional Uterine Bleeding (DUB). Immaturity of the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis leads to anovulatory cycles, resulting in unopposed estrogen stimulation, endometrial hyperplasia, and consequently, prolonged and heavy bleeding. Other causes include endocrine dysfunction (PCOS, hypothyroidism), hematological disorders (ITP, Von-Willebrand's disease, leukemia), pelvic tumors, and pregnancy complications.

**Management of Puberty Menorrhagia:** Management aims to arrest the acute bleeding, regulate subsequent cycles, and treat underlying anemia.

**1. General Measures & Supportive Therapy:**

- The girl requires adequate explanation, reassurance, and psychological support.
- Prescribe adequate bed rest.
- Correction of anemia using oral/parenteral hematinics.
- Blood transfusion may be required if bleeding is severe and anemia is profound.

**2. Medical Management:**

- **Progestin Therapy (First-line):** Medroxyprogesterone acetate or norethisterone 5 mg is given thrice daily until the bleeding stops. Bleeding is usually controlled within 3–7 days. The medication is continued for 21 days. Normal cycles usually resume after 2–3 courses of this therapy.
- **Emergency Management (For heavy/unresponsive bleeding):** Conjugated equine estrogen 20–40 mg IV is administered every 6–8 hours. Once the acute bleeding is controlled, the patient is switched to combined oral contraceptive pills (containing 50 µg of estrogen).
- **GnRH Analogs:** Can be used for short-term management in refractory cases.

**3. Surgical Evaluation & Management:**

- If the patient remains unresponsive to medical therapy, Examination Under Anesthesia (EUA) and Dilatation & Curettage (D&C) are indicated.
- This is done to exclude any organic lesions, pelvic pathology, or pregnancy complications.
- The curetted material must be sent for histopathological examination.


7. Ovulation induction drugs in infertility.
**OVULATION INDUCTION DRUGS IN INFERTILITY**

**Introduction and Prerequisites** Ovulation induction is the medical treatment of anovulation or oligo-ovulation to promote the release of one or more mature oocytes. It has the highest success rate among infertility treatments.

- **Prerequisites:** Before starting induction, it is mandatory to confirm normal semen parameters, bilateral tubal patency, a normal uterine cavity, and an adequate ovarian reserve (Day 3 FSH <10 IU/L, AMH >0.5 ng/mL, and Antral Follicle Count >10).

**Classification of Ovulation Induction Drugs**

1. **Selective Estrogen Receptor Modulators (SERMs):** Clomiphene Citrate, Tamoxifen
2. **Aromatase Inhibitors:** Letrozole, Anastrozole
3. **Gonadotropins:** hMG, uFSH, rFSH, rLH, hCG
4. **GnRH and its Analogs:** Pulsatile GnRH, GnRH Agonists, GnRH Antagonists
5. **Adjuvants for Biochemical Abnormalities:** Metformin, Dopamine Agonists, Dexamethasone, Thyroxine.

---

**1. Clomiphene Citrate (CC)**

- **Nature:** It is a nonsteroidal triphenylethylene compound with both antiestrogenic and weakly estrogenic properties,.
- **Mechanism of Action:** It blocks estrogen receptors in the hypothalamus, preventing the negative feedback of endogenous estrogen. This increases the GnRH pulse amplitude, leading to increased pituitary FSH and LH secretion, which stimulates follicular growth,.
- **Indications:** Normogonadotropic-normoprolactinemic anovulation, Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome (PCOS) with oligomenorrhea/amenorrhea, and hypothalamic amenorrhea.
- **Dose:** Started between Day 2 and Day 5 of the menstrual cycle at 50 mg/day for 5 days. It can be stepped up by 50 mg to a maximum of 250 mg/day.
- **Results & Drawbacks:** Ovulation occurs in 80% of cases, but the cumulative pregnancy rate is lower (50-70%) due to its peripheral antiestrogenic effects on the cervical mucus (making it thick and hostile) and the endometrium (hindering implantation). Conjugated estrogen is sometimes added as an adjuvant.
- **Side Effects:** Hot flashes, nausea, visual symptoms, multiple pregnancy (mostly twins, ~5%), and rarely ovarian hyperstimulation,,.

**2. Aromatase Inhibitors (Letrozole)**

- **Mechanism of Action:** Inhibits the enzyme aromatase in ovarian granulosa cells, blocking the conversion of androgens to estrogens. The resulting low estrogen removes negative feedback on the hypothalamic-pituitary axis, increasing FSH release. The accumulation of intraovarian androgens also enhances follicular sensitivity to FSH,.
- **Advantages over CC:** It does not have peripheral antiestrogenic effects on the endometrium or cervical mucus. It also promotes monofollicular development, making the risk of multiple pregnancies extremely low,.
- **Dose:** 2.5 mg or 5 mg daily for 5 days, from Day 3 to Day 7 of the cycle.
- **Indications:** Often used as a first-line drug or in clomiphene-resistant PCOS cases.

**3. Gonadotropins**

- **Preparations:** Human Menopausal Gonadotropin (hMG - 75 IU FSH + 75 IU LH), purified/highly purified urinary FSH (uFSH), recombinant FSH (rFSH - given SC), and hCG (to trigger ovulation).
- **Indications:** Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (WHO Group I), CC or Letrozole resistant cases (e.g., severe PCOS), unexplained infertility, and for Controlled Ovarian Stimulation (COS) in Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART) such as IVF,.
- **Regimen:** Starts with a low dose (e.g., 75 IU IM/day) from Day 2 to Day 5, continued for 7-10 days using either a "step-up" or "step-down" protocol depending on the response,.
- **Monitoring:** Essential to prevent Ovarian Hyperstimulation Syndrome (OHSS). Requires serial Transvaginal Sonography (TVS) for folliculometry and serum estradiol (E2) estimations. Optimum levels are E2 of 150-300 pg/mL per mature follicle and follicular diameter of 18-20 mm.
- **Trigger:** Once optimum follicular size is reached, 5000-10000 IU of hCG is given to trigger ovulation, which occurs ~36 hours later.
- **Complications:** High risk of multiple pregnancy (10-30%), OHSS (1-20%), and increased miscarriage and ectopic pregnancy rates,,.

**4. GnRH and GnRH Analogs**

- **Pulsatile GnRH:** Administered via an IV or SC portable minipump giving a pulse every 60-90 minutes. It mimics physiologic secretion and is highly effective for hypothalamic amenorrhea and isolated gonadotropin deficiency (Kallmann’s syndrome). It carries a low risk of OHSS and multiple pregnancies,,,.
- **GnRH Agonists (e.g., Leuprolide, Buserelin) & Antagonists (e.g., Cetrorelix):** Used in ART to induce pituitary downregulation (desensitization), preventing a premature LH surge during superovulation with gonadotropins,,.

**5. Adjuvant Therapies for Biochemical Abnormalities**

- **Insulin Sensitizers (Metformin):** Used for PCOS patients with hyperinsulinemia and insulin resistance (BMI >25). 500 mg thrice daily reduces hyperandrogenism and significantly increases ovulation rates when combined with Clomiphene,,.
- **Dopamine Agonists (Bromocriptine, Cabergoline):** Drug of choice for anovulation caused by hyperprolactinemia (with or without galactorrhea). Normalizes prolactin and restores ovulation in 80% of cases,,.
- **Dexamethasone:** 0.25–0.5 mg at bedtime may be used to suppress adrenal androgens in hyperandrogenic states, improving responsiveness to ovulation drugs,.
- **Thyroxine:** For correcting subclinical or overt hypothyroidism, which otherwise disrupts GnRH pulsatility and prevents ovulation,,.

8. Conservative management of Utero vaginal prolapse.
**CONSERVATIVE MANAGEMENT OF UTERO-VAGINAL PROLAPSE**

**Indications for Conservative Management** Conservative management is primarily indicated for:

- Asymptomatic women.
- Mild degree of prolapse.
- Old women who are absolutely unfit for surgery or are unwilling to undergo an operation.
- Pelvic organ prolapse (POP) in early pregnancy and puerperium.
- Patients who are waiting for surgical correction.

**General Measures and Lifestyle Modifications**

- **Avoidance of Aggravating Factors:** Patients are advised to avoid strenuous activities, heavy weight lifting, chronic cough, and constipation.
- **Weight Management:** Obese patients should be advised on weight reduction to decrease intra-abdominal pressure.
- **Contraception:** Avoid future pregnancies too soon and having too many pregnancies by practicing contraception.

**Medical and Physical Therapy**

- **Pelvic Floor Exercises:** Kegel exercises are recommended to voluntarily squeeze and strengthen the pelvic floor muscles.
- **Hormone Therapy:** Estrogen replacement therapy may be prescribed for postmenopausal women to improve minor degrees of prolapse and atrophy of supporting structures.

**Pessary Treatment** A pessary does not cure the prolapse but effectively relieves the symptoms by stretching the hiatus urogenitalis, thereby preventing vaginal and uterine descent.

- **Types of Pessaries:** The commonly used types are the Hodge-Smith pessary (made of rubber, silicone, or ebonite) and the Ring pessary (made of silicone and rubber).
- **Additional Benefits:** Pessary use can improve associated urinary symptoms, such as voiding problems and urgency.
- **Contraindications:** Use of a pessary is contraindicated in the presence of active pelvic infection/sepsis, a fixed retroverted uterus, and gross relaxation of the pelvic floor muscles.
- **Follow-up Care:** The pessary must be removed, examined, cleaned, and reinserted usually at an interval of 8–10 months. At each visit, the patient should be evaluated for symptoms such as vaginal bleeding, pain, offensive discharge, and voiding difficulties.
- **Complications of Pessary Use:** Potential complications include vaginal discharge with a bad odor, vaginal bleeding, pelvic pain (especially with a large-size pessary), pessary incarceration, and formation of pessary ulcers. Pessary ulcers are typically treated with local estrogen applications. Rarely, forgotten pessaries can be a precursor to vaginal cancer.

**Management During Pregnancy and Puerperium**

- **In Early Pregnancy:** The prolapsed cervix should be replaced inside the vagina and kept in position using a ring pessary. The pessary is retained until the 18th to 20th week of pregnancy, after which the enlarged uterus naturally sits on the pelvic brim.
- **If the Pelvic Floor is Extremely Lax:** The patient should be advised bed rest with the foot end of the bed raised by about 20 cm.
- **Management of Edema:** If the prolapsed mass is hugely edematous and congested, it should be covered with gauze soaked in glycerine and acriflavine to relieve the swelling.
- **Late Pregnancy:** The patient should preferably be admitted to the hospital at the 36th week of pregnancy.
- **During Puerperium:** If subinvolution of the uterus is evident, a ring pessary may be utilized until the involution process is fully complete.

9. Role of HRT for menopausal symptoms
**ROLE OF HORMONE REPLACEMENT THERAPY (HRT) FOR MENOPAUSAL SYMPTOMS**

**Introduction and Objectives** Hormone Replacement Therapy (HRT), also referred to as Hormone Therapy (HT), is indicated in menopausal women to overcome the short-term and long-term consequences of oestrogen deficiency and to maintain the quality of life during the menopausal years.

**Indications for HRT**

- Relief of menopausal vasomotor symptoms (hot flashes and night sweats).
- Relief of urogenital atrophy and associated symptoms.
- Prevention of osteoporosis.
- Specific conditions necessitating hormone replacement: premature ovarian failure, gonadal dysgenesis, and surgical or radiation menopause.

**Benefits of HRT**

- **Vasomotor Symptoms:** Provides 70–80% improvement in hot flashes, upper body sweating, and palpitations.
- **Urogenital Atrophy:** Highly effective in reversing atrophic epithelial changes in the vagina, urinary bladder, and urethra. It relieves dyspareunia, vaginal dryness, urgency, dysuria, and recurrent urinary tract infections.
- **Osteoporosis and Fracture Prevention:** Increases bone mineral density (BMD) by 2–5% and significantly decreases the risk of vertebral and hip fractures by 25–50%. Oestrogen inhibits osteoclastic activity, increases calcium absorption from the gut, and stimulates calcitonin secretion.
- **Colorectal Cancer:** Associated with a 20% reduction in the incidence of colorectal cancer.
- **Cardiovascular System:** Offers possible cardioprotection by preventing the oxidation of low-density lipoproteins (LDL), improving glucose metabolism, and stimulating the release of vasodilators like nitric oxide and prostacyclin.

**Principles of Prescribing and Regimens** The principal hormone used in HRT is oestrogen; however, the regimen heavily depends on the presence or absence of the uterus.

- **Oestrogen-Only Therapy (ERT):** Ideal and recommended exclusively for women who have undergone a hysterectomy. Commonly used preparations include conjugated equine oestrogen (CEE) 0.3–0.625 mg/day or micronized oestradiol 1–2 mg/day.
- **Combined Oestrogen and Progestin Therapy:** Mandatory for women with an intact uterus to prevent oestrogen-induced endometrial proliferation, hyperplasia, and adenocarcinoma.
    - _Cyclic Progestin:_ Oestrogen is given continuously, and a progestin (e.g., medroxyprogesterone acetate [MPA] 1.5–5 mg/day or dydrogesterone 5–10 mg/day) is added for the last 10 to 14 days of each month.
    - _Continuous Combined Therapy:_ Both oestrogen and progestin are given continuously to prevent endometrial hyperplasia, though initial irregular bleeding may occur.
- **Levonorgestrel Intrauterine System (LNG-IUS):** A specialized device releasing 10 µg of LNG per day can be used. It provides excellent endometrial protection against hyperplasia without causing systemic progestin side effects, allowing oestrogen to be administered safely via any route.
- **Tibolone:** A 19-nortestosterone derivative with weak oestrogenic, progestogenic, and androgenic properties. Administered at 2.5 mg/day, it prevents osteoporosis, hot flashes, and vaginal atrophy, while also increasing libido and keeping the endometrium atrophic.
- **Topical/Vaginal Oestrogen:** Conjugated equine vaginal oestrogen cream (1.25 mg daily) or vaginal tablets are highly effective for treating isolated urogenital atrophy and urinary symptoms in women who do not wish to undergo systemic HRT.

**Routes of Administration**

- **Oral:** Easiest and most common, but subject to first-pass hepatic metabolism.
- **Transdermal (Patch/Gel):** Avoids first-pass hepatic metabolism. Risks of venous thromboembolism (VTE) and gallbladder disease are not increased compared to the oral route.
- **Subdermal Implants:** Provide physiological E2 to E1 ratios and can be kept for 6 months; highly suitable for patients post-hysterectomy.

**Risks and Adverse Effects**

- **Endometrial Cancer:** Risk is significantly increased if unopposed oestrogen is given to a woman with an intact uterus.
- **Breast Cancer:** Long-term use of combined oestrogen and progestin therapy slightly increases the risk of breast cancer (Relative Risk 1.26).
- **Venous Thromboembolism (VTE):** Risk is increased with the use of combined oral oestrogen and progestin.
- **Cardiovascular Risks:** Combined HRT shows a relative hazard (RR 1.29) for coronary heart disease, though hypertension itself is not a direct risk of HRT.
- **Gallbladder Disease:** Increased incidence due to a rise in cholesterol content within the bile.

**Contraindications to HRT**

- **Absolute:** Known, suspected, or history of breast cancer or other oestrogen-dependent neoplasms; undiagnosed genital tract bleeding; history of VTE or active deep vein thrombosis (DVT); active liver disease; and severe organic heart disease.
- **Relative (Requires Caution):** Prior cholestatic jaundice, gallbladder disease, and prior endometriosis.

**Guidelines for Monitoring and Continuation**

- **Duration and Dosage:** HRT should be prescribed at the lowest effective dose for the shortest period of time necessary to control symptoms, provided the benefits continue to outweigh the risks.
- **Monitoring Protocol:** A baseline parameter evaluation and annual check-ups are mandatory. This includes a physical and pelvic examination, blood pressure recording, breast examination with mammography, cervical cytology, and transvaginal sonography (TVS) to ensure endometrial thickness remains < 5 mm. Any irregular bleeding during HRT must be thoroughly investigated with an endometrial biopsy or hysteroscopy.


10. Medical management of ectopic pregnancy.

**MEDICAL MANAGEMENT OF ECTOPIC PREGNANCY**

Medical management is a highly effective, conservative approach for unruptured ectopic pregnancies, drastically reducing operative morbidity, hospital stay, and cost while improving the chances of subsequent intrauterine pregnancies.

**CRITERIA FOR MEDICAL THERAPY** Patient selection is crucial for the success of medical management. The patient must fulfill the following criteria:

- Hemodynamically stable.
- Asymptomatic and compliant for follow-up.
- Serum $\beta$-hCG level is less than 3,000 IU/L.
- Tubal diameter (ectopic mass) is less than 4 cm.
- No embryonic/fetal cardiac activity visible on Transvaginal Sonography (TVS).
- No sonographic evidence of intra-abdominal hemorrhage or tubal rupture.

**CONTRAINDICATIONS TO METHOTREXATE (MTX) THERAPY**

- **Absolute Contraindications:** Breastfeeding, hepatic, renal, or hematologic dysfunction, significant anemia, and immunodeficiency states.
- **Relative Contraindications:** Sensitivity to Methotrexate, presence of embryonic cardiac motion, active pulmonary disease, and a gestational sac > 3.5 cm.

**DRUGS USED**

- **Systemic Agent:** Methotrexate (MTX) is the most commonly used systemic drug. It is a folic acid antagonist that blocks dihydrofolate reductase, preventing DNA, RNA, and protein synthesis in the actively dividing trophoblast cells.
- **Local Agents (Salpingocentesis):** Delivered directly into the ectopic mass under sonographic or laparoscopic guidance. Agents include Methotrexate, Potassium Chloride (KCl), Prostaglandin (PGF$_{2\alpha}$), hyperosmolar glucose, or actinomycin. Local injection of mifepristone into the unruptured sac is also utilized.

**METHOTREXATE DOSAGE REGIMENS** **1. Single-Dose Regimen (Preferred for uncomplicated cases)**

- A single dose of Methotrexate $50 mg/m^2$ is administered intramuscularly (IM).

**2. Multi-Dose Regimen (For advanced gestations or embryonic cardiac activity)**

- Methotrexate $1 mg/kg$ IM is given on Days 1, 3, 5, and 7.
- Folinic acid (Leucovorin) $0.1 mg/kg$ IM is given as rescue therapy on Days 2, 4, 6, and 8.

**MONITORING AND FOLLOW-UP**

- **Single-Dose Monitoring:** Serum $\beta$-hCG levels are measured on Day 4 and Day 7 post-treatment.
    - If the decline in $\beta$-hCG between Day 4 and Day 7 is $\ge$ 15%, the patient is followed up weekly until the level drops below 10 mIU/mL.
    - If the decline is < 15%, a second dose of MTX $50 mg/m^2$ is administered on Day 7.
- **Multi-Dose Monitoring:** Serum $\beta$-hCG is monitored weekly until the level falls below 5.0 mIU/mL.
- **Surgical Intervention:** Surgery (Laparoscopic salpingostomy or salpingectomy) is indicated if $\beta$-hCG levels plateau or rise despite medical therapy, if tubal rupture occurs, or if fetal cardiac activity persists.

**SIDE EFFECTS OF METHOTREXATE** Side effects are more common with the multi-dose regimen.

- **Common:** Flatulence (most common), mucositis, gastroenteritis, hepatitis, anemia, and bone marrow depression.
- **Separation Pain:** Patients may experience pelvic pain shortly after therapy. This is caused by the death of trophoblast cells and the separation of the ectopic mass from the tubal wall, which generally subsides on its own.

**MEDICAL MANAGEMENT IN UNCOMMON ECTOPIC PREGNANCIES**

- **Interstitial Pregnancy:** Systemic or local MTX can be used if hemodynamically stable.
- **Cervical Pregnancy:** Managed with systemic MTX or local intra-sac injection of KCl.
- **Cesarean Scar Pregnancy:** Systemic (IM) MTX or local injection directly into the gestation sac.
- **Ovarian Pregnancy:** Systemic MTX is used when surgical risk is high or for persistent Gestational Trophoblastic Neoplasia (GTN) postoperatively.
- **Abdominal Pregnancy:** Systemic MTX with USG-guided feticide in early cases; in advanced cases, it is used as an adjunct therapy to surgery.
- **Heterotopic Pregnancy:** Medical management relies exclusively on local feticide (KCl or hyperosmolar glucose). Systemic MTX is strictly avoided unless the concurrent intrauterine pregnancy is nonviable or the patient wishes to terminate it.

**PROGNOSIS** The overall success rate of Methotrexate medical therapy in properly selected cases is 90%. It provides an excellent outcome for the preservation of future fertility.

11. Endometrial sampling methods.
**ENDOMETRIAL SAMPLING METHODS**

Endometrial sampling is a frequently performed, rapid, safe, and inexpensive diagnostic test employed to obtain endometrial tissue for histopathological or cytohormonal evaluation.

**INDICATIONS**

- **Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (AUB):** To exclude organic lesions like endometrial polyps, hyperplasia, or carcinoma.
- **Postmenopausal Bleeding:** Mandatory to exclude endometrial malignancy.
- **Infertility Work-up:** To determine the functional state of the endometrium and to detect ovulation or luteal phase defect (LPD).
- **Suspected Infections:** Endometrial tuberculosis (sent for culture, PCR, and guinea pig inoculation).
- **Periodic Screening:** During hormone replacement therapy (HRT).

**METHODS OF ENDOMETRIAL SAMPLING**

**1. Outpatient/Office Procedures (Blind Biopsy)** These procedures are performed in the outpatient department (OPD) without the need for general anesthesia.

- **Pipelle Endometrial Sampler:** It is currently the instrument of first choice for endometrial sampling. It consists of a thin, flexible plastic cannula (2–4 mm diameter) with a plunger inside. When the plunger is withdrawn, adequate endometrial tissue is obtained due to suction action. It is highly reliable, well-accepted by patients, and has a failure rate of less than 8%.
- **Sharman’s Curette:** A metallic curette used primarily in infertility work-ups where only a small strip of endometrium is sufficient to study hormonal reflection.
- **Vabra Aspirator:** Another suction device used to aspirate endometrial tissue.

_Limitations of blind procedures:_ Being blind procedures, they may miss focal intrauterine pathologies like endometrial polyps or submucous fibroids.

**2. Hysteroscopy and Directed Biopsy (Gold Standard)**

- **Principle:** Allows direct visualization of the endometrial cavity using a fiberoptic telescope and taking a targeted biopsy from the specific offending site.
- **Advantages:** It is superior to blind curettage as focal lesions (polyps, submucous fibroids) are often missed by blind D&C. Hysteroscopy and directed biopsy (H and B) has largely replaced conventional D&C for both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes.

**3. Inpatient Surgical Procedures**

- **Dilatation and Curettage (D&C):** It is the most reliable method to study the endometrium when a large tissue mass is needed. It involves dilatation of the cervical canal followed by thorough uterine curettage under general or regional anesthesia. It is especially indicated for endometrial tuberculosis, postmenopausal bleeding, or when OPD sampling fails to provide a diagnostic result.
- **Fractional Curettage:** The definitive method of diagnosis and staging for suspected endometrial carcinoma. It is performed in an orderly step: first, endocervical curettage is done, then the cervical canal is dilated, and finally, the uterine cavity (fundus and body) is curetted. Specimens are collected in separate containers to determine the exact extent of the malignant growth.

**TIMING OF ENDOMETRIAL BIOPSY**

- **For Infertility (to detect ovulation):** Biopsy should be performed in the secretory phase, typically on the 21st–23rd day of a regular 28-day cycle. Subnuclear vacuolation is the earliest histologic evidence of ovulation, appearing 36–48 hours post-ovulation.
- **For Acyclic/Irregular Bleeding:** Biopsy can be done at any time, preferably soon after the bleeding starts or within 24 hours of menstruation.

**COMPLICATIONS OF ENDOMETRIAL SAMPLING**

- **Uterine Perforation and Hemorrhage:** Can occur during sounding or curettage, especially in a soft, pregnant, or malignant uterus.
- **Infection:** Flaring up of pelvic inflammatory disease (PID).
- **Uterine Synechiae (Asherman's Syndrome):** Overzealous and vigorous curettage can destroy the basal layer of the endometrium, leading to intrauterine adhesions, subsequent amenorrhea, or hypomenorrhea.

12. Diagnosis and management of endometriosis.
**DIAGNOSIS OF ENDOMETRIOSIS**

The diagnosis of endometriosis relies on a combination of clinical evaluation, imaging, and surgical visualization with histological confirmation.

**1. Clinical Diagnosis**

- **Symptoms:** The classic triad consists of progressively increasing secondary dysmenorrhea (co-menstrual), deep dyspareunia, and infertility. Other symptoms include chronic pelvic pain and abnormal uterine bleeding (menorrhagia, premenstrual spotting). Symptoms are not strictly proportionate to the extent of the lesion; however, deep penetrating lesions (>5 mm) cause more severe pain.
- **Speculum Examination:** May reveal bluish "powder-burn" lesions in the posterior fornix or cervix that are tender and may bleed on touch.
- **Bimanual/Rectovaginal Examination:** Classical findings include a fixed retroverted uterus, nodularity in the pouch of Douglas, nodular uterosacral ligaments, and the presence of unilateral or bilateral adnexal masses (chocolate cysts). Often, no abnormal findings are detected in early stages.

**2. Serum Markers**

- **CA 125:** A moderate elevation of serum CA 125 is seen in severe endometriosis. While not specific for diagnosis, it is useful for monitoring therapeutic response and detecting disease recurrence.
- **Other markers:** Monocyte Chemotactic Protein (MCP-1) and Glycodelin levels may be elevated in the peritoneal fluid and serum.

**3. Imaging Modalities**

- **Transvaginal Sonography (TVS):** Useful for detecting ovarian endometriomas (chocolate cysts). It typically shows a thick-walled cyst with homogeneous internal echoes.
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** The best non-invasive diagnostic tool (91–95% sensitivity and specificity). It shows characteristic hyperintensity on T1-weighted images and hypointensity on T2-weighted images. Highly superior for identifying deep infiltrating endometriosis (DIE) and deep-seated endometriomas.

**4. Laparoscopy (Gold Standard)**

- Double puncture laparoscopy is the "gold standard" for diagnosis, assessment of the site/size/extent, and staging of the disease.
- Classic appearances include black "powder burns" or "match stick" spots on the peritoneum. Other subtle lesions include red flame-shaped areas, yellow-brown patches, and white peritoneal scarring.
- Allows simultaneous therapeutic intervention (ablation/adhesiolysis) and targeted biopsy.

**5. Histology**

- Definitive confirmation is by microscopic identification of endometrial glands, endometrial stroma, and hemosiderin-laden macrophages in the excised tissue.

---

**MANAGEMENT OF ENDOMETRIOSIS**

Endometriosis is a progressive disease. Management depends on the patient's age, location and severity of the lesion, desire for fertility, and severity of symptoms. The primary goals are relief of pain, improvement of fertility, and prevention of recurrence.

**1. Expectant Management**

- **Indications:** Minimal to mild endometriosis, asymptomatic patients, unmarried or young married women planning to conceive immediately, or women approaching menopause.
- **Protocol:** Observation coupled with Nonsteroidal Anti-inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs) or prostaglandin synthetase inhibitors (e.g., Ibuprofen 800–1200 mg or mefenamic acid 150–600 mg daily) for pain relief. Patients are encouraged to conceive, as pregnancy naturally improves the condition.

**2. Medical (Hormonal) Therapy** The principle is to induce atrophy of endometriotic implants. It is suppressive rather than curative, aiming to create a hypoestrogenic state ("pseudopregnancy", "pseudomenopause", or "medical oophorectomy").

- **Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs):** Induces "pseudopregnancy" with endometrial decidualization and atrophy. Improves symptoms in 80% of cases and relieves dysmenorrhea.
- **Progestogens:** E.g., Medroxyprogesterone acetate, Norethisterone, or Dienogest (2 mg/day). These inhibit pituitary gonadotropins and cause endometrial atrophy.
- **Levonorgestrel-Releasing IUD (LNG-IUS):** Highly effective in reducing dysmenorrhea, pelvic pain, and menorrhagia; particularly useful for rectovaginal endometriosis.
- **Danazol:** A synthetic steroid that creates a "pseudomenopause" (hypoestrogenic, hyperandrogenic state). Dose is 400–600 mg/day for 6-9 months. It has a high rate of androgenic side effects (weight gain, acne). Gestrinone is an alternative with fewer side effects.
- **GnRH Agonists (e.g., Leuprolide, Goserelin):** Induces a "medical oophorectomy" by suppressing pituitary GnRH receptors, maintaining estrogen at low levels. Highly effective for rapid symptom relief. "Add-back" therapy (low-dose estrogen/progestin) is given for prolonged use to prevent bone demineralization and vasomotor symptoms.
- **Aromatase Inhibitors & GnRH Antagonists:** Letrozole or Anastrozole reduce estrogen levels directly in endometriotic tissues. Oral GnRH antagonists (Elagolix) control pain effectively without the initial flare effect.

**3. Surgical Management**

- **Conservative Surgery:** Aims to destroy endometriotic lesions, relieve pain, and restore pelvic anatomy while preserving reproductive function.
    - Usually performed laparoscopically via electrodiathermy or laser vaporization.
    - Ovarian endometriomas <3 cm are aspirated and irrigated; for cysts >3 cm to 4 cm, laparoscopic cystectomy (peeling off the cyst wall) is performed. Note: Extensive destruction of the ovarian cortex reduces ovarian reserve.
    - Laparoscopic Uterosacral Nerve Ablation (LUNA) or Presacral Neurectomy (PSN) can be done for severe midline pain.
- **Definitive Surgery:** Indicated for advanced stage disease, severe symptoms failing medical therapy, or in women who have completed their family.
    - Includes Total Hysterectomy with Bilateral Salpingo-Oophorectomy (BSO) and maximal resection of endometriotic implants.

**4. Management of Infertility associated with Endometriosis**

- Medical suppression alone does not improve fertility.
- Mild to moderate cases: Fertility is improved by conservative laparoscopic surgery (adhesiolysis and ablation). Controlled Ovarian Hyperstimulation (COH) with Intrauterine Insemination (IUI) is effective empirically.
- Severe/Extensive pelvic disease (Grade III-IV): Extensive surgery removing large endometriomas may be harmful and reduce ovarian reserve. Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART) such as In Vitro Fertilization and Embryo Transfer (IVF-ET) is the preferred management approach.


13. List risk factors for endometrial carcinoma.
**RISK FACTORS FOR ENDOMETRIAL CARCINOMA**

The risk factors for endometrial carcinoma can be broadly categorized into hyperestrogenic states, metabolic disorders, reproductive factors, genetic predisposition, and iatrogenic causes.

**1. Hyperestrogenic States (Unopposed Estrogen)** Persistent stimulation of the endometrium with unopposed estrogen is the single most important factor for the development of endometrial cancer.

- **Persistent Anovulation:** Conditions like Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome (PCOS) lead to chronic anovulation and continuous estrogenic stimulation without progesterone opposition.
- **Estrogen-Secreting Tumors:** Functioning ovarian tumors, such as Granulosa cell tumors and Thecomas, produce excess endogenous estrogen.
- **Atypical Endometrial Hyperplasia:** Considered a direct precursor, preceding carcinoma in about 25% of cases (Type 1). The presence of atypical endometrial hyperplasia carries a high relative risk (RR: 8–29).

**2. "Corpus Cancer Syndrome" & Metabolic Factors** The classic triad of the "Corpus Cancer Syndrome" encompasses obesity, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus.

- **Obesity:** Extremely significant risk factor (Relative Risk [RR]: 2–10). It leads to a high level of free estradiol due to the peripheral conversion of androgens to estrogen in adipose tissue and low levels of sex hormone-binding globulin.
- **Diabetes and Hypertension:** Frequently co-exist with obesity and independently elevate the risk.

**3. Reproductive and Menstrual Factors**

- **Age:** It is predominantly a disease of postmenopausal women (about 75% of cases), with a median age of 60 years.
- **Nulliparity:** Highly associated with endometrial cancer (RR: 2–3). It is present in about 30% of women who develop the disease.
- **Late Menopause:** Menopause occurring beyond 52 years of age prolongs the duration of endogenous estrogen exposure (RR: 2–4).
- **Menstrual Irregularities:** A history of irregular and excessive premenopausal bleeding is frequently noted.

**4. Genetic and Familial Factors**

- **Lynch Syndrome (Hereditary Nonpolyposis Colorectal Cancer - HNPCC):** Women with this autosomal dominant syndrome (due to mismatch repair gene mutations like MLH1, MSH2) have a 40–60% lifetime risk of developing endometrial cancer, representing a Relative Risk of 20.
- **Family History:** A personal or family history of breast, ovary, colon, or endometrial cancer significantly increases susceptibility.
- **BRCA Mutations:** Carriers of BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations have a slightly increased risk.

**5. Iatrogenic Causes**

- **Unopposed Estrogen Replacement Therapy:** Administration of estrogen without adding a progestin in postmenopausal (non-hysterectomized) women significantly elevates the risk (RR: 4–8).
- **Tamoxifen Therapy:** Although an antiestrogen used for breast cancer treatment, it possesses weak estrogenic properties on the endometrium, thereby increasing the risk of endometrial hyperplasia and carcinoma with long-term use.
- **Radiation Menopause:** A history of prior pelvic radiation to induce menopause is a recognized risk factor.

**6. Associated Pelvic Pathology**

- **Uterine Fibroids:** Coexist with endometrial carcinoma in about 30% of cases, likely sharing a similar hyperestrogenic pathophysiological background.



14. Complications of fibroid uterus.

**COMPLICATIONS OF FIBROID UTERUS (LEIOMYOMA)**

Uterine fibroids (leiomyomas), though predominantly benign, can lead to a multitude of secondary changes, clinical emergencies, and reproductive complications. The complications are broadly classified as follows:

**1. SECONDARY CHANGES (DEGENERATIONS AND VASCULAR CHANGES)** As the tumor outgrows its blood supply, it undergoes various secondary changes.

- **Hyaline Degeneration:** The most common secondary change (65%), affecting all sizes of fibroids except very tiny ones. The central, least vascular part undergoes hyaline changes of both muscle and fibrous tissue.
- **Cystic Degeneration:** Usually occurs following menopause, formed by the liquefaction of hyalinized areas. It can mimic an ovarian cyst or pregnancy.
- **Fatty Degeneration:** Occurs at or after menopause, with fat globules depositing mainly in the muscle cells.
- **Calcific Degeneration (10%):** Usually involves subserous fibroids with a small pedicle or in postmenopausal women. Precipitation of calcium carbonate or phosphate occurs, and complete calcification is termed a "womb stone".
- **Red (Carneous) Degeneration:** Predominantly occurs in large fibroids during the second half of pregnancy or puerperium (5–10%). It is vascular in origin and presents with an acute abdomen, pain, and a raw-beef appearance on cut section.
- **Necrosis:** Circulatory inadequacy leads to central necrosis, particularly in submucous polyps or pedunculated subserous fibroids.
- **Atrophy:** Occurs postmenopausally due to the loss of estrogenic support.
- **Vascular Changes:** Dilatation of the vessels (telangiectasia) or lymphatic channels (lymphangiectasis) inside the myoma can occur.

**2. HEMORRHAGE AND SEVERE ANEMIA**

- **Chronic Bleeding:** Persistent menorrhagia (hypermenorrhea) or metrorrhagia is a classic symptom, eventually leading to severe iron-deficiency anemia.
- **Intraperitoneal Hemorrhage:** A rare but life-threatening complication resulting from the rupture of a surface vein overlying a subserous fibroid, leading to massive intraperitoneal hemorrhage and shock.
- **Intracapsular Hemorrhage:** Can occur within the substance of the tumor.

**3. TORSION**

- Torsion exclusively involves a **subserous pedunculated fibroid**.
- It precipitates an acute abdomen with severe pain and can lead to gangrene/necrosis of the tumor.

**4. INFECTION**

- Infection typically gains access through the thinned and sloughed surface epithelium of a submucous fibroid, most commonly following delivery or abortion.
- Severe uncontrolled infection can lead to pelvic peritonitis or life-threatening septicemia.

**5. SARCOMATOUS CHANGE (MALIGNANCY)**

- Malignant transformation into a sarcoma (usually leiomyosarcoma) is extremely rare, occurring in less than 0.1% of cases.
- It should be suspected if there is rapid enlargement of the fibroid, postmenopausal bleeding, or recurrence following myomectomy.

**6. PRESSURE SYMPTOMS** Depending on their anatomical location, fibroids can compress adjacent pelvic viscera.

- **Urinary Tract:** Anterior or central cervical fibroids press on the bladder, causing urinary frequency, dysuria, or acute retention of urine. Broad ligament fibroids can compress the ureter, leading to hydroureter, hydronephrosis, and secondary pyelonephritis.
- **Bowel:** Posterior wall or posterior cervical fibroids can become impacted in the pouch of Douglas, causing rectal compression and severe constipation.

**7. REPRODUCTIVE AND OBSTETRICAL COMPLICATIONS**

- **Infertility:** Fibroids are implicated in about 30% of subfertility cases. Causes include distortion or elongation of the uterine cavity preventing sperm ascent, impaired rhythmic uterine contractions, cornual tubal block, and defective endometrial nidation due to congestion or ulceration.
- **During Pregnancy:** Increased incidence of miscarriage, preterm labor, and intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) due to defective implantation and reduced space. Red degeneration and impaction causing acute urinary retention are also notable risks.
- **During Labor:** Complications include uterine inertia, failure of the presenting part to engage, malpresentation (e.g., breech, transverse), cervical dystocia, obstructed labor, and an increased rate of cesarean sections.
- **During Puerperium:** High risk of postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) due to uterine atony or morbidly adherent placenta over the myoma site. Increased risks of subinvolution, secondary PPH, puerperal sepsis, lochiometra, and pyometra. Rarely, a fundal submucous fibroid polyp can cause chronic uterine inversion.

**8. LIFE-THREATENING COMPLICATIONS (SUMMARY)** The major life-threatening emergencies related to fibroid uterus include:

1. Severe anemia from persistent heavy menstrual bleeding.
2. Massive intraperitoneal hemorrhage from a ruptured subserous vein.
3. Severe infection leading to peritonitis or septicemia.
4. Sarcomatous malignant transformation.


5. Prevention of RH Isoimmunisation.
**PREVENTION OF RH ISOIMMUNIZATION (ALLOIMMUNIZATION)**

The prevention of Rh alloimmunization in an Rh-negative woman relies on three cardinal principles:

1. Prevention of active immunization using Rh anti-D immunoglobulin.
2. Prevention or minimization of fetomaternal hemorrhage during obstetric procedures.
3. Avoidance of mismatched blood transfusion.

**1. PREVENTION OF ACTIVE IMMUNIZATION (ROLE OF ANTI-D IMMUNOGLOBULIN)** To prevent active immunization of an Rh-negative, unimmunized mother, Rh anti-D immunoglobulin (IgG) is administered intramuscularly.

**Mechanism of Action:** It acts via Antibody-Mediated Immune Suppression (AMIS) through three possible mechanisms:

- The injected anti-D antibody blocks the Rh-antigen sites on the fetal red cells.
- The intact antibody-coated fetal red cells are rapidly removed from the maternal circulation by the spleen and lymph nodes.
- Central inhibition occurs where fetal red cells coated with anti-D antibodies interfere with the production of IgG from maternal B cells.

**Dosage and Schedule of Administration:**

- **Postpartum Prophylaxis:** A standard dose of 300 µg (1500 IU) is administered intramuscularly within 72 hours (preferably earlier) following the delivery of an Rh-positive baby, provided the infant's direct Coombs' test is negative. This standard dose protects the mother from a fetomaternal hemorrhage of up to 30 mL of fetal whole blood or 15 mL of fetal red cells.
- **Routine Antenatal Anti-D Prophylaxis (RAADP):** Even with postpartum prophylaxis, a 1–2% failure rate exists due to silent antepartum fetomaternal hemorrhage. Administering one dose of 300 µg Rh-immunoglobulin prophylactically at around 28 weeks of gestation reduces the third-trimester alloimmunization risk to a mere 0.1%.
- **Early Pregnancy Complications:** All Rh-negative unsensitized women must receive 50 µg of Rh-immunoglobulin IM within 72 hours of a spontaneous or induced abortion, ectopic pregnancy, hydatidiform mole, or Chorionic Villus Sampling (CVS) occurring in the first trimester (<12 weeks).
- **Late Pregnancy Complications:** For any of the above events occurring beyond 12 weeks of gestation, the full dose of 300 µg is mandated.
- **Other Indications for Anti-D Prophylaxis:** Genetic amniocentesis, cordocentesis, embryo reduction, placenta previa with bleeding, placental abruption, External Cephalic Version (ECV), abdominal trauma, and Intrauterine Fetal Death (IUFD).

**Assessment of Massive Fetomaternal Hemorrhage (Kleihauer-Betke Test):**

- To calculate the appropriate dose of anti-D in suspected massive fetomaternal bleeds, the Kleihauer-Betke acid elution test is utilized.
- Because fetal erythrocytes containing HbF are resistant to acid elution, they appear as dark red, refractile bodies on the peripheral smear, whereas adult erythrocytes appear as "ghosts".
- If the volume of fetomaternal hemorrhage exceeds 30 mL of whole blood, the required dose of Rh-immune globulin is calculated at 10 µg for every 1 mL of fetal whole blood.

**2. PREVENTION OR MINIMIZATION OF FETOMATERNAL BLEED** Strict obstetric precautions must be taken to minimize the transfer of fetal blood into the maternal circulation:

- **During Labor and Delivery:** Prophylactic ergometrine with the delivery of the anterior shoulder should preferably be withheld, as it may facilitate a larger fetoplacental bleed.
- **Placental Management:** Routine manual removal of the placenta should be avoided. If manual removal is absolutely necessary, it must be performed very gently.
- **During Cesarean Section:** Care must be taken to prevent fetal blood from spilling into the maternal peritoneal cavity.
- **Obstetric Procedures:** Forcible attempts at external cephalic version under anesthesia should be strictly avoided. Amniocentesis must always be performed under direct ultrasound guidance following placental localization to prevent injury and bloody tap.
- **Antepartum Hemorrhage:** Abdominal palpation should be minimized as much as possible in cases of abruptio placentae.

**3. AVOIDANCE OF MISMATCHED BLOOD TRANSFUSION**

- It is a critical and lifelong rule to avoid transfusing Rh-positive blood to an Rh-negative female from her birth until menopause.
- If an inadvertent transfusion of Rh-positive blood occurs, Rh-immunoglobulin must be administered to prevent sensitization.


15.b. Symptoms signs and management of Bacterial Vaginosis

**BACTERIAL VAGINOSIS**

**INTRODUCTION AND ETIOPATHOGENESIS** Bacterial vaginosis (BV) is the most common cause of symptomatic vaginal discharge, accounting for 15–50% of cases.

- **Causative Organisms:** It is caused by the synergistic action of _Gardnerella vaginalis_ (previously known as _Haemophilus vaginalis_) along with anaerobic organisms such as _Bacteroides_ species, _Peptococcus_ species, _Mobiluncus_, _Prevotella_, and _Mycoplasma hominis_.
- **Pathology:** There is a marked decrease or absence of the normal protective, acid-forming _Lactobacilli_ in the vagina. The condition is termed "vaginosis" rather than vaginitis because there is an absence of vaginal inflammation on biopsy.

**RISK FACTORS**

- Multiple or new sexual partners.
- Early age of sexual intercourse.
- Cigarette smoking.
- Intrauterine Contraceptive Device (IUCD) use.
- Immunosuppression.

**CLINICAL FEATURES** **Symptoms:**

- **Vaginal Discharge:** The primary symptom is a creamy, grayish-white, to green-yellow vaginal discharge.
- **Odor:** The discharge characteristically has a foul, unpleasant "fishy" odor.
- **Absence of Pruritus:** Unlike candidiasis or trichomoniasis, BV is typically non-irritating and non-pruritic.

**Signs:**

- On speculum examination, the discharge is homogeneous, grayish-white, and typically adherent to the vaginal walls.
- There are absolutely no signs of local inflammation (erythema or edema).

**COMPLICATIONS** If left untreated, BV can lead to significant gynecological and obstetrical complications:

- **Gynecological:** Recurrent infections leading to Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID), PID following abortion, and vaginal cuff cellulitis following hysterectomy.
- **Obstetrical:** Second-trimester miscarriage, pregnancy loss before 20 weeks, preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), preterm labor and birth, endometritis, and a decreased success rate with in vitro fertilization (IVF) treatments.

**DIAGNOSIS** **1. Amsel’s Diagnostic Criteria (Gold Standard for Clinical Diagnosis):** The presumptive diagnosis of BV requires the presence of at least **three out of the four** following criteria:

- **Homogeneous vaginal discharge:** Thin, grayish-white, and adherent to the vaginal walls.
- **Vaginal pH > 4.5:** Tested using litmus paper.
- **Positive Whiff Test:** A characteristic fishy (amine) odor is emitted when a drop of the vaginal discharge is mixed with 10% Potassium Hydroxide (KOH) solution.
- **Presence of Clue Cells (>20% of cells):** On a wet mount saline microscopy, clue cells are seen. These are vaginal squamous epithelial cells heavily covered with coccobacilli, giving them a stippled or granular appearance that obscures the cell borders.

**2. Gram-Stained Vaginal Smear (Hay/Ison Criteria):** Shows a preponderance of _Gardnerella_ or _Mobiluncus_ morphotypes with a significant reduction or complete absence of normal _Lactobacilli_.

_(Note: Routine bacterial vaginal culture has no role in the diagnosis of Bacterial Vaginosis.)_

**MANAGEMENT (CDC Guidelines - 2015)** The goal of treatment is to eradicate the anaerobic overgrowth and restore normal vaginal flora. The cure rate with appropriate therapy is about 80%.

**First-Line Medical Therapy:**

- **Metronidazole:** 500 mg orally, twice daily (BID) for 7 days.
- **Tinidazole:** 2 g orally for 3 days.

**Alternative Therapy:**

- **Clindamycin:** 300 mg orally, twice daily (BID) for 7 days.
- **Clindamycin Ovules:** 100 mg intravaginally, once at bedtime for 3 days.

**Partner Management:** The sexual partner should be treated simultaneously to prevent recurrence.


16. Complications of LSCS.
**COMPLICATIONS OF LOWER SEGMENT CESAREAN SECTION (LSCS)**

The complications of Cesarean Section (LSCS) are related either to the inherent hazards of the operation itself or due to anesthesia. They are broadly grouped into maternal and fetal complications.

**I. MATERNAL COMPLICATIONS**

**A. Intraoperative Complications**

- **Hemorrhage:** Can be due to uterine atony or uterine lacerations.
- **Extension of Uterine Incision:** The incision may extend to one or both sides, potentially involving the uterine vessels, leading to severe hemorrhage or broad ligament hematoma formation.
- **Uterine Lacerations:** Lower uterine incisions may lacerate and extend laterally or inferiorly into the vagina.
- **Bladder Injury:** Rare in primary CS but may occur during repeat procedures. If it occurs, it is repaired with a two-layer closure and requires continuous bladder drainage for 7-10 days.
- **Ureteral Injury:** Extremely rare (1 in 1,000 cases); usually occurs while controlling bleeding from lateral extensions.
- **Gastrointestinal Tract Injury:** Rare, unless there are prior pelvic or abdominal adhesions.
- **Morbidly Adherent Placenta (Placenta Accreta):** Commonly encountered in cases with placenta previa and a history of prior cesarean delivery. It often requires a total cesarean hysterectomy to control hemorrhage.
- **Anesthetic Hazards:** Include failure in intubation, ventilation issues, and risk of aspiration.

**B. Postoperative Complications — Immediate**

- **Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH):** Blood loss is often underestimated. It is mostly related to uterine atony, though blood coagulation disorders may rarely occur. Secondary PPH can also occur later.
- **Shock:** Mostly hypovolemic (related to blood loss). It may also occur if the surgery is performed after prolonged labor without correcting pre-existing dehydration and ketoacidosis.
- **Infections:** Risk factors include prolonged labor, prolonged rupture of membranes, and repeated vaginal examinations. Common sites include:
    - Uterus (endomyometritis)
    - Urinary tract
    - Abdominal wound
    - Peritoneal cavity (peritonitis)
    - Lungs (pulmonary infection/pneumonia)
    - Septic pelvic thrombophlebitis
- **Anesthetic Complications:**
    - _General Anesthesia:_ Aspiration of gastric contents leading to aspiration pneumonitis (Mendelson's syndrome), atelectasis, nausea, vomiting, and sore throat.
    - _Regional (Spinal/Epidural) Anesthesia:_ Hypotension and post-spinal headache.
- **Intestinal Obstruction:** May be paralytic ileus (following peritonitis) or mechanical (due to adhesions or bands).
- **Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT) & Thromboembolism:** Risk of DVT and pulmonary embolism is significantly higher following cesarean section compared to vaginal delivery.
- **Wound Complications:** Assessed during stitch removal, these include sanguineous or frank pus discharge, hematoma, wound dehiscence (intact peritoneum), burst abdomen (involving the peritoneal coat), and rarely, necrotizing fasciitis.

**C. Postoperative Complications — Remote (Long-term)**

- **Gynecological:** Menstrual excess or irregularities, chronic pelvic pain, and backache.
- **General Surgical:** Development of incisional hernia, and intestinal obstruction due to intraperitoneal adhesions and bands.
- **Future Pregnancy Complications:**
    - Risk of uterine scar rupture or scar dehiscence during subsequent pregnancies or labor.
    - Increased risk of abnormal placentation, including placenta previa and Placenta Accreta Spectrum (PAS) disorders.
    - Increased need for repeat cesarean delivery or peripartum hysterectomy in subsequent pregnancies.

**II. FETAL COMPLICATIONS**

- **Iatrogenic Prematurity:** Occurs when fetal maturity is uncertain prior to an elective procedure.
- **Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS):** Higher risk of neonatal respiratory morbidity (2-3% to 4-5%) compared to vaginal birth.
- **Birth Trauma:** Accidental scalpel injury (lacerations) to the baby during the uterine incision.

**III. MATERNAL AND PERINATAL MORTALITY**

- **Maternal Mortality:** The overall maternal mortality ranges between 6 to 22 per 100,000 procedures, often compounded by adverse patient profiles and suboptimal circumstances.

16b. Course of ureter and injuries of ureter during hysterectomy

**COURSE AND RELATIONS OF THE PELVIC URETER**

The pelvic ureter is a retroperitoneal structure measuring about 13 cm in length with a diameter of 5 mm.

**Course and Anatomical Relations:**

- **Entry into the Pelvis:** It enters the pelvis in front of the bifurcation of the common iliac artery over the sacroiliac joint, situated behind the root of the mesentery on the right side and the apex of the mesosigmoid on the left side.
- **Lateral Pelvic Wall:** As it courses downwards in contact with the peritoneum, it lies anterior to the internal iliac artery and behind the ovary, forming the posterior boundary of the ovarian fossa.
- **Pelvic Floor and Broad Ligament:** On reaching the ischial spine, it lies over the pelvic floor and courses forwards and medially on the base of the broad ligament.
- **Crossing of Uterine Artery:** About 1.5 to 2 cm lateral to the cervix at the level of the internal os, the ureter is crossed anteriorly (from above) by the uterine artery.
- **Ureteric Tunnel (Tunnel of Wertheim):** The ureter enters the ureteric tunnel, lying close to the supravaginal part of the cervix (about 1.5 cm lateral to it).
- **Entry into Bladder:** It traverses a short distance on the anterior fornix of the vagina, pierces the lateral angle of the bladder, and courses obliquely into the bladder wall for about 2 cm before opening into the base of the trigone.

**Normal Constrictions in the Pelvic Ureter:**

1. Where it crosses the pelvic brim.
2. Where it is crossed by the uterine artery.
3. In the intravesical part.

**Identification of Ureter During Surgery:** The ureter is recognized by its pale glistening appearance, the presence of longitudinal blood vessels on its surface, and the elicitation of peristalsis upon stimulation with a surgical instrument.

---

**INJURIES TO THE URETER DURING HYSTERECTOMY**

Due to its close anatomical association with the genital organs, the ureter is highly vulnerable during gynecological surgery. About 75% of ureteral injuries result from gynecological operations, predominantly abdominal procedures.

**Common Sites of Ureteric Injury During Hysterectomy (High Yield):**

1. **At the Infundibulopelvic Ligament:** The ureter runs parallel to the ovarian vessels at the pelvic brim. Injury occurs during clamping and ligation of the ovarian vessels (infundibulopelvic ligament).
2. **By the Side of the Cervix (Crossing of Uterine Artery):** Injury occurs while clamping the cardinal ligament and descending cervical artery (uterine artery pedicle), exactly where the uterine artery crosses the ureter 1.5 cm lateral to the internal os. This risk is higher during re-clamping of a slipped uterine artery pedicle.
3. **At the Vaginal Angle (Anterior Fornix):** The ureter traverses the anterior vaginal fornix within the ureteric tunnel (tunnel of Wertheim) to enter the bladder. Injury occurs while clamping the vaginal angles.
4. **During Pelvic Peritonization:** The ureter lies in the posterior leaf of the broad ligament peritoneum and may be inadvertently caught in sutures during closure.

**Predisposing Risk Factors (Pathological Conditions):** Risk of injury increases significantly when normal pelvic anatomy is distorted by:

- Cervical or broad ligament fibroids.
- Pelvic endometriosis (causes dense fibrosis).
- Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) / tubo-ovarian mass with dense adhesions.
- Ovarian or gynecological malignancies requiring radical hysterectomy.
- Pelvic hematoma.

**Types and Nature of Ureteric Injuries:**

- **Simple kinking or angulation:** Due to closely placed sutures causing obstruction.
- **Ligature incorporation:** Accidental tying of the ureter.
- **Crushing injury:** Due to clamp application, leading to subsequent necrosis.
- **Ischemic injury:** Resulting from overzealous stripping/denudation of the ureteric sheath, destroying its longitudinal blood supply and leading to sloughing necrosis.
- **Transection:** Partial or complete cutting of the ureter.
- **Thermal injury:** From monopolar/bipolar diathermy or laser energy during laparoscopic procedures.

**Prevention of Ureteric Injuries:**

- **Anatomical Knowledge & Exposure:** Operate from known anatomical areas into the unknown, ensuring adequate exposure of the pelvic organs.
- **Direct Visualization and Palpation:** The ureter must be visualized or palpated throughout its pelvic course whenever possible.
- **Preservation of Sheath:** Meticulous dissection must be performed without damaging the ureteric sheath to preserve its longitudinal blood vessels.
- **Avoid Blind Clamping:** Never blindly clamp bleeding vessels deep in the pelvis, especially if a pedicle slips.
- **Preoperative/Intraoperative Aids:** Intravenous urography (IVU) can be done preoperatively to ascertain the course of the ureter in large pelvic tumors. Placement of ureteral catheters (stents) or Uriglow catheters can facilitate identification and dissection during complex surgeries.

17. Emergency contraception.
**EMERGENCY CONTRACEPTION (POSTCOITAL CONTRACEPTION / INTERCEPTION)**

**Definition** Emergency contraception (EC), also referred to as interception, is a method used to prevent conception following an accidental unprotected sexual exposure, specifically around the time of ovulation. It is strictly an emergency measure and should not be used as a regular method of contraception.

The risk of pregnancy following a single episode of unprotected sexual intercourse (UPSI) around the time of ovulation ranges from 8% to 30%.

**Indications for Emergency Contraception**

- Unprotected sexual intercourse (UPSI) on any day of the natural cycle.
- Condom rupture or slippage.
- Missed oral contraceptive pill or delay in taking the progestin-only pill (POP) for more than 3 hours.
- Sexual assault or rape.
- First-time intercourse, which is frequently unplanned.
- UPSI from day 21 after childbirth (unless lactational amenorrhea criteria are met).
- UPSI from day 5 after an abortion, miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, or uterine evacuation of gestational trophoblastic disease (GTD).

**METHODS OF EMERGENCY CONTRACEPTION**

The available methods, ranked in order of their effectiveness, include Copper IUDs, Selective Progesterone Receptor Modulators (Ulipristal acetate), Antiprogesterones (Mifepristone), and Hormonal pills (Levonorgestrel and Yuzpe method).

**1. Copper Intrauterine Device (Cu-IUCD) - The Gold Standard**

- **Effectiveness:** It is the most effective method of emergency contraception (failure rate <0.1% to 1%).
- **Timing:** Should be inserted within 5 days of UPSI, or within 5 days of ovulation, whichever is later.
- **Mechanism of Action:** Ionized copper is toxic to sperms and ova, inhibiting fertilization. It also alters the endometrium to prevent blastocyst implantation (both pre- and post-fertilization effects).
- **Advantage:** It can be retained in place for up to 10 years to provide continuous, regular contraception.

**2. Selective Progesterone Receptor Modulators (SPRMs) & Antiprogesterones**

- **Ulipristal Acetate (UPA):** The second most effective method.
    - _Dose:_ 30 mg taken orally as a single dose as soon as possible, or within 120 hours of coitus.
    - _Mechanism:_ It acts by suppressing follicular and endometrial growth, thereby delaying ovulation and inhibiting implantation.
    - _Failure Rate:_ 0% to 2%.
    - _Contraindications:_ Severe hepatic dysfunction and severe asthma.
- **Mifepristone (RU 486):** An antiprogesterone that binds competitively to progesterone receptors, nullifying endogenous progesterone.
    - _Dose:_ A single dose of 100 mg taken within 5 days of intercourse or on the 27th day of the cycle.
    - _Mechanism:_ Prevents implantation by its antiprogesterone effect.
    - _Failure Rate:_ 0% to 0.6%.

**3. Hormonal Methods (Morning-After Pills)**

- **Levonorgestrel (LNG / E. pills):** The third most effective method.
    - _Dose:_ 1.50 mg taken as a single dose, OR 0.75 mg taken in two doses at a 12-hour interval.
    - _Timing:_ The first dose should ideally be taken within 72 hours, though it can be used up to 120 hours post-coitus (ineffective after 96 hours according to some tables).
    - _Failure Rate:_ 0% to 1%.
- **Yuzpe Method (Combined Hormonal Regimen):**
    - _Dose:_ 2 tablets of Ovral (0.25 mg levonorgestrel + 50 µg ethinyl estradiol) taken as early as possible (<72 hours), followed by 2 more tablets 12 hours later.
    - _Failure Rate:_ 0% to 2%.
    - _Drawback:_ High incidence of intense nausea and vomiting. An oral antiemetic (e.g., metoclopramide 10 mg) should be prescribed 1 hour before each dose.
- **Estrogen Alone:** Ethinyl estradiol 2.5 mg taken orally twice daily for 5 days, starting within 72 hours. This is highly associated with intense nausea and vomiting.
- **Centchroman (Saheli):** A nonsteroidal antiestrogenic compound that can also be safely used as an emergency contraceptive.

**Mechanism of Action of Hormonal Emergency Contraception** The precise mechanism remains unclear, but multiple possibilities exist:

- Prevention or delay of ovulation if the drug is taken early in the cycle.
- Interference with fertilization.
- Rendering the endometrium unfavorable, thus preventing implantation (though this may not apply to LNG pills).
- Interference with the function of the corpus luteum, potentially causing luteolysis.

**Management in Case of Method Failure** If emergency contraception fails and pregnancy ensues, no adverse fetal effects or teratogenicity have been observed from the hormones. However, the patient must be counseled, and induced abortion should be offered as an option.


18. Differential diagnosis for mass per vagina
**DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS FOR MASS PER VAGINA**

The complaint of a "mass per vagina" or a "sensation of something coming down" is a common gynecological presentation. The differential diagnosis encompasses structural prolapse, benign or malignant neoplastic growths, and congenital anomalies.

**1. PELVIC ORGAN PROLAPSE (POP)** This is the most common cause of a vaginal mass and includes the descent of the vaginal wall and/or the uterus.

- **Uterine Prolapse:** Descent of the uterus and cervix. In second-degree prolapse, the external os protrudes outside the introitus. In third-degree (procidentia), the entire uterus and inverted vagina lie outside the introitus.
- **Cystocele / Cystourethrocele:** Bulge of the anterior vaginal wall containing the bladder and/or urethra. Characterized by transverse rugosities on the mucosa, reducibility, and a positive impulse on coughing.
- **Rectocele / Enterocele:** Bulge of the posterior vaginal wall containing the rectum or loops of bowel in the pouch of Douglas.

**2. FIBROID POLYP** A pedunculated submucous fibroid or a cervical fibroid polyp can protrude through the external os into the vagina or even outside the introitus.

- **Differentiating Features:** The mass is usually shaggy with a broad leading part, and no opening is visible on the leading part. Bimanual examination reveals the pedicle coming through the cervical canal, while the uterus itself is in a normal position.
- **Uterine Sound Test:** An uterine sound can be passed into the uterine cavity alongside the pedicle, confirming it is a polyp and not an inversion.

**3. CHRONIC UTERINE INVERSION** This is a condition where the uterus is turned inside out, with the fundus prolapsing through the cervix into the vagina. It may be obstetric in origin or secondary to a fundal submucous myoma or sarcoma.

- **Differentiating Features:** Inspection reveals a globular, shaggy mass with no opening on the leading part. On bimanual examination, the cervical rim is felt high up (in incomplete inversion), and a cup-shaped depression is felt at the fundus. Rectal examination confirms the absence of the uterine body in its normal position.
- **Uterine Sound Test:** The uterine sound cannot be passed all around between the mass and the cervical canal, distinguishing it from a fibroid polyp.

**4. CONGENITAL ELONGATION OF THE CERVIX** Often confused with uterine prolapse, this condition involves an abnormal elongation of the vaginal part of the cervix.

- **Differentiating Features:** It is usually unassociated with actual vault prolapse. The vaginal fornices are exceptionally deep and narrow, and the cervix appears conical. The external os lies below the level of the ischial spines, but the uterine body is of normal size. The passage of a uterine sound reveals a markedly increased uterocervical canal length.

**5. VAGINAL WALL CYSTS (e.g., GARTNER'S DUCT CYST)** A Gartner's duct cyst arises from Wolffian duct remnants and typically presents as a cystic swelling on the anterolateral wall of the vagina, which can be confused with a cystocele.

- **Differentiating Features:** Unlike a cystocele, a Gartner's cyst has well-defined margins, is tense and shiny with a loss of overlying vaginal rugosities, is not reducible, and has no impulse on coughing. If a metal catheter is introduced into the urethra, its tip will not be felt just underneath the vaginal mucosa of the cyst. Epidermal inclusion cysts on the posterior vaginal wall can similarly mimic a rectocele.

**6. CERVICAL POLYP** These are benign outgrowths, usually from the endocervical canal, appearing as small, reddish, slippery masses protruding out of the external os. They frequently present with contact bleeding or intermenstrual bleeding rather than just a mass.

**7. MALIGNANT NEOPLASMS**

- **Fungating Cervical Malignancy:** An exophytic, cauliflower-like carcinoma of the cervix can fill the upper vagina. It is indurated, highly friable, and bleeds readily to touch.
- **Prolapsed Hypertrophied Ulcerated Cervix:** Chronic prolapse with severe decubitus ulceration can mimic a malignancy and must be evaluated with cytology and biopsy.
- **Sarcoma Botryoides:** This is a highly aggressive embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma typically found in female infants and children (usually prior to 6 years of age). It presents as a pinkish, "grape-like" polypoidal and edematous mass filling the vagina and protruding from the introitus, often accompanied by blood-stained watery discharge.

**8. URETHRAL PATHOLOGY** Conditions at the external urethral meatus can be perceived as a vaginal mass by the patient.

- **Urethral Caruncle:** A small, angry red, pea-shaped fleshy mass protruding from the external urethral meatus, typically seen in postmenopausal women.
- **Prolapse of the Urethral Mucosa:** Can mimic a caruncle but involves circumferential eversion of the urethral mucosa.


19. Sequelae of pelvic inflammatory disease

**SEQUELAE OF PELVIC INFLAMMATORY DISEASE (PID)**

Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID) can lead to significant immediate and remote complications. Approximately one in four women with acute PID will suffer from long-term sequelae. The complications and sequelae of PID are broadly categorized into immediate complications and late sequelae.

**A. LATE SEQUELAE (REMOTE COMPLICATIONS)** These are the long-term consequences that significantly impact a woman's reproductive and general health:

- **Infertility:** This is one of the most severe sequelae. The infertility rate is about 12% after a single episode of PID, increases to 25% after two episodes, and rises to 50% after three episodes. It is primarily due to cornual block or structural damage to the wall of the fallopian tube, loss of cilia, or the formation of a tubo-ovarian mass.
- **Ectopic Pregnancy:** There is a 6-fold to 10-fold increased risk of ectopic pregnancy due to tubal damage, narrowing, and impaired peristalsis.
- **Chronic Pelvic Pain (CPP):** Persistent, dull aching chronic pelvic pain and dysmenorrhea are very common sequelae.
- **Dyspareunia and Marital Disharmony:** Deep dyspareunia often develops due to pelvic adhesions, fixed retroverted uterus, or chronic infection, which can lead to significant marital disharmony.
- **Pelvic Adhesive Disease:** Post-inflammatory fibrosis leads to the formation of dense pelvic adhesions, which can distort pelvic anatomy.
- **Hydrosalpinx, Pyosalpinx, and Tubo-Ovarian Abscess:** Chronic inflammation can lead to the permanent closure of tubal ostia, resulting in fluid or pus accumulation (hydrosalpinx or pyosalpinx) and the formation of chronic tubo-ovarian masses.
- **Chronic Pelvic Inflammation / Residual Infection:** The patient may suffer from residual, low-grade chronic infection characterized by periodic acute exacerbations.
- **Intestinal Obstruction:** Dense pelvic and intestinal adhesions formed secondary to the inflammatory exudate can lead to mechanical intestinal obstruction.
- **Menstrual Abnormalities:** Patients may experience abnormal uterine bleeding (AUB) secondary to the chronic inflammatory state and pelvic congestion.
- **Endometriosis:** Associated as a late sequela in some chronic cases.
- **Chronic Ill Health:** Persistent pain, infection, and psychological distress lead to overall chronic ill health.
- **Ultimate Surgical Endpoint:** In severe, obstinate cases with recurrent flare-ups and destroyed pelvic anatomy, the patient may eventually require a hysterectomy.

**B. IMMEDIATE COMPLICATIONS** If the acute episode is not treated promptly and adequately, it can lead to life-threatening emergencies:

- **Peritonitis:** Pelvic peritonitis or even generalized peritonitis.
- **Septicemia:** Gram-negative or polymicrobial septicemia which can further produce metastatic complications like septic arthritis or myocarditis.
- **Endotoxic Shock:** Especially following postabortal or puerperal PID.
- **Tubo-Ovarian / Pelvic Abscess:** Rapid formation and potential rupture of an abscess in the pouch of Douglas or adnexa.
- **Thrombophlebitis and Embolism:** Pelvic septic thrombophlebitis (usually developing after 7–10 days) which carries a high risk of fatal pulmonary embolism.
- **Renal Failure:** Oliguria or anuria and Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) can manifest in fulminant cases.


20. Complications of hysterectomy
**COMPLICATIONS OF HYSTERECTOMY**

The complications of hysterectomy can be broadly classified into intraoperative, postoperative (immediate and early), and remote (late) complications. They also vary slightly depending on the route and extent of the surgery (e.g., abdominal, vaginal, radical, or laparoscopic).

**I. INTRAOPERATIVE COMPLICATIONS**

- **Hemorrhage:** Excessive bleeding during the procedure.
- **Visceral Injury:** Inadvertent injury to adjacent organs such as the intestine, bladder, or ureter.
- **Anesthetic Hazards:** Complications like atelectasis, pulmonary edema, or embolism.

**II. POSTOPERATIVE COMPLICATIONS (IMMEDIATE AND EARLY)**

- **Hemorrhagic Shock:** Resulting from severe hypovolemia.
- **Postoperative Hemorrhage:**
    - _Primary Hemorrhage:_ Occurs due to the slipping of a ligature, most commonly at the vaginal angle.
    - _Secondary Hemorrhage:_ Occurs between 7–14 days postoperatively due to sepsis, causing bleeding from the vault or sloughing of the uterine/ovarian artery.
- **Urinary Complications:**
    - _Retention of urine:_ Typically due to pain and reflex spasm.
    - _Anuria:_ May arise from inadequate fluid replacement (prerenal) or bilateral ureteric obstruction (postrenal).
    - _Cystitis:_ Often resulting from catheterization.
    - _Incontinence:_ Overflow or stress incontinence may occur. True incontinence immediately after surgery suggests direct injury to the bladder or ureter, whereas incontinence developing 7–14 days later is usually due to sloughing and necrosis leading to a vesicovaginal fistula (VVF) or ureterovaginal fistula (UVF).
- **Pyrexia (Fever):** Common causes include cystitis, abdominal wound infection, vault cellulitis, pelvic hematoma, thrombophlebitis, peritonitis, or pulmonary infections like atelectasis or pneumonia.
- **Wound Complications and Sepsis:**
    - Pelvic or rectus sheath hematomas, which may cause low-grade fever.
    - Wound dehiscence, particularly common with vertical incisions or in immunocompromised patients.
    - Necrotizing fasciitis, a rare but life-threatening infection causing extensive tissue necrosis in the superficial and subcutaneous layers.
- **Gastrointestinal Complications:** Paralytic ileus and mechanical intestinal obstruction.

**III. LATE AND REMOTE COMPLICATIONS**

- **Vault Granulation:** More common when catgut sutures are used, less common with Vicryl.
- **Incisional Hernia:** More frequent following midline vertical incisions compared to low transverse incisions.
- **Vault Prolapse:** Can occur, though it is less common following abdominal hysterectomy compared to vaginal hysterectomy.
- **Fallopian Tube Prolapse:** Prolapse of the tube through the vaginal vault is a rare complication.
- **Psychosexual Issues:** Depression, psychiatric symptoms, and sexual dysfunction.
- **Residual Ovarian Syndrome:** If one or both ovaries are conserved, 1–3% of patients may develop multiple cystic follicles and periovarian adhesions, presenting with chronic pelvic pain and dyspareunia, requiring relaparotomy.

**IV. COMPLICATIONS SPECIFIC TO THE TYPE OF HYSTERECTOMY**

- **Vaginal Hysterectomy:** Immediate complications notably include vault cellulitis, pelvic abscess, and thrombophlebitis.
- **Radical Hysterectomy:**
    - High risk of fistula formation: Ureteric fistula (1%) and vesicovaginal fistula (0.5%).
    - Bladder dysfunction (atony) due to the extensive damage of sympathetic and parasympathetic nerve fibers. Continuous catheterization is required for 6–10 days.
    - Neuropathies due to direct injury to nerves (femoral, obturator, sciatic, pudendal, genitofemoral).
    - Lymphocyst formation in the pelvis and lymphedema of the legs following lymph node dissection.
- **Laparoscopic Hysterectomy:**
    - Port-site complications such as port-site hernia.
    - Injury to inferior epigastric vessels during the insertion of accessory trocars.
    - Extraperitoneal insufflation causing surgical or omental emphysema.
    - Thermal injuries to the bowel or ureter from electrosurgical instruments (e.g., diathermy or laser).

21. Follow up of vesicular mole.
**OBJECTIVES OF FOLLOW-UP** Routine follow-up is strictly mandatory for all cases of vesicular mole (hydatidiform mole) for at least 6 months to 1 year. The prime objective is to detect the development of persistent gestational trophoblastic disease (GTD) or neoplasia (GTN), such as choriocarcinoma, which occurs in 15–20% of cases and is mostly confined to this period.

**FOLLOW-UP SCHEDULE**

- **Initial Phase:** Check-ups are done at an interval of one week until the serum $\beta$-hCG level becomes negative. This usually happens by 4–8 weeks.
- **Subsequent Phase:** Once the hCG is negative within 56 days (or for 3 consecutive weeks), the patient is followed up at 1-month intervals for 6 months.
- **Late Phase:** Follow-up is then continued every 2–3 months for the next 6 months (totaling 1 year of follow-up).

**CLINICAL EVALUATION AT EACH VISIT**

- **History:** Enquire about relevant symptoms such as persistent ill health, irregular vaginal bleeding, continuing amenorrhea, or respiratory symptoms like persistent cough, breathlessness, and hemoptysis.
- **Abdominovaginal Examination:** Evaluate for:
    1. Involution of the uterus (subinvolution is a warning sign).
    2. Ovarian size (theca lutein cysts usually regress spontaneously within 2 months of evacuation).
    3. Appearance of secondary metastatic deposits, particularly in the anterior vaginal wall or fornices.
- Pelvic examination or transvaginal ultrasonography (TVS) is typically done one week after molar evacuation.

**INVESTIGATIONS**

- **Serum $\beta$-hCG Assay:** Assays are carried out at every visit. As the hCG molecule has structural heterogeneity and exists in different isoforms, the test should include assays for all isoforms of hCG. Normal levels should be reached within 3 months.
- **Chest Radiograph (X-ray):** If the pre-evacuation chest X-ray was normal, it is only repeated if the serum hCG titer plateaus or rises, to check for 'cannon ball' shadows suggestive of pulmonary metastasis.

**CONTRACEPTIVE ADVICE** The patient must strictly avoid pregnancy for at least 1 year following the treatment of a molar pregnancy. A new pregnancy will cause a rise in hCG, obscuring the detection of developing GTN.

- **Intrauterine Contraceptive Device (IUCD):** Absolutely contraindicated due to the high risk of uterine perforation and its frequent association with irregular vaginal bleeding, which confuses the clinical picture of choriocarcinoma.
- **Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs):** Can be prescribed safely, but only _after_ the serum hCG values have returned to normal.
- **Injectable Progestins / Implants:** Depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA) or progestin implants can be used safely.
- **Barrier Methods:** Condoms can be used safely.
- **Surgical Sterilization:** Can be offered if the woman is older ($\ge$ 40 years) and has completed her family.

**UNFAVORABLE MANIFESTATIONS (WARNING SIGNS OF GTN)** During follow-up, the following findings are highly suspicious of persistent GTN or choriocarcinoma:

1. Serum hCG titers remain elevated, plateau for 3 or more consecutive weeks, or re-elevate after an initial negative report.
2. Irregular vaginal bleeding or persistent amenorrhea.
3. Persistently soft, enlarged, and subinvoluted uterus.
4. Persistence of theca lutein cysts.
5. Appearance of respiratory symptoms or vaginal metastases.

_Note: In women with high-risk factors (e.g., age >40, initial $\beta$-hCG $\ge$ 100,000 IU/mL, previous molar pregnancy, or those unlikely to comply with follow-up), prophylactic chemotherapy may be considered to prevent uterine invasion and metastasis_.


22. Chronic pelvic pain
**DEFINITION** Chronic pelvic pain (CPP) is defined as noncyclic (nonmenstrual) pain of 6 months duration or more, localized to the pelvis, anterior abdominal wall below the pelvis, or lower back, which is severe enough to cause functional disability requiring medical or surgical treatment.

**EPIDEMIOLOGY** CPP is a common problem, accounting for about 10% of gynecologic outpatient visits. Approximately 20–30% of laparoscopies and 10% of all hysterectomies are performed for CPP.

**ETIOLOGY** The causes of CPP are broadly classified into gynecologic and nongynecologic.

**1. Gynecologic Causes**

- **Cyclic:**
    - Dysmenorrhea (spasmodic or congestive).
    - Intermenstrual pain (Mittelschmerz).
    - Premenstrual syndrome.
    - Pelvic congestion syndrome.
    - Endometriosis and Adenomyosis.
    - Ovarian remnant syndrome.
    - Uterine fibroids and IUCD.
- **Acyclic:**
    - Endometriosis and Adenomyosis.
    - Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) and tubo-ovarian abscess.
    - Pelvic adhesion disease (secondary to PID, endometriosis, or postsurgical).
    - Uterine displacement (fixed retroversion or prolapse).
    - Uterine fibroids and Ovarian cysts (functional or neoplastic).
    - Intrauterine device (IUD).
    - Trapped or residual ovarian syndrome.
    - Idiopathic (pelvic varicosities, psychosomatic).

**2. Nongynecologic Causes**

- **Gastrointestinal:** Irritable bowel syndrome, appendicitis, constipation, diverticulitis, and adhesions.
- **Urological:** Interstitial cystitis, urethral syndrome, calculi, chronic urinary tract infection (UTI), and urinary tract stones.
- **Orthopedic:** Diseases of the lumbar bones, ligaments, muscles of the lumbosacral region, and coccydynia.
- **Neurological:** Nerve entrapment or compression, neuralgia (pudendal, ilioinguinal, or genitofemoral nerve), and pyriformis syndrome.
- **Hernias:** Inguinal, femoral, or obturator.
- **Musculoskeletal:** Muscular strain, myofascial pain, levator ani syndrome, and fibromyositis.
- **Miscellaneous:** Physical, sexual, or psychiatric factors.

**DIAGNOSIS** A meticulous history and thorough clinical examination (abdominal and vaginal) with the possible pathologies in mind often clinch the diagnosis.

- **Cornett Sign:** Used to differentiate localized pain over the anterior abdominal wall (due to nerve entrapment or myofascial pain) from intra-abdominal pain. The patient is asked to raise her head and shoulder from a lying down posture; anterior abdominal wall pain decreases or is relieved. Local infiltration of 0.25% bupivacaine provides pain relief and acts as a confirmatory test.
- **Trigger Point (TRP) Evaluation:** Identification of hyperirritable areas within a muscle band (pelvic floor, abdominal wall, or pelvic girdle) causing CPP.
- **Therapeutic Tests:** A pessary test may be used for mobile retroverted uterus or mild prolapse; if symptoms are relieved after 3 months, surgical correction is advisable. Cyclic combined oral contraceptive (OC) pills for 3 months can be used to regress functional ovarian cysts or relieve midmenstrual pain.

**INVESTIGATIONS** Investigations are tailored to substantiate the clinical diagnosis:

- **Laboratory Tests:** Complete hemogram (infection), thyroid profile, and blood sugar (neuropathy). Midstream urine for microscopic examination and culture to rule out UTI. Cervical and vaginal discharge for Gram stain, hanging drop, and culture (aerobic/anaerobic) for PID.
- **Ultrasonography (TVS/CDS):** Highly valuable for detecting fibroids, adenomyosis, ovarian cysts, ovarian torsion, and urinary calculi.
- **Laparoscopy (Gold Standard):** An invaluable diagnostic tool that acts as a "one-stop" diagnostic and therapeutic procedure. It detects minimal endometriosis and pelvic adhesions. About 50% of cases with normal clinical findings show an abnormality on laparoscopy. A negative finding holds value by reassuring the patient and relieving psychosomatic factors.
- **Radiological Imaging:** X-rays of the lumbosacral region and hip joints (for orthopedic lesions). CT scan or MRI are utilized mainly for malignant conditions or complex cases.
- **Endoscopy:** Cystoscopy, sigmoidoscopy, or colonoscopy in selected cases.

**MANAGEMENT** **Principles of Treatment**

1. Arrive at a definite diagnosis of the underlying disorder.
2. Establish the relationship between the pathology and the symptoms.
3. Evaluate psychosomatic factors (cause or effect).
4. Utilize a multidisciplinary approach involving a psychologist, especially when no gross pathology is detected.

**1. Medical Management of Pain**

- **Assurance and Counseling:** Sympathetic handling often ameliorates the pain.
- **Analgesics:** Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) like Ibuprofen, Naproxen, and COX-2 inhibitors (Celecoxib, Ketorolac).
- **Neurolytic Agents:** Tricyclic antidepressants (Amitriptyline, Imipramine), Serotonin uptake inhibitors (Sertraline, Fluoxetine), and Ion channel blockers/anticonvulsants (Gabapentin, Pregabalin, Carbamazepine, Lamotrigine) for neuropathic pain.
- **Hormonal Therapy:** Oral contraceptive pills, progestogens, danazol, or GnRH analogs are indicated for young patients with minimal endometriosis, spasmodic dysmenorrhea, or midmenstrual pain.
- **Polypharmacy:** Combining drugs acting on different sites may improve pain relief.

**2. Surgical Management**

- **Conservative/Minimal Invasive Surgery:** Laparoscopic laser ablation or diathermy for pelvic endometriosis and laparoscopic adhesiolysis.
- **Nerve Ablation:** Presacral neurectomy (PSN) and laparoscopic uterine nerve ablation (LUNA) are considered for intractable midline dysmenorrhea when conservative management has failed.
- **Uterine Suspension:** Ventrosuspension or plication of round ligaments for deep dyspareunia.
- **Definitive Surgery:** Hysterectomy (with or without bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy) is the ideal treatment for women with completed childbearing who suffer from severe adenomyosis or pelvic endometriosis unresponsive to medical therapy.

**3. Other Modalities**

- **Transcutaneous Electric Nerve Stimulation (TENS):** Helps by inhibiting the transmission of nerve impulses via unmyelinated fibers.
- **Cognitive and Behavioral Therapy (CBT):** Highly beneficial for chronic pain management and addressing psychosomatic components.
- **Biofeedback and Electrical Stimulation:** Useful for inactivating trigger points and relaxing taut muscle bands.
