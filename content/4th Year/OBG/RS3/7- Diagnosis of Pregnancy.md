1. Weight gain during pregnancy— significance
**MATERNAL WEIGHT GAIN DURING PREGNANCY**

**NORMAL PATTERN OF WEIGHT GAIN**

- In the early weeks, the patient may lose weight because of nausea or vomiting.
- During subsequent months, the weight gain is progressive until the last 1 or 2 weeks, when the weight remains static.
- The total weight gain during the course of a singleton pregnancy for a healthy woman averages 11-12 kg (24 lb).
- This average total weight gain is distributed as 1 kg in the first trimester, and 5 kg each in the second and third trimesters.
- During the second half of pregnancy, the average weight gain is 1 kg per fortnight.

**DISTRIBUTION OF WEIGHT GAIN AT TERM** Nearly 60% of the total gestational weight gain is due to the combined weight of the fetus, placenta, and amniotic fluid. The total weight gain of 12 kg at term is approximately distributed as follows:

- **Fetus:** 3.3 kg.
- **Placenta:** 0.6 kg.
- **Liquor (Amniotic fluid):** 0.8 kg.
- **Uterus:** 0.9 kg.
- **Breasts:** 0.4 kg.
- **Blood volume:** 1.2 kg.
- **Extracellular fluid:** 1.3 kg.
- **Maternal stores (fat and protein):** 3.5 kg.

**RECOMMENDATIONS BASED ON PRE-PREGNANCY BODY MASS INDEX (BMI)** Ideally, the recommended weight gain should depend on the woman's pre-pregnancy Body Mass Index (BMI) level, as maternal nutrition and weight gain are directly related to the newborn's weight.

- **Underweight (BMI <18.5 kg/m²):** Recommended total weight gain is 28-40 lbs, and the patient may be allowed to gain up to 18 kg.
- **Normal weight (BMI 18.5-24.9 kg/m²):** Recommended total weight gain is 25-35 lbs (11-16 kg). Women with a normal BMI should eat adequately to gain the optimum weight.
- **Overweight (BMI 25.0-29.9 kg/m²):** Recommended total weight gain is 15-25 lbs, and these women should limit their weight gain to 7 kg.
- **Obese (BMI ≥30.0 kg/m²):** Recommended total weight gain is 11-20 lbs, and they should not gain more than 7 kg. Intentional weight loss is not recommended in pregnancy.

**CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE OF WEIGHT CHECKING** A single weight checking is of little value except to identify whether the patient is overweight or underweight. However, periodic and regular weight checking at subsequent visits is of vital importance to detect obstetrical abnormalities.

- **Rapid or Excessive Weight Gain:** A rapid gain in weight of more than 0.5 kg (1 lb) a week or more than 2 kg (4 lb) a month in the later months of pregnancy is considered significant. Any excess weight gain may be due to excess fluid retention and could be the first and earliest sign of pre-eclampsia, often appearing even before visible edema. Specifically, an excessive weight gain of more than 4 lb (1.8 kg) a week in the later months is a highly pathological warning sign of pre-eclampsia. Additionally, excessive weight gain increases the risk of antepartum and intrapartum complications, including fetal macrosomia.
- **Stationary or Falling Weight:** If the maternal weight gain is less than normal, remains stationary, or falls during the second half of pregnancy, one should be highly suspicious of intrauterine growth restriction (FGR/IUGR) or intrauterine fetal death (IUFD). In cases of prolonged or post-term pregnancy, regular periodic weight checking classically reveals a stationary or even falling maternal weight due to decreasing amniotic fluid and placental insufficiency.
- **Multiple Pregnancy:** Multiple pregnancy imposes physical changes on the mother in excess of those seen in a singleton pregnancy, characteristically presenting with an unusual and increased rate of weight gain not explained by pre-eclampsia or obesity.

2. Diagnosis of pregnancy (in first trimester).
**DIAGNOSIS OF PREGNANCY IN THE FIRST TRIMESTER (FIRST 12 WEEKS)**

**I. SUBJECTIVE SYMPTOMS (PRESUMPTIVE SYMPTOMS)**

- **Amenorrhea:** Cessation of menstruation is the earliest and most common symptom during the reproductive period. Occasionally, slight cyclic bleeding ("placental sign") may occur up to 12 weeks until the decidual space is obliterated by the fusion of the decidua vera with the decidua capsularis.
- **Morning Sickness (Nausea and Vomiting):** Inconsistently present in about 70% of cases, commonly appearing soon after the missed period and resolving by 12–14 weeks.
- **Frequency of Micturition:** A troublesome symptom between 8 and 12 weeks. It is caused by the bulky anteverted uterus resting on the bladder fundus, mucosal congestion, and maternal osmoregulation changes leading to polyuria. The symptom disappears as the uterus straightens up after 12 weeks.
- **Breast Discomfort:** A feeling of fullness and a 'pricking sensation' is evident as early as 6–8 weeks, especially in primigravidae.
- **Fatigue:** A frequent symptom occurring early in pregnancy.

**II. OBJECTIVE SIGNS** **1. Breast Changes (Most valuable in primigravidae):**

- Visible enlargement and vascular engorgement with delicate veins visible under the skin by 6–8 weeks.
- Increased pigmentation of the nipple and primary areola.
- **Montgomery's Tubercles:** Enlarged sebaceous glands become prominent.
- **Colostrum:** Thick yellowish secretion can be expressed from the nipples as early as the 12th week.

**2. Abdominal Signs:**

- The uterus remains a pelvic organ until the 12th week; thus, it may just be felt per abdomen as a suprapubic bulge.

**3. Pelvic Signs:**

- **Vaginal Signs:**
    - **Jacquemier's (or Chadwick's) Sign:** Dusky hue or bluish discoloration of the vestibule and anterior vaginal wall, visible at about 8 weeks due to local vascular congestion.
    - **Osiander's Sign:** Increased pulsation felt through the lateral vaginal fornices at 8 weeks.
    - The vaginal walls become softened, and a copious non-irritating mucoid discharge appears by 6 weeks.
- **Cervical Signs:**
    - **Goodell's Sign:** Softening of the cervix as early as 6 weeks. The pregnant cervix feels soft like the lips of the mouth, whereas the non-pregnant cervix feels firm like the tip of the nose.
    - Speculum examination reveals bluish discoloration of the cervix due to increased vascularity.
- **Uterine Signs:**
    - **Size, Shape, and Consistency:** The uterus enlarges to the size of a hen's egg at 6 weeks, a cricket ball at 8 weeks, and a fetal head by 12 weeks. Its shape changes from pyriform to globular by 12 weeks. The uterus feels soft and elastic.
    - **Piskacek's Sign:** Asymmetrical enlargement of the uterus (one half feels more firm than the other) which may occur due to lateral implantation.
    - **Hegar's Sign:** Elicited between 6 and 10 weeks. Because the upper body is enlarged by the growing fetus and the cervix remains comparatively firm, the empty lower uterine segment feels extremely soft. On bimanual examination, the abdominal and vaginal fingers seem to appose.
    - **Palmer's Sign:** Regular and rhythmic uterine contractions elicited during bimanual examination as early as 4–8 weeks.

**III. INVESTIGATIONS** **1. Immunological Tests for Pregnancy:**

- **Principle:** Detects hCG (specifically the $\beta$ subunit) in maternal serum or urine using specific antibodies.
- **ELISA:** Highly sensitive and specific. It can detect hCG in serum (1–2 mIU/mL) as early as 8–9 days post-ovulation, or about 5 days before the first missed period.
- Urine card tests typically become positive on the first day of the missed period.

**2. Ultrasonography (TVS) [Absolute / Positive Signs]:** Transvaginal ultrasonography (TVS) provides absolute confirmation of pregnancy and is the most accurate method for establishing gestational age in the first trimester.

- **Gestational Sac:** Visible as an intradecidual sac at 4.5–5 weeks.
- **Yolk Sac:** The first embryonic structure seen; appears at 5.5 weeks.
- **Fetal Pole & Cardiac Activity:** An embryo (CRL ~3 mm) with visible cardiac motion appears at 6 weeks.
- **Embryonic Movements:** Can be detected by 7 weeks.
- **Crown-Rump Length (CRL):** Measurement between 7 and 12 weeks is the most accurate parameter for estimating gestational age (variation ± 5 days).
- **Doppler USG:** Can reliably pick up the fetal heart rate by the 10th week.
3. Describe immunological tests for diagnosis of pregnancy
**IMMUNOLOGICAL TESTS FOR DIAGNOSIS OF PREGNANCY**

**Principle** Immunological pregnancy tests depend on the detection of the antigen, Human Chorionic Gonadotropin (hCG), present in the maternal urine or serum using commercially available polyclonal or monoclonal antibodies. hCG is a glycoprotein comprising an $\alpha$ (alpha) subunit and a $\beta$ (beta) subunit. The $\alpha$-subunit is structurally identical to those of Luteinizing Hormone (LH), Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH), and Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone (TSH). However, the $\beta$-subunit is structurally distinct and unique to hCG. Antibodies are developed specifically against this highly specific $\beta$-subunit, which allows for its accurate detection.

**Types of Tests** **1. Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA):**

- It is a highly sensitive and specific test.
- **Sensitivity:** It can detect hCG in serum at levels as low as 1-2 mIU/mL.
- **Timing:** Because of its high sensitivity, it can detect pregnancy as early as 5 days before the first missed period.

**2. Two-Site Sandwich Immunoassay (Membrane ELISA / Card Tests):**

- This is a rapid screening test widely used in clinical practice and as home testing kits.
- **Sensitivity:** 30-50 mIU/mL for urine samples.
- **Time Taken:** It yields results rapidly in 4-5 minutes.
- **Inference:** A positive test is confirmed by the appearance of color bands in both the control window and the test window.
- **Timing:** It generally becomes positive on the first day of the missed period (i.e., the 28th day of a regular cycle).

**Selection of Time and Specimen Collection**

- **Time of Detection:** Using a sensitive test, hCG can be detected in maternal serum or urine by 8-9 days after ovulation, which corresponds to 3-4 days before the expected date of menses. The test is considered unreliable after 12 weeks of pregnancy.
- **Collection of Urine:** The patient is advised to collect the first voided urine in the morning in a clean container. The container should not be washed with soap, as it may interfere with the test.

**Accuracy and Limitations**

- The diagnostic accuracy of these tests is very high, ranging from 98.6% to 99%.
- In a nonpregnant state, hCG levels are normally below 1 mIU/mL.
- **Limitations:** The accuracy of the test can be adversely affected by the presence of: (i) Hemoglobin, (ii) Albumin, (iii) LH, and (iv) Immunological diseases.

**False-Positive Results** False-positive hCG test results are rare but can occur under specific clinical conditions. A false-positive result may be caused by:

- Administration of exogenous hCG injections.
- Delayed clearance of hCG from the body due to renal failure.
- Presence of physiological pituitary hCG.
- Neoplasms producing hCG, such as tumors of the ovary, lung, bladder, or gastrointestinal tract.
- The presence of heterophile antibodies in the maternal serum.

**Other Clinical Applications (Uses) of Pregnancy Tests** Apart from the diagnosis of a normal intrauterine pregnancy, immunological estimation of hCG is employed in several other obstetric conditions:

- **Diagnosis of Ectopic Pregnancy:** Helps in confirming pregnancy and correlating with ultrasound findings.
- **In Vitro Fertilization (IVF):** Used to monitor pregnancy following IVF and embryo transfer.
- **Gestational Trophoblastic Diseases:** Essential for diagnosing and sequentially following up cases of hydatidiform mole and choriocarcinoma, where hCG levels are characteristically elevated.


3. Jacquemier’s sign in pregnancy.
**JACQUEMIER'S SIGN (CHADWICK'S SIGN)**

**Definition** Jacquemier's sign is defined as the dusky hue or bluish discoloration of the vestibule and the anterior vaginal wall.

**Synonyms** It is also commonly referred to as Chadwick's sign. It was first described by Jacquemier in 1836 and later by Chadwick in 1887.

**Time of Appearance** The sign becomes clinically visible at about the 8th week of pregnancy. It is one of the classical objective signs elicited during the first trimester (6–12 weeks) of pregnancy.

**Pathophysiology (Mechanism of Causation)** The characteristic color change is entirely due to the physiological adaptations of the genital tract during early pregnancy:

- During pregnancy, the vaginal walls become markedly hypertrophied, edematous, and highly vascular.
- There is a profound increase in the blood supply to the venous plexus surrounding the vaginal walls.
- This intense local vascular congestion and pooling of blood impart the typical bluish coloration to the vaginal mucosa.

**Clinical Significance in Obstetrics**

- **Diagnostic Value:** It is classified under the _probable signs_ of pregnancy.
- **Method of Elicitation:** It is observed during a routine internal (speculum/vaginal) examination of the patient.
- **Diagnostic Category:** Along with Osiander's sign (increased pulsation in lateral fornices) and Goodell's sign (softening of the cervix), Jacquemier's sign forms a critical triad of objective vaginal signs used for the clinical diagnosis of first-trimester pregnancy.

4. Goodell’s sign and Osiander’s sign

**GOODELL'S SIGN**

- **Definition:** It is an objective cervical sign of early pregnancy characterized by the marked softening of the cervix.
- **Time of Onset:** It becomes clinically evident as early as the 6th week of pregnancy. This softening occurs a little earlier in multiparous women compared to primigravidae.
- **Progression:** The softening characteristically begins at the margin of the external os and then progressively spreads upward.
- **Clinical Palpation:** On internal examination, the pregnant cervix feels soft, resembling the feel of the "lips of the mouth". This is in stark contrast to the nonpregnant state, where the cervix feels firm, resembling the "tip of the nose".
- **Clinical Significance:** It serves as a major diagnostic aid during the first trimester (first 12 weeks) to clinically confirm early pregnancy.

**OSIANDER'S SIGN**

- **Definition:** It is an objective vaginal sign of pregnancy characterized by increased pulsations felt through the lateral vaginal fornices.
- **Time of Onset:** This sign is typically appreciated at the 8th week of pregnancy.
- **Clinical Significance:** It is elicited during an internal pelvic examination in the first trimester (between 6-12 weeks). Along with other pelvic changes, it is classified as one of the key "probable signs" of pregnancy.

5. Hegar’s sign
**HEGAR'S SIGN**

**Definition and Significance** Hegar's sign is an important presumptive clinical sign of early pregnancy characterized by the marked softening of the lower segment of the uterus.

**Incidence and Timing**

- **Incidence:** It is present in approximately two-thirds of pregnancy cases.
- **Timing of Appearance:** It can be clinically demonstrated between 6 and 10 weeks of gestation. It typically appears a little earlier in multiparous women compared to primigravidae.

**Anatomical and Physiological Basis** This sign is based on the fact that during early pregnancy, there is a variation in the consistency of different parts of the uterus. It depends on three distinct features:

1. The upper part of the body of the uterus is enlarged by the growing fetus.
2. The lower part of the body of the uterus is empty and becomes extremely soft.
3. The cervix remains comparatively firm.

**Method of Elicitation**

- It is elicited through a bimanual pelvic examination.
- Two fingers of one hand are placed internally in the anterior vaginal fornix.
- The fingers of the other hand are placed abdominally behind the uterus.
- **Findings:** Because of the extreme softness and compressibility of the empty lower uterine segment, the abdominal and vaginal fingers seem to appose (meet each other) below the body of the uterus.

**Clinical Precaution** The bimanual examination to elicit Hegar's sign must be performed with utmost gentleness to avoid the risk of provoking an abortion.


6. Quickening.
**QUICKENING**

**Definition** Quickening, often described as the "feeling of life," denotes the first perception of active fetal movements by the pregnant woman.

**Time of Appearance**

- It is a subjective symptom that characteristically appears during the second trimester (13–28 weeks).
- In primigravidae, it is usually felt at about the 18th week of gestation.
- In multiparae, it is typically experienced about 2 weeks earlier (around the 16th week) because the woman is already familiar with the sensation from previous pregnancies.

**Clinical Significance in Obstetrics**

- **Diagnosis of Pregnancy:** It is classified as a presumptive symptom of pregnancy.
- **Estimation of Expected Date of Delivery (EDD):** The appearance of quickening serves as a useful and reasonably accurate clinical guide to calculate the EDD, especially in women who are unsure of their last menstrual period (LMP). A rough idea of the probable date of delivery is deduced by:
    - Adding **22 weeks** to the date of quickening in primigravidae.
    - Adding **24 weeks** to the date of quickening in multiparae.
- **Assessment of Fetal Maturity:** The date of quickening is utilized as one of the important clinical parameters (alongside LMP, fundal height, and fetal heart sounds) in determining fetal maturity.
