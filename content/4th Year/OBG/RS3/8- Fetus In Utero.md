1. Define—lie and attitude
**LIE**

**Definition** The lie refers to the relationship of the long axis of the fetus to the long axis of the centralized uterus or maternal spine.

**Types of Lie** There are primarily three types of lie, with an additional "unstable" category:

- **Longitudinal Lie (99.5%):** This is the most common lie. The fetal ovoid's poles (cephalic and podalic) are placed at the upper and lower parts of the uterine cavity. The preponderance of this lie is due to the fetus accommodating comfortably along the long axis of the ovoid shape of the uterine cavity at term.
- **Transverse Lie:** The long axis of the fetus lies perpendicularly to the maternal spine or centralized uterine axis.
- **Oblique Lie:** The fetal axis is placed at an acute angle or obliquely to the maternal spine. Both transverse and oblique lies are collectively referred to as shoulder presentations during labor.
- **Unstable Lie:** A condition where the presentation of the fetus constantly changes even beyond the 36th week of pregnancy, a time when it normally should have stabilized.

---

**ATTITUDE**

**Definition** The relation of the different parts of the fetus to one another is called the attitude of the fetus.

**Universal Attitude (Normal)** The universal and normal attitude of the fetus is that of **flexion**.

- During the later months of pregnancy, the head, trunk, and limbs of the fetus maintain an attitude of flexion on all joints.
- This folded posture forms an ovoid mass that corresponds approximately to the shape of the uterine ovoid.
- The characteristic flexed attitude may be modified by the amount of liquor amnii present.

**Abnormal Attitudes (Exceptions to Flexion)** Deviations from the universal attitude of flexion can modify the course of labor. These include:

- **Extension of the head:** Depending on the degree of extension, this leads to a deflexed vertex, brow, or face presentation.
- **Extension of the legs:** This is characteristically seen in specific types of breech presentations, such as a frank breech where the legs are extended at the knee joints.

**Clinical Diagnosis** The attitude of the fetal head can be inferred during abdominal examination via the first pelvic grip (fourth Leopold maneuver) by noting the relative positions of the sincipital and occipital poles. In a well-flexed head, the sincipital pole is placed at a higher level, whereas in a deflexed state, both poles remain at the same level.

2. Second pelvic grip

**PELVIC GRIPS (OBSTETRIC GRIPS)**

In obstetrical abdominal examination, there are two grips used to evaluate the lower pelvic pole of the uterus: **Pawlik’s grip** (Third Leopold maneuver) and the **Bilateral Pelvic grip** (Fourth Leopold maneuver). In standard obstetric terminology, the bilateral pelvic grip is often referred to as the "First pelvic grip", making Pawlik's grip the alternate or "Second" pelvic grip. For a comprehensive exam answer, both grips evaluating the pelvic region are detailed below.

**GENERAL PRECAUTIONS FOR PALPATION**

- Palpation must be conducted with the utmost gentleness, as clumsy or purposeless palpation is uninformative and can cause undue uterine irritability.
- Palpation must be temporarily suspended during a Braxton-Hicks contraction or a uterine contraction in active labor.

---

**1. PAWLIK'S GRIP (THIRD LEOPOLD MANEUVER)** This is a one-handed grip used to assess the presenting part at the lower pole of the uterus.

- **Position of the Examiner:** The examination is performed while facing toward the patient's face.
- **Technique:** The overstretched thumb and four fingers of the right hand are placed over the lower pole of the uterus. The ulnar border of the palm is kept resting on the upper border of the symphysis pubis.
- **Action:** The fingers and the thumb are approximated to grasp the presenting part distinctly.
- **Clinical Findings:**
    - **Mobility:** It tests the mobility of the presenting part from side to side, provided the head is not engaged.
    - **Engagement:** If the presenting part is not engaged, it can be distinctly grasped and is mobile. However, to properly diagnose engagement, it is better to palpate gently using the two-handed pelvic grip (Fourth Leopold) rather than prodding with Pawlik's grip, which can be painful for the patient in non-experienced hands.
    - **Transverse Lie:** In the case of a transverse lie, Pawlik's grip will be found empty.

---

**2. BILATERAL PELVIC GRIP (FOURTH LEOPOLD / FIRST PELVIC GRIP)** This is a two-handed deep pelvic grip and is considered the most informative and comfortable pelvic grip for the woman.

- **Position of the Examiner:** The examination is performed while facing toward the patient's feet.
- **Technique:** Four fingers of both hands are placed on either side of the midline in the lower pole of the uterus, parallel to the inguinal ligaments. The fingers are then pressed downward and backward in a manner of approximating the fingertips to palpate the fetal part.
- **Clinical Findings:**
    - **Presentation:** A cephalic presentation is evident if a smooth, hard, and globular mass is felt in the lower pole.
    - **Attitude of the Head:** The relative positions of the sincipital and occipital poles are determined. In a well-flexed head, the sincipital pole is placed at a higher level than the occiput. In a deflexed state, both the sincipital and occipital poles remain at the same horizontal level.
    - **Presenting Part:** A vertex presentation is diagnosed when the cephalic prominence (the sinciput) is palpated on the same side toward which the fetal limbs lie.
    - **Engagement:** Engagement occurs when the greatest horizontal plane (the biparietal diameter) has passed the plane of the pelvic brim.
        - _If Engaged:_ Both the sinciput and occiput are not easily felt per abdomen (though the sincipital pole may be felt with difficulty). When trying to push downward on the lower abdomen, there is a **divergence** of the examining fingers of both hands.
        - _If Not Engaged:_ There is an absence of the sincipital and occipital poles entering the true pelvis, and there is a **convergence** of the fingertips during downward palpation.
