1. MI 
	**Myocardial Infarction (MI)**
	
	- **Context:** Most important & feared consequence of Coronary Artery Disease (CAD). It's a manifestation of **Ischaemic Heart Disease (IHD)**.
	- **Definition (Implicit):** Acute or chronic cardiac disability from imbalance between myocardial O2 supply and demand. Involves myocardial necrosis due to ischaemia.
	
	**Clinical Features**
	
	- Typically **sudden onset**.
	- **Pain:**
	    - Usually **sudden, severe, crushing, prolonged**.
	    - Location: Substernal or precordial.
	    - Unrelieved by rest or nitroglycerin.
	    - Often radiating to one or both arms, neck, and back.
	- **Indigestion:** Epigastric or substernal discomfort (interpreted as 'heartburn') with nausea and vomiting.
	- Severe chest pain, dyspnoea and shock (if death is somewhat delayed).
	
	**Investigations** Diagnosis is made based on:
	
	1. **Clinical features**.
	2. **ECG changes:** One of the most important parameters.
	    - Most characteristic: **ST segment elevation (STEMI)** in acute MI.
	    - Other changes: T wave inversion, appearance of wide deep Q waves.
	3. **Serum Cardiac Markers:** Proteins/enzymes released from necrotic heart muscle.
	    - Measurement is helpful for diagnosis and management.
	    - Important markers nowadays (Time course shown in Fig. 14.21):
	        - **Troponins (I and T):** Highly specific and sensitive, remain elevated for days [Source likely implies this, Fig 14.21 shows this].
	        - Creatine Kinase-MB (CK-MB): Elevated, less specific than troponins, levels return to normal faster [Source likely implies this, Fig 14.21 shows this].
	        - Myoglobin: First marker to elevate, lacks cardiac specificity, rapidly excreted (normal within 24 hrs).
	
	**Morphologic Features (Gross & Microscopic)**
	
	- Vary according to the **age of the infarct**.
	- Most often found in the **left ventricle**. Can be multifocal.
	- Size: Vary from 4 to 10 cm.
	
	**Sequential Changes (Gross)**
	
	- **0-6 hours:** No change or pale appearance. TTC/NBT test negative. Early infarcts (3-6 hrs) detectable by histochemical stain for dehydrogenases (TTC/NBT stains normal red/blue, infarct area fails to stain).
	- **6-12 hours:** Affected myocardium slightly paler and drier.
	- **~24 hours:** Develops cyanotic, red-purple, blotchy areas of haemorrhage (due to blood stagnation).
	- **48-72 hours:** Develops a yellow border (neutrophilic infiltration).
	- **3-7 days:** Hyperaemic border, yellow and soft centre.
	- **10 days:** Periphery reddish-purple (granulation tissue growth). Necrotic muscle resorbed, infarct shrinks, becomes pale grey.
	- **End of 6 weeks:** Replaced by thin, grey-white, hard, shrunken fibrous scar.
	- **2-3 months:** Scar is well developed.
	- **Reperfusion Injury:** Infarct appears **haemorrhagic** rather than pale.
	
	**Sequential Changes (Microscopic)**
	
	- **Hallmark:** Ischaemic **coagulative necrosis** of affected myocardium. Cells/structures outlines preserved, but details lost (no intact nuclei/cytoplasm).
	- **Peripheral Inflammation:** At the periphery, inflammatory reaction noted.
	    - Initially: Neutrophils predominate.
	    - Later: Macrophages and fibroblasts appear.
	- **Early Changes (Special Techniques - EM, Chemical, Histochemical):** Seen before light microscopic changes.
	    - EM (<30 mins): Disappearance of perinuclear glycogen (5 mins), mitochondrial swelling (20-30 mins), sarcolemma disruption, nuclear chromatin clumping.
	    - Chemical/Histochemical (30-60 mins): Glycogen depletion, increased lactic acid, loss of K+, increase of Na+, influx of Ca++ (causing irreversible injury). Leakage of enzymes into blood.
	- **First few hours:** Wavy fibres [Source Table 14.4 suggests this is first week, but typically earlier].
	- **First week:** Neutrophilic infiltrate, myocyte necrosis, macrophage infiltration [Source Table 14.4, Fig 14.18].
	- **Third week:** Necrosed muscle removed, replaced by collagen fibres. Pigmented macrophages, lymphocytes, plasma cells prominent.
	- **Fourth to sixth week:** Increased collagenous tissue, decreased vascularity, fewer inflammatory cells. Contracted fibrocollagenic scar formed. Pigmented macrophages may persist.
	- **Healed Infarct:** Replaced by **fibrous scar tissue**. Scar is thin, grey-white, firm/hard. May show dystrophic calcification.
	- **Reperfusion Injury (Microscopic):** Myofibres show **contraction band necrosis** (transverse, thick eosinophilic bands).
	
	**Types of MI (based on depth)**
	
	- **Transmural (Full thickness):**
	    - Involves the entire ventricular wall.
	    - Most common (95% cases).
	    - Definition: Full-thickness, solid.
	    - Distribution: Specific area of coronary supply.
	    - Pathogenesis: >75% coronary stenosis. Atherosclerotic plaque with superimposed thrombosis and intramural haemorrhage significant in ~90% cases.
	    - Coronary thrombosis: Common.
	    - Epicarditis: Common.
	    - Usually has a thin rim of preserved subendocardial myocardium (perfused by ventricular blood).
	- **Subendocardial (Laminar):**
	    - Occupy the inner subendocardial half (or inner third to half) of the myocardium.
	    - Less frequent.
	    - Definition: Inner third to half, patchy.
	    - Distribution: Circumferential.
	    - Pathogenesis: Reduced coronary perfusion due to atherosclerosis (not necessarily >75% stenosis), aortic stenosis, or haemorrhagic shock. Subendocardium is normally least perfused and more vulnerable.
	    - Coronary thrombosis: Rare. However, superimposed thrombosis is frequently encountered, supporting fibrinolytic therapy.
	    - Epicarditis: None.
	    - Produce less well-defined gross changes than transmural.
	
	**Common Locations**
	
	- Most often in the **left ventricle**.
	- Locations affected by stenosis of major coronary arteries:
	    - Left anterior descending coronary artery (40-50%): Anterior part of LV, anterior 2/3 of interventricular septum, apex.
	    - Right coronary artery (30-40%): Posterior part of LV, posterior 1/3 of interventricular septum.
	    - Left circumflex coronary artery (15-20%): Lateral wall of LV.
	
	**Complications**
	
	- Only 10-20% cases recover without major complications. 80-90% develop one or more.
	- Immediate mortality (sudden cardiac death) is about 25%.
	- Important complications:
	    - **Arrhythmias:** Most common (80-90% cases). Due to ischaemic injury/irritation to conduction system, electrolyte imbalance (K+ loss), increased lactate/fatty acids. Can range from sinus changes to ventricular fibrillation (responsible for sudden death).
	    - **Cardiac Failure:** Result of impaired cardiac function.
	    - **Cardiogenic Shock:** Results from low cardiac output due to massive MI. Pathogenesis presented in Flowchart 5.9.
	    - **Rupture:** Occurs in up to 5% cases, often fatal. Most often ventricular wall rupture into pericardial cavity (haemopericardium, tamponade). Can also rupture through interventricular septum or papillary muscle. Usually occurs in the first week.
	    - **Cardiac Aneurysm:** Develops in ~5% of patients. Occurs in healed infarcts (thin, fibrous scar tissue). Impairs function, common site for mural thrombi.
	    - Mural Thrombosis/Systemic Embolism [Source suggests this is a complication of aneurysm, but also occurs directly over infarct].
	    - Pericarditis (Dressler's syndrome) [Source Table 14.3 suggests epicarditis is common in transmural infarcts, complications section doesn't explicitly list Dressler's, but this is a common post-MI issue outside the provided text].
	
	**Salvage & Reperfusion Injury**
	
	- Ischaemic injury reversible if perfusion restored within **first 30 minutes**.
	- Interventions for salvage: Thrombolytic therapy (<30 mins door-to-needle), PTCA, stenting, CABG.
	- Late reperfusion risks **ischaemic reperfusion injury**.
	- Mechanism of reperfusion injury: Rapid influx of Ca++, generation of toxic oxygen free radicals.
	- Gross appearance after reperfusion: **Haemorrhagic**.
	- Microscopic appearance after reperfusion: **Contraction band necrosis**.
