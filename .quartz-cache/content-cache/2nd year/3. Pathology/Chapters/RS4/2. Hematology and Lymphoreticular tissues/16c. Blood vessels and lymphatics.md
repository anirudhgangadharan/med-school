	**ATHEROSCLEROSIS**
	
	- **Definition:**
	    
	    - Atherosclerosis is a **thickening and hardening of large and medium-sized muscular arteries**.
	    - It primarily involves the **tunica intima** and is characterised by **fibrofatty plaques or atheromas**.
	    - The term derives from 'athero' (porridge/soft lipid core) and 'sclerosis' (scarring/connective tissue).
	    - It is the **commonest and most important of the arterial diseases**, affecting the aorta and large arteries.
	- **Aetiopathogenesis (Risk Factors & Mechanism):**
	    
	    - **Risk Factors:**
	        - **Major Risk Factors (Modifiable by Life Style and/or Therapy)**:
	            - **Dyslipidaemias:** Considered the **most important major risk factor**. Hypercholesterolaemia has a directly proportionate relationship with atherosclerosis. High **LDL cholesterol** (< 100 mg/dl desirable) carries the **maximum risk**; lowering LDL is a management goal. Low **HDL cholesterol** (> 50 mg/dl desirable) is harmful; raising HDL is a management goal. Diet high in saturated/trans fats raises cholesterol; low saturated/high poly-unsaturated/omega-3 fats lower it.
	            - **Hypertension**: A risk factor for all clinical manifestations.
	            - **Diabetes Mellitus**.
	            - **Smoking**.
	        - **Constitutional Risk Factors (Non-Modifiable)**:
	            - Increasing **age**.
	            - **Male sex**.
	            - **Genetic abnormalities/influences** and familial predisposition (e.g., Familial hypercholesterolaemia due to LDL receptor gene mutations).
	        - A combination of factors has an **additive effect**.
	    - **Pathogenesis (Reaction-to-Injury Hypothesis):** Commonly explains atherogenesis. Involves:
	        - **Endothelial injury**.
	        - Adhesion and migration of **circulating monocytes**.
	        - Intimal **smooth muscle cell proliferation** (facilitated by biomolecules like nitric oxide, endothelin; synthesis of matrix proteins - collagen, elastic fibres, proteoglycans).
	        - The role of **dyslipidaemia** (hypercholesterolaemia): phagocytic cells (macrophages, smooth muscle cells) accumulate lipids (triglycerides, cholesterol, cholesterol esters) intracellularly, forming **foam cells**. Atherosclerotic plaques contain cholesterol and cholesterol esters derived from lipoproteins.
	        - **Thrombosis** forming on the plaque surface.
	- **Morphology:**
	    
	    - **Gross Appearance:**
	        - Thickening and hardening of the arterial wall.
	        - Presence of **fibrofatty plaques**.
	        - Progression seen from fatty streaks/gelatinous lesions to full blown atheromatous lesions.
	        - **Complicated atheromas** may show:
	            - **Dystrophic calcification** (in the core, differs from Mönckeberg’s medial calcific sclerosis).
	            - **Ulceration** (discharge of lipid/debris emboli).
	            - **Thrombosis** (superimposed).
	            - **Haemorrhage** (into the plaque).
	            - **Aneurysm formation** and eventual rupture (due to secondary changes in media/adventitia causing weakening).
	        - Significant stenotic lesions cause **luminal narrowing**, e.g., >75% reduction in coronary arteries. Coronary plaques are often eccentrically located.
	    - **Microscopic Appearance:**
	        - Early lesions: Fatty streaks.
	        - Fully developed plaque (Fibrofatty plaque): Has a **superficial fibrous cap** and a **cellular or soft centre**.
	        - The soft centre contains lipid-rich material (cholesterol/cholesterol esters), often with cholesterol crystals. Smooth muscle cells and macrophages (**foam cells**) are found within the intima.
	        - Microscopy varies with age but features are invariably present.
	        - **Complicated plaques** show corresponding microscopic features like **dystrophic calcification** in the core and signs of ulceration, thrombosis, or haemorrhage.
	- **Pathophysiology & Clinical Features:**
	    
	    - Clinical effects depend on affected arteries.
	    - They result from:
	        - **Slow luminal narrowing:** Causes ischaemia and atrophy. Significant stenosis (e.g., >75% in coronaries) can lead to chronic ischaemic myocardial damage.
	        - **Sudden luminal occlusion:** Causes infarction necrosis. For example, acute myocardial infarction is often precipitated by coronary thrombosis on an ulcerated chronic atheromatous plaque.
	        - **Plaque propagation:** Via formation of thrombi and emboli.
	        - **Aneurysm formation and eventual rupture**.
	    - Major effects occur on the **heart** (Coronary Artery Disease/IHD, sudden cardiac death related to critical narrowing), **brain** (stroke), **aorta** (aneurysmal dilatation), **intestine** (ischaemia), and **lower extremities** (gangrene).

	2. **Glomus Tumour (Glomangioma)**

	**I. General Features**:
	
	- **Nature**: A rare, **true benign tumour**.
	- **Origin**: Arises from **contractile glomus cells**. These cells are typically found in **arteriovenous shunts (Sucquet-Hoyer anastomosis)**.
	
	**II. Common Locations**:
	
	- Most often found in the **dermis of the fingers or toes**, particularly **under a nail**.
	- Less commonly, it can occur in the mucosa of the stomach and nasal cavity.
	
	**III. Clinical Features**:
	
	- **Size**: Usually small, often **less than 1 cm in diameter**.
	- **Appearance**: May be single or multiple, flat or slightly elevated.
	- **Colour**: Typically **red-blue nodules**.
	- **Key Symptom**: Characterised by **extreme pain**.
	
	**IV. Morphological Features (Microscopy)**:
	
	- Histologically, the tumour consists of **blood-filled vascular channels**.
	- These channels are **lined by endothelial cells**.
	- The vascular channels are characteristically **surrounded by nests and masses of glomus cells**.
