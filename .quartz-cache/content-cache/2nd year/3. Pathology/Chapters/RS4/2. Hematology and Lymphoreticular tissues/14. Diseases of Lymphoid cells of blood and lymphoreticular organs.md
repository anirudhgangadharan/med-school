1. Hodgkin's Lymphoma=classification(microscopic variants), gross and microscopy, 
	**Hodgkin's Lymphoma (HD)**
	
	- **Definition:** HD is a malignant lymphoid neoplasm primarily arising within lymph nodes.
	- **Key Feature:** Central to the diagnosis is the essential identification of the **Reed-Sternberg (RS) cell**. These are the minority neoplastic cells (1-5%) amidst a majority background of reactive non-neoplastic cells.
	
	**Classification (Microscopic Variants)**
	
	- While the older Rye classification divided HD into 4 subtypes, the **WHO classification** divides HD into **2 main groups**:
	    1. **Nodular Lymphocyte-Predominant HD (NLPHL)**
	    2. **Classic HD (CHL)** - This group includes the 4 subtypes of the older Rye classification:
	        - Lymphocyte-predominance type (now mostly covered by NLPHL, but also includes a diffuse form)
	        - Nodular sclerosis type (most common, ~70%)
	        - Mixed cellularity type (~22%)
	        - Lymphocyte-depletion type (least common, ~1%)
	
	**Gross Morphology**
	
	- Affected lymph nodes are typically **enlarged**.
	- May initially be discrete but can form a **matted mass** due to infiltration into surrounding connective tissue.
	- Sectioned surface appears **grey-white and fleshy**.
	- Nodular sclerosis type may show **nodule formation** due to scarring/collagen bands. Mixed cellularity and lymphocyte depletion types may show **necrosis**.
	
	**Microscopic Features**
	
	- **General Hallmark:** Presence of **Reed-Sternberg (RS) cells** and their variants, interspersed within a prominent **reactive mixed inflammatory infiltrate**. The background includes varying numbers of lymphocytes, histiocytes, eosinophils, neutrophils, and plasma cells.
	    
	- **Reed-Sternberg (RS) Cell:**
	    
	    - Large cell (~15-45 µm) with abundant amphophilic cytoplasm [Fig 12.30, Source 129].
	    - Characteristic **bilobed or polylobulated nucleus** resulting in **mirror-image nuclei** [Fig 12.30, Source 129].
	    - Each nuclear lobe contains a large, **prominent, eosinophilic nucleolus** with a clear halo, giving an "**owl's eye**" appearance [Fig 12.30, Source 129].
	- **RS Cell Variants & Associated Subtypes:**
	    
	    - **Lacunar cells:** Characteristic of **Nodular Sclerosis** type [Fig 12.16, Fig 12.18, Fig 33.3, Source 37, 41, 45, 130, 143]. Large cells within clear spaces (lacunae) in formalin-fixed tissue. Have multi-lobulated nuclei and small nucleoli [Fig 12.30, Source 129].
	    - **Polyploid type (or popcorn/L&H cells):** Characteristic of **Nodular Lymphocyte-Predominant** type [Fig 12.16, Source 37, 41, 44, 134, 135]. Larger cells with multilobulated nuclei resembling popcorn kernels; nucleoli are inconspicuous [Fig 12.30, Source 129, 135].
	    - **Pleomorphic RS cells:** Feature of **Lymphocyte Depletion** type [Fig 12.16, Source 37, 41, 43, 133, 134]. Have pleomorphic and atypical nuclei.
	    - **Classic/Typical RS cells:** Frequent in **Mixed Cellularity** [Fig 12.18, Fig 33.4, Source 42, 131, 144] and **Lymphocyte Depletion** types.
	- **Microscopic Features by Subtype (WHO/Rye):**
	    
	    - **Nodular Sclerosis:** Presence of **broad bands of collagen** dividing the lymphoid tissue into nodules [Fig 12.18, Fig 33.3, Source 38, 41, 130, 143]. Characterised by **lacunar cells**. Background infiltrate is present.
	    - **Mixed Cellularity:** **Heterogeneous cellular infiltrate** replacing the lymph node, including lymphocytes, histiocytes, eosinophils, neutrophils, and plasma cells [Fig 12.18, Fig 33.4, Source 42, 131, 144]. **Typical RS cells are frequent**. Fibrosis and focal necrosis may be present.
	    - **Lymphocyte Depletion:** Lymph node is **depleted of lymphocytes**. **Numerous Hodgkin cells and RS cells** (including pleomorphic variants) are seen. Two variants: Diffuse fibrotic (hypocellular with diffuse fibrosis) and Reticular (more cellular with atypical histiocytes).
	    - **Nodular Lymphocyte-Predominant:** Characterised by a **nodular growth pattern**. Composed predominantly of **small lymphocytes** with a **sparse number of neoplastic cells** (L&H cells or popcorn cells). Hodgkin and RS cells are generally not found.
	
	**Immunophenotype (Relevant for Classification)**
	
	- **Classic HD RS cells (except NLPHL):** Express **CD15 and CD30**. Usually CD45 negative.
	- **Nodular Lymphocyte-Predominant HD (NLPHL) LP cells:** **CD45 positive**, **CD20 positive**, but **negative for CD15 and CD30**. Express BCL6.
	
	**Clinical Relevance**
	
	- Prognosis varies significantly by subtype and stage.
	- Lymphocyte-predominance (NLPHL) has an excellent prognosis.
	- Nodular Sclerosis has a very good prognosis.
	- Mixed Cellularity has a good/intermediate prognosis.
	- Lymphocyte Depletion has the worst outcome and is usually disseminated at diagnosis.
2. Multiple myeloma , laboratory findings
	**Multiple Myeloma (Plasma Cell Myeloma)**
	
	- **Definition:** A **malignant, multifocal proliferation of plasma cells** derived from a single clone (monoclonal). It is the most important and common syndrome in the group of plasma cell disorders.
	- **Key Features (Classic Triad for Diagnosis):**
	    1. **Marrow plasmacytosis** of more than **10%**.
	    2. **Radiologic evidence of lytic bony lesions** (punched-out defects).
	    3. Demonstration of **serum and/or urine M component** (monoclonal paraprotein).
	
	**Aetiology/Risk Factors**
	
	- Primarily affects the **elderly** (peak incidence 5th-6th decades), increasing with age. Rare under 40.
	- More common in **males** than females.
	- Factors implicated:
	    - Prolonged **radiation exposure**.
	    - Genetic predisposition.
	    - Chronic antigenic stimulation associated with chronic infections (HIV, chronic osteomyelitis) and chronic inflammatory disorders (rheumatoid arthritis).
	    - Exposure to chemicals like benzene, herbicides, and insecticides.
	
	**Laboratory Findings**
	
	- **Peripheral Blood:**
	    - **Anaemia:** Almost always present, typically mild to severe, progressive, and normocytic normochromic. Ranges from 6-10 g/dL.
	    - **Thrombocytopenia:** Usually moderately to severely reduced (<50,000/µl). Platelets usually normal. Bleeding tendencies correlate with thrombocytopenia.
	    - **Leucocytes:** WBC count usually normal, but there may be neutropenia in advanced stages due to marrow infiltration. Smear shows **rouleaux formation** of red blood cells due to increased immunoglobulins. Rarely, circulating myeloma cells may be seen.
	    - **ESR:** **High** due to high gamma globulin (immunoglobulin) and rouleaux formation.
	    - Bleeding time: Increased.
	- **Bone Marrow Examination:**
	    - **Cellularity:** Usually **hypercellular**.
	    - **Myeloma Cells:** Constitute **>10% of marrow cellularity**. Often form clumps or sheets.
	        - Myeloma cells are neoplastic plasma cells.
	        - May vary in size (small differentiated to large immature).
	        - Often large, oval cells with abundant pale blue cytoplasm.
	        - Nucleus: Round to oval, **eccentric**, often lacks classical cart-wheel chromatin but nucleoli frequently present. Perinuclear clearing/hof is typical.
	        - May be binucleate or multinucleate.
	        - Cytoplasm may contain Russell bodies (hyaline globules of immunoglobulin).
	    - **Erythropoiesis:** Diminished/suppressed but normoblastic.
	    - **Myelopoiesis:** Normal. Myeloid cells are adequate in number.
	    - **Megakaryopoiesis:** Usually reduced or absent, or gradually decreased.
	    - **Differentiation from reactive plasmacytosis:** Reactive plasma cells are mature and do not exceed 10% of total marrow cells.
	- **Serum Findings:**
	    - **Total Serum Protein:** Rise in total serum protein concentration due to **paraproteinaemia** (M component).
	    - **M Component:** Abnormal immunoglobulins or parts (paraproteins). Detected as a single narrow homogeneous **M-band** on electrophoresis, most commonly in the gamma region.
	        - Most frequent: IgG (~50%), IgA (25%), IgD (1%).
	        - ~20% have only light chains in serum/urine (light chain myeloma).
	    - **Normal Immunoglobulins (IgG, IgA, IgM) and Albumin:** Depressed.
	    - **Hypercalcaemia:** Present due to destruction of bone.
	    - **Hyperuricaemia:** From tumour mass necrosis and uraemia.
	    - **Serum β2 Microglobulin:** Increased. Higher levels signify **poor prognosis**.
	    - Renal function tests (urea, creatinine, BUN): Raised with renal involvement.
	- **Urine Findings:**
	    - **Bence-Jones (BJ) Proteins:** Excreted in urine in about two-thirds of cases. Consist of kappa (k) or lambda (λ) light chains. Detection is part of diagnostic criteria. BJ proteinuria can lead to **myeloma kidney** (renal failure) [671 (implied from index), 124].
	    - Increased β-2 microglobulins and other globulins.
	- **Cytogenetics:** Several chromosomal alterations observed, including translocations t(11;14), t(4;14), and deletion of 13q. Larger number of cytogenetic abnormalities associated with poorer prognosis.
	- **Immunophenotyping:** Myeloma cells express CD138 [Immunohistochemistry section in general pathology, but specific source linking CD138 to myeloma in the provided text is not present. _This information is outside the provided sources_]. Plasma cell neoplasms express CD20, CD22, CD11c, and CD25 (IL-2 receptor α-chain). Annexin A1 is the most specific marker for hairy cell leukaemia, not multiple myeloma.
	
	**Gross Morphology (Osseous Lesions)**
	
	- Primarily affects **bone marrow**, but other organs involved later.
	- Usually involves **multiple bones**, especially those with red marrow (skull, spine, ribs, pelvis, later long bones).
	- Lesions begin in the medullary cavity, erode cancellous bone, and destroy the cortex.
	- Radiographically: Appear as **"punched out," rounded, 1-2 cm sized defects**.
	- Grossly: Normal bone marrow is replaced by **soft, gelatinous, reddish-grey tumours**.
	- Affected bone may show focal or diffuse **osteoporosis**.
	
	**Microscopy (Bone Marrow)**
	
	- See Bone Marrow Examination findings above.
	- Key microscopic feature is the presence of **myeloma cells (>10%)**, often in clumps or sheets.
	- Morphology of myeloma cells varies, but eccentric nucleus, perinuclear halo, and sometimes Russell bodies are characteristic.
	- Reactive plasmacytosis (<10% mature plasma cells) must be distinguished.
	
	**Clinical Relevance**
	
	- Diagnosis relies on the **classic triad** of plasmacytosis, lytic lesions, and M component.
	- Associated with complications like hypercalcaemia, renal failure (myeloma kidney), increased susceptibility to infections, and bone lesions leading to pain and fractures. POEMS syndrome is a rare association.
	- Prognosis is generally poor, with a median survival of about 2 years after diagnosis in older studies. Factors like λ-light chain secretion, higher β2 microglobulin, and cytogenetic abnormalities indicate poor prognosis. Treatment includes chemotherapy and potentially stem cell transplant.
3. Bence Jones proteins and its demonstration /importance
	**Bence-Jones (BJ) Proteins**
	
	- **What are they?**
	    
	    - **Light chains of γ-globulin**.
	    - Consist of either **kappa (k) or lambda (l) light chains**.
	    - Abnormal immunoglobulins or their parts.
	- **Where are they found?**
	    
	    - Excreted in **urine** in about two-third cases of multiple myeloma.
	    - Can also be present in the **serum**.
	- **Clinical Association:**
	    
	    - Primarily associated with **Multiple Myeloma**.
	    - Can also be found in other **paraproteinaemias**.
	- **Demonstration/Detection:**
	    
	    - **Part of the diagnostic criteria** for Multiple Myeloma is the demonstration of serum and/or urine M component, which includes BJ proteins.
	    - Detected as a single narrow homogeneous **M-band component** on serum electrophoresis, most commonly in the region of γ-globulin.
	    - Specific test in urine: **Heat and acetic acid test**.
	        - **Procedure:** Take urine sample (5 mL), acidify with 3% acetic acid if alkaline. Heat upper portion to boiling.
	        - **Specific for BJ proteins:** They precipitate at a lower temperature (56°C). They disappear on further heating above 90°C. They reappear on cooling to a lower temperature again.
	        - **If albumin is also present:** Heat urine to boiling (this precipitates albumin), filter out the albumin precipitates, then repeat the heat and acetic acid test (specifically the temperature-controlled heating and cooling steps) on the filtered sample.
	- **Clinical Importance:**
	    
	    - Their demonstration in serum and/or urine as an M component is one of the **three key features** in the classic diagnostic triad for Multiple Myeloma. The triad includes marrow plasmacytosis >10%, radiologic evidence of lytic bony lesions, and demonstration of serum and/or urine M component.
	    - Excessive excretion of BJ proteins can lead to **renal failure**, often referred to as "myeloma kidney" (implied consequence of light chain deposition).
