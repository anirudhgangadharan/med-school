1.  A 45-year-old female present to OPD with easy fatigability and breathlessness from two months. She complains of menorrhagia since 6 months. On examination she has pallor and koilonychia.
	a. What is your diagnosis?
	b. Describe the peripheral smear and bone marrow findings in this case.
	c. Discuss RBC indices with reference to this case.
	### a. Diagnosis
	
	- **Iron Deficiency Anaemia (IDA)**.
	    - **Clinical Relevance:** Menorrhagia leads to **chronic blood loss**, a common cause of IDA in women of reproductive age. **Koilonychia (spoon-shaped nails)** is a characteristic sign of chronic iron deficiency. Pallor, fatigability, and breathlessness are general symptoms of anaemia due to lowered oxygen-carrying capacity and tissue hypoxia. IDA is the **most common nutritional disorder** globally.
	
	### b. Peripheral Smear and Bone Marrow Findings
	
	#### Peripheral Blood Film (PBF)
	
	- **Red Blood Cells (RBCs):**
	    - **Microcytic hypochromic** morphology (smaller and paler than normal).
	    - **Increased central pallor**, appearing as a thin rim of haemoglobin at the periphery (e.g., **ring or pessary cells** in severe cases).
	    - Marked **anisocytosis** (variation in size) and **poikilocytosis** (variation in shape).
	    - Presence of **pencil/cigar-shaped cells** and **target cells**.
	    - **Normoblasts** (nucleated RBCs) are **uncommon**.
	- **White Blood Cells (WBCs):** Usually **normal** count. Eosinophilia may be present if due to hookworm infestation.
	- **Platelets:** Generally **normal** count.
	- **Reticulocyte Count:** **Low** for the degree of anaemia, indicating impaired marrow response.
	
	#### Bone Marrow
	
	- **Cellularity:** **Increased (moderately hypercellular)** due to erythroid hyperplasia (compensatory response).
	- **Myeloid:Erythroid (M:E) Ratio:** **Decreased** (e.g., 2:1 to 1:2, normal 2:1 to 4:1).
	- **Erythropoiesis:** **Normoblastic erythropoiesis** with a predominance of **small polychromatic normoblasts (micronormoblasts)**. Cytoplasmic maturation characteristically **lags behind** nuclear maturation.
	- **Marrow Iron Stores:** **Deficient reticuloendothelial iron stores** and **absence of siderotic iron granules** from developing normoblasts. This is considered the **"gold standard" test** for iron deficiency, confirmed by a **negative Prussian blue reaction**.
	- **Other cells:** Myeloid, lymphoid, and megakaryocytic cells are generally normal in number and morphology.
	
	### c. RBC Indices
	
	Red Blood Cell (RBC) indices are significantly altered in IDA due to defective haemoglobin synthesis.
	
	- **Mean Corpuscular Volume (MCV):**
	    - **Significantly reduced**, typically **<80 fL** (Normal: 82–98 fL). Indicates **microcytic** red blood cells.
	- **Mean Corpuscular Haemoglobin (MCH):**
	    - **Reduced**, typically **<25 pg** (Normal: 27–32 pg). Reflects decreased haemoglobin weight per RBC.
	- **Mean Corpuscular Haemoglobin Concentration (MCHC):**
	    - **Reduced**, generally **<27 g/dL** (Normal: 31–36 g/dL). This is a **hallmark of hypochromic** red blood cells, indicating lower haemoglobin concentration within the cells.
	- **Red Cell Distribution Width (RDW):**
	    - **Increased**, typically **>15%** (Normal: 11.5–14.5%).
	    - An increased RDW signifies **anisocytosis** (variation in red cell size) and is often one of the **earliest laboratory indicators of iron deficiency anaemia**.
	    - **Clinical Pearl:** RDW is useful in differentiating IDA (increased RDW, low MCV) from thalassaemia trait (normal RDW, low MCV).

2. A 38-year-old male presents with weakness, fatigue, dyspnea, and complaints of recurrent bleeding piles. On examination: pallor (++), spoon-shaped nails and smooth tongue was noted.
	a. What is your provisional diagnosis?
	b. What will be the laboratory findings, which will confirm your provisional diagnosis?
	c. What are the differential diagnosis on peripheral blood smear examination findings?
	### Provisional Diagnosis
	
	a. Your provisional diagnosis is **Iron Deficiency Anaemia (IDA)**. * This is strongly suggested by the patient's symptoms of **weakness, fatigue, and dyspnoea**, which are general features of anaemia. * The recurrent **bleeding piles** indicate chronic blood loss, which is the most common cause of IDA in adult males. * The examination findings of **pallor (++)** confirm anaemia. * The presence of **spoon-shaped nails (koilonychia)** and a **smooth tongue (atrophic glossitis)** are characteristic epithelial tissue changes seen in long-standing chronic iron deficiency anaemia.
	
	### Laboratory Findings for Confirmation
	
	b. To confirm the diagnosis of Iron Deficiency Anaemia, the following laboratory findings would be expected:
	
	- **Peripheral Blood Film (PBF) and Red Cell Indices:**
	    
	    - **Haemoglobin (Hb)** and **Haematocrit (PCV)** will be **decreased**.
	    - **Red cells** will show characteristic **microcytic hypochromic morphology** (small and pale).
	        - There will be **increased central pallor**. In severe cases, only a thin rim of haemoglobin might be seen at the periphery (ring or pessary cell).
	        - **Anisocytosis** (variation in size) and **poikilocytosis** (variation in shape), including **pencil/cigar-shaped cells**, will be noted.
	        - **Target cells**, elliptical forms, and polychromatic cells are often present.
	    - **Mean Corpuscular Volume (MCV)**: **Significantly reduced, typically <80 fL** (normal range: 82–98 fL).
	    - **Mean Corpuscular Haemoglobin (MCH)**: **Reduced, typically <25 pg** (normal range: 27–32 pg).
	    - **Mean Corpuscular Haemoglobin Concentration (MCHC)**: **Reduced, typically <27 g/dL** (normal range: 31–36 g/dL).
	    - **Red Cell Distribution Width (RDW)**: **Increased and >15%** (this is often the earliest sign of iron deficiency).
	    - **Reticulocyte count**: Will be **low** for the degree of anaemia.
	    - **White blood cells (WBCs)** and **platelets** are generally **normal**.
	- **Bone Marrow Findings (if performed, not routinely essential but helpful in complicated cases):**
	    
	    - **Marrow cellularity**: **Increased** due to erythroid hyperplasia, leading to a **decreased myeloid-erythroid (M:E) ratio**.
	    - **Erythropoiesis**: Will show **normoblastic erythropoiesis with a predominance of small polychromatic normoblasts (micronormoblasts)**, where cytoplasmic maturation lags behind.
	    - **Marrow iron stores**: Will be **deficient or absent** when stained with Prussian blue reaction (this is considered the "gold standard" test for absent iron stores).
	- **Biochemical Findings:**
	    
	    - **Serum iron level**: Will be **low** (often under 50 µg/dl; normal: 40-140 µg/dl).
	    - **Total Iron Binding Capacity (TIBC)**: Will be **high** (normal: 250-450 µg/dl), resulting in **<10% transferrin saturation** (normal: 33%).
	    - **Serum ferritin**: Will be **very low** (normal: 30-250 ng/ml), indicating depleted tissue iron stores.
	    - **Reduced reticulocyte haemoglobin** is an earlier indicator of IDA.
	    - **Increased Red Cell Protoporphyrin (RBC protoporphyrin)** and **Increased Transferrin Receptor (TFR)** are also observed.
	
	### Differential Diagnosis on Peripheral Blood Smear Examination Findings
	
	c. When a peripheral blood smear shows **microcytic hypochromic anaemia**, the main differential diagnoses include:
	
	1. **Thalassaemia Syndromes (especially Thalassaemia minor/trait and Thalassaemia major)**:
	    
	    - **PBF findings**: Similar to IDA, showing **severe microcytosis and hypochromia**, marked anisopoikilocytosis, basophilic stippling, and many target cells.
	    - **Distinguishing features**:
	        - RBC count is often **increased or normal** (in contrast to IDA where it's decreased).
	        - **RDW is typically normal** in thalassaemia trait, unlike the increased RDW in IDA.
	        - **Haemoglobin electrophoresis** is confirmatory, showing increased HbA2 and/or HbF.
	        - Osmotic fragility test shows **increased resistance to haemolysis**.
	        - Serum iron, TIBC, and ferritin levels are usually normal or elevated, as it is a disorder of globin synthesis, not iron deficiency.
	2. **Anaemia of Chronic Disorders (ACD)**:
	    
	    - **PBF findings**: Can be **normocytic normochromic**, but **may also be slightly microcytic and hypochromic**.
	    - **Distinguishing features**:
	        - **Serum iron is reduced**, but **TIBC is low-to-normal** (in contrast to IDA's high TIBC).
	        - **Serum ferritin is increased**, as iron is sequestered in macrophages, which is a key distinguishing feature from IDA.
	        - Bone marrow typically shows **normal erythroid maturation** with **increased iron in macrophages** but reduced stainable iron in red cell precursors.
	3. **Sideroblastic Anaemia**:
	    
	    - **PBF findings**: Can present as **microcytic hypochromic** anaemia, but often shows a **dimorphic blood picture** (a dual population of red cells, both microcytic hypochromic and macrocytic/normochromic).
	    - **Distinguishing features**:
	        - Bone marrow examination is key, revealing **ring sideroblasts** (iron-laden mitochondria around the nucleus of normoblasts), which are pathognomonic.
	        - **Serum iron and ferritin are typically raised** with almost complete saturation of TIBC.
	4. **Lead Poisoning**:
	    
	    - **PBF findings**: Characteristically presents with **microcytic hypochromic anaemia** and prominent **basophilic stippling**.
	    - **Distinguishing features**: Diagnosis is confirmed by elevated blood lead levels.

	a. Your provisional diagnosis is **Sickle Cell Anaemia (HbSS disease)**. * This is strongly supported by the presence of **sickle-shaped cells on peripheral smear**. * The patient's acute symptoms align with complications of Sickle Cell Anaemia: * **Severe anaemia** (Haematocrit 18%). * **Acute Chest Syndrome**: Chest pain, nonproductive cough, and bilateral pulmonary infiltrates are characteristic. * **Vaso-occlusive/Pain Crisis**: Pain in the bones of the hand (**dactylitis**) is a common manifestation, resulting from blockage of microcirculation by sickled red cells causing hypoxic injury and infarction. Severe abdominal pain and cramping also indicate vaso-occlusive crisis of abdominal viscera. * **Splenic Sequestration Crisis**: Splenomegaly with a sudden, significant drop in haematocrit (to 18%) is highly suggestive of acute splenic sequestration, especially in a child or young adolescent, where blood gets trapped in the spleen. * Fever is a common accompanying symptom in sickle cell crises.
	
	### Laboratory Findings for Confirmation
	
	b. To confirm the diagnosis of Sickle Cell Anaemia, the following laboratory findings would be expected:
	
	- **Haematological Findings:**
	    
	    - **Haemoglobin (Hb)** and **Haematocrit (Hct)**: Already noted as severely decreased (Hct 18%), confirming significant anaemia.
	    - **Red Blood Cells (RBCs)**:
	        - Morphology: Beyond the observed sickle-shaped cells, the smear would show **normocytic normochromic to mildly hypochromic** RBCs. There would be **moderate to severe anisopoikilocytosis** (variation in size and shape). **Target cells** and **nucleated red cells** are also commonly seen.
	    - **Reticulocyte Count**: **Increased** (typically 3-10%) due to compensatory erythropoiesis, reflecting ongoing haemolysis, unless an aplastic crisis (e.g., due to Parvovirus B19) is superimposed, in which case it would be low.
	    - **Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR)**: Characteristically **reduced** in Sickle Cell Anaemia because sickle cells do not form rouleaux.
	    - **White Blood Cells (WBCs)**: Often normal, but **neutrophilia with a shift to the left** (increased immature neutrophils) can be seen in active haemolysis or infection.
	    - **Platelets**: Generally normal, but can be **decreased (thrombocytopenia)** in acute sequestration crisis or if disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) complicates the condition.
	- **Bone Marrow Examination:**
	    
	    - Would reveal **hypercellularity** due to **compensatory normoblastic erythroid hyperplasia**, as the marrow tries to compensate for the rapid destruction of red cells.
	- **Biochemical Findings:**
	    
	    - **Serum Bilirubin**: **Raised unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin** is expected due to increased extravascular haemolysis.
	    - **Serum Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH)**: **Increased** due to red cell breakdown.
	    - **Serum Haptoglobin**: **Reduced** or absent, as it is consumed in binding free haemoglobin released during haemolysis.
	    - **Iron Status**: Typically shows **raised serum iron, serum ferritin, and transferrin saturation**, as iron is continuously liberated from destroyed red cells.
	    - **Urine Urobilinogen**: **Increased**.
	- **Specific Diagnostic/Confirmatory Tests:**
	    
	    - **Sickling Test**: A **positive sickling test** (induced by a reducing agent like 2% sodium metabisulfite) would confirm the presence of HbS.
	    - **Haemoglobin Electrophoresis or High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC)**: This is **confirmatory** for the specific haemoglobinopathy. In homozygous Sickle Cell Anaemia (HbSS), it would show **predominant HbS (70-90%) and elevated HbF (10-30%), with no HbA**.
	
	### Differential Diagnosis on Peripheral Blood Smear Examination Findings
	
	c. Given that the peripheral blood smear explicitly shows **sickle-shaped cells**, the differential diagnoses primarily revolve around other **sickle cell syndromes**, which require further laboratory tests (like haemoglobin electrophoresis) for definitive sub-typing:
	
	1. **Sickle Cell Anaemia (HbSS Disease)**: This is the most severe form, resulting from homozygous inheritance of the HbS gene, and is the most probable diagnosis given the patient's severe multi-system involvement.
	2. **Sickle Cell Trait (HbAS)**: This heterozygous state is usually asymptomatic, but sickling can occur under extreme hypoxic conditions. However, it is unlikely to cause such severe and widespread clinical manifestations as seen in this patient.
	3. **Sickle-Beta Thalassaemia (HbS/Beta-Thalassaemia)**: This involves co-inheritance of the HbS gene and a beta-thalassaemia gene. The clinical severity can vary widely depending on the type of beta-thalassaemia and the amount of HbA produced.
	4. **Sickle-Haemoglobin C Disease (HbSC Disease)**: This results from co-inheritance of the HbS gene and the HbC gene. **1. Diagnosis of Sickle Cell Anaemia**

- **Clinical Suspicion**: Consider in individuals of black race with haemolytic anaemia.
- **Laboratory Findings**:
    - Anaemia: **Moderate to severe** (haemoglobin 6-9 g/dL).
    - ESR: **Reduced** because sickle cells do not form rouleaux.
    - Reticulocyte count: **Increased** (3-10%).
    - Serum bilirubin: **Raised unconjugated** (predisposes to pigment gallstones).
    - Iron status: Raised serum iron, serum ferritin, and transferrin saturation; serum haptoglobin reduced.
- **Peripheral Blood Film (PBF)**:
    - RBCs: **Characteristic sickle cells** (long, curved cells with pointed ends), **target cells**, anisopoikilocytosis.
    - Features of splenic atrophy (due to autosplenectomy from recurrent splenic infarction): **Howell-Jolly bodies**.
    - Normocytic normochromic to mildly hypochromic.
- **Diagnostic/Confirmatory Tests**:
    - **Sickling Test**: **Positive**. Sickling is induced by adding a reducing substance (e.g., 2% sodium metabisulfite) to blood. Red cells with HbS show sickled and holly leaf appearance, which is diagnostic.
    - **Haemoglobin Electrophoresis**: **Predominance of HbS (70-90%)**, presence of **HbF (10-30%)**, and **no normal HbA**.
    - **Solubility Test for Haemoglobin S**: Solution appears **turbid** if HbS is present.
    - **High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC)**: Useful for confirmation.

**2. Megaloblastic Anaemia—Laboratory Diagnosis**

- **Definition**: Disorders caused by **impaired DNA synthesis**, characterized by a distinctive abnormality in haematopoietic precursors in the bone marrow where the **maturation of the nucleus is delayed relative to that of the cytoplasm**.
- **Underlying Defect**: Deficiency of **vitamin B12 and/or folic acid**.
- **General Laboratory Findings**:
    - Haemoglobin: Reduced to a variable degree.
    - Reticulocyte count: Generally **low to normal** in untreated cases.
    - Biochemical findings: **Increased serum unconjugated bilirubin and LDH** (due to ineffective erythropoiesis causing marrow cell breakdown). Serum iron and ferritin may be normal or elevated. Serum homocysteine is raised.
- **Peripheral Blood Smear (PBF)**:
    - **RBCs**: Characteristic **macrocytosis** (MCV usually >120 fL), marked **anisocytosis** and **poikilocytosis**, and presence of **macro-ovalocytes** (diagnostic).
    - Evidence of dyserythropoiesis: **Basophilic stippling**, **Howell-Jolly bodies** (purple nuclear remnants), and occasionally Cabot rings or normoblasts.
    - **WBCs**: Total WBC count may be reduced (**leucopenia**). Characteristic **hypersegmented neutrophils** (having more than 5 nuclear lobes) are a specific morphological sign. Occasional myelocytes may be seen.
    - **Platelets**: May be moderately reduced in severely anaemic patients; bizarre forms may be seen.
    - _Dimorphic blood picture_ (macrocytic and microcytic-hypochromic) can occur if associated with iron deficiency.
- **Bone Marrow Findings**:
    - **Cellularity**: Markedly **hypercellular**.
    - **Myeloid-Erythroid (M:E) Ratio**: **Decreased/reversed** (e.g., 1:1 to 1:6, normal 2:1 to 4:1) due to erythroid hyperplasia.
    - **Erythropoiesis**: Characteristic **megaloblastic erythropoiesis**. Megaloblasts are abnormal, large, nucleated erythroid precursors exhibiting **nuclear-cytoplasmic asynchrony** (nuclei are less mature than the development of cytoplasm). This leads to ineffective erythropoiesis (developing megaloblasts die in the marrow).
    - **Myelopoiesis**: Granulocyte precursors also affected, displaying nuclear-cytoplasmic asynchrony with **giant forms of metamyelocytes and band cells**.
    - **Megakaryopoiesis**: Usually normal in number but may show abnormal morphology (e.g., hypersegmented nuclei).
    - **Marrow Iron**: Increased in number and size of iron granules in erythroid precursors and reticulum cells. Ring sideroblasts are rare.
    - Chromosomes: May show random chromosomal abnormalities.

**3. Hereditary Spherocytosis—Molecular Pathology and Morphology**

- **Definition**: A rare inherited haemolytic anaemia resulting from a **defect in the red cell membrane**.
- **Etiopathogenesis/Molecular Pathology**:
    - Primarily an **autosomal dominant** disorder.
    - Caused by **mutations in genes encoding red cell membrane proteins**, most commonly **ankyrin, band 3, α-spectrin, or band protein 4.2**.
    - These defects lead to a weakness in the connection between the lipid bilayer and the underlying cytoskeleton, causing the red cell membrane to lose fragments as the cells age.
    - The loss of membrane surface area with preserved cell volume forces the red cells to assume a **spherical shape (spherocytes)**.
    - These rigid, inflexible spherocytes get **trapped in the spleen**, leading to their premature destruction (extravascular haemolysis).
- **Morphology (PBF)**:
    - **Microspherocytes**: Small, dark-staining (hyperchromic) red blood cells that **lack central pallor**. These are the most distinctive feature.
    - Polychromasia due to reticulocytosis.
- **Clinical Features**: Often presents with the classic triad of **intermittent jaundice, splenomegaly, and spherocytes in the peripheral smear**.
- **Laboratory Features (Key Diagnostic)**:
    - Reticulocytosis (5-20%).
    - MCV: Usually normal or slightly decreased; **MCHC: Increased** (often >35 g/dL), which is a valuable diagnostic clue.
    - **Osmotic Fragility Test: Increased** (red cells lyse more readily in hypotonic saline solutions).
    - Direct Coombs' Test: **Negative** (helps distinguish from autoimmune haemolytic anaemia).

**4. Beta-Thalassaemia (Thalassaemia Major)**

- **Definition**: A hereditary haemolytic anaemia characterized by **reduced or absent synthesis of the β-globin chains** of haemoglobin.
- **Classification**: Homozygous form (β⁰/β⁰ or β⁺/β⁺ or double heterozygous β⁰/β⁺). It is the most severe form of congenital haemolytic anaemia.
- **Pathophysiology**:
    - Imbalance in globin chain synthesis: Reduced β-chains lead to **excess α-chains**.
    - **Ineffective Erythropoiesis**: Unpaired α-chains are unstable, aggregate into insoluble precipitates, damage erythroid precursor membranes, leading to their apoptosis and intramedullary destruction. This results in poorly haemoglobinized (hypochromic) and small (microcytic) red cells.
    - **Haemolysis**: Remaining red cells are damaged and undergo premature destruction.
    - **Compensatory Mechanisms**: Bone marrow hyperplasia leads to **bone expansion** (e.g., **thalassaemic facies** like "chipmunk face" and "crew-cut appearance" on skull X-ray), and **extramedullary haematopoiesis** (causing hepatosplenomegaly).
    - **Iron Overload**: A major complication due to repeated blood transfusions and increased dietary absorption, leading to **haemosiderosis and secondary haemochromatosis** affecting organs like heart, liver, and pancreas.
- **Clinical Features**: Manifestations typically begin after 6 months of life (as HbF is replaced by HbA/HbS). Severe chronic haemolytic anaemia, growth retardation, and bony deformities are prominent.
- **Laboratory Findings**:
    - Anaemia: Usually **severe** (Haemoglobin 3-8 g/dL).
    - RBC Count: May be increased or normal (contrast with iron deficiency anaemia).
    - Reticulocytosis: Generally present (5-15%).
    - **Red Cell Indices**: **MCV, MCH, and MCHC are significantly reduced** (microcytic hypochromic anaemia).
    - Serum Bilirubin (unconjugated): Generally raised.
    - Serum Haptoglobin: Markedly reduced.
    - **Serum Iron Profile**: **Serum iron, serum ferritin, and transferrin saturation are markedly increased**; Total Iron Binding Capacity (TIBC) is reduced.
    - WBC count: Often raised with a left shift. Platelet count usually normal.
    - **Osmotic Fragility Test: Decreased** (increased resistance to saline haemolysis).
    - **Haemoglobin Electrophoresis**: Confirmatory. Shows **increased amounts of HbF (30-90%)** and **increased HbA2 (diagnostic, 4-8%)**, with almost complete absence or variable amounts of HbA.

**5. Bone Marrow Findings in Megaloblastic Anaemia**

- **Cellularity**: Markedly **hypercellular**.
- **Myeloid-Erythroid (M:E) Ratio**: **Decreased/reversed** (e.g., 1:1 to 1:6, normal 2:1 to 4:1), reflecting pronounced erythroid hyperplasia.
- **Erythropoiesis**: Characterized by **megaloblastic erythropoiesis**.
    - **Megaloblasts**: Abnormal, large, nucleated erythroid precursors.
    - Exhibit **nuclear-cytoplasmic asynchrony**: The nuclei are less mature (large, fine, sieve-like, open chromatin) than the cytoplasm, which haemoglobinizes normally or faster.
    - **Ineffective Erythropoiesis**: Many developing megaloblasts undergo intramedullary destruction.
- **Myelopoiesis**: Granulocyte precursors show affected maturation with **giant metamyelocytes and band cells**.
- **Megakaryopoiesis**: Megakaryocytes are usually present in normal numbers but may show abnormal morphology (e.g., hypersegmented nuclei, agranular cytoplasm).
- **Marrow Iron**: **Increased** iron stores in reticulum cells and increased iron granules in erythroid precursors (siderotic granules). Ring sideroblasts are rare.

**7. RBC Indices**

Red cell indices are calculated values that provide information about the average size and haemoglobin content of red blood cells, crucial for morphological classification and diagnosis of anaemias.

- **Mean Corpuscular Volume (MCV)**:
    - **Definition**: Average **volume** of a single red blood cell.
    - **Units**: Femtoliters (fL).
    - **Normal Range**: 82–98 fL (Harsh Mohan: 85 ± 8 fL, 77-93 fL).
    - **Significance**:
        - **Microcytic Anaemia**: MCV <80 fL (e.g., Iron Deficiency Anaemia, Thalassaemia).
        - **Normocytic Anaemia**: MCV 80–100 fL (e.g., Acute Blood Loss, Haemolytic Anaemia, Anaemia of Chronic Disease).
        - **Macrocytic Anaemia**: MCV >100 fL (e.g., Megaloblastic Anaemia).
- **Mean Corpuscular Haemoglobin (MCH)**:
    - **Definition**: Average **weight** of haemoglobin in a single red blood cell.
    - **Units**: Picograms (pg).
    - **Normal Range**: 27–32 pg (Harsh Mohan: 29.5 ± 2.5 pg).
    - **Significance**: Generally reduced in microcytic anaemias (<26 pg) and may be raised in macrocytic anaemias (>33 pg). Limited value in differential diagnosis compared to MCHC.
- **Mean Corpuscular Haemoglobin Concentration (MCHC)**:
    - **Definition**: Average **concentration** of haemoglobin within a red blood cell, relative to its volume.
    - **Units**: g/dL.
    - **Normal Range**: 31–35 g/dL (Harsh Mohan: 32.5 ± 2.5 g/dL).
    - **Significance**:
        - Considered to be of greater clinical significance as it is independent of red cell count and size.
        - **Low** (<31 g/dL): Indicates **hypochromasia** (e.g., Iron Deficiency Anaemia, Thalassaemia, Sideroblastic Anaemia).
        - **Normal**: Usually in macrocytic and normocytic anaemias.
        - **High** (>36 g/dL): Indicates hyperchromasia (e.g., spherocytosis, neonatal blood, megaloblastic anaemia).
- **Red Cell Distribution Width (RDW)**:
    - **Definition**: Quantitative measure of **anisocytosis** (variation in red cell size).
    - **Normal Range**: 11.5–14.5%.
    - **Significance**:
        - **Increased RDW**: Indicates heterogeneity in red cell size and/or shape (e.g., early iron deficiency anaemia).
        - Useful in distinguishing **Iron Deficiency Anaemia (increased RDW with low MCV)** from **Thalassaemia Trait (normal RDW with low MCV)**.

**8. Anaemia—Definition and Classification by Aetiology**

- **Definition**: Anaemia is defined as a **reduced haemoglobin concentration in blood below the lower limit of the normal range for the age and sex of the individual**.
    - **Adult males**: Lower limit of normal Haemoglobin is 13.0 g/dL.
    - **Adult females**: Lower limit of normal Haemoglobin is 11.5 g/dL.
    - **Functional Definition**: A decrease in the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood.
- **Pathophysiology**: Subnormal haemoglobin leads to lowered oxygen-carrying capacity, resulting in tissue hypoxia and impaired function. The body initiates compensatory physiological adaptations.
- **Aetiological (Pathophysiologic) Classification of Anaemias**:
    - **I. Anaemia Due to Increased Blood Loss**:
        - **Acute Post-Haemorrhagic Anaemia**: Sudden, significant blood loss (e.g., trauma).
        - **Chronic Blood Loss**: Slow, insidious blood loss (e.g., chronic gastrointestinal bleeding), often leading to iron deficiency anaemia.
    - **II. Anaemias Due to Impaired Red Cell Production**:
        - **a) Cytoplasmic Maturation Defects**:
            - **Deficient Haem Synthesis**: Most commonly **Iron Deficiency Anaemia**.
            - **Deficient Globin Synthesis**: **Thalassaemic Syndromes**.
        - **b) Nuclear Maturation Defects**:
            - **Vitamin B12 and/or Folic Acid Deficiency**: Causes **Megaloblastic Anaemia** due to impaired DNA synthesis.
        - **c) Defect in Stem Cell Proliferation and Differentiation**:
            - **Aplastic Anaemia**: Primary bone marrow failure affecting all cell lines, leading to pancytopenia.
            - **Pure Red Cell Aplasia**: Affects only the erythroid series.
        - **d) Anaemia of Chronic Disorders**: Associated with chronic systemic diseases (e.g., chronic inflammation/infections, chronic renal disease, endocrine deficiencies, liver disease).
        - **e) Bone Marrow Infiltration**: Normal marrow replaced by abnormal cells (e.g., in **Leukaemias, Lymphomas, Myelofibrosis, Multiple Myeloma**, metastatic cancers, granulomatous conditions).
        - **f) Congenital Anaemia**: Inherited disorders (e.g., Sideroblastic Anaemia, Congenital Dyserythropoietic Anaemia).
    - **III. Anaemias Due to Increased Red Cell Destruction (Haemolytic Anaemias)**:
        - **A. Intracorpuscular Defects (Hereditary/Intrinsic)**: Problems within the red blood cell itself.
            - **Abnormalities of Red Cell Membrane**: (e.g., **Hereditary Spherocytosis**, Hereditary Elliptocytosis, Hereditary Stomatocytosis).
            - **Red Cell Enzyme Defects (Enzymopathies)**: (e.g., G6PD Deficiency, Pyruvate Kinase Deficiency).
            - **Disorders of Haemoglobin (Haemoglobinopathies)**:
                - **Structurally Abnormal Haemoglobins**: (e.g., **Sickle Syndromes**, HbC disease).
                - **Reduced Globin Chain Synthesis**: (e.g., **Thalassaemias**).
        - **B. Extracorpuscular Defects (Acquired/Extrinsic)**: Problems outside the red blood cell.
            - **Antibody-Mediated (Immunohaemolytic Anaemias)**: (e.g., Autoimmune Haemolytic Anaemia - Warm/Cold Antibody Type, Drug-induced, Isoimmune Haemolytic Anaemia like Haemolytic Disease of Newborn, Transfusion Reactions).
            - **Mechanical Trauma**: (e.g., Microangiopathic Haemolytic Anaemia in DIC, prosthetic heart valves).
            - **Direct Toxic Effects**: (e.g., **Malaria**, bacterial infections).
            - **Acquired Red Cell Membrane Abnormalities**: (e.g., Paroxysmal Nocturnal Haemoglobinuria - PNH).
            - **Splenomegaly (Hypersplenism)**: Excessive removal of RBCs by an enlarged spleen.
