1. Describe different types of cellular adaptations to Stress with, examples:
	Cellular adaptations are **reversible changes** in cell size, number, phenotype, metabolic activity, or function, allowing cells to **survive and function** in altered internal or external conditions [R446]. These responses occur in response to either **physiological needs** (e.g., hormonal stimulation, increased workload) or **non-lethal pathological stimuli** [R447, H37]. If the stimulus persists, adapted cells may undergo injury or progress to cell death or malignancy [H42].
	
	**Key Concepts for Medical School Exams:**
	
	- **Reversibility**: Adaptive changes are generally reversible upon withdrawal of the stimulus [H42].
	- **Mechanisms**: Involve altered cell surface receptor binding, changes in protein synthesis signals, and synthesis of new proteins like heat-shock proteins (HSPs) [H43].
	- **Types**:
	    1. **Atrophy**
	    2. **Hypertrophy**
	    3. **Hyperplasia**
	    4. **Metaplasia**
	    5. **Dysplasia**
	
	---
	
	### 1. Atrophy
	
	- **Definition**: **Reduction in the size and/or number of parenchymal cells** in an organ or its parts, leading to decreased organ size and function [H37, H53].
	- **Morphology**: Reduced organelles, decreased synthesis of DNA, RNA, and protein [R455].
	- **Examples**:
	    - **Physiological**: Decreased size of uterus post-partum.
	    - **Pathological**:
	        - **Disuse atrophy**: Muscle wasting in immobilised limbs.
	        - **Denervation atrophy**: Muscle atrophy after nerve damage.
	        - **Ischaemic atrophy**: Brain atrophy in atherosclerosis.
	        - **Nutritional atrophy**: Cachexia in chronic diseases.
	        - **Endocrine atrophy**: Testicular atrophy in hypopituitarism.
	        - **Pressure atrophy**: Kidney atrophy due to hydronephrosis.
	
	---
	
	### 2. Hypertrophy
	
	- **Definition**: **Increase in the size of individual parenchymal cells**, leading to enlargement of the organ or tissue **without an increase in cell number** [H38, H53, R447].
	- **Causes**: Increased functional demand or hormonal stimulation [H44, R448]. Primarily affects muscles and non-dividing cells [H44].
	- **Mechanism**: Increased synthesis of cellular proteins, activation of signal transduction pathways (growth factors, agonists), and transcription factors [R449, R450, R451]. May involve re-expression of embryonic/foetal genes (e.g., ANF in cardiac hypertrophy) [R452].
	- **Morphology**:
	    - **Gross**: Enlarged, heavier organ [H45, R453].
	    - **Microscopy**: Increased cell size, prominent nuclei, and increased numbers of organelles (e.g., mitochondria, myofibrils) [H45, H225, R453].
	    - **Types**:
	        - **Concentric hypertrophy**: Increased wall thickness _without_ chamber dilatation (e.g., left ventricular hypertrophy in hypertension) [H224, H226].
	        - **Eccentric hypertrophy**: Increased wall thickness _with_ chamber dilatation (e.g., left ventricular hypertrophy with dilatation in valvular incompetence) [H224, H226].
	- **Examples**:
	    - **Physiological**:
	        - Skeletal muscle hypertrophy in athletes/bodybuilders [R448].
	        - Uterine smooth muscle enlargement during pregnancy (also hyperplasia) [H44, R448].
	    - **Pathological**:
	        - Cardiac muscle hypertrophy in systemic hypertension or aortic stenosis [H44, H219, H233, R449].
	        - Urinary bladder smooth muscle hypertrophy due to urethral obstruction (e.g., prostatic hyperplasia) [R449].
	        - Pyloric stenosis leading to stomach muscular layer hypertrophy [R449].
	
	---
	
	### 3. Hyperplasia
	
	- **Definition**: **Increase in the number of parenchymal cells** in an organ or tissue, leading to its enlargement [H46, H56, R453].
	- **Mechanism**: Increased recruitment of cells from the resting (G0) phase to undergo mitosis [H46].
	- **Affected Cells**: Occurs in labile cells (e.g., epithelial cells of skin, GI tract, bone marrow) and stable cells (e.g., liver, pancreas, kidney, smooth muscle), but _not_ permanent cells (e.g., neurons, cardiac/skeletal muscle) [H46].
	- **Morphology**: Enlargement of the affected organ/tissue due to increased cell count, increased DNA synthesis, and mitoses [H49, R453].
	- **Examples**:
	    - **Physiological**:
	        - **Hormonal**: Female breast at puberty, pregnancy, lactation; pregnant uterus; normal endometrium proliferation post-menstruation [H48, R453].
	        - **Compensatory**: Liver regeneration after partial hepatectomy [H48].
	    - **Pathological**:
	        - Skin warts due to Human Papilloma Virus (HPV) [H49, R49].
	        - Intraductal epithelial hyperplasia in fibrocystic breast disease [H49].
	        - Benign prostatic hyperplasia in old age [H48].
	- **Clinical Relevance**: Can be a precursor to **dysplasia and neoplasia** if the stimulus persists [H47, R456].
	
	---
	
	### 4. Metaplasia
	
	- **Definition**: A **reversible change** where one adult cell type (epithelial or mesenchymal) is **replaced by another adult cell type**, usually in response to chronic irritation or abnormal stimuli [H49, H56, R454]. It often reverts to normal if the stimulus is removed [H56, R454].
	- **Causes**: Chronic irritation (mechanical, chemical, infective) [H50, R454], tissue damage, repair, and regeneration [R454]. The new cell type is typically more resistant to the altered environment [R454].
	- **Types & Examples**:
	    - **Epithelial Metaplasia**:
	        - **Squamous Metaplasia**: Common. Specialised epithelium (e.g., pseudostratified columnar ciliated) replaced by stratified squamous epithelium.
	            - Bronchus in chronic smokers [H50, R455].
	            - Cervix [H50, H718].
	            - Bladder in presence of stones.
	        - **Columnar Metaplasia**: Squamous epithelium replaced by columnar epithelium.
	            - Barrett's oesophagus (squamous to columnar) due to chronic gastric reflux [H51, R456].
	        - **Intestinal Metaplasia**: Gastric glands replaced by intestinal-like cells [R456].
	    - **Mesenchymal Metaplasia**: Less common. Connective tissue (e.g., fibrous tissue) replaced by another mesenchymal tissue.
	        - Osseous metaplasia (e.g., bone formation in soft tissues after trauma) [H51].
	- **Clinical Relevance**: If the noxious stimulus persists, metaplasia can progress to **dysplasia and ultimately neoplasia** [H42, R456].
	
	---
	
	### 5. Dysplasia
	
	- **Definition**: **Disordered cellular development** (meaning "bad moulding"), often accompanied by hyperplasia or metaplasia [H41, H52, H303, R582]. It represents a **disturbance in growth regulation** [R582].
	- **Features (Microscopic hallmarks)**:
	    - **Cellular and Nuclear Pleomorphism**: Variation in size and shape of cells and nuclei [H52, H305, R583].
	    - **Increased Nuclear-to-Cytoplasmic (N:C) ratio**: Nuclei disproportionately large (can be 1:1) [H135, H305, R579, R583].
	    - **Hyperchromatism**: Dark-staining nuclei due to increased chromatin [H52, H133, H306, R583].
	    - **Loss of Polarity**: Disorganised arrangement of cells, disturbed orientation [H52, H132, H305, R579, R583].
	    - **Increased Mitotic Activity**: Mitotic figures more numerous and may be seen at all levels of the epithelium, not just the basal layer [H52, H305, R584].
	    - **Prominent Nucleoli** [H135].
	- **Sites**: Most commonly in squamous epithelium of:
	    - **Uterine cervix** (e.g., cervical intraepithelial neoplasia - CIN / Squamous intraepithelial lesion - SIL) [H52, H305, R584].
	    - Respiratory tract [H52, R584].
	    - Skin [R584].
	    - Can also occur in columnar mucosa (e.g., colon in ulcerative colitis, Barrett's oesophagus), ductal lining (breast), and prostatic glands [R585].
	- **Classification**: Graded as **mild, moderate, or severe** depending on the thickness of epithelium involved [H305, R585].
	    - **Mild dysplasia (CIN-1/L-SIL)**: Abnormal cells extend up to one-third of epithelial thickness [H305].
	    - **Moderate dysplasia (CIN-2)**: Abnormal cells extend up to two-thirds [H305].
	    - **Severe dysplasia (CIN-3)**: Abnormal cells extend from 75-90% thickness [H305].
	    - **Carcinoma in situ (CIN-3)**: Full-thickness involvement by atypical cells, but **without invasion of the underlying basement membrane** [H305, H586].
	- **Clinical Significance**: Dysplasia is considered a **pre-malignant lesion** [H52, H303, R585]. It may regress if the inciting stimulus is removed, but it can also **progress to carcinoma in situ and eventually invasive cancer** [H42, H52, H303, R586].
	1. Necrosis—definition, morphological features of various types with examples.
	Necrosis is a **pathological process** of cell death occurring in living tissue, caused by the progressive degradative action of enzymes following harmful injury. It is considered an "**accidental**" and unregulated form of cell death, resulting from severe damage to cell membranes and loss of ion homeostasis. Unlike apoptosis, necrosis is _always_ accompanied by an **inflammatory reaction** in the surrounding tissue.
	
	### General Morphological Features
	
	- **Cytoplasmic Changes**:
	    - **Increased eosinophilia**: Cytoplasm becomes more pink (acidophilic).
	    - **Glassy, homogeneous appearance**: Due to loss of glycogen particles.
	    - **Vacuolated/ "moth-eaten" appearance**: When enzymes digest cytoplasmic organelles.
	    - Presence of **myelin figures** (derived from damaged cell membranes).
	- **Nuclear Changes**: Result from DNA degradation.
	    - **Pyknosis**: Nucleus shrinks and becomes a dark, dense, basophilic mass.
	    - **Karyorrhexis**: Nuclear fragmentation into small, dispersed bits.
	    - **Karyolysis**: Dissolution of the nucleus, resulting in pale or absent staining.
	
	### Types of Necrosis (Morphological Patterns)
	
	1. **Coagulative Necrosis**
	    
	    - **Definition**: The most common type, where the **outline of dead tissues (underlying tissue architecture) is preserved** for at least a few days.
	    - **Causes**: Primarily **ischaemia** (sudden cessation of blood flow), and less often bacterial or chemical agents. Ischaemia denatures and coagulates structural proteins and enzymes, blocking proteolysis.
	    - **Organs Affected**: All solid organs except the brain. Common examples include **infarcts** of the heart, kidney, spleen, and in **dry gangrene** of a limb.
	    - **Gross Morphology**:
	        - Early stage: Pale, firm, and slightly swollen, often called an **infarct**.
	        - Later: Becomes more yellowish, softer, and shrunken.
	        - Typically **wedge-shaped** in organs like the kidney and spleen, with the occluded vessel at the apex.
	    - **Microscopic Morphology**:
	        - **"Tombstones"**: Outlines of cells are retained, and the cell type can still be recognised, but cytoplasmic and nuclear details are lost.
	        - Cells are swollen and have more **eosinophilic cytoplasm**.
	        - Shows nuclear changes of pyknosis, karyorrhexis, and karyolysis.
	        - The necrosed focus is eventually infiltrated by inflammatory cells (neutrophils, macrophages) that phagocytose dead cells.
	    - **Clinical Relevance**: Myocardial infarcts (heart muscle dies after 20-30 minutes of ischaemia).
	2. **Liquefaction (Colliquative) Necrosis**
	    
	    - **Definition**: Dead tissue rapidly undergoes **softening and transforms into a liquid viscous mass**.
	    - **Causes**:
	        - **Ischaemic injury to the central nervous system (CNS)**.
	        - **Suppurative infections** (bacterial or fungal), which stimulate leukocyte enzyme accumulation.
	    - **Mechanism**: Due to the dominant role of **hydrolytic enzymes** liberated from dead cells (autolysis) and leukocytes (heterolysis).
	    - **Organs Affected**:
	        - **Brain infarcts** (e.g., cerebral infarct). This is the _only_ organ where ischaemia typically causes liquefaction necrosis instead of coagulative.
	        - **Abscess cavities** (localized collection of pus).
	        - Seen in **wet gangrene** and pancreatic necrosis.
	    - **Gross Morphology**: The affected area is soft with a liquefied centre containing necrotic debris. It may later form a cyst wall.
	    - **Microscopic Morphology**: The cystic space contains necrotic cell debris and macrophages. The cyst wall is formed by proliferating capillaries, inflammatory cells, and gliosis (proliferating glial cells) in the brain, or proliferating fibroblasts in an abscess cavity.
	3. **Caseous Necrosis**
	    
	    - **Definition**: A distinctive type that combines features of both coagulative and liquefactive necrosis, resembling **dry, clumpy cheese**.
	    - **Causes**: Characteristic of **tuberculous infections** (due to Type IV hypersensitivity reaction and histotoxic effects of lipopolysaccharides from _Mycobacterium tuberculosis_).
	    - **Organs Affected**: Most common in the lung and lymph nodes.
	    - **Gross Morphology**: Foci resemble dry cheese; they are soft, granular, and yellowish. In lymph nodes, they appear as "map-like areas".
	    - **Microscopic Morphology**:
	        - Centre of the necrosed focus contains **structureless, eosinophilic material** with scattered granular debris of disintegrated nuclei; cellular outlines cannot be identified.
	        - The surrounding tissue shows characteristic **granulomatous inflammatory reaction**. This includes **epithelioid cells** (modified macrophages), interspersed **Langhans' giant cells** (nuclei arranged in horseshoe pattern), and a peripheral mantle of lymphocytes.
	        - Caseous necrotic material may undergo **dystrophic calcification**.
	4. **Fat Necrosis**
	    
	    - **Definition**: Focal areas of **fat destruction**, occurring mainly at fat-rich anatomical locations.
	    - **Types**:
	        - **Enzymatic Fat Necrosis**: Occurs in adipose tissue around an **acutely inflamed pancreas** (e.g., acute pancreatitis).
	            - **Mechanism**: Liberation of pancreatic lipases from injured or inflamed tissue. Lipase splits triglyceride esters, liberating free fatty acids that combine with calcium to form **calcium soaps** (fat saponification).
	            - **Gross Morphology**: Appears as **chalky-white areas**.
	            - **Microscopic Morphology**: Necrosed fat cells have a cloudy shadowy appearance. Foci of calcium soaps are identified as amorphous, granular, and basophilic material, surrounded by an inflammatory reaction.
	        - **Traumatic Fat Necrosis**: Occurs in tissues with high fat content (e.g., breast, thigh) following severe trauma.
	5. **Fibrinoid Necrosis**
	    
	    - **Definition**: Characterised by the deposition of **fibrin-like proteinaceous material** in the tissue matrix, which has the staining properties of fibrin and obscures underlying cellular detail.
	    - **Causes**: Encountered in various examples of **immunologic tissue injury** (e.g., immune complex vasculitis, autoimmune diseases), arterioles in **hypertension**, and peptic ulcer. Also seen in **Aschoff bodies** in rheumatic heart disease.
	    - **Microscopic Morphology**: Appears as brightly **eosinophilic, hyaline-like deposition** in the vessel wall. Often surrounded by nuclear debris of neutrophils (leucocytoclasis). Best appreciated with phosphotungstic acid haematoxylin (PTAH) stain.
	6. **Gangrene (Gangrenous Necrosis)**
	    
	    - **Definition**: **Massive necrosis with superadded putrefaction**, most often following coagulative necrosis due to ischaemia.
	    - **Types**:
	        - **Dry Gangrene**:
	            - **Causes**: Arterial occlusion (e.g., severe atherosclerosis, Buerger's disease).
	            - **Sites**: Begins in the **distal part of a limb**, usually the lower limb (foot, toes).
	            - **Gross Morphology**: Affected part is **dry, shrunken, and dark brown or black** ("mummy foot"). A **clear line of demarcation** is present between the gangrenous and viable tissue.
	            - **Microscopic Morphology**: Predominantly **coagulative necrosis**. Shows blurring and smudging of cell outlines. The line of demarcation consists of granulation tissue.
	        - **Wet Gangrene**:
	            - **Causes**: Due to venous blockage (e.g., strangulated hernia, intussusception, volvulus) or in naturally moist tissues and organs (e.g., gangrene bowel, lungs). Also seen in diabetic foot and bed sores.
	            - **Gross Morphology**: Affected part is **soft, swollen, putrid, and dark**. **No clear line of demarcation** is distinct.
	            - **Microscopic Morphology**: Predominantly **liquefactive necrosis**. Bacterial colonies and thrombi in vessel walls may be identified.
	        - **Gas Gangrene**: A special form of wet gangrene caused by gas-forming _Clostridium perfringens_ (Gram-positive anaerobic bacteria).
	
	### Clinical Relevance
	
	- **Enzyme Markers**: Necrosis causes leakage of intracellular proteins into circulation, allowing detection of tissue-specific damage. For instance, elevated serum levels of **cardiac troponins (cTn), creatine kinase (CK-MB), SGOT, and LDH** are useful guides for heart muscle death in myocardial infarction.
	- **Prognosis**: The type and extent of necrosis can determine prognosis, as seen in the severe outcomes of massive brain infarction or widespread wet gangrene.
	- **Differentiation from Apoptosis**: Critical for diagnosis and understanding disease processes (e.g., apoptosis can be physiological, involves single cells, no inflammation; necrosis is always pathological, involves tissue, and causes inflammation).
	    - **Apoptosis**: "Programmed cell death". "Falling off" of fragments (apoptotic bodies). Cell shrinkage, nuclear condensation and fragmentation, cytoplasmic blebbing. Affects single cells or small clusters. **No inflammatory reaction**.
	    - **Necrosis**: "Unregulated cell death". Digestion of tissue by hydrolytic enzymes. Cell swelling, membrane damage. Affects groups of cells or tissue. **Always accompanied by inflammatory reaction**.
2.  Pathological calcification
Pathological calcification is the **abnormal deposition of calcium salts** in tissues other than osteoid or enamel. This process is often accompanied by the deposition of small amounts of iron, magnesium, and other minerals. It presents in two distinct forms: **dystrophic calcification** and **metastatic calcification**.

---

### **1. Dystrophic Calcification**

- **Definition**: Deposition of calcium salts in **dying or dead tissues**. It occurs with **normal** calcium metabolism and **normal** serum calcium levels.
- **Causes & Examples**:
    - **Necrotic Tissue**:
        - **Caseous necrosis** (e.g., in tuberculosis, chronic inflammation). Grossly resembles dry cheese, soft, granular, and yellowish.
        - **Enzymatic fat necrosis** (e.g., in acute pancreatitis). Appears as chalky-white areas.
        - Dead parasites (e.g., hydatid cyst, Schistosoma eggs, cysticercosis).
    - **Degenerating Tissue**:
        - Aging or damaged **heart valves** (e.g., calcific aortic stenosis).
        - **Atherosclerosis**, especially in advanced atheromatous plaques in aorta and coronaries.
        - **Mönckeberg’s medial calcific sclerosis**: Calcification exclusively in the **tunica media** of muscular arteries in old age, producing rigid "pipestem-like" tubes without narrowing the lumen. No significant inflammation is seen.
        - Goitre of the thyroid.
        - Dense old scars, and walls of long-standing cysts (e.g., epidermal and pilar cysts).
        - Thrombi (e.g., phleboliths in veins).
    - **Psammoma Bodies**: Concentric calcified spherules that develop from mineral deposition on single necrotic cells. These are characteristic in certain tumours, including:
        - **Papillary carcinoma of thyroid**.
        - Papillary serous cystadenoma/cystadenocarcinoma of ovary.
        - Meningioma.
        - Papillary carcinoma of the kidney.
- **Mechanism**: Involves increased binding of phosphates with necrotic and degenerated tissue, which then binds to calcium to form calcium phosphate precipitates. Injured mitochondria play a role in calcium uptake.
- **Morphology**:
    - **Gross**: Appears as **fine, white granules or clumps**, feeling gritty and sand-like. In incised vessels with atherosclerosis, the diseased intima may "crack like an egg-shell".
    - **Microscopic**: Calcium salts appear as **deeply basophilic, irregular, and granular clumps**. They can be intracellular, extracellular, or both. In complicated atheromatous plaques, deposits are seen in the necrotic area and soft lipid pool.
    - **Special Stains**:
        - **von-Kossa stain** produces a black colour.
        - Alizarin red S produces a red staining.
        - Perl’s stain (Prussian blue reaction) can demonstrate accompanying iron deposits.
- **Clinical Relevance**: Can cause **organ dysfunction** (e.g., calcification of heart valves leading to stenosis).

---

### **2. Metastatic Calcification**

- **Definition**: Deposition of calcium salts in **apparently normal tissues**. It is invariably associated with **deranged calcium metabolism and hypercalcaemia**.
- **Causes**: Conditions that lead to **hypercalcaemia**:
    - **Increased Parathyroid Hormone (PTH) secretion** (e.g., primary hyperparathyroidism).
    - **Destruction of bone tissue** (e.g., primary bone tumours, multiple myeloma, leukaemia, metastatic tumours to bone).
    - Hypervitaminosis D.
    - Idiopathic hypercalcaemia of infancy (Williams syndrome).
    - Renal causes (e.g., renal tubular acidosis).
    - Excessive intestinal absorption of calcium.
    - Excessive mobilisation of calcium from bones.
    - Less common causes include milk-alkali syndrome and sarcoidosis.
- **Sites**: Although it can occur in any normal tissue, it preferentially affects organs that lose acid or have rapid pH changes:
    - **Kidneys**: Especially in the tubular epithelial cells, basement membrane, and tubular lumina, leading to **nephrocalcinosis**.
    - **Lungs**: In the alveolar walls and septa.
    - **Stomach**: On the acid-secreting fundal glands and interstitial tissues of the gastric mucosa.
    - **Blood vessels**: On the internal elastic lamina of systemic arteries and pulmonary veins.
    - Cornea.
    - Synovium of the joint.
- **Pathogenesis**: Occurs due to excessive binding of inorganic phosphate ions with elevated calcium ions, forming calcium phosphate precipitates.
- **Morphology**: Grossly, appears as fine, white granules or clumps. Histologically, similar to dystrophic calcification, calcium salts appear as deeply basophilic, irregular, granular clumps. Deposits in the kidney in nephrocalcinosis are seen in tubular epithelial cells.
- **Clinical Relevance**: Can lead to symptoms such as **renal colic, band keratopathy** (calcium deposits in cornea), visceral metastatic calcification, polyuria, and renal failure. Unlike dystrophic calcification, it **does not primarily cause organ dysfunction** through the calcification itself, but rather indicates an underlying metabolic derangement.

4. Differences between necrosis and apoptosis

Both **necrosis** and **apoptosis** are forms of cell death, but they differ significantly in their mechanisms, morphological features, and physiological or pathological contexts.

### Necrosis

- **Definition**: A **localised area of death of tissue** in a living organism. It is the sum of morphological changes indicating cell death in living tissue, caused by progressive degradation by hydrolytic enzymes released from dead cells.
- **Nature**: Considered an "**accidental**" and unregulated form of cell death. It results from severe damage to cell membranes and loss of ion homeostasis.
- **Inflammatory Response**: **Always accompanied by an inflammatory reaction** in the surrounding tissue.
- **Mechanism**: Involves cell death by **ATP depletion, membrane damage, and free radical injury**. Lysosomal membranes are damaged, leading to the escape and activation of hydrolytic enzymes that digest cellular components.

### Apoptosis

- **Definition**: A form of "**coordinated and internally programmed cell death**". It is a tightly regulated "**cell suicide**" program.
- **Nature**: A controlled and regulated process.
- **Inflammatory Response**: **Not accompanied by any inflammation** or collateral tissue damage.
- **Mechanism**: Triggered by intrinsic (mitochondrial) or extrinsic (death receptor-initiated) pathways, leading to the activation of **caspases**. This results in the degradation of the cell's own nuclear DNA and proteins. Mitochondria play a pivotal role.

    - **Mechanism**: Due to the dominant role of **hydrolytic enzymes** liberated from dead cells (autolysis) and leukocytes (heterolysis).
    - **Organs Affected**:
        - **Brain infarcts** (e.g., cerebral infarct). This is the _only_ organ where ischaemia typically causes liquefaction necrosis instead of coagulative.
        - **Abscess cavities** (localized collection of pus).
        - Seen in **wet gangrene** and pancreatic necrosis.
    - **Gross Morphology**: The affected area is soft with a liquefied centre containing necrotic debris. It may later form a cyst wall.
    - **Microscopic Morphology**: The cystic space contains necrotic cell debris and macrophages. The cyst wall is formed by proliferating capillaries, inflammatory cells, and gliosis (proliferating glial cells) in the brain, or proliferating fibroblasts in an abscess cavity.
3. **Caseous Necrosis**
    
    - **Definition**: A distinctive type that combines features of both coagulative and liquefactive necrosis, resembling **dry, clumpy cheese**.
    - **Causes**: Characteristic of **tuberculous infections** (due to Type IV hypersensitivity reaction and histotoxic effects of lipopolysaccharides from _Mycobacterium tuberculosis_).
    - **Organs Affected**: Most common in the lung and lymph nodes.
    - **Gross Morphology**: Foci resemble dry cheese; they are soft, granular, and yellowish. In lymph nodes, they appear as "map-like areas".
    - **Microscopic Morphology**:
        - Centre of the necrosed focus contains **structureless, eosinophilic material** with scattered granular debris of disintegrated nuclei; cellular outlines cannot be identified.
        - The surrounding tissue shows characteristic **granulomatous inflammatory reaction**. This includes **epithelioid cells** (modified macrophages), interspersed **Langhans' giant cells** (nuclei arranged in horseshoe pattern), and a peripheral mantle of lymphocytes.
        - Caseous necrotic material may undergo **dystrophic calcification**.
4. **Fat Necrosis**
    
    - **Definition**: Focal areas of **fat destruction**, occurring mainly at fat-rich anatomical locations.
    - **Types**:
        - **Enzymatic Fat Necrosis**: Occurs in adipose tissue around an **acutely inflamed pancreas** (e.g., acute pancreatitis).
            - **Mechanism**: Liberation of pancreatic lipases from injured or inflamed tissue. Lipase splits triglyceride esters, liberating free fatty acids that combine with calcium to form **calcium soaps** (fat saponification).
            - **Gross Morphology**: Appears as **chalky-white areas**.
            - **Microscopic Morphology**: Necrosed fat cells have a cloudy shadowy appearance. Foci of calcium soaps are identified as amorphous, granular, and basophilic material, surrounded by an inflammatory reaction.
        - **Traumatic Fat Necrosis**: Occurs in tissues with high fat content (e.g., breast, thigh) following severe trauma.
5. **Fibrinoid Necrosis**
    
    - **Definition**: Characterised by the deposition of **fibrin-like proteinaceous material** in the tissue matrix, which has the staining properties of fibrin and obscures underlying cellular detail.
    - **Causes**: Encountered in various examples of **immunologic tissue injury** (e.g., immune complex vasculitis, autoimmune diseases), arterioles in **hypertension**, and peptic ulcer. Also seen in **Aschoff bodies** in rheumatic heart disease.
    - **Microscopic Morphology**: Appears as brightly **eosinophilic, hyaline-like deposition** in the vessel wall. Often surrounded by nuclear debris of neutrophils (leucocytoclasis). Best appreciated with phosphotungstic acid haematoxylin (PTAH) stain.
6. **Gangrene (Gangrenous Necrosis)**
    
    - **Definition**: **Massive necrosis with superadded putrefaction**, most often following coagulative necrosis due to ischaemia.
    - **Types**:
        - **Dry Gangrene**:
            - **Causes**: Arterial occlusion (e.g., severe atherosclerosis, Buerger's disease).
            - **Sites**: Begins in the **distal part of a limb**, usually the lower limb (foot, toes).
            - **Gross Morphology**: Affected part is **dry, shrunken, and dark brown or black** ("mummy foot"). A **clear line of demarcation** is present between the gangrenous and viable tissue.
            - **Microscopic Morphology**: Predominantly **coagulative necrosis**. Shows blurring and smudging of cell outlines. The line of demarcation consists of granulation tissue.
        - **Wet Gangrene**:
            - **Causes**: Due to venous blockage (e.g., strangulated hernia, intussusception, volvulus) or in naturally moist tissues and organs (e.g., gangrene bowel, lungs). Also seen in diabetic foot and bed sores.
            - **Gross Morphology**: Affected part is **soft, swollen, putrid, and dark**. **No clear line of demarcation** is distinct.
            - **Microscopic Morphology**: Predominantly **liquefactive necrosis**. Bacterial colonies and thrombi in vessel walls may be identified.
        - **Gas Gangrene**: A special form of wet gangrene caused by gas-forming _Clostridium perfringens_ (Gram-positive anaerobic bacteria).

### Clinical Relevance

- **Enzyme Markers**: Necrosis causes leakage of intracellular proteins into circulation, allowing detection of tissue-specific damage. For instance, elevated serum levels of **cardiac troponins (cTn), creatine kinase (CK-MB), SGOT, and LDH** are useful guides for heart muscle death in myocardial infarction.
- **Prognosis**: The type and extent of necrosis can determine prognosis, as seen in the severe outcomes of massive brain infarction or widespread wet gangrene.
- **Differentiation from Apoptosis**: Critical for diagnosis and understanding disease processes (e.g., apoptosis can be physiological, involves single cells, no inflammation; necrosis is always pathological, involves tissue, and causes inflammation).
    - **Apoptosis**: "Programmed cell death". "Falling off" of fragments (apoptotic bodies). Cell shrinkage, nuclear condensation and fragmentation, cytoplasmic blebbing. Affects single cells or small clusters. **No inflammatory reaction**.
    - **Necrosis**: "Unregulated cell death". Digestion of tissue by hydrolytic enzymes. Cell swelling, membrane damage. Affects groups of cells or tissue. **Always accompanied by inflammatory reaction**.
2. Pathological calcification
Pathological calcification is the **abnormal deposition of calcium salts** in tissues other than osteoid or enamel. This process is often accompanied by the deposition of small amounts of iron, magnesium, and other minerals. It presents in two distinct forms: **dystrophic calcification** and **metastatic calcification**.

---

### **1. Dystrophic Calcification**

- **Definition**: Deposition of calcium salts in **dying or dead tissues**. It occurs with **normal** calcium metabolism and **normal** serum calcium levels.
- **Causes & Examples**:
    - **Necrotic Tissue**:
        - **Caseous necrosis** (e.g., in tuberculosis, chronic inflammation). Grossly resembles dry cheese, soft, granular, and yellowish.
        - **Enzymatic fat necrosis** (e.g., in acute pancreatitis). Appears as chalky-white areas.
        - Dead parasites (e.g., hydatid cyst, Schistosoma eggs, cysticercosis).
    - **Degenerating Tissue**:
        - Aging or damaged **heart valves** (e.g., calcific aortic stenosis).
        - **Atherosclerosis**, especially in advanced atheromatous plaques in aorta and coronaries.
        - **Mönckeberg’s medial calcific sclerosis**: Calcification exclusively in the **tunica media** of muscular arteries in old age, producing rigid "pipestem-like" tubes without narrowing the lumen. No significant inflammation is seen.
        - Goitre of the thyroid.
        - Dense old scars, and walls of long-standing cysts (e.g., epidermal and pilar cysts).
        - Thrombi (e.g., phleboliths in veins).
    - **Psammoma Bodies**: Concentric calcified spherules that develop from mineral deposition on single necrotic cells. These are characteristic in certain tumours, including:
        - **Papillary carcinoma of thyroid**.
        - Papillary serous cystadenoma/cystadenocarcinoma of ovary.
        - Meningioma.
        - Papillary carcinoma of the kidney.
- **Mechanism**: Involves increased binding of phosphates with necrotic and degenerated tissue, which then binds to calcium to form calcium phosphate precipitates. Injured mitochondria play a role in calcium uptake.
- **Morphology**:
    - **Gross**: Appears as **fine, white granules or clumps**, feeling gritty and sand-like. In incised vessels with atherosclerosis, the diseased intima may "crack like an egg-shell".
    - **Microscopic**: Calcium salts appear as **deeply basophilic, irregular, and granular clumps**. They can be intracellular, extracellular, or both. In complicated atheromatous plaques, deposits are seen in the necrotic area and soft lipid pool.
    - **Special Stains**:
        - **von-Kossa stain** produces a black colour.
        - Alizarin red S produces a red staining.
        - Perl’s stain (Prussian blue reaction) can demonstrate accompanying iron deposits.
- **Clinical Relevance**: Can cause **organ dysfunction** (e.g., calcification of heart valves leading to stenosis).

---

### **2. Metastatic Calcification**

- **Definition**: Deposition of calcium salts in **apparently normal tissues**. It is invariably associated with **deranged calcium metabolism and hypercalcaemia**.
- **Causes**: Conditions that lead to **hypercalcaemia**:
    - **Increased Parathyroid Hormone (PTH) secretion** (e.g., primary hyperparathyroidism).
    - **Destruction of bone tissue** (e.g., primary bone tumours, multiple myeloma, leukaemia, metastatic tumours to bone).
    - Hypervitaminosis D.
    - Idiopathic hypercalcaemia of infancy (Williams syndrome).
    - Renal causes (e.g., renal tubular acidosis).
    - Excessive intestinal absorption of calcium.
    - Excessive mobilisation of calcium from bones.
    - Less common causes include milk-alkali syndrome and sarcoidosis.
- **Sites**: Although it can occur in any normal tissue, it preferentially affects organs that lose acid or have rapid pH changes:
    - **Kidneys**: Especially in the tubular epithelial cells, basement membrane, and tubular lumina, leading to **nephrocalcinosis**.
    - **Lungs**: In the alveolar walls and septa.
    - **Stomach**: On the acid-secreting fundal glands and interstitial tissues of the gastric mucosa.
    - **Blood vessels**: On the internal elastic lamina of systemic arteries and pulmonary veins.
    - Cornea.
    - Synovium of the joint.
- **Pathogenesis**: Occurs due to excessive binding of inorganic phosphate ions with elevated calcium ions, forming calcium phosphate precipitates.
- **Morphology**: Grossly, appears as fine, white granules or clumps. Histologically, similar to dystrophic calcification, calcium salts appear as deeply basophilic, irregular, granular clumps. Deposits in the kidney in nephrocalcinosis are seen in tubular epithelial cells.
- **Clinical Relevance**: Can lead to symptoms such as **renal colic, band keratopathy** (calcium deposits in cornea), visceral metastatic calcification, polyuria, and renal failure. Unlike dystrophic calcification, it **does not primarily cause organ dysfunction** through the calcification itself, but rather indicates an underlying metabolic derangement.

---

### **3. Key Differences: Dystrophic vs. Metastatic Calcification**

| :------------------------ | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Tissue Affected**       | **Dead or degenerated tissues**                                                                                                                        | **Normal, living tissues**                                                                   |
| **Calcium Metabolism**    | **Normal**                                                                                                                                             | **Deranged**                                                                                 |

2. Differences between necrosis and apoptosis
Both **necrosis** and **apoptosis** are forms of cell death, but they differ significantly in their mechanisms, morphological features, and physiological or pathological contexts.

### Necrosis

- **Definition**: A **localised area of death of tissue** in a living organism. It is the sum of morphological changes indicating cell death in living tissue, caused by progressive degradation by hydrolytic enzymes released from dead cells.
- **Nature**: Considered an "**accidental**" and unregulated form of cell death. It results from severe damage to cell membranes and loss of ion homeostasis.
- **Inflammatory Response**: **Always accompanied by an inflammatory reaction** in the surrounding tissue.
- **Mechanism**: Involves cell death by **ATP depletion, membrane damage, and free radical injury**. Lysosomal membranes are damaged, leading to the escape and activation of hydrolytic enzymes that digest cellular components.

### Apoptosis

- **Definition**: A form of "**coordinated and internally programmed cell death**". It is a tightly regulated "**cell suicide**" program.
- **Nature**: A controlled and regulated process.
- **Inflammatory Response**: **Not accompanied by any inflammation** or collateral tissue damage.
- **Mechanism**: Triggered by intrinsic (mitochondrial) or extrinsic (death receptor-initiated) pathways, leading to the activation of **caspases**. This results in the degradation of the cell's own nuclear DNA and proteins. Mitochondria play a pivotal role.

### Differences Between Apoptosis and Necrosis

| Feature                | Apoptosis                                                                                                                                                                                                                             | Necrosis                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| :--------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Definition**         | **Programmed and coordinated cell death**                                                                                                                                                                                             | **Cell death with degradation of tissue by hydrolytic enzymes**                                                                                                                                                                              |
| **Causative Agents**   | Physiologic and pathologic processes (e.g., loss of normal cell survival signals, low-dose injurious agents, viral infections)                                                                                                        | Hypoxia, toxins, chemical and physical agents, microbial agents, immunological injury (severe, often uncontrollable injury)                                                                                                                  |
| **Involvement**        | **Death of single cells** or small clusters of cells                                                                                                                                                                                  | **Death of many adjacent cells**                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| **Cell Size**          | Cell **shrinkage**                                                                                                                                                                                                                    | Cell **swelling initially**                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| **Cell Membrane**      | Cytoplasmic **blebs** on membrane; membranes remain intact initially.                                                                                                                                                                 | **Membrane disruption**                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| **Organelles**         | Lysosomes and other organelles **intact**                                                                                                                                                                                             | Damaged organelles, **lysosomal breakdown** with liberation of hydrolytic enzymes                                                                                                                                                            |
| **Nuclear Changes**    | **Chromatin condensation** (pyknosis); followed by nuclear fragmentation (karyorrhexis).                                                                                                                                              | Nuclear disruption, pyknosis (shrinking), karyorrhexis (fragmentation), and karyolysis (dissolution).                                                                                                                                        |
| **Inflammation**       | **No inflammatory reaction**                                                                                                                                                                                                          | **Inflammatory reaction always present**                                                                                                                                                                                                     |

2. Pigments in Health and disease
	Pigments are coloured substances that can be either normal constituents of cells or abnormal accumulations within them. Understanding their presence and alterations is crucial in diagnostic pathology.
	
	### I. Endogenous Pigments
	
	These are synthesised within the body, often derived from normal metabolic processes.
	
	2. **Melanin**
	    
	    - **Origin**: **Brown-black, non-haemoglobin-derived pigment** produced by melanocytes through oxidation of tyrosine by tyrosinase. Stored as cytoplasmic granules in melanophores.
	    - **Normal Location**: Hair, skin, mucosa (some places), choroid of the eye, meninges, and adrenal medulla.
	    - **Disorders of Pigmentation**:
	        - **Generalised Hyperpigmentation**:
	            - **Addison's disease**.
	            - **Chloasma** (during pregnancy on face, nipples, genitalia).
	            - Chronic arsenical poisoning ("raindrop pigmentation").
	        - **Generalised Hypopigmentation**:
	            - **Albinism** (genetic deficiency of tyrosinase).
	        - **Localised Hyperpigmentation**: (e.g., freckles, lentigo, benign naevi, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation).
	        - **Localised Hypopigmentation**: (e.g., vitiligo, piebaldism).
	    - **Special Stains**:
	        - **DOPA reaction**: Used for differentiating amelanotic melanoma from other anaplastic tumours by demonstrating melanin granules in tumour cells.
	        - **Fontana-Masson stain**: Reveals melanin granules in the cytoplasm of tumour cells, even if not evident on H&E.
	    - **Clinical Relevance**: **Malignant melanoma** (melanocarcinoma) arises from melanocytes, appearing as a flat or elevated naevus with variegated pigmentation and irregular borders . Melanoma cells may be melanotic (pigmented) or amelanotic (unpigmented), the latter still staining positive for HMB-45, S-100, and Melan-A immunohistochemically.
	3. **Haemoprotein-derived Pigments**
	    
	    - Derived from haemoglobin, cytochromes, and their breakdown products.
	    - **Haemosiderin**:
	        - **Origin**: **Golden-yellow pigment granules** containing ferritin and haemosiderin, resulting from local or systemic excess of iron.
	        - **Causes of Accumulation**:
	            - **Local excesses**:
	                - **Bruises** (changing colours due to biliverdin/bilirubin formation).
	                - **Brown induration of lungs** (in chronic venous congestion/left heart failure, where alveolar macrophages ("heart failure cells") phagocytose haemosiderin from microhaemorrhages).
	            - **Systemic excesses (Haemosiderosis)**: Due to increased dietary iron absorption, excessive red cell destruction (haemolytic anaemias), or repeated blood transfusions.
	        - **Microscopy**: Coarse, golden, granular pigment in cytoplasm.
	        - **Special Stain**: **Perl's Prussian blue reaction** (converts ferric ions to blue-black ferric ferrocyanide). Useful in diagnosing iron overload.
	        - **Clinical Relevance**: Severe accumulation can lead to **Haemochromatosis**, characterised by damage to liver (micronodular cirrhosis, chocolate-brown colour), pancreas (diffuse interstitial fibrosis, atrophy of cells leading to **diabetes mellitus**), and skin (bronze pigmentation), forming the triad of "**bronze diabetes**".
	    - **Acid Haematin (Haemozoin)**:
	        - **Origin**: Brown-black pigment containing haem in ferric form.
	        - **Types**:
	            - **Malarial pigment**: Seen in chronic malaria, deposited in macrophages and hepatocytes. **Negative for Perl's Prussian blue stain**.
	            - **Formalin pigment**: Formed in blood-rich tissues preserved in acidic formalin solution.
	        - **Microscopy**: Granular, brown-black pigment.
	    - **Bilirubin**:
	        - **Origin**: Normal, **non-iron containing pigment** from porphyrin ring of haem moiety of haemoglobin.
	        - **Clinical Relevance**: Increased levels in blood cause **jaundice** (icterus). Can be unconjugated (indirect) or conjugated (direct), rising due to increased production, hepatocellular disease, or obstruction in excretion [23, 94, 582t, 583-585].
	4. **Lipofuscin (Wear and Tear Pigment)**
	    
	    - **Origin**: **Yellowish-brown intracellular lipid pigment** (lipochrome). Expression of residual bodies, likely derived from **lipid peroxidation** of subcellular membranes.
	    - **Clinical Relevance**: Often found in atrophied cells of old age (hence "wear and tear pigment"). Can also accumulate rapidly in wasting diseases. Seen in myocardial fibres, hepatocytes, Leydig cells of testes, and neurons in senile dementia. Associated with **brown atrophy** of the heart due to myocardial wasting.
	    - **Microscopy**: Coarse, golden-brown granular pigment, often perinuclear.
	    - **Staining**: Stains with fat stains, fluorescent, and has positive acid-fast staining.
	
	### II. Exogenous Pigments
	
	These are introduced into the body from external sources.
	
	5. **Carbon (Anthracotic Pigment)**:
	    
	    - **Origin**: **Inhaled dust** (e.g., carbon dust, coal dust). Most common exogenous pigment.
	    - **Clinical Relevance**: Leads to **pneumoconiosis** (e.g., coal-workers' pneumoconiosis). Deposition in the lung is common, benign, and asymptomatic in most urban dwellers.
	    - **Microscopy**: Black pigment, often found in macrophages and connective tissue along lymphatic pathways.
	6. **Tattooing**:
	    
	    - **Origin**: Injected insoluble metallic and vegetable pigments.
	    - **Persistence**: Phagocytosed by dermal macrophages, remaining stable in the dermis for life.
	7. **Other Ingested Pigments**:
	    
	    - **Argyria**: Generalised grey-blue discoloration of the skin and internal organs due to prolonged ingestion of silver compounds.
	    - **Arsenic**: Chronic arsenical poisoning can cause "raindrop pigmentation of the skin".
3. Metaplasia? What is it ? Significance? Types with examples
	### Metaplasia
	
	**Definition**
	
	- **Metaplasia** is defined as a **reversible change** where **one adult cell type is replaced by another adult cell type**.
	- It typically occurs in response to **abnormal stimuli** (e.g., chronic irritation, tissue damage, repair, regeneration).
	- This is a form of **cellular adaptation**, allowing cells to survive and function in an altered environment, though often at the expense of normal function.
	
	**Significance**
	
	- **Reversibility**: Metaplasia is usually a **fully reversible adaptive response**; if the noxious stimulus is removed (e.g., cessation of smoking), the metaplastic epithelium may return to normal.
	- **Protection**: The replacing cell type is usually **more suited to the altered environment**. For example, a stronger, less specialised epithelium might replace a delicate one.
	- **Increased Risk of Malignancy**:
	    - If the abnormal stimulus persists for a long time, **epithelial metaplasia may progress to dysplasia and further into cancer**.
	    - The metaplastic epithelium, being less specialised (e.g., squamous type), can lead to deprivation of protective functions (e.g., mucus secretion), making it **more prone to infection**.
	
	**Types with Examples**
	
	Metaplasia is broadly divided into two types: **Epithelial** and **Mesenchymal**.
	
	1. **Epithelial Metaplasia**: This is the more common type.
	    
	    - **Squamous Metaplasia**: Replacement of specialised columnar or transitional epithelium by **stratified squamous epithelium**. Often due to chronic irritation (mechanical, chemical, or infective).
	        - **Bronchus**: In chronic smokers, normal pseudostratified columnar ciliated epithelium is replaced by squamous epithelium. (A classic example of pathological adaptation).
	        - **Uterine Cervix**: Associated with chronic infection in the endocervix, columnar epithelium can be replaced by squamous epithelium. This can progress to dysplasia and carcinoma _in situ_.
	        - **Renal Pelvis & Urinary Bladder**: Transitional epithelium replaced by squamous epithelium in chronic infection and stones.
	        - **Vitamin A Deficiency**: Squamous metaplasia can occur in the nose, bronchi, urinary tract, lacrimal, and salivary glands.
	        - **Skin**: Formation of skin warts (due to HPV) or pseudocarcinomatous hyperplasia at the margin of non-healing ulcers.
	        - **Breast**: Intraductal epithelial hyperplasia in fibrocystic change.
	    - **Columnar Metaplasia**: Transformation to columnar epithelium.
	        - **Barrett's Oesophagus**: Normal squamous epithelium of the oesophagus is replaced by columnar (intestinal-type) epithelium due to chronic acid reflux.
	        - **Stomach**: Intestinal metaplasia in healed chronic gastric ulcer (gastric glands replaced by intestinal-type cells).
	        - **Bronchiectasis/Chronic Bronchitis**: Pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium replaced by columnar type.
	        - **Cervical Erosion**: Endocervical glandular mucosa everted into the vagina.
	2. **Mesenchymal Metaplasia**: Less common, involves transformation of one adult mesenchymal tissue to another.
	    
	    - **Osseous Metaplasia**: Formation of bone in fibrous tissue, cartilage, or myxoid tissue.
	        - **Arterial Wall**: In old age, such as in Mönckeberg's medial calcific sclerosis, where calcium salts are deposited in the media of muscular arteries, potentially leading to bone formation.
	        - **Soft Tissues**: As seen in myositis ossificans (bone formation in muscle after trauma).
	        - **Cartilage**: In the larynx and bronchi of elderly individuals.
	        - **Scars**: In scars of prolonged chronic inflammation.
	        - **Tumour Stroma**: In the fibrous stroma of tumours, e.g., leiomyoma.
	    - **Cartilaginous Metaplasia**: Formation of cartilage. (Implied by type of mesenchymal metaplasia mentioned in source).
4. Psamomma bodies
	**Psammoma Bodies**
	
	- **Definition & Nature**:
	    
	    - A form of **dystrophic calcification**.
	    - Characterised by the progressive deposition of **multiple layers of mineral (calcium salts)** around a **single necrotic cell**.
	- **Morphological Features**:
	    
	    - Appear as **small, concentric, calcified spherules**.
	    - Often have a **lamellated shape**.
	- **Clinical Relevance (Key Associated Conditions)**:
	    
	    - **Papillary carcinoma of the thyroid**: Found in approximately half of these cases, appearing as typical small, concentric, calcified spherules in the stroma.
	    - **Papillary serous cystadenoma of the ovary**.
	    - **Papillary serous cystadenocarcinoma of the ovary**: While associated, their **mere presence is not indicative of malignancy**.
	    - **Meningioma**: Particularly seen in the transitional type, where they form within whorled patterns of tumour cells.
	    - **Papillary carcinoma of the kidney**.
5. Autophagy with examples
	
	**Autophagy**
	
	Autophagy, meaning "**self-eating**", is a fundamental cellular process with significant roles in both health and disease.
	
	**I. Definition & Mechanism**
	
	- **Definition**: A process where **lysosomal enzymes digest the cell's own components** during periods of stress.
	- **Mechanism**:
	    - It is a **survival mechanism**, particularly crucial during **severe nutrient deficiency**.
	    - Starved cells utilise autophagy to survive by **eating their own contents** (e.g., organelles, proteins, membranes) and **recycling** these components to provide nutrients and energy.
	    - If this adaptive mechanism fails, the cells may die.
	
	**II. Clinical Relevance: Autophagy in Cancer**
	
	- The role of autophagy in cancer development and progression is **complex**, acting as a "**double-edged sword**".
	- **Tumour Suppression**:
	    - Certain **autophagy genes** (e.g., Beclin-1 and some Atg genes) promote autophagy and act as **tumour suppressors**.
	    - These genes can be **deleted or mutated in many cancers**, suggesting that impaired autophagy can contribute to cancer development by allowing cells to grow unchecked.
	- **Tumour Survival/Resistance**:
	    - Cancer cells may **corrupt the autophagy process** to provide them with nutrients for continued growth and survival.
	    - Autophagy can **protect cancer cells** when they are deprived of nutrients or oxygen (e.g., due to therapy or insufficient blood supply).
	    - This implies that inhibiting autophagy might be a therapeutic strategy in some cancers, while enhancing it could be beneficial in others.
6. Fatty liver-causes and labelled diagram
	### Fatty Liver (Steatosis)
	
	**Definition**
	
	- **Fatty change (steatosis)** is the **intracellular accumulation of triglycerides** and other lipids within **parenchymal cells**, most commonly in the liver.
	- It is a **reversible** process if the underlying cause is addressed.
	
	**Causes (Etiology)** Fatty liver can result from conditions causing **excess fat** or **liver cell damage**.
	
	1. **Conditions with Excess Fat**:
	    
	    - **Obesity**.
	    - **Diabetes mellitus**.
	    - Congenital hyperlipidaemia.
	2. **Liver Cell Damage** (impaired fat metabolism):
	    
	    - **Alcoholic liver disease** (most common cause).
	    - **Starvation**.
	    - Protein calorie malnutrition.
	    - Chronic illnesses (e.g., tuberculosis).
	    - Acute fatty liver in late pregnancy.
	    - Hypoxia (e.g., anaemia, cardiac failure).
	    - **Hepatotoxins** (e.g., carbon tetrachloride, chloroform, ether, aflatoxins, other poisons).
	    - Drug-induced liver cell injury (e.g., methotrexate, steroids, halothane anaesthetic, tetracycline).
	    - Reye's syndrome.
	    - Choline deficiency.
	
	**Pathogenesis** Accumulation of triglycerides in hepatocytes occurs due to defects in normal fat metabolism. Key mechanisms include:
	
	3. **Increased entry of free fatty acids (FFAs)** into the liver (from diet or adipose tissue).
	4. Increased synthesis of fatty acids by the liver.
	5. **Decreased oxidation of fatty acids** into ketone bodies, leading to increased esterification to triglycerides.
	6. Increased alpha-glycerophosphate, promoting triglyceride formation.
	7. **Decreased synthesis of 'lipid acceptor protein'** (apoproteins), essential for lipoprotein formation.
	8. **Block in the excretion of lipoproteins** from the liver into plasma.
	    - _Clinical Relevance:_ In **chronic alcoholism**, the pathogenesis is multifactorial, involving increased lipolysis, increased FFA synthesis, decreased triglyceride utilisation/oxidation, and blocked lipoprotein excretion.
	
	**Morphology**
	
	- **Gross Appearance**:
	    
	    - The liver is typically **enlarged** with a **tense, glistening capsule** and rounded margins.
	    - The cut surface bulges slightly and is **pale-yellow to yellow** and **greasy to touch**.
	    - Can be severe, producing irreversible cell injury and cell death.
	- **Microscopic Appearance**:
	    
	    - Characterised by **numerous lipid vacuoles in the cytoplasm of hepatocytes**.
	    - Fat is dissolved during routine paraffin embedding (H&E sections) and appears as **non-staining clear vacuoles**.
	    - **Microvesicular Steatosis**: Initially, small vacuoles around the nucleus.
	    - **Macrovesicular Steatosis**: With progression, vacuoles enlarge, pushing the **nucleus to the periphery of the cell** (giving a **signet ring appearance**).
	    - Hepatocytes with large lipid vacuoles may rupture and coalesce to form **fatty cysts**.
	    - Infrequently, **lipogranulomas** (collections of lymphocytes, macrophages, giant cells) may appear in reaction to extravasated fat.
	    - _Special Stains for Fat_: To demonstrate fat, **frozen sections** of unfixed tissue are used with stains such as **Sudan dyes (Sudan III, IV, Sudan Black B)** or **Oil Red O** (imparting an orange-red or red colour). Osmic acid also stains fat black.

7. Marfan syndrome
	**Marfan Syndrome**
	
	- **Definition & Etiology**:
	    
	    - An **autosomal dominant disease**.
	    - Characterised by a **genetic defect in fibrillin**.
	    - Fibrillin is a **connective tissue protein** essential for elastic tissue formation.
	- **Clinical Relevance & Associations**:
	    
	    - **Cardiovascular System** (most critical involvement):
	        - Associated with **dissecting aneurysm** of the aorta. This occurs in non-hypertensive cases where there is an underlying connective tissue disorder like Marfan syndrome.
	        - Strongly associated with **myxomatous degeneration of the mitral valve** (also known as "floppy valve syndrome" or "mitral valve prolapse"). This association is observed in approximately **90% of patients** with myxomatous degeneration of the mitral valve.
	        - Can be a cause of **aortic insufficiency** (failure of the aortic valve to close completely).
