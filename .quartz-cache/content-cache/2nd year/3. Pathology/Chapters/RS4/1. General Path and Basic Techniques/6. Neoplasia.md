1. Metastasis (routes and spread of malignant tumors)
	**Metastasis (Routes and Spread of Malignant Tumours)**
	
	Metastasis is the process of **spread of tumour** by invasion, leading to the formation of **discontinuous secondary tumour masses** at distant sites. It is one of the cardinal features of malignant tumours, distinguishing them from benign neoplasms. Metastasis **greatly reduces patient survival** and is the most common cause of cancer death.
	
	**I. Factors Favouring Metastasis**:
		- **Larger, more aggressive, and rapidly-growing tumours** are generally more likely to metastasise.
		- **Poorly differentiated tumours** show aggressive growth patterns.
	
	**II. Pathways of Spread**: Malignant tumours can spread via three main pathways: lymphatic, haematogenous, and along body cavities/natural passages.
	
	A. **Lymphatic Spread**:
	
	- **Primary Route**: Most common pathway for **carcinomas**. Some sarcomas may also spread this way.
	- **Mechanism**:
	    - **Lymphatic Permeation**: Cancer cells invade lymphatic walls and may form continuous growth.
	    - **Lymphatic Emboli**: Malignant cells detach to form emboli, carried to regional draining lymph nodes.
	    - **Lodgement**: Tumour emboli typically lodge and grow in the **subcapsular sinus** of the lymph node.
	- **Regional Nodal Metastasis**: Regional lymph nodes draining the primary tumour are commonly involved (e.g., axillary nodes from breast carcinoma, hilar nodes from bronchogenic carcinoma).
	- **Clinical Relevance/Variations**:
	    - **Sentinel Lymph Node Biopsy**: Identification and dissection of the first draining lymph node (sentinel node) is crucial for assessing regional nodal status and prognosis, particularly in breast cancer and melanoma.
	    - **Skip Metastasis**: Lymph nodes distant from the primary site may be involved first, bypassing nearer nodes, due to venous-lymphatic anastomoses or obliteration of lymphatics.
	    - **Retrograde Metastasis**: Tumour cells spread against lymph flow, often due to lymphatic obstruction, causing metastases at unusual sites (e.g., prostate cancer to supraclavicular lymph nodes).
	    - **Virchow’s Lymph Node**: Preferential metastasis to the supraclavicular lymph node from abdominal cancers (e.g., stomach, colon).
	    - Not all lymph node enlargements are metastatic; reactive hyperplasia can occur.
	
	B. **Haematogenous Spread (Blood-borne)**:
	
	- **Primary Route**: Common for **sarcomas**, but also frequent in certain carcinomas (e.g., lung, breast, thyroid, kidney, liver, prostate, ovary).
	- **Vessel Invasion**: Tumour cells easily invade thin-walled **capillaries and venules**; thick-walled arteries are more resistant. Renal cell carcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma show a strong propensity for venous invasion.
	- **Common Sites of Metastasis ("Seed-Soil" Theory)**: Liver and lungs are the most frequently involved organs.
	    - **Liver**: Receives portal venous drainage (e.g., from bowel, pancreas, spleen).
	    - **Lungs**: Receive systemic venous drainage.
	    - Other common sites: **Brain**, **bones**, kidneys, and adrenals.
	    - **Bone Metastasis**: Vertebral column is a common site (e.g., from thyroid and prostate cancers) via paravertebral plexuses. Bone metastases can be osteolytic (radiolucent, e.g., lung cancer) or osteoblastic (radiodense, e.g., prostate cancer).
	- **Resistant Organs**: Spleen, heart, and skeletal muscle are generally resistant to tumour metastasis.
	- **Retrograde Spread**: Occurs at unusual sites due to venous obstruction.
	- **Gross Appearance**: Typically appears as **multiple, rounded nodules of varying sizes** scattered throughout the organ. Liver metastases may show characteristic **umbilication**.
	- **Microscopic Appearance**: Secondary deposits generally **reproduce the structure of the primary tumour**.
	
	C. **Spread along Body Cavities and Natural Passages**:
	
	- **Transcoelomic Spread (Seeding)**: Cancers invade through serosal walls of coelomic cavities (peritoneum, pleura, pericardium), where fragments detach and implant elsewhere (e.g., ovarian, GI tract, lung cancers). **Pseudomyxoma peritonei** is a notable example involving abundant mucin in the peritoneal cavity.
	- **Epithelium-lined Surfaces**: Rare, as intact epithelium resists penetration. Exceptions include spread via fallopian tubes (endometrium to ovaries), bronchi to alveoli, or ureters.
	- **Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF)**: Malignant tumours of ependyma and leptomeninges may shed cells into CSF, producing metastases in other CNS sites (e.g., medulloblastomas, ependymomas, leukaemias, lymphomas, adenocarcinomas).
	- **Implantation**: Very rare, via surgical instruments (scalpel, needles) or direct contact.
	
	**III. Mechanism and Biology of Invasion and Metastasis (Metastatic Cascade)**: This is a complex, multi-step process involving interaction between tumour cells and the extracellular matrix (ECM).
	
	1. **Aggressive Clonal Proliferation and Angiogenesis**: Development of rapidly proliferating tumour cell clones. New, vulnerable blood vessels (angiogenesis) are essential for growth and invasion.
	2. **Loosening of Tumour Cells**: Loss of cell-to-cell adhesion (e.g., reduced **E-cadherin** expression) allows cells to detach.
	3. **Degradation of ECM**: Tumour cells overexpress proteases and matrix-degrading enzymes (e.g., **metalloproteinases**, collagenases, gelatinase), which break down the ECM. Cleavage products can also promote tumour growth and chemotaxis.
	4. **Attachment to ECM Components**: Tumour cells attach to new ECM sites.
	5. **Migration/Locomotion**: Tumour cells move through the degraded ECM.
	6. **Intravasation**: Tumour cells penetrate the basement membrane of blood vessels or lymphatic channels to enter circulation.
	7. **Survival in Circulation and Tumour Emboli Formation**: Circulating tumour cells are susceptible to mechanical stress and immune attack. Survival is enhanced by forming aggregates with platelets.
	8. **Extravasation**: Tumour cells exit the circulation by attaching to vascular endothelium and moving into extravascular space.
	9. **Survival and Growth of Metastatic Deposit**: Lodgement in a favourable environment, where host and tumour-derived growth factors (e.g., PDGF, FGF, TGF-β, VEGF) promote growth.
	
	**IV. Exceptions (Malignant Tumours that Rarely Metastasise)**:
	
	- **Gliomas of the central nervous system (CNS)**.
	- **Basal cell carcinoma of the skin**.

	1. Chemical carciogenesis
	**Chemical Carcinogenesis**
	
	**I. Definition & General Concept**:
	
	- **Carcinogenesis** (or oncogenesis/tumorigenesis) is the **mechanism of induction of tumours**.
	- A **carcinogen** is an agent known or suspected to cause tumours.
	- The first evidence linking a chemical to cancer was in **1775** by **Sir Percival Pott**, who observed scrotal skin cancer in chimney sweeps due to chronic soot exposure.
	- The first **experimental induction of cancer** was achieved in **1914** by Yamagiwa and Ichikawa, using coal tar on rabbit skin.
	
	**II. Classification of Chemical Carcinogens**: Chemical carcinogens are categorised into two main types:
	
	- **Direct-Acting Carcinogens**:
	    - These chemicals can induce cellular transformation **without prior metabolic activation**.
	    - Examples include **alkylating agents** and **acylating agents**.
	- **Indirect-Acting Carcinogens (Procarcinogens)**:
	    - These compounds **require metabolic conversion** within the body to become "ultimate carcinogens".
	    - **Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons** (e.g., benzo[a]pyrene in cigarette smoke, soot, coal tar) are the most potent and extensively studied indirect-acting carcinogens.
	    - Other examples: **Aromatic amines** and **azo dyes** (e.g., benzidine linked to bladder cancer; azo dyes to hepatocellular carcinoma), and **naturally-occurring products** (e.g., **aflatoxin B1** from _Aspergillus flavus_, implicated in hepatocellular carcinoma, especially with co-existent hepatitis B).
	
	**III. Mechanism of Chemical Carcinogenesis (Multistep Process)**: Chemical carcinogenesis is a complex, multi-step process, and once initiated, the tumour process **does not require the continued presence of the carcinogen**.
	
	1. **Metabolic Activation**:
	    - The **vast majority** of chemical carcinogens are **indirect-acting** and require metabolic activation.
	    - This occurs primarily in the **liver** via **mono-oxygenases of the cytochrome P-450 system** in the endoplasmic reticulum.
	    - The **carcinogenic potency** depends on the balance between activation and inactivation reactions, and the genes coding for cytochrome P-450-dependent enzymes.
	2. **Reactive Electrophiles**:
	    - Both direct-acting carcinogens and activated indirect-acting carcinogens become **highly reactive electrophilic groups**.
	    - These electrophiles then **bind to electron-rich portions** of other cellular molecules, primarily **DNA**, but also RNA and other proteins.
	3. **Target Molecules & Mutagenesis**:
	    - The **primary target** of electrophiles is **DNA**, leading to **mutagenesis**.
	    - The damage to DNA may be repaired by cellular enzymes; however, if unrepaired, it can lead to an "initiated cell".
	    - A classic example of defective DNA repair leading to cancer predisposition is **xeroderma pigmentosum**.
	    - The most frequently affected genes by chemical carcinogens are the **RAS oncogene** (a growth promoter) and the **p53 gene** (a tumour suppressor).
	4. **The Initiated Cell**:
	    - The unrepaired and permanent **DNA damage** becomes fixed **only if the altered cell undergoes at least one cycle of proliferation**.
	    - This transfers the permanent, irreversible change to the progeny of cells, creating an "initiated cell" that is **vulnerable to promoters**.

**IV. Initiator Carcinogens vs. Promoter Carcinogens**:

|Feature|**Initiator Carcinogens**|**Promoter Carcinogens**|
|:--|:--|:--|
|**Mechanism**|**Induction of mutation**|**Not mutagenic**; stimulate proliferation of initiated cells|
|**Dose**|Single for a short time|Repeated dose exposure, for a long time|
|**Response**|Sudden response|Slow response|
|**Change**|**Permanent, irreversible**|**May be reversible**|
|**Sequence**|**Applied first**, then followed by promoter|**Applied after** prior exposure to initiator; ineffective alone|
|**Effectivity**|Effective alone if exposed in large dose|Not effective alone|
|**Molecular Changes**|Most common mutation of **RAS oncogene, p53 anti-oncogene**|**Clonal expansion** of mutated cells|
|**Examples**|Most chemical carcinogens, radiation|Hormones, phorbol esters|

**V. Tests for Chemical Carcinogenicity**:

- The **Ames' test** is a common _in vitro_ method to evaluate a chemical's ability to induce mutation (mutagenicity).
- It uses a mutant strain of _Salmonella typhimurium_ that cannot synthesise histidine.
- If the test chemical is mutagenic, it will induce mutations, leading to the growth of bacterial colonies on histidine-free culture medium.
- Most carcinogenic chemicals test positive in the Ames' test.

3. Microbial carcinogenesis (biological carcinogenesis)
	**Microbial (Biological) Carcinogenesis**
	
	**I. Definition & General Concept**:
	
	- **Biological carcinogens** are transmissible biological agents implicated in tumour development, chiefly microorganisms.
	- It is a major category of carcinogens, alongside chemical and physical agents.
	
	**II. Classification of Biological Carcinogens**: Biological carcinogenesis involves various microbial agents, including viruses, bacteria, parasites, and fungi.
	
	**III. Viral Oncogenesis (Viral Carcinogenesis)**:
	
	- **Most significant** among biological carcinogens.
	- **General Aspects**:
	    - **Epidemiologic studies** provide strong support for the etiologic role of oncogenic viruses.
	    - Involves the **persistence of viral DNA** in the host genome and **expression of specific transforming viral genes**.
	    - Viral infection alone is usually **not sufficient** for oncogenesis; it is one step in a **multistep process** of cancer development.
	    - Generally, continued presence of DNA or RNA viruses causes activation of growth-promoting pathways or inhibition of tumour-suppressor products in infected cells.
	- **Mode of DNA Viral Oncogenesis**:
	    - Infected host cells may either undergo viral replication (leading to cell lysis) or **viral DNA integration** into the host cell DNA.
	    - **Integration** is essential for neoplastic transformation, requiring the expression of **virus-specific T-(transforming protein) antigens**.
	- **Specific DNA Oncogenic Viruses**:
	    1. **Human Papillomavirus (HPV)**:
	        - **Infects immature squamous cells**; replication occurs in maturing cells.
	        - **Types & Associated Lesions**:
	            - **Low-risk types (e.g., HPV 6, 11)**: Associated with anogenital warts (condyloma acuminatum) and low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (L-SIL/CIN-1). These types usually remain **episomal** (non-integrated).
	            - **High-risk types (e.g., HPV 16, 18)**: Strongly implicated in **cervical cancer** (70-100% of cases), anogenital cancers, and oropharyngeal cancers. These types **integrate into the host genome**.
	        - **Mechanism of Oncogenesis (High-risk HPV)**: Integration leads to **overexpression of viral genes E6 and E7**.
	            - **E6 protein**: **Inactivates tumour suppressor gene p53** (by degrading it) and stimulates **telomerase** expression (leading to immortalisation of cells).
	            - **E7 protein**: **Inactivates tumour suppressor gene RB-1** and other cell cycle regulators, promoting uncontrolled cell proliferation.
	    2. **Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV)**:
	        - **Associated Human Cancers**: **Burkitt's lymphoma** (African endemic form, 90% EBV-positive), **nasopharyngeal carcinoma** (virtually all cases, prevalent in South-East Asia), and some B-cell lymphomas.
	        - **Mechanism of Oncogenesis**: EBV infects B lymphocytes via CD21 receptor. Latent infection immortalises B cells.
	            - Viral oncogenes **LMP1** and **EBNA-2** are crucial.
	            - **LMP1**: Dysregulates cell proliferation and survival, activates growth signalling pathways (e.g., _NF-kB_, JAK/STAT), and **activates BCL2** to prevent apoptosis.
	            - **EBNA-2**: Activates cyclin D, promoting cell proliferation.
	            - EBV also evades host immune attack.
	    3. **Hepadnaviruses (Hepatitis B Virus - HBV)**:
	        - **Associated Human Cancer**: **Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)**. HBV (a DNA virus) and HCV (an RNA virus) are involved in 70-80% of HCC worldwide.
	        - **Epidemiological Evidence**: High incidence of HCC in regions with high HBV prevalence (e.g., Far-East, Africa). HBV-infected cases have a significantly higher risk (approx. 200x) of developing HCC.
	        - **Mechanism (Multifactorial)**: Chronic persistent HBV infection leads to **repetitive cycles of inflammation, hepatocyte degeneration/death, and regeneration**, causing DNA damage.
	            - HBV genome contains the **HBx gene**, whose protein activates growth signalling pathways and **inactivates p53**.
	            - HBV DNA can integrate into the human genome, causing mutations and deletions.
	    4. **Human Herpesvirus Type 8 (HHV-8 / Kaposi's Sarcoma-associated Herpesvirus - KSHV)**:
	        - **Associated Human Cancers**: **Kaposi's sarcoma** and pleural effusion B cell lymphoma.
	        - **Mechanism**: Viral DNA is found in tumour cells. Overexpression of KSHV oncoproteins (e.g., v-cyclin, v-IRF, LANA) increases host cell proliferation and survival. HHV-8 can also downregulate MHC-I expression, enabling immune evasion.
	    5. **Adenoviruses & Poxviruses**:
	        - **Adenoviruses**: Not known to cause human tumours.
	        - **Poxviruses**: Cause molluscum contagiosum (a benign lesion) in humans.
	- **Mode of RNA Viral Oncogenesis (Retroviruses)**:
	    - RNA viruses (retroviruses) contain RNA and **reverse transcriptase**.
	    - Reverse transcriptase synthesises a single viral DNA strand from the RNA template, then a complementary DNA strand, forming **double-stranded viral DNA (provirus)**.
	    - The **provirus integrates into the host cell genome**, inducing mutation and transforming the cell.
	    - **Slow transforming viruses**: Cause neoplastic transformation by **insertional mutagenesis**, where viral DNA integrates near proto-oncogenes, damaging the host cell genome.
	- **Specific RNA Oncogenic Viruses**:
	    1. **Human T-cell Leukaemia Virus Type 1 (HTLV-1)**:
	        - **Associated Human Cancer**: **Adult T-cell Leukaemia/Lymphoma (ATLL)**.
	        - **Mode of Infection**: Sexual intercourse, blood products, breastfeeding.
	        - **Mechanism**: HTLV-1 genome contains the unique **pX region**, encoding viral oncoproteins **TAX** and **REX**.
	            - **TAX protein**: Upregulates expression of cellular genes controlling T-cell replication, interacts with _NF-kB_ to activate cytokine genes (e.g., interleukins), and inactivates tumour suppressor genes (_CDKN2A/p16_, _p53_), stimulating the cell cycle. This leads to polyclonal, then monoclonal, T-cell proliferation.
	    2. **Hepatitis C Virus (HCV)**:
	        - **Associated Human Cancer**: **Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)**.
	        - **Mechanism**: Similar to HBV, involves chronic persistent infection leading to inflammation, cell death, and regeneration. HCV core protein may activate growth-promoting pathways. Associated with lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma.
	
	**IV. Bacterial Carcinogenesis**:
	
	- **Helicobacter pylori**:
	    - **Associated Human Cancers**: **Gastric adenocarcinomas** and **gastric lymphomas (MALTomas)**.
	    - **Mechanism**:
	        - **Gastric Adenocarcinoma**: H. pylori causes **chronic inflammation (chronic gastritis)**, leading to gastric atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, and eventually cancer. Strains with **cytotoxin-associated A (CagA) gene** initiate signals for unregulated growth factor stimulation.
	        - **Gastric Lymphoma (MALToma)**: H. pylori is linked to **t(11;18) translocation** in MALT lymphomas.
	
	**V. Parasitic Carcinogenesis**:
	
	- **Schistosoma haematobium**: Infection of the urinary bladder is associated with a high incidence of **squamous cell carcinoma of the urinary bladder**.
	- **Clonorchis sinensis (Chinese liver fluke)**: Implicated in the causation of **cholangiocarcinoma** (bile duct cancer).
	
	**VI. Fungal Carcinogenesis**:
	
	- **Aspergillus flavus**: This fungus grows in stored grains and liberates **aflatoxin B1**.
	- **Associated Human Cancer**: **Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)**, especially when concomitant **viral hepatitis B** is present.
	
	**VII. Clinical Relevance & Key Points**:
	
	- The understanding of microbial carcinogenesis has led to the development of **specific vaccines** (e.g., Hepatitis B vaccines to lower HBV-related HCC, HPV vaccines against cervical cancer) and targeted therapies.
	- **Persistent chronic infection** and inflammation are common underlying themes in microbial carcinogenesis, providing a fertile ground for genetic mutations and uncontrolled cell proliferation.
	- Many microbial carcinogens act by affecting key cellular regulatory genes, such as **proto-oncogenes (e.g., RAS, MYC)** and **tumour suppressor genes (e.g., p53, RB)**.

4. Paraneoplastic syndrome
	**Paraneoplastic Syndromes (PNS)**
	
	**I. Definition & Concept**:
	
	- **Symptom complexes** in cancer patients.
	- **Key Distinction**: These symptoms are **not explained by**:
	    - Direct tumour mass effects
	    - Local or distant spread (metastasis) of the tumour
	    - The usual hormone elaboration by the tissue of origin of the tumour
	
	**II. Frequency & Clinical Significance**:
	
	- Occur in **10-15% of patients with advanced cancer**.
	- **High Yield Clinical Relevance**:
	    - May be the **earliest manifestation of a latent (occult) cancer**, allowing for earlier diagnosis.
	    - Can be **mistaken for metastatic disease**, leading to inappropriate treatment.
	    - May present **severe clinical problems**, occasionally fatal.
	    - Certain PNS-causing tumour products can be useful in **monitoring recurrence** after treatment.
	
	**III. Pathogenesis/Mechanisms**:
	
	- Malignant tumours produce a **variety of biologically active substances**, including:
	    - **Ectopic hormones** or hormone-like substances
	    - Cytokines
	    - Autoantibodies (though not explicitly stated for all PNS, it's a general mechanism for some).
	
	**IV. Major Types and Associated Cancers**:
	
	1. **Endocrine Syndromes (Ectopic Hormone Production)**:
	    
	    - **Hypercalcaemia**:
	        - Most common PNS.
	        - Mechanism: Elaboration of **parathormone-like substance**.
	        - Associated Cancers: **Squamous cell carcinoma of the lung**, **carcinoma kidney**, **breast**, **adult T-cell leukaemia lymphoma**.
	    - **Cushing’s Syndrome**:
	        - Mechanism: Elaboration of **ACTH or ACTH-like substance**.
	        - Associated Cancers: Most often **small cell carcinoma of the lung** (10% of cases), **pancreatic carcinoma**, **neurogenic tumours**.
	    - **Polycythaemia**:
	        - Mechanism: Secretion of **erythropoietin**.
	        - Associated Cancers: **Renal cell carcinoma**, **hepatocellular carcinoma**, **cerebellar haemangioma**.
	    - **Syndrome of Inappropriate ADH (SIADH) / Hyponatraemia**:
	        - Mechanism: Elaboration of **ADH**.
	        - Associated Cancers: **Small cell carcinoma of the lung**.
	    - **Hypoglycaemia**:
	        - Mechanism: Elaboration of **insulin-like substance**.
	        - Associated Cancers: **Fibrosarcomas**, **islet cell tumours of pancreas**, **mesothelioma**.
	    - **Carcinoid Syndrome**:
	        - Mechanism: Serotonin, bradykinin.
	        - Associated Cancers: Bronchial carcinoid tumour, carcinoma pancreas, stomach.
	    - **Hypocalcaemia**:
	        - Mechanism: Calcitonin production.
	        - Associated Cancers: Small cell carcinoma of the lung.
	2. **Neuromuscular Syndromes**:
	    
	    - **Myasthenia gravis**: Immunologic.
	        - Associated Cancers: Thymoma.
	    - Other neuromuscular disorders (e.g., in Lung (small cell Ca), Breast).
	3. **Osseous, Joint and Soft Tissue Syndromes**:
	    
	    - **Hypertrophic osteoarthropathy** and **clubbing of fingers**: (e.g., in Lung cancer).
	4. **Haematologic Syndromes**:
	    
	    - **Thrombophlebitis (Trousseau’s phenomenon)**.
	5. **Cutaneous Syndromes**:
	    
	    - **Acanthosis nigricans**.
	    - **Seborrheic dermatitis** and **exfoliative dermatitis**: Immunologic (e.g., in Bowel, Lymphoma).
	6. **Renal Manifestations**: (Details not provided in source).
	    
	7. **Amyloidosis (Secondary)**:
	    
	    - **AA protein** type.
	    - Associated Cancers: **Kidney, lymphoma, solid tumours**.
	    - Primary amyloidosis (AL protein) associated with **multiple myeloma**.

	Here is a concise, high-yield, note-style summary of markers for epithelial carcinoma and general tumour markers, optimized for medical school exams:
	
	**Tumour Markers & Markers of Epithelial Carcinoma**
	
	**I. Tumour Markers (General)**
	
	- **Definition**: Products elaborated by malignant tumours that can be detected in cells, blood, or other body fluids.
	- **Key Concept**: Many of these products are also produced by normal body cells, so their biochemical estimation reflects total substance, **lacking sensitivity and specificity for primary diagnosis alone**.
	- **High-Yield Usefulness**:
	    - **Adjunct to pathologic diagnosis**: Not for primary cancer diagnosis.
	    - **Prognostic & therapeutic purposes**.
	    - **Monitoring recurrence** or effectiveness of therapy after treatment (e.g., after surgical resection, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy).
	    - **Detection of clinically silent early cancer** (e.g., in uterine cervix via Pap smears).
	    - **Detection of dissemination (metastasis)** or recurrence during follow-up.
	
	**II. Types of Tumour Markers & Associated Cancers**
	
	- **Oncofetal Antigens**: Normally expressed in embryonic life, reappear in certain cancers.
	    - **Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP)**: Hepatocellular carcinoma, non-seminomatous germ cell tumours of testis. Also elevated in cirrhosis.
	    - **Carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA)**: Cancer of bowel, pancreas, breast. Elevated in 100% of metastatic colorectal cancers, 20-40% of early lesions, and 60-70% of advanced primary lesions. Primarily prognostic, not diagnostic.
	- **Enzymes**:
	    - **Prostate Acid Phosphatase (PAP)**: Prostatic carcinoma.
	    - **Neuron-Specific Enolase (NSE)**: Neuroblastoma, oat cell carcinoma lung.
	    - **Lactic Dehydrogenase (LDH)**: Lymphoma, Ewing’s sarcoma. Also raised in ALL due to increased turnover of leukaemic cells.
	- **Hormones**:
	    - **Human Chorionic Gonadotropin (hCG)**: Trophoblastic tumours, non-seminomatous germ cell tumours of testis.
	    - **Calcitonin**: Medullary carcinoma thyroid.
	    - **Catecholamines & Vanillylmandelic Acid (VMA)**: Neuroblastoma, pheochromocytoma.
	    - **Ectopic Hormone Production**: Paraneoplastic syndromes.
	        - **ACTH/ADH**: Small cell carcinoma of the lung.
	        - **Erythropoietin**: Renal cell carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, cerebellar haemangioblastoma.
	        - **Insulin-like substance**: Fibrosarcomas, islet cell tumours of pancreas, mesothelioma.
	        - **Parathormone-like substance**: Squamous cell carcinoma of the lung, carcinoma kidney, breast, adult T-cell leukaemia lymphoma.
	        - **Gastrin**: Small cell carcinoma of lung.
	        - **Calcitonin & Growth Hormone**: Less often produced by small cell carcinoma of lung.
	- **Cancer Associated Proteins**:
	    - **CA-125**: Ovary.
	    - **CA 15-3**: Breast.
	    - **CA 19-9**: Colon, pancreas, breast.
	    - **Monoclonal Immunoglobulins**: Multiple myeloma, other gammopathies.
	    - **Prostate Specific Antigen (PSA)**: Prostate carcinoma. **Clinical Pearl**: PSA is specific for prostatic diseases but not specific for prostatic cancer, can be elevated in BPH, and has low sensitivity/specificity for cancer diagnosis.
	
	**III. Markers of Epithelial Carcinoma (Specifics)**
	
	- **Immunohistochemistry (IHC)**: Essential for categorizing undifferentiated cancers and determining origin of poorly differentiated metastatic tumours.
	    - **Cytokeratin**: Expressed by **all epithelial cells**. Positive in carcinomas, including poorly differentiated carcinomas. Used to distinguish carcinomas from lymphomas, melanomas, and sarcomas.
	    - **Epithelial Membrane Antigen (EMA)**: Positive in various carcinomas.
	    - **CEA**: Colonic carcinoma.
	    - **Thyroglobulin**: Thyroid carcinoma.
	    - **CA 125**: Ovarian cancers.
	    - **Nuclear Receptors for Oestrogen and Progesterone (ER/PR)**: Carcinoma of breast. **Clinical Relevance**: ER-PR positive cases respond better to adjuvant endocrine therapy, indicating a better prognosis.
	    - **HER2/neu (ERBB2)**: Overexpressed in breast cancer (25% cases), carcinoma of lungs, ovary, stomach. Detection by IHC or FISH. **Clinical Relevance**: Overexpression indicates likely response to trastuzumab (Herceptin) therapy.
	- **Molecular Markers**:
	    - **EGFR (ERBB1) mutations**: Overexpression of EGFR protein or amplification of the EGFR gene in non-small cell carcinoma (NSCC) of the lung (70% cases) and glioblastomas (50% cases). Targeted molecular therapy with EGFR-TK inhibitors.
	    - **K-RAS mutations**: Common in ~1/3rd of human tumours, including 85% of pancreatic cancers and 45% of colon cancer.
	    - **p53 (TP53) mutations**: Loss-of-function mutations in >50% of cancers, including lung, colon, and breast carcinomas.
	    - **3p allele loss**: Small cell carcinoma of lung, renal cell carcinoma.
	- **Other Relevant Markers/Features**:
	    - **Mucin**: Secretion by adenocarcinoma (e.g., gastric, colonic, ovarian mucinous carcinoma). Can be detected by mucicarmine stain.
	    - **Glucogen**: Clear cell type renal cell carcinoma. Glycogen also found in clear cell type adenocarcinoma of cervix. Detected by PAS stain (dissolves in aqueous fixatives).
	    - **Chromogranin, Synaptophysin, NSE**: Neuroendocrine markers, positive in small cell carcinoma of lung.
