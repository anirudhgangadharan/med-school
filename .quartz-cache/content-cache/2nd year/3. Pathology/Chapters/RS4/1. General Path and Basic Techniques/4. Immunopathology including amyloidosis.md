1. HIV disease and opportunistic infections
	**HIV Disease and Opportunistic Infections**
	
	**I. Introduction & Etiology**
	
	- **Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS)** is caused by the retrovirus **Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV)**.
	- It is characterised by **infection and depletion of CD4+ T lymphocytes** leading to **severe immunosuppression**, predisposing to **opportunistic infections**, secondary **neoplasms**, and **neurologic manifestations**.
	- HIV is a **single-stranded RNA virus** (a lentivirus), containing **reverse transcriptase**.
	    - **HIV-1** is most common globally (e.g., US, Europe, Central Africa, India - subtype C).
	    - **HIV-2** is common in West Africa and parts of India.
	- **Structure**: Spherical enveloped virus (90-120 nm).
	    - **Viral Core**: Contains major **capsid protein p24**, nucleocapsid protein p7/p9, two copies of ssRNA genome, and three viral enzymes (**reverse transcriptase, integrase, protease**).
	    - **Envelope**: Composed of host-derived lipid and two viral glycoproteins: **gp120** (knob-like spikes) and **gp41** (transmembrane anchoring pedicle).
	
	**II. Routes of Transmission**
	
	1. **Sexual Transmission**: Main mode of spread (75% of cases), typically heterosexual promiscuity in India; homosexual/bisexual in industrialised world. Risk increased with coexisting STDs, especially genital ulceration.
	2. **Parenteral Transmission**: Via blood, blood products, or contaminated needles/syringes.
	    - Intravenous drug abusers (sharing needles).
	    - Haemophiliacs (historically, pre-1985 blood products).
	    - Blood transfusion recipients (screening has reduced risk).
	3. **Perinatal Transmission (Mother-to-Infant)**: Major mode in children.
	    - _In utero_ (transplacental).
	    - **Perinatal** (during vaginal delivery through infected birth canal).
	    - **After birth** (ingestion of breast milk).
	4. **Occupational Transmission**: Small risk for healthcare workers (e.g., needle-stick injury).
	
	- **Not transmitted by casual contact or insect bites**.
	
	**III. Pathogenesis & Immune Impact**
	
	- **Life Cycle of HIV**:
	    1. **Infection**: **gp120** on HIV virion selectively binds to **CD4 molecule receptor** on host cells (primarily **CD4+ T cells**, but also monocytes/macrophages, dendritic cells, microglial cells). Requires chemokine coreceptors (CCR5 or CXCR4) for fusion.
	    2. **Internalisation & Uncoating**: Virion enters host cell cytoplasm.
	    3. **Viral DNA Formation**: **Reverse transcriptase** converts viral RNA into double-stranded DNA (proviral DNA). Frequent mutations during this step contribute to drug resistance.
	    4. **Integration**: Viral integrase protein inserts proviral DNA into host cell DNA, becoming **HIV provirus**. Can remain latent for long periods.
	    5. **Replication**: Host cell DNA transcripts for viral RNA, producing new viral particles which bud from the cell membrane, ultimately **destroying the host CD4+ T cells**. Released cytokines contribute to infection spread.
	- **Depletion of CD4+ T cells** is the central event leading to profound immunosuppression.
	    - Normal CD4+/CD8+ ratio (2:1) is **inverted** (0.5 or less).
	    - HIV-infected monocytes/macrophages act as a reservoir for the virus without being destroyed.
	    - **B-cell polyclonal activation** leads to hypergammaglobulinemia but with **impaired humoral immunity**, causing susceptibility to encapsulated bacteria.
	
	**IV. Natural History & Clinical Phases**
	
	- Typically progresses through 3 phases in immunocompetent hosts.
	    1. **Acute HIV Syndrome (Early Acute Phase)**: Occurs 3-6 weeks post-exposure.
	        - High plasma viraemia, followed by seroconversion (anti-HIV antibodies).
	        - Sudden but temporary reduction in CD4+ T cells, followed by return to normal.
	        - Flu-like illness (fever, myalgia, rash, sore throat, aseptic meningitis) in 50-70% of adults, resolving spontaneously.
	    2. **Middle Chronic Phase (Clinical Latency Period)**: Prolonged asymptomatic state (7-10 years untreated).
	        - Virus continuously replicates in lymphoid tissues (lymph nodes, spleen, main reservoir).
	        - Progressive decrease of CD4+ T cells in peripheral blood and lymphoid tissue.
	        - Minor opportunistic infections (e.g., oral/vaginal candidiasis, herpes zoster, mycobacterial tuberculosis) may occur.
	    3. **Final Crisis Phase (Progression to AIDS)**: Defined by specific clinical features and **CD4+ T cell count <200/µL**.
	        - Profound immunosuppression, leading to severe opportunistic infections, secondary tumours, and neurological disease.
	        - Clinical manifestations due to progressive deterioration of the immune system.
	
	**V. Pathological Lesions & Clinical Manifestations (due to immunosuppression)**
	
	- **Opportunistic Infections**: (Major cause of death in HIV/AIDS).
	    - **Fungal**:
	        - **Candidiasis** (oral thrush, oesophageal candidiasis).
	        - **Pneumocystis pneumonia** (_Pneumocystis jirovecii_, formerly _carinii_) – very common in HIV/AIDS.
	        - **Cryptococcosis** (meningitis, pulmonary, disseminated).
	        - **Histoplasmosis**.
	        - Aspergillosis, Mucormycosis.
	    - **Bacterial**:
	        - **Mycobacterial infections** (especially _M. tuberculosis_ – high incidence, rapid progression, often extrapulmonary involving lymph nodes, pleura, pericardium, meningitis; and _M. avium-intracellulare_).
	        - **Bacillary dysentery**, _Salmonella_, _Shigella_, _Staphylococci_.
	    - **Viral**:
	        - Herpes simplex virus (recurrent oral/genital ulcers).
	        - Varicella zoster (shingles).
	        - Cytomegalovirus (CMV retinitis, pneumonitis, GI involvement).
	        - Human Papillomavirus (HPV) (warts).
	    - **Protozoal**:
	        - **Toxoplasmosis** (encephalitis).
	        - Cryptosporidium (chronic diarrhoea).
	- **Secondary Tumours** (End-stage feature).
	    - **Kaposi’s Sarcoma**: Strongly associated with **HHV-8**.
	    - **Non-Hodgkin B-cell Lymphomas**: (e.g., Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, Burkitt's lymphoma; associated with HIV and EBV).
	    - **Squamous Cell Carcinoma** (e.g., cervical, oral, anal).
	- **Central Nervous System (CNS) Manifestations**: Occur in almost all cases; important cause of morbidity/mortality.
	    - **HIV Encephalopathy (AIDS-Dementia Complex)**: Direct effect of HIV on CNS, infecting microglial cells.
	    - Meningitis (tuberculous, cryptococcal, aseptic).
	    - Progressive multifocal leucoencephalopathy (PML).
	    - Peripheral neuropathies, myelopathy.
	    - Neurons are not directly invaded, but affected by cytokines/gp120 from infected macrophages.
	- **Other Systemic Manifestations**:
	    - **Persistent Generalised Lymphadenopathy (PGL)**: Earliest symptom of primary HIV infection; enlarged lymph nodes (>1 cm at 2+ extrainguinal sites for >3 months). Histologically, marked follicular hyperplasia in early stage, later follicular involution and lymphocyte depletion.
	    - **Wasting Syndrome**: Most important systemic manifestation; profound weight loss ("Slim's disease").
	    - Gastrointestinal (chronic diarrhoea, candidiasis, ulcers).
	    - Pulmonary (pneumonias, ARDS).
	    - Mucocutaneous lesions (rashes, infections, tumours).
	    - Haematologic (anaemia, leucopenia, thrombocytopenia).
	    - Hepatobiliary (co-infection with HBV/HCV, opportunistic infections).
	    - Cardiovascular (HIV-associated cardiomyopathy, pericardial effusion).
	    - Renal (Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis - FSGS, collapsing glomerulopathy).
	    - Drug-induced toxic effects from treatment.
	
	**VI. Diagnosis**
	
	1. **Tests for Establishing HIV Infection**:
	    - **Antibody Tests**:
	        - **ELISA**: Most sensitive **screening test** for anti-HIV antibodies.
	        - **Western Blot**: **Confirmatory test** for specific antibodies against gag, pol, env antigens.
	    - **Direct Detection of HIV**:
	        - **p24 antigen capture assay**. (p24 antigen is a marker of HIV infection and used for diagnosis).
	        - **HIV RNA assay methods** (e.g., Reverse Transcriptase PCR).
	        - **DNA-PCR** (for proviral DNA).
	        - Culture of HIV.
	2. **Tests for Defects in Immunity**: Used for diagnosis and monitoring treatment.
	    - **CD4+ T cell counts**: Progressive fall is paramount for diagnosis and staging (CDC categories).
	    - Rise in CD8+ T cells.
	    - **Reversal of CD4+:CD8+ T cell ratio**.
	    - Polyclonal hypergammaglobulinaemia.
	    - Thrombocytopenia.
	3. **Tests for Opportunistic Infections/Secondary Tumours**: Aspiration or biopsy methods.
2. Type I Hypersensitivity reaction-pathogenesis
	
	**Type I Hypersensitivity Reaction: Pathogenesis**
	
	**Definition**: Type I hypersensitivity reaction is a **pathological, excessive and injurious immune response to antigen** (allergen). It occurs **rapidly** (within 5–10 minutes) after the interaction of antigen with IgE antibodies bound to mast cells in a sensitised person. It is also known as an **immediate or IgE-mediated reaction** and represents an **inappropriately triggered, excessive, or undesirable immune response**.
	
	**Characteristics**:
	
	- **Rapid onset**: Occurs within minutes (5–10 minutes) and subsides within 60 minutes.
	- **Antibody-mediated**: Mediated by **IgE antibodies** (also called reagin antibodies).
	- **Genetic susceptibility**: Occurs in **genetically susceptible individuals** previously sensitised to the antigen, with a strong genetic or hereditary linkage regarding the IgE response to allergens. This involves the ability to respond to an allergen and to produce an IgE antibody response.
	- **Antigen type**: Triggered by **exogenous antigens (allergens)** like dust, pollen, food, drugs, or microbes.
	
	**Pathogenesis (Mechanism of Injury)**: The process involves two main phases: **sensitisation** upon first exposure, followed by the **reaction** upon re-exposure to the antigen.
	
	1. **Initial Contact (Sensitisation Phase)**:
	    
	    - Upon first exposure to an **allergen**, the host's immune system mounts a strong **TH2 response**.
	    - **B lymphocytes** are activated and differentiate to form **IgE-secreting plasma cells**.
	    - These IgE antibodies then bind to high-affinity **Fc-epsilon (FcεR1) receptors** present on the surface of **mast cells** and basophils. Mast cells are concentrated near blood vessels and nerves, and in subepithelial tissues, making them common sites for Type I hypersensitivity reactions.
	    - **Sensitisation** occurs, making these IgE-bearing mast cells ready to react upon subsequent antigen exposure.
	2. **Re-exposure and Mediator Release**:
	    
	    - Upon **re-exposure** to the same allergen, the antigen cross-links the IgE antibodies on the surface of the sensitised mast cells.
	    - This cross-linking triggers **mast cell degranulation**, leading to the rapid release of preformed (primary) and newly synthesised (secondary) chemical mediators.
	3. **Mediators and Their Effects**:
	    
	    - **Preformed (Primary) Mediators** (released immediately, within minutes):
	        - **Histamine**: The primary preformed vasoactive mediator. Stored in mast cell granules. Causes **vasodilation**, **increased vascular permeability** (leading to oedema/wheal), **smooth muscle contraction** (e.g., bronchoconstriction), and increased glandular secretions (e.g., nasal/lacrimal). Responsible for the immediate transient response.
	        - **Serotonin (5-HT)**: Also a vasoactive amine involved in early inflammatory response. Causes similar effects to histamine.
	        - **Chemotactic factors**: For neutrophils and eosinophils.
	        - **Proteoglycans**: Like heparin and chondroitin sulfate.
	    - **Newly Synthesised (Secondary) Mediators** (synthesised and released hours later, involved in late-phase reaction):
	        - **Lipid mediators**: Derived from arachidonic acid.
	            - **Leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4)**: Potent inducers of **vascular permeability** and **bronchial smooth muscle contraction**, thousands of times more powerful than histamine.
	            - **Leukotriene B4 (LTB4)**: Potent **chemotactic agent** for neutrophils, eosinophils, and monocytes.
	            - **Prostaglandin D2 (PGD2)**: Causes bronchospasm and increased mucus secretion.
	            - **Platelet-Activating Factor (PAF)**: Increases **leukocyte adhesion** to endothelium, promotes chemotaxis, and causes platelet aggregation, vasoconstriction, and bronchoconstriction.
	        - **Cytokines**:
	            - **TNF (Tumour Necrosis Factor), IL-1, chemokines**: Promote leukocyte recruitment in the late phase.
	            - **IL-4, IL-5**: Amplify the TH2 response.
	            - **IL-13**: Stimulates mucus secretion by epithelial cells.
	
	**Clinical Manifestations**: The severity varies from **local irritant reactions** (e.g., hives/urticaria, allergic rhinitis, eczema, mild bronchoconstriction) to **severe, life-threatening systemic anaphylaxis** (e.g., due to penicillin, bee stings). The reaction involves both an **immediate response** (vasodilation, vascular leakage, smooth muscle spasm) and a **late-phase reaction** (infiltration of eosinophils, neutrophils, basophils, monocytes, and TH2 cells, with mucosal epithelial cell damage).
3. Type II Hyper sensitivity reaction
	
	Here is a concise, high-yield, note-style summary of Type II Hypersensitivity reactions, optimised for medical school exams:
	
	---
	
	### **Type II Hypersensitivity Reaction**
	
	**(Antibody-Mediated / Cytotoxic Hypersensitivity)**
	
	**1. Definition:**
	
	- A pathological immune response where **humoral antibodies (IgG or IgM)** react with **antigens present on the surface of specific cells or fixed in the extracellular matrix (tissues)**, leading to **lysis of target cells** or tissue injury [HM1: 46; RN: 503, 504].
	
	**2. Key Characteristics:**
	
	- **Antibodies Involved:** Primarily **IgG and IgM** [HM1: 41, 47, 57; RN: 504, 529].
	- **Antigens Involved:**
	    - **Endogenous** (normal molecules intrinsic to cell membranes or extracellular matrix, e.g., self-antigens in autoimmune diseases) [RN: 504].
	    - **Exogenous** (foreign antigens that get adsorbed onto cell surfaces or extracellular matrix, e.g., drug metabolites acting as haptens) [RN: 504].
	- **Onset Time:** Generally appears **within 15-30 minutes** after antigen exposure [HM1: 41, 46]. However, onset can be **longer** (e.g., in myasthenia gravis and thyroiditis [HM1: 46]). _(Note: One source indicates a broader range of 6-36 hours for reaction development [RN: 529])._
	- **Tissue Specificity:** **Tissue-specific** [HM1: 47, 57]. The reaction occurs when antibodies bind to antigens on specific cells (most often blood cells) or tissues [HM1: 47].
	- **Key Mediators:** Involves the **complement system**, **tissue macrophages**, **platelets**, **natural killer (NK) cells**, **neutrophils**, and **eosinophils** [HM1: 47]. Complement and Fc receptors on leukocytes play a crucial role [RN: 508].
	
	**3. Etiology / Causes:**
	
	- **HLA-linked conditions** and exposure to **foreign tissues/cells** [HM1: 41].
	- Autoimmune processes where the body produces **autoantibodies against self-antigens** [RN: 504, 527].
	- Drug-induced reactions where drugs or their metabolites act as **haptens** and bind to cell surfaces [HM1: 50; RN: 504, 505].
	
	**4. Pathogenesis (Mechanism of Injury):** Three principal mechanisms lead to cellular and tissue damage [RN: 504]:
	
	- **Opsonization and Phagocytosis:**
	    - IgG antibodies (e.g., autoantibodies) are produced against target antigens on cell surfaces [RN: 505].
	    - These antigen-antibody complexes activate the **classical complement pathway** [HM1: 47; RN: 505].
	    - Cells coated with antibodies (opsonized) and/or complement components (C3b, iC3b) are recognized by **Fc receptors** and **complement receptors** on phagocytic cells (e.g., **macrophages**), leading to their **phagocytosis and lysis** (often in the spleen) [HM1: 47; RN: 505, 507, 508, 509].
	- **Direct Cell Lysis through Membrane Attack Complex (MAC):**
	    - Binding of IgG or IgM antibodies to cell surface antigens activates the complement system [HM1: 47].
	    - This generates the **Membrane Attack Complex (MAC)** (C5-C9), which directly disrupts the cell membrane integrity, creating pores and causing osmotic **lysis of the target cell** [HM1: 48; RN: 506, 507].
	- **Tissue Injury by Complement and Fc Receptor-Mediated Inflammation:**
	    - Antibodies bind to antigens in fixed tissues (e.g., basement membranes) and activate the complement system [RN: 508].
	    - Complement products (e.g., **C5a, C3a – anaphylatoxins**) act as **chemotactic agents**, recruiting and activating acute inflammatory cells (e.g., **neutrophils**) to the site of deposition [HM1: 47; RN: 509, 430].
	    - These activated inflammatory cells release **lysosomal enzymes** and **reactive oxygen species**, causing localised tissue damage and inflammation [RN: 509].
	    - Inflammation can also be induced by antibody binding directly to **Fc receptors on leukocytes** [RN: 509].
	- **Antibody-Mediated Cellular Dysfunction (without cell death):**
	    - Antibodies bind to **cell surface receptors** but do not cause cell injury or inflammation. Instead, they either **stimulate** or **inhibit** the normal function of the cell [RN: 510].
	
	**5. Clinical Manifestations / Examples (Clinically Relevant Conditions):**
	
	- **Blood Cell Reactions (most common) [HM1: 48]:**
	    - **Autoimmune Haemolytic Anaemia (AIHA):** Autoantibodies against red blood cell (RBC) membrane antigens [HM1: 48; RN: 511]. (Can be 'warm' IgG or 'cold' IgM) [HM1: 288, 289].
	    - **Transfusion Reactions:** Preformed antibodies in recipient react with incompatible donor RBCs [HM1: 49; RN: 506, 511].
	    - **Haemolytic Disease of the Newborn (Erythroblastosis Foetalis):** Maternal IgG antibodies cross placenta and destroy foetal RBCs [HM1: 49; RN: 506, 511].
	    - **Idiopathic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (ITP):** Autoantibodies destroy platelets [HM1: 49; RN: 511].
	    - **Drug-induced Cytopenias:** Drugs (haptens) bind to blood cells, leading to antibody-mediated destruction (e.g., penicillin-induced haemolytic anaemia) [HM1: 50; RN: 506].
	- **Tissue-Specific Reactions:**
	    - **Goodpasture's Syndrome:** Antibodies against glomerular basement membrane (GBM) antigens in kidney (and lung) [RN: 512]. _(Note: Some sources also classify Goodpasture's syndrome under Type III Hypersensitivity [HM1: 43, 52])._
	    - **Graves' Disease (Primary Hyperthyroidism):** Stimulating antibodies bind to TSH receptors on thyroid cells, causing hyperthyroidism [HM1: 50; RN: 510, 512].
	    - **Myasthenia Gravis:** Blocking antibodies interfere with acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction, causing muscle weakness [HM1: 50; RN: 510, 512].
	    - **Hyperacute Transplant Rejection:** Preformed humoral antibodies in the recipient react immediately with donor antigens on the transplanted organ [HM1: 30, 51; RN: 506, 552, 553].
	    - **Acute Rheumatic Fever:** Antibodies against streptococcal bacterial proteins cross-react with myocardial proteins (molecular mimicry), causing myocarditis [RN: 532].
	    - **Pernicious Anaemia:** Autoantibodies against intrinsic factor or gastric parietal cells [RN: 512, 599].
	    - **Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus:** Autoimmune destruction of pancreatic islet β-cells [HM1: 42]. _(Note: Other sources classify Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus predominantly under Type IV Hypersensitivity [RN: 525, 528])._
	
	**6. Diagnosis / Monitoring:**
	
	- Detection of specific **IgG or IgM antibodies** against relevant target antigens (e.g., by ELISA, immunofluorescence).
	- **Coombs' Test** (Direct and Indirect) to detect antibodies or complement on red blood cells (e.g., in AIHA, transfusion reactions) [HM1: 48, 130, 288, 289].
	- Functional assays for stimulating or blocking antibodies (e.g., TSH receptor antibodies in Graves' disease, acetylcholine receptor antibodies in Myasthenia Gravis).
4. Type III Hypersensitivity reaction
	Here is a concise, high-yield, note-style summary of Type III Hypersensitivity reactions, optimised for medical school exams:
	
	---
	
	### **Type III Hypersensitivity Reaction**
	
	**(Immune Complex-Mediated / Arthus Reaction)**
	
	**1. Definition:**
	
	- A **pathological, excessive, and injurious immune response** to antigen.
	- Characterised by the formation of **Antigen-Antibody (Ag-Ab) complexes** (also known as immune complexes) in the circulation or locally.
	- These complexes then **deposit in tissues** (e.g., blood vessels, glomeruli, joints), leading to **complement activation** and a subsequent **self-sustaining acute inflammatory reaction**.
	- The recruited **inflammatory cells** (primarily neutrophils and monocytes) release damaging substances, causing tissue injury.
	
	**2. Key Characteristics:**
	
	- **Antibodies Involved:** Predominantly **IgG and IgM**. IgA is also mentioned in some classifications.
	- **Antigens Involved:** **Soluble exogenous** (e.g., viral, bacterial, fungal, parasitic components) or **endogenous autoantigens**.
	- **Onset Time:**
	    - **Not an immediate reaction**.
	    - Onset occurs about **6 to 12 hours** after antigen exposure, and the effects are prolonged.
	- **Tissue Specificity:** **Not tissue specific**; deposition can occur in various organs/tissues.
	- **Key Mediators:** **Complement components** (C3a, C5a, C5b-9 MAC), **neutrophils**, and **monocytes**.
	- **Immunopathology:** Typically involves **acute inflammatory reaction, neutrophil infiltration, and vasculitis**. May lead to **fibrinoid necrosis**.
	
	**3. Etiology / Causes:**
	
	- Occurs when pathogenic immune complexes **fail to be adequately cleared** by the body's immune and phagocytic systems.
	- **Persistence of low-grade microbial infection** (e.g., chronic bacterial or viral infections where weak antibody responses lead to persistent complex formation).
	- **Autoimmune diseases** (due to continuous production of autoantibodies against self-antigens).
	- **Repeated inhalation of antigens** (e.g., from moulds, plants, or animals).
	
	**4. Pathogenesis (Mechanism of Injury):**
	
	- **Formation of Immune Complexes:** Formed by the interaction of soluble antibodies with soluble or insoluble antigens.
	    - **Medium-sized immune complexes with a slight antigen excess are the most pathogenic**.
	    - Small and very large complexes are generally cleared without significant pathology.
	- **Deposition of Immune Complexes:** Pathogenic immune complexes deposit in tissues. Common sites of deposition include:
	    - **Blood vessel walls** (leading to vasculitis).
	    - **Renal glomeruli** (causing glomerulonephritis).
	    - **Joints** (resulting in arthritis).
	- **Inflammatory Reaction and Tissue Damage:**
	    - Deposited complexes **activate the complement system** (via the classical pathway).
	    - Complement products (e.g., C5a) act as **chemotactic agents**, recruiting acute inflammatory cells (neutrophils, monocytes) to the site of deposition.
	    - These activated inflammatory cells release **lysosomal enzymes** and **reactive oxygen species**, causing localised tissue damage and inflammation.
	    - This destructive process can result in characteristic **fibrinoid necrosis** in affected vessel walls.
	
	**5. Clinical Manifestations / Examples (Clinically Relevant Conditions):**
	
	- **Systemic Immune Complex Disease (Acute Serum Sickness):** A historical example following administration of large amounts of foreign serum, now rare.
	- **Local Immune Complex Disease (Arthus Reaction):** A localised area of tissue necrosis, typically in the skin, due to acute immune complex vasculitis. Can be experimentally induced by intracutaneous antigen injection.
	- **Immune Complex Glomerulonephritis:** A common manifestation, where complexes deposit in the renal glomeruli.
	- **Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE):** A classical systemic autoimmune disease where autoantibodies (e.g., anti-DNA) form complexes, leading to widespread tissue injury, particularly lupus nephritis.
	- **Rheumatoid Arthritis:** A chronic inflammatory disease affecting joints, also involving immune complex deposition. (Note: Some sources also link Rheumatic Heart Disease to Type II hypersensitivity via molecular mimicry).
	- **Polyarteritis Nodosa (PAN) & Wegener’s Granulomatosis:** Systemic vasculitides where immune complexes play a role.
	- **Henoch-Schönlein Purpura:**.
	- **Drug-induced Vasculitis:**.
	- **Farmer’s Lung:** An example of hypersensitivity pneumonitis due to inhaled antigens.
	- **Goodpasture's Syndrome:** (Note: There is conflicting information across sources; it is listed under both Type II and Type III hypersensitivity).
	
	**6. Diagnosis / Monitoring:**
	
	- **Decreased serum C3 levels** during the active phase can indicate complement activation and monitor disease activity.
	- **Tissue biopsy** shows immune complex deposition (e.g., granular deposits of IgG, IgM, C3 by immunofluorescence).

	Amyloidosis is a critical topic for medical examinations, covering fundamental concepts of protein misfolding diseases. Here is a high-yield, note-style summary:
	
	### Amyloidosis
	
	**1. Definition**
	
	- Amyloid is a **pathologic fibrillar protein** deposited in the **extracellular space** in various tissues and organs of the body.
	- It is a group of diseases characterised by the **extracellular deposition of abnormal fibrillar proteins**, leading to structural and functional damage to affected tissues.
	
	**2. Physical and Chemical Nature of Amyloid**
	
	- **Physical Nature:**
	    - All types of amyloid are composed of **non-branching fibrils** measuring **7–10 nm** in diameter.
	    - Each fibril consists of **cross-β-pleated sheet polypeptide chains** which are wound around one another. This configuration is responsible for its characteristic staining properties.
	    - Amyloid also contains a minor part (5%) known as the **P-component** (amyloid P-component or AP), a glycoprotein that appears pentagonal or doughnut-shaped ultrastructurally. This P-component is present in all forms of amyloid.
	- **Chemical Nature and Types of Amyloid Proteins (Fibril Proteins):**
	    - Amyloid material is primarily composed of **fibril proteins (95%)** and a minor component of **non-fibrillar proteins (5%)**.
	    - There are **over 20 different biochemical forms** of amyloid proteins, but three major distinct forms are:
	        - **AL (Amyloid Light Chain) protein:**
	            - Composed of **complete immunoglobulin (Ig) light chains** (predominantly lambda chains) or their N-terminal fragments.
	            - Produced by **plasma cells** and associated with monoclonal B-cell proliferations like **multiple myeloma** or other plasma cell dyscrasias.
	        - **AA (Amyloid-Associated) protein:**
	            - A non-immunoglobulin protein derived from a larger precursor called **Serum Amyloid-Associated (SAA) protein**.
	            - SAA is an **acute phase reactant protein** synthesised in the liver in response to chronic inflammatory and traumatic conditions.
	            - Found in **secondary amyloidosis**, which is associated with chronic inflammation, infections (e.g., TB, leprosy), autoimmune diseases (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis), and cancers.
	        - **Aβ (Amyloid β-peptide) protein:**
	            - Distinct from Aβ2M.
	            - Deposited in **cerebral amyloid angiopathy** and **neurofibrillary tangles in Alzheimer’s disease**.
	        - **Aβ2M (Amyloid β2-microglobulin):**
	            - Derived from **β2-microglobulin**, a component of MHC class I.
	            - Deposited in **haemodialysis-associated amyloidosis**, typically affecting bones and joints due to its inability to be filtered by dialysis membranes.
	        - **ATTR (Amyloid Transthyretin):**
	            - Derived from **transthyretin**, a serum transport protein for thyroxine and retinol.
	            - Found in **heredofamilial amyloidosis** (due to mutations in TTR gene) and **senile systemic amyloidosis** (due to deposition of normal TTR protein in elderly patients, mainly affecting the heart).
	        - **ACal (Amyloid Calcitonin):**
	            - Derived from calcitonin, a hormone precursor protein.
	            - Seen in **medullary carcinoma of the thyroid**.
	
	**3. Classification of Amyloidosis** Amyloidosis can be classified in several ways:
	
	- **Based on Cause:**
	    - **Primary Amyloidosis:** With unknown cause, where deposition is the disease itself. Often associated with plasma cell dyscrasias.
	    - **Secondary (Reactive) Amyloidosis:** As a complication of some underlying known chronic disease (e.g., chronic inflammation, cancer).
	- **Based on Extent of Amyloid Deposition:**
	    - **Systemic (Generalised) Amyloidosis:** Involving multiple organs.
	    - **Localised Amyloidosis:** Involving one or two organs or sites.
	- **Based on Precursor Biochemical Proteins:** Specific types like AL, AA, ATTR, Aβ, Aβ2M, ACal, etc..
	- **Clinicopathological Classification (Widely Accepted):**
	    - **A. Systemic (Generalised) Amyloidosis:**
	        1. **Primary (AL) Amyloidosis:** Associated with plasma cell dyscrasias. Organs commonly involved: **Heart, bowel, skin, nerves, kidney**.
	        2. **Secondary (Reactive) (AA) Amyloidosis:** Associated with chronic inflammation, cancers. Organs commonly involved: **Liver, spleen, kidneys, adrenals**.
	        3. **Haemodialysis-associated (Aβ2M) Amyloidosis:** Associated with chronic renal failure. Organs commonly involved: **Synovium, joints, tendon sheaths**.
	        4. **Heredofamilial (ATTR, AA, Others):** Rare hereditary forms, e.g., familial Mediterranean fever.
	    - **B. Localised Amyloidosis:**
	        1. **Senile Cardiac (ATTR):** Seen in elderly, deposits in heart and aorta.
	        2. **Senile Cerebral (Aβ, APrP):** Seen in Alzheimer's disease.
	        3. **Endocrine (Hormone precursors):** Seen in type 2 diabetes (Islets of Langerhans), medullary carcinoma thyroid.
	        4. **Tumour-forming (AL):** Isolated tumour-like deposits (e.g., lungs, larynx, skin, urinary bladder, tongue, eye).
	
	**4. Pathogenesis of Amyloidosis**
	
	- **General Mechanism:**
	    - Amyloidosis is a disorder of **abnormal protein folding or misfolding**.
	    - Normally, misfolded proteins are degraded by proteasomes (intracellularly) or macrophages (extracellularly).
	    - In amyloidosis, there is a **failure of control mechanisms**, leading to the production and accumulation of misfolded proteins that exceed the degradation capacity. These unstable misfolded proteins self-associate and are deposited as insoluble fibrils in extracellular tissues.
	    - Non-fibrillar components like AP, apoE, and glycosaminoglycans play a role in **fibril stabilisation** by protein aggregation and protecting them from solubilisation or degradation.
	- **Two main pathways leading to fibril deposition:**
	    1. **Production of abnormal amounts of normal protein:** Example: Increased SAA synthesis during inflammation. In genetically prone individuals, defective monocyte-derived enzymes may lead to incomplete breakdown of SAA, forming insoluble AA molecules.
	    2. **Production of abnormally folded proteins:** These proteins have an inherent tendency to fold improperly and form fibrils (e.g., mutant transthyretin in hereditary amyloidosis).
	
	**5. Staining Characteristics and Demonstration by Special Stains and its Appearances**
	
	- **Routine H&E Staining:**
	    - Amyloid appears as **extracellular, homogeneous, structureless, eosinophilic hyaline material**.
	    - Often seen in relation to blood vessels.
	    - Small deposits are difficult to detect with H&E alone.
	- **Special Stains and their Results:**
	    - **Congo Red Stain (Most Important & Specific):**
	        - Under **ordinary light microscopy**, amyloid stains **pink-red** (Congophilia).
	        - Under **polarising microscopy**, it exhibits a characteristic **apple-green birefringence**. This is the **confirmatory method** for tissue diagnosis of amyloidosis.
	    - **Metachromatic Stains (Rosaniline Dyes like Methyl Violet/Cresyl Violet):**
	        - Amyloid has the property of **metachromasia**, meaning the dye reacts with amyloid and undergoes a **colour change**, typically staining **rose-pink** while surrounding tissue is blue.
	    - **Iodine Stain (Gross Examination):**
	        - On the cut surface of a gross specimen, Lugol’s iodine imparts a **mahogany brown colour** to the amyloid-containing area.
	        - On addition of dilute sulfuric acid, it turns **blue**. This starch-like property is due to the AP component.
	    - **Thioflavin T/S:**
	        - Under **ultraviolet light**, amyloid exhibits **fluorescence**. Thioflavin-S is less specific.
	    - **Immunohistochemistry:**
	        - Used to classify the **biochemical type** of amyloid by using corresponding antibody stains against specific amyloid proteins (e.g., anti-AA, anti-lambda (λ), anti-kappa (κ), transthyretin antibody stains).
	        - For mere confirmation of any type of amyloid, **anti-AP stain** is most useful since the P component is present in all forms.
	
	**6. Spleen in Amyloidosis - Morphology** Amyloid deposition in the spleen can occur in two distinct patterns:
	
	- **1. Sago Spleen (White Pulp Involvement):**
	    - **Grossly:** Splenic enlargement is **not marked**. The cut surface shows characteristic **translucent pale and waxy nodules resembling sago grains**.
	    - **Microscopically:** Amyloid deposits primarily in the **walls of the arterioles of the white pulp** and may subsequently extend to replace the follicles.
	- **2. Lardaceous Spleen (Red Pulp Involvement):**
	    - **Grossly:** There is generally **moderate to marked splenomegaly** (weight up to 1 kg, or even 1000-5000g in chronic cases). The cut surface shows **map-like areas of pale, waxy translucency** resembling lard (pig fat).
	    - **Microscopically:** The deposits involve the **red pulp in the walls of splenic sinuses** and small arteries, and in the connective tissue. Progressive accumulation of amyloid leads to deformity, pressure atrophy, and disappearance of liver cells.
	- **General Microscopic Confirmation for both:** Confirmation is achieved by observing **Congophilia** (red-pink colour) with Congo red staining, which then demonstrates **apple-green birefringence** under polarising microscopy.
	- In long-standing chronic splenic congestion, haemorrhages may get organised and show diffuse fibrosis together with iron pigment and calcium deposits, so-called **siderotic nodules or Gamna-Gandy bodies**.
