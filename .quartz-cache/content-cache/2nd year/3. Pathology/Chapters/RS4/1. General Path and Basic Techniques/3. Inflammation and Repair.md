1. Vascular changes in acute inflammation
	**Vascular Changes in Acute Inflammation**
	
	Acute inflammation is a **local response of living mammalian tissues to injury** from various agents (microbial, immunological, physical, chemical). It serves to **bring cells (phagocytic leukocytes) and molecules** (antibodies, complement proteins) from the circulation to the site of injury to eliminate the offending agents.
	
	**I. Components of Acute Inflammation**
	
	1. **Reactions of blood vessels (vascular changes)**.
	2. Reactions of leukocytes (cellular events).
	
	**II. Reactions of Blood Vessels (Vascular Changes)**
	
	- **Purpose**: To deliver circulating cells, fluids, and plasma proteins from the circulation to sites of infection or tissue injury.
	- Consist of: **Changes in vascular flow and calibre** and **increased vascular permeability**.
	
	**A. Changes in Vascular Flow and Calibre**
	
	1. **Transient Vasoconstriction**: The immediate vascular response is a **transient vasoconstriction of arterioles**, lasting 3-5 seconds in mild injury, or up to 5 minutes in severe injury.
	2. **Progressive Vasodilatation**:
	    - This follows vasoconstriction and involves mainly **arterioles, venules, and capillaries**.
	    - Occurs within half an hour of injury.
	    - **Effect**: Leads to increased blood volume in the microvascular bed, responsible for **redness (rubor)** and **warmth (calor)** at the site of acute inflammation.
	    - **Mediators**: **Histamine, prostaglandins, kinins, and nitric oxide (NO)**.
	3. **Slowing of Blood Flow (Stasis)**:
	    - Elevated local hydrostatic pressure from vasodilatation causes **transudation of fluid** into the extracellular space, leading to **swelling (tumour)**.
	    - Concentration of red blood cells (RBCs) in small vessels and increased blood viscosity lead to **slowing of blood flow**.
	    - **Stasis** results in localised redness.
	4. **Leukocyte Redistribution**:
	    - **Margination**: As blood flow slows, leukocytes (mainly neutrophils) move towards the peripheral column and **accumulate along the endothelial surface of vessels**.
	    - **Rolling**: Marginated leukocytes weakly attach, detach, and bind again, causing them to **roll along the endothelial surface**.
	5. **Triple Response (Lewis Experiment)**: A clinical demonstration of haemodynamic changes:
	    - **Red line**: Local vasodilatation of capillaries and venules (within seconds).
	    - **Flare**: Reddish appearance surrounding the red line due to vasodilatation of adjacent arterioles.
	    - **Wheal**: Swelling/oedema from transudation of fluid into the extravascular space.
	
	**B. Increased Vascular Permeability (Vascular Leakage)**
	
	- **Definition**: The escape of **protein-rich fluid** (exudate) from the circulation into the interstitial tissue or body cavities, causing **oedema**.
	- **Mechanisms of Increased Permeability** (can act singly or in combination):
	    1. **Endothelial cell contraction**:
	        - **Most common mechanism**.
	        - Affects **venules exclusively**.
	        - Mediated by **histamine, bradykinin, leukotrienes**, and neuropeptide substance P.
	        - **Immediate transient response**: Begins immediately after injury, usually reversible, and short-duration (15-30 minutes).
	        - _Example_: Mild thermal injury to skin.
	    2. **Direct endothelial injury**:
	        - Causes **cell necrosis and physical gaps** at detached endothelial cells.
	        - Affects **all levels of microvasculature** (venules, capillaries, arterioles).
	        - Can be **immediate sustained leakage** (hours to days) or **delayed prolonged leakage** (2-12 hours onset, lasts hours to days).
	        - _Examples_: Severe bacterial infections (immediate sustained); moderate thermal injury, radiation injury (delayed prolonged).
	    3. **Leukocyte-mediated endothelial injury**:
	        - Activated leukocytes release proteolytic enzymes and toxic oxygen species, causing endothelial injury and increased vascular leakiness.
	        - Affects mostly **venules and capillaries**.
	        - A **delayed, prolonged response**.
	    4. **Increased transcytosis**: Enhanced transport of fluids and proteins through channels (vesiculo-vacuolar organelle) in endothelial cells.
	    5. **Leakiness in neovascularisation**: Newly formed capillaries during repair or in tumours are excessively leaky until endothelial cells mature.
	
	**III. Key Mediators of Vascular Changes**
	
	- **Cell-derived Mediators**:
	    
	    - **Vasoactive Amines**:
	        - **Histamine**: Stored in mast cells, basophils, platelets; released by physical injury, immune reactions; causes vasodilation and increased vascular permeability.
	        - **Serotonin (5-HT)**: Stored mainly in platelets; causes vasoconstriction, bronchoconstriction, and at low concentrations, vasodilation and increased vascular permeability.
	    - **Arachidonic Acid (AA) Metabolites**:
	        - Derived from cell membrane phospholipids via phospholipase A2.
	        - **Prostaglandins (PGE2, PGD2, PGI2)**: Produced via cyclooxygenase pathway; cause **vasodilation** and increased vascular permeability.
	        - **Leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4, LTE4)**: Produced via lipoxygenase pathway; cause **increased vascular permeability** and bronchoconstriction. **LTB4** is chemotactic.
	    - **Platelet-activating Factor (PAF)**: Source from platelets, basophils, mast cells, neutrophils, macrophages, endothelial cells; causes vascular reactions (vasoconstriction, vasodilation, increased permeability) and cellular reactions (leukocyte adhesion, chemotaxis).
	    - **Nitric Oxide (NO)**: Soluble gas produced by endothelial cells, macrophages; causes **vasodilation** and controls inflammatory responses.
	    - **Cytokines (TNF, IL-1)**: Produced by activated macrophages; cause **endothelial activation**, increased expression of adhesion molecules, and systemic effects like fever.
	    - **Chemokines**: Chemotactic cytokines that attract various leukocytes.
	- **Plasma-derived Mediators**:
	    
	    - **Complement System**: Group of plasma proteins; activation leads to **C3a and C5a (anaphylatoxins)**, which activate mast cells, basophils, and increase vascular permeability. C5a is also chemotactic for leukocytes.
	    - **Kinin System**: Produces **bradykinin**, which causes **increased vascular permeability and pain**. Kallikrein activates Hageman factor and is chemotactic.
	    - **Clotting System**.
2. Phagocytosis and killing
	**Phagocytosis and Killing in Acute Inflammation**
	
	Phagocytosis is a critical process in acute inflammation, defined as the **engulfment of solid particulate material by cells** (cell-eating). This function is primarily performed by **phagocytes**, notably **polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs)** and **macrophages**.
	
	**I. Phagocytosis and Clearance of the Offending Agent** The process of phagocytosis involves a sequence of events to recognise, internalise, and digest foreign material, microorganisms, or cellular debris.
	
	1. **Recognition of Microbes and Dead Cells**:
	    
	    - The first step in inflammatory responses is the **recognition of microbes, necrotic cells, and foreign substances** by pattern-recognition receptors on cell surfaces and circulating proteins.
	    - Leukocytes possess receptors for various substances, including opsonins.
	2. **Opsonisation**:
	    
	    - This is the process of **coating a particle** (e.g., microbe) with **opsonins** to increase its phagocytosis.
	    - **Opsonins** include:
	        - **IgG opsonin**: The Fc fragment of immunoglobulin G, which coats bacteria, allowing PMNs to bind via their receptors.
	        - **C3b opsonin**: A fragment generated by complement pathway activation, strongly chemotactic for attracting PMNs to bacteria.
	        - **Lectins**: Carbohydrate-binding proteins in plasma that bind to bacterial cell walls.
	        - **Acute phase proteins** (e.g., C-reactive protein).
	        - **Collectins** and **Mannose-binding lectins**.
	3. **Engulfment (Internalisation)**:
	    
	    - The opsonised particle or microbe, bound to the phagocyte's surface, is engulfed by the formation of **cytoplasmic pseudopods** around the particle.
	    - This envelops the particle in a **phagocytic vacuole (phagosome)**.
	    - The phagosome then fuses with one or more **lysosomes** of the cell to form a larger vacuole called a **phagolysosome**.
	    - _Clinical Relevance_: In **Chédiak-Higashi syndrome**, there is no formation of phagolysosomes due to aberrant fusion of organelles.
	
	**II. Killing and Degradation** Killing and degradation of ingested microbial agents/particles occur within the phagolysosome, mainly through oxidative and non-oxidative mechanisms.
	
	4. **Intracellular Mechanisms (Bactericidal)**:
	    - **Oxidative (Oxygen-dependent) Bactericidal Mechanism**:
	        - This is an important microbicidal killing mechanism via **oxidative damage** by the production of **reactive oxygen metabolites (ROS)**.
	        - A phase of **increased oxygen consumption ('respiratory burst')** by activated phagocytic leukocytes is essential, requiring **NADPH oxidase**.
	        - **NADPH oxidase** reduces oxygen to **superoxide ion (O2•)**.
	        - Superoxide is then converted to **hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)**, which, in the presence of **myeloperoxidase (MPO)** and a halide ion (e.g., Cl-), forms **hypochlorite (HOCl•)**. The **MPO-halide system** is the **most powerful microbicidal mechanism**.
	        - H2O2 can also be converted to the highly destructive **hydroxyl radical (•OH)**.
	        - _Clinical Relevance_: Oxygen-derived radicals may be released extracellularly from leukocytes, causing **tissue damage during inflammation**. Antioxidant mechanisms in serum and tissues protect against this. Inactivation of antiproteases (e.g., $\alpha$1-antitrypsin) by ROS can lead to tissue damage (e.g., emphysema).
	    - **Reactive Nitrogen Species**:
	        - **Nitric oxide (NO)**, a soluble free radical gas, is produced from L-arginine by **nitric oxide synthase (NOS)**.
	        - The **inducible NOS (iNOS)** type is involved in microbial killing in macrophages, producing NO when activated by cytokines (e.g., IFN-$\gamma$) or microbial products.
	        - NO can react with superoxide to produce **peroxynitrite (ONOO•)**, a highly reactive free radical that attacks lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids of microbes and host cells.
	    - **Non-oxidative Pathways**:
	        - **Lysosomal enzymes**: Released into the phagolysosome.
	        - **Granule enzymes and other proteins**: (e.g., cationic protein, lysozyme, proteases, acid hydrolases) found in neutrophil and macrophage granules contribute to degradation.
	        - _Clinical Relevance_: Normally, harmful enzymes are controlled by anti-proteases (e.g., **$\alpha$1-anti-trypsin**), deficiency of which can lead to sustained tissue damage.
	
	**III. Key Cells and Their Roles in Phagocytosis and Killing**
	
	- **Neutrophils (PMNs)**:
	    
	    - Normally the most numerous circulating leukocytes (40-75%) and the **first line of defense** against invading organisms, sometimes called 'microphages'.
	    - Their cytoplasm contains **lysosomal granules** (primary/azurophilic, secondary, tertiary).
	    - They are rapidly recruited to inflammation sites and are **short-lived**.
	- **Monocytes and Macrophages**:
	    
	    - Circulating monocytes are immature macrophages. They enter tissues and differentiate into macrophages.
	    - Macrophages are the **predominant cells in chronic inflammation**.
	    - They are **slow to respond but long-lived**.
	    - Functions include **phagocytosis** and **killing/elimination of ingested microbes and necrotic tissue**.
	    - Macrophages also act as **antigen-presenting cells (APCs)** to T-cells in adaptive immune responses.
	    - They secrete various **cytokines** (e.g., IL-1, TNF-$\alpha$, IL-8) and **prostaglandins**, which are chemical mediators of inflammation.
3. Chemical mediators of inflammation
	**Chemical Mediators of Inflammation**
	
	Chemical mediators are a large and increasing number of endogenous chemical substances that mediate the process of acute inflammation.
	
	**I. General Features**:
	
	- **Source**: Released either from **cells** (stored in granules or synthesised de novo) or derived from **plasma proteins** (synthesised in the liver, require activation).
	- **Stimuli**: Released in response to injurious agents, dead/damaged tissues, or by other mediators (secondary mediators).
	- **Targets**: Act on different target cells, which may be the cells that produced them or other body cells.
	- **Actions**: Diverse, including increased vascular permeability, vasodilation, chemotaxis, fever, pain, and tissue damage.
	- **Short-lived**: Have a short lifespan, rapidly removed by enzymatic inactivation, antioxidants, regulatory proteins, or spontaneous decay.
	
	**II. Classification**: A. Cell-derived Mediators B. Plasma Protein-derived Mediators (Plasma Proteases)
	
	---
	
	**A. Cell-derived Mediators**:
	
	1. **Vasoactive Amines**:
	    
	    - **Histamine**:
	        - **Source**: Stored in **mast cell** granules (richest source), basophils, and platelets.
	        - **Stimuli**: Physical injury (trauma, cold, heat), immune reactions (Ag-Ab binding to mast cells), anaphylatoxins (C3a, C5a), interleukins.
	        - **Actions**: **Vasodilation**, **increased vascular (venular) permeability**, itching, pain, increased mucus secretion.
	    - **Serotonin (5-HT)**:
	        - **Source**: Platelets, chromaffin cells of GIT, nervous tissue, mast cells.
	        - **Actions**: Similar to histamine, but less potent.
	    - **Neuropeptides (e.g., Substance P, Neurokinin A, VIP, Somatostatin)**:
	        - **Source**: Central and peripheral nervous systems, sensory nerves, various leukocytes.
	        - **Actions**: Increased vascular permeability, transmission of pain stimuli, mast cell degranulation, vasodilation.
	2. **Arachidonic Acid (AA) Metabolites (Eicosanoids)**:
	    
	    - **Source**: Derived from **cell membrane phospholipids** by **phospholipase A2**.
	    - **Pathways**:
	        - **Cyclooxygenase (COX) Pathway** (via COX-1 and COX-2):
	            - **Prostaglandins (PGD2, PGE2, PGF2-α)**:
	                - **PGD2 & PGE2**: Vasodilation, increased venular permeability, bronchoconstriction, inhibit inflammatory cell function. PGE2 causes cytokine-induced **fever**.
	                - PGF2-α: Vasodilation, bronchoconstriction.
	                - **Clinical Relevance**: Responsible for **pain** and **fever**. Many **anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs, COX-2 inhibitors)** act by inhibiting COX activity.
	            - **Thromboxane A2 (TXA2)**: Vasoconstrictor, promotes **platelet aggregation**.
	            - **Prostacyclin (PGI2)**: Vasodilation, bronchodilation, **inhibits platelet aggregation**.
	            - **Resolvins**: Inhibit production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, anti-inflammatory.
	        - **Lipooxygenase Pathway** (via lipo-oxygenase, predominant in neutrophils):
	            - **5-HETE**: Potent **chemotactic agent for neutrophils**.
	            - **Leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4, LTE4, LTB4)**:
	                - **LTB4**: Chemotactic for phagocytic cells, stimulates phagocytic cell adherence.
	                - **LTC4, LTD4, LTE4 (Slow-Reacting Substances of Anaphylaxis - SRS-As)**: Cause smooth muscle contraction (vasoconstriction, **bronchoconstriction**), increased vascular permeability.
	                - **Clinical Relevance**: Involved in **bronchospasm in asthma**.
	            - **Lipoxins (LXA4, LXB4)**: Regulate and counterbalance leukotriene actions, **inhibit inflammation** (e.g., inhibit neutrophil chemotaxis and recruitment).
	3. **Lysosomal Components**:
	    
	    - **Neutrophil Granules**:
	        - **Primary (Azurophil)**: Contain **myeloperoxidase**, acid hydrolases, lysozyme, defensins, phospholipase, cathepsin G, elastase, protease. Myeloperoxidase causes oxidative lysis; proteases degrade extracellular matrix components (collagen, basement membrane).
	        - **Secondary (Specific)**: Contain alkaline phosphatase, lactoferrin, gelatinase, collagenase, lysozyme.
	        - **Clinical Relevance**: Harmful tissue destruction by proteases is checked by **antiproteases (α1-antitrypsin, α2-macroglobulin)**.
	    - **Monocyte and Tissue Macrophage Granules**: Release acid proteases, collagenase, elastase, plasminogen activator (more active in chronic inflammation).
	4. **Platelet Activating Factor (PAF)**:
	    
	    - **Source**: IgE-sensitised basophils/mast cells, other leukocytes, endothelium, platelets.
	    - **Actions**: Increased vascular permeability, vasodilation (low conc.)/vasoconstriction, bronchoconstriction, adhesion of leukocytes to endothelium, chemotaxis, **platelet aggregation**.
	5. **Cytokines and Chemokines**:
	    
	    - **General**: Polypeptide substances produced by activated lymphocytes (lymphokines), monocytes (monokines), and other cells. Regulate immune and inflammatory responses.
	    - **Tumour Necrosis Factor (TNF-α) & Interleukin-1 (IL-1)**:
	        - **Major cytokines of inflammation**.
	        - **Source**: Activated **monocytes/macrophages**, mast cells, B/T/NK cells.
	        - **Actions**: Endothelial activation (expression of adhesion molecules), leukocyte cytotoxicity, fibroblast proliferation, and **systemic effects** (fever, shock, anorexia, hepatic acute phase protein production, leucocytosis, cachexia, DIC).
	    - **Interleukin-6 (IL-6)**: Similar sources/targets to IL-1. Major roles include hepatic acute phase protein production, differentiation/growth of T and B cells.
	    - **Interleukin-8 (IL-8)**: Chemokine. Induces migration of neutrophils, macrophages, T cells; stimulates histamine release from basophils; stimulates angiogenesis.
	    - **Interferon-γ (IFN-γ)**: Produced by T cells and NK cells. Activates macrophages and NK cells, stimulates Ig secretion by B cells, differentiates T helper cells.
	    - **Other Chemokines (e.g., MCP-1, Eotaxin, PF-4)**: Act as chemoattractants for specific inflammatory cells.
	    - **Anti-inflammatory Cytokines**: **IL-10 and Transforming Growth Factor-β (TGF-β)** produced by macrophages and other cells **inhibit inflammation**. TGF-β is also a potent fibrogenic agent.
	6. **Free Radicals (Oxygen Metabolites & Nitric Oxide)**:
	    
	    - **Oxygen-derived Metabolites (Reactive Oxygen Species - ROS)**:
	        - **Types**: Superoxide (O2–), H2O2, Hydroxyl radical (OH–).
	        - **Source**: Released from activated neutrophils and macrophages.
	        - **Actions**: Endothelial cell damage (increased vascular permeability), activation of proteases, tissue matrix damage, damage to other cells.
	        - **Clinical Relevance**: Counteracted by **antioxidants (e.g., Vitamins E, A, C, ceruloplasmin)**.
	    - **Nitric Oxide (NO)**:
	        - **Source**: Endothelial cells, activated macrophages, neurons.
	        - **Actions**: Vasodilation, anti-platelet activating agent, microbicidal action.
	        - **Clinical Relevance**: Produced from L-arginine by nitric oxide synthase (NOS).
	
	---
	
	**B. Plasma Protein-derived Mediators (Plasma Proteases)**: These are products derived from activation and interaction of four interlinked systems: **Kinin, Clotting, Fibrinolytic, and Complement**.
	
	- **Hageman Factor (Factor XIIa)**: Plays a **key role** in activating all four systems. Activated by contact with basement membrane, bacterial endotoxins, or by leakage through endothelial gaps.
	
	1. **Kinin System**:
	    
	    - **Mechanism**: Factor XIIa -> Kallikrein (from prekallikrein) -> **Bradykinin** (from high molecular weight kininogen).
	    - **Actions of Bradykinin**: Smooth muscle contraction, vasodilation, **increased vascular permeability**, and **pain**.
	    - **Actions of Kallikrein**: Potent activator of Hageman factor, converts C5 to C5a (chemotactic).
	2. **Clotting System**:
	    
	    - **Products**: Fibrin -> fibrinopeptides (increase permeability, chemotactic); Thrombin (fibrin formation, endothelial activation, leukocyte adhesion).
	3. **Fibrinolytic System**:
	    
	    - **Plasmin**: Cleaves fibrin, activates C3, generates vasoactive fragments.
	4. **Complement System**:
	    
	    - **Activation Pathways**:
	        - **Classical pathway**: Through antigen-antibody complexes.
	        - **Alternate pathway**: Via non-immunologic agents (bacterial toxins, IgA).
	        - **Lectin pathway**: Plasma mannose-binding lectin binds to microbes.
	    - **Functions**:
	        - **C3a, C4a, C5a (Anaphylatoxins)**: Activate mast cells/basophils to release histamine, cause **increased vascular permeability**, oedema, augment phagocytosis.
	        - **C3b**: Acts as an **opsonin** (enhances phagocytosis).
	        - **C5a**: Potent **chemotactic** for leukocytes.
	        - **Membrane Attack Complex (MAC) (C5b-C9)**: Forms pores in cell membranes, leading to cell/bacterial lysis.
	        - **Clinical Relevance**: **Hereditary angioedema** is associated with C1 inhibitor deficiency.
	
	---
	
	**III. Regulation of Inflammation**: Inflammation is self-limited and tightly regulated to prevent excessive tissue damage. Mechanisms include:
	
	- **Short Half-life**: Mediators are produced only as long as stimulus persists and are rapidly degraded.
	- **Acute Phase Reactants (APRs)**: Proteins like **C-reactive protein (CRP)** and **fibrinogen**. Protect normal cells from toxic molecules and clear waste. Fibrinogen contributes to **raised ESR**.
	- **Antiproteases**: (e.g., α1-antitrypsin, α2-macroglobulin) protect tissues from proteolytic damage.
	- **Antioxidants**: (e.g., ceruloplasmin, Vitamins E, A, C) eliminate excess oxygen free radicals.
	- **Endogenous Glucocorticoids**: Act as anti-inflammatory agents.
	- **Anti-inflammatory Mediators**: Resolvins and lipoxins inhibit inflammation. IL-10 and TGF-β also have anti-inflammatory actions.
	- **Free Cytokine Receptors**: Circulating receptors for cytokines correlate with disease activity.
	- **Neutrophil Apoptosis**: Neutrophils have a short lifespan and die by apoptosis.
4. What is the pathology of tuberculoid and lepromatous leprosy?
	Leprosy, also known as Hansen's disease, is a **chronic, granulomatous, slowly progressive infectious disease** caused by the bacillus _Mycobacterium leprae_. This organism closely resembles _Mycobacterium tuberculosis_ but is **less acid-fast**. It primarily affects cooler parts of the body, including the **skin, peripheral nerves, upper respiratory tract, eyes, and testes**. The disease spectrum is largely determined by the **host's immune response**, particularly the **cell-mediated immunity (TH1 vs. TH2 lymphocyte response)**.
	
	The Ridley and Jopling classification categorises leprosy into a spectrum with two polar forms: **Tuberculoid Leprosy (TT)**, representing high immune resistance, and **Lepromatous Leprosy (LL)**, representing low immune resistance.
	
	---
	
	### I. Tuberculoid Leprosy (TT)
	
	Represents the **high-resistance pole** of the disease spectrum, characterised by a **strong cell-mediated immune (TH1) response** which effectively limits bacillary proliferation.
	
	1. **Clinical Features:**
	    
	    - **Single or very few lesions**.
	    - Localized, **well-demarcated**, red or **hypopigmented** skin patches with raised outer edges and depressed pale centres.
	    - **Distinct sensory disturbance** is present due to significant nerve involvement.
	2. **Pathology:**
	    
	    - **Grossly**: Skin lesions are hyperpigmented at the margins with a pale centre. Small nerves may be destroyed.
	    - **Microscopically**:
	        - **Granulomas**: **Well-formed, circumscribed, non-caseating granulomas** (also called "hard tubercles") are characteristic.
	        - **Cellular Composition**: Composed of **epithelioid cells** (modified macrophages resembling epithelial cells, often with slipper-shaped vesicular nuclei), **Langhans' giant cells** (multinucleated giant cells with nuclei arranged in a horseshoe pattern), and a **peripheral mantle of lymphocytes**.
	        - **Epidermal Relation**: The granulomas **erode the basal layer of the epidermis**, meaning **no "clear zone"** (subepidermal uninvolved dermis) is present between the granuloma and the epidermis.
	        - **Nerve Involvement**: Lesions have a **predilection for dermal nerves**, which may be **destroyed and infiltrated by epithelioid cells and lymphocytes**. _Mycobacterium leprae_ is the only bacterium that invades peripheral nerves.
	        - **Bacillary Load**: **Lepra bacilli are few or absent**; they are generally not demonstrable with Fite-Faraco stain, hence it is considered a **"paucibacillary" type**.
	
	---
	
	### II. Lepromatous Leprosy (LL)
	
	Represents the **low-resistance pole** of the disease spectrum, characterised by a **weak or absent cell-mediated immune (TH1) response** leading to widespread bacillary proliferation.
	
	3. **Clinical Features:**
	    
	    - **Multiple, symmetrical, diffuse skin lesions**.
	    - Macular, papular, or nodular lesions; nodular lesions may coalesce to give a characteristic **"leonine facies"** (thickening of facial skin).
	    - Sensory disturbance is less severe compared to tuberculoid leprosy.
	4. **Pathology:**
	    
	    - **Grossly**: Development of **nodules or masses** in the skin, particularly on the face, hands, and feet. The skin lesions are often hypoesthetic or anesthetic.
	    - **Microscopically**:
	        - **Foamy Macrophages**: Contains large aggregates of **lipid-laden foamy macrophages**, known as **"lepra cells"** or **"Virchow cells"**. These cells are diagnostic of LL.
	        - **Bacillary Load**: Lepra cells are **heavily laden with acid-fast bacilli** (_M. leprae_). The bacilli may appear as compact globular masses (known as **"globi"**) or arranged in a parallel fashion resembling **"cigarettes-in-a-pack"**. This makes it a **"multibacillary" type**.
	        - **Epidermal Relation**: The dermal infiltrate of lepra cells **characteristically does not encroach upon the basal layer of the epidermis**, leaving an **uninvolved "clear zone"** or "Grenz zone" between the epidermis and the infiltrate.
	        - **Overlying Epidermis**: The overlying epidermis is often **thinned out and flat**, and may even ulcerate.
	
	---
	
	### III. Special Stains for _Mycobacterium leprae_
	
	_M. leprae_ can be demonstrated in tissue sections and smears using:
	
	- **Acid-fast (Ziehl-Neelsen or ZN) staining**: Similar to _M. tuberculosis_ staining, but requires a **lower concentration (5%) of sulphuric acid for decolourisation** as _M. leprae_ is less acid-fast.
	- **Fite-Faraco staining procedure**: A modification of the ZN procedure, considered better for more adequate staining of tissue sections.
	- **Gomori methenamine silver (GMS) staining**.

1. Factors influencing complications of wound healing and process of repair and regeneration
	The process of **healing** is the body's fundamental response to injury, aiming to restore normal structure and function. It primarily involves two interconnected processes: **regeneration** and **repair**.
	
	**I. Healing: Regeneration and Repair**
	
	1. **Regeneration**: This is the ideal outcome, involving the **restoration of original tissue** by the proliferation of parenchymal cells. It fully restores an injured or lost tissue to its original state.
	2. **Repair**: This involves **healing by the proliferation of connective tissue**, ultimately resulting in **fibrosis and scarring**. Repair occurs when tissue regeneration is not possible or insufficient, filling defects that cannot be healed by regeneration alone. While repair is a healing process, it can also cause tissue dysfunction.
	
	**II. Factors Influencing the Pattern of Healing (Regeneration vs. Repair)**
	
	The proportion of regeneration and repair depends on two main factors:
	
	3. **Proliferative Capacity of the Tissue**:
	    
	    - **Labile (Continuously Dividing) Tissues**: Cells proliferate throughout life, capable of replacing lost cells from stem cells. Healing predominantly occurs by regeneration.
	        - _Examples_: Haematopoietic cells of the bone marrow, surface epithelia of the skin, oral cavity, vagina, cervix, and gastrointestinal tract.
	    - **Stable Tissues**: Cells retain the capacity to proliferate but typically do so only in response to injury or loss of tissue.
	        - _Examples_: Parenchymal cells of solid organs (e.g., liver, kidneys, pancreas, endocrine glands), endothelial cells, and fibroblasts.
	    - **Permanent Tissues**: Cells lose their ability to proliferate after birth and cannot regenerate. Damage to these tissues results in healing solely by repair and scar formation.
	        - _Examples_: Neurons of the brain and spinal cord, and cardiac muscle cells. Limited stem cell replication may occur in some areas of the adult brain and heart.
	4. **Extent of Tissue Injury**:
	    
	    - **Mild and Short Duration Injury**: Damaged tissue is typically healed by **regeneration** with minimal or no significant scarring. This also requires the survival and integrity of the basement membrane and extracellular matrix (ECM) framework.
	    - **Severe and Chronic Injury**: Healing occurs by **fibrous tissue formation (scarring)**. This happens when severe tissue injury damages both parenchymal cells and the ECM framework, or in cases of chronic inflammation.
	
	**III. Factors Influencing Wound Healing (Cutaneous Wounds)**
	
	Wound healing, particularly of skin wounds, provides a classical example of a combination of regeneration and repair. Various factors can influence this process:
	
	**A. Local Factors**:
	
	5. **Factors that Delay Wound Healing**:
	    
	    - **Infection**: The **single most important cause for delay in healing**. It causes persistent tissue injury and inflammation.
	    - **Mechanical Factors**: Movement of the wounded area can compress blood vessels and separate wound edges, delaying healing.
	    - **Foreign Bodies**: Including unnecessary sutures, fragments of steel, glass, or bone, which interfere with healing and can cause intense inflammatory reactions and infection.
	    - **Size and Type of Wound**: Large excisional wounds or those caused by blunt trauma heal slower.
	    - **Location of Injury**: Wounds over skin covering bone with little intervening tissue (e.g., anterior tibia) prevent wound contraction and delay healing.
	    - **Blood Supply**: Poor blood supply slows healing (e.g., varicose ulcers of the legs). Bed sores (decubitus ulcers) result from prolonged pressure diminishing blood flow.
	    - **Ionising Radiation**: Decreases the repair process and delays granulation tissue formation.
	    - **Complications**: Existing complications of healing can further delay the process.
	6. **Factors that Favour Wound Healing**:
	    
	    - **Size and Type of Wound**: Small surgical incisions heal quickly with less scar formation.
	    - **Blood Supply**: Wounds in richly vascularised areas (e.g., face) heal faster than those with poor supply (e.g., foot).
	    - **Ultraviolet Light**: Exposure to UV light facilitates healing.
	
	**B. Systemic Factors**:
	
	7. **Nutritional Deficiencies**: Delay wound healing.
	    - **Protein deficiency**: (e.g., malnutrition).
	    - **Vitamin C deficiency**: Inhibits collagen synthesis and retards healing. Vitamin C is essential for collagen synthesis and acts as an antioxidant and reducing agent.
	    - **Trace elements**: Copper and zinc deficiency. Zinc acts as a cofactor in collagenase.
	    - **Vitamin A deficiency**.
	8. **Age**: Wound healing is more rapid in young individuals compared to the aged and debilitated, who may have poor blood supply.
	9. **Metabolic Status**: Conditions like **uncontrolled diabetes mellitus** are associated with delayed healing due to microangiopathy and increased susceptibility to infection.
	10. **Circulatory Status**: Inadequate blood supply (e.g., due to arteriosclerosis) or venous abnormalities (e.g., varicose veins) retard venous drainage and delay healing.
	11. **Hormones**: **Glucocorticoids** have anti-inflammatory effects and inhibit collagen synthesis, thereby impairing wound healing.
	12. **Haematological Abnormalities**: Defects in neutrophil functions (e.g., chemotaxis, phagocytosis), neutropenia, and bleeding disorders can slow the healing process.
	13. **Systemic Infection**: Delays wound healing.
	
	**IV. Complications of Wound Healing**
	
	During the course of healing, several complications can occur:
	
	14. **Inadequate Granulation Tissue Formation**: This can lead to:
	    
	    - **Wound Dehiscence**: The splitting open or rupture of a wound. It is the **most common life-threatening complication after abdominal surgery**, often due to increased abdominal pressure or mechanical stress (e.g., vomiting, coughing). It results from insufficient ECM deposition or inadequate collagen cross-linking.
	    - **Ulceration**: Wounds can ulcerate due to inadequate angiogenesis during healing (e.g., in patients with atherosclerotic peripheral vascular disease or varicose veins). Non-healing neuropathic/trophic ulcers may occur in regions devoid of sensation (e.g., diabetic peripheral neuropathy).
	    - **Incisional Hernia**: A weak scar, especially after laparotomy, that leads to a defect in the abdominal wall.
	15. **Excessive Scar Formation**:
	    
	    - **Hypertrophied Scars**: Raised scars that result from excessive accumulation of ECM, primarily collagen. They typically remain confined to the borders of the initial wound.
	    - **Keloid Formation**: An exuberant scar where the tissue grows beyond the boundaries of the original wound and does not regress. Keloids have tumour-like projections of connective tissue and are more common in individuals with darker skin. They may recur larger after surgical excision. This is due to excessive deposition of type III collagen.
	    - **Excessive Contraction (Contractures)**: An exaggeration of wound contraction, particularly in large surface wounds healed by secondary intention, mediated by **myofibroblasts** in granulation tissue. Common sites include palms (Dupuytren's contracture), soles, and anterior thorax. It can lead to obstruction (e.g., intestinal strictures).
	16. **Other Complications**:
	    
	    - **Infection**: Entry of bacteria into the wound.
	    - **Implantation (Epidermal) Cyst**: Formation due to the persistence of epithelial cells in the wound after healing.
	    - **Pigmentation**: Healed wounds may have a rust-like colour due to haemosiderin staining, or from retained particulate material.
	    - **Neoplasia**: Rarely, a scar may become the site for developing carcinoma later (e.g., **squamous cell carcinoma in Marjolin's ulcer**, a scar following burns).
	
	**V. Role of Key Cells and Mediators in Repair and Healing**
	
	- **Granulation Tissue**: A specialised tissue that is the **hallmark of healing wounds**. It is pink, soft, and granular. Microscopically, it consists of proliferating fibroblasts, new small blood vessels (angiogenesis), loose connective tissue, and scattered chronic inflammatory cells (mainly macrophages). The new capillaries are thin-walled and leaky, contributing to oedema.
	- **Angiogenesis (Neovascularisation)**: The process of forming new blood vessels from existing ones. It is essential for providing nutrients to healing tissues. Key growth factors include **Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF)** and **Fibroblast Growth Factor (FGF-2)**. Newly formed capillaries in neovascularisation are excessively leaky.
	- **Fibroblasts & Myofibroblasts**: Fibroblasts proliferate and produce collagen fibres, forming the scar. Myofibroblasts, with features intermediate between fibroblasts and smooth muscle cells, are crucial for **wound contraction**.
	- **Macrophages**: Essential in the repair process. They clear offending agents and dead tissue, provide growth factors for cell proliferation, and secrete cytokines that stimulate fibroblast proliferation and connective tissue synthesis.
	- **Extracellular Matrix (ECM)**: Provides structural support for fibroblasts and myofibroblasts. It comprises collagen, adhesive glycoproteins, basement membrane, elastic fibres, and proteoglycans. **Collagen types I, III, and V** are fibrillar and form the main portion of connective tissue in scars. ECM undergoes significant remodelling, regulated by matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and their inhibitors.
	- **Transforming Growth Factor-beta (TGF-$\beta$)**: A potent **fibrogenic agent** that stimulates ECM synthesis. It also regulates collagen synthesis by smooth muscle cells. TGF-$\beta$ and **IL-10** are anti-inflammatory cytokines involved in the termination of inflammation.
	- **Cytokines (e.g., IL-1, TNF-$\alpha$)**: Activate endothelial cells, recruit and activate leukocytes, induce fever, and contribute to systemic acute-phase reactions. In wound healing, they stimulate smooth muscle cell proliferation and ECM production.
	- **Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS)**: Generated by activated neutrophils and macrophages. While ROS destroy microbes, they can also cause **endothelial cell damage** (increasing vascular permeability), activate proteases, inactivate antiproteases, and damage other cells, contributing to **tissue damage during inflammation**. They are also produced in **ischaemia-reperfusion injury**, exacerbating damage.
	
	**VI. Wound Strength**
		The strength of a wound increases over time. After stitch removal (around 7th day), wound strength is approximately **10%** of normal unwounded skin. It rapidly increases over the next 4 weeks and reaches **70-80%** of the tensile strength of unwounded skin by about 3 months. This strength is primarily due to fibrillar collagens, mostly type I collagen.
	
	Wound healing by **first intention (primary union)** is the most straightforward and efficient form of cutaneous healing, typically occurring in **clean, uninfected, surgically incised wounds** with **minimal tissue loss** where the wound edges are **approximated by surgical sutures**. This process leads to a **neat linear scar**.
2. Mechanism of wound healing by first intention (primary union)
	
	### Mechanism of Healing by First Intention
	
	The process unfolds in a sequential manner, involving both **regeneration** (of epithelial cells) and **repair** (fibrosis/scar formation).
	
	1. **Initial Haemorrhage & Clot Formation (Immediately after injury)**:
	    
	    - The space between the approximated wound surfaces is immediately filled with **blood, which then clots**.
	    - This clot contains **fibrin, fibronectin, and complement components**.
	    - The clot serves to **seal the wound** against dehydration and infection, and acts as a **scaffold** for migrating cells.
	    - The external surface of the clot dries to form a **scab**.
	2. **Acute Inflammatory Response (Within 24 hours)**:
	    
	    - **Neutrophils** are the first inflammatory cells to appear at the margins of the incision, migrating into the clot. They release proteolytic enzymes to clear debris.
	    - By the **3rd day**, neutrophils are largely **replaced by macrophages**. Macrophages play a central role in clearing debris and initiating repair.
	3. **Epithelial Changes (Within 24-48 hours)**:
	    
	    - **Basal cells of the epidermis** at both cut margins begin to **proliferate and migrate** towards the incisional space, forming **epithelial spurs**.
	    - A continuous layer of **epithelium covers the wound in approximately 48 hours**.
	    - The regenerated epidermal cells separate the underlying viable dermis from the superficial necrotic material and clot, leading to the eventual shedding of the scab.
	    - By the **5th day**, a multilayered new epidermis is formed, which undergoes differentiation and keratinisation.
	4. **Organisation & Granulation Tissue Formation (By 3rd-7th day)**:
	    
	    - By the **3rd day**, **fibroblasts invade** the wound area.
	    - **New collagen fibrils begin to appear around the 5th day** and become dominant as healing progresses.
	    - Granulation tissue progressively fills the wound space.
	    - The acute inflammatory response begins to subside during this phase.
	5. **Scar Maturation & Remodelling (10 days to Weeks/Months)**:
	    
	    - By **10-14 days**, leukocytic infiltration, oedema, and angiogenesis diminish significantly.
	    - There is **increased accumulation of collagen**, converting the granulation tissue scaffolding into a **pale, avascular scar**.
	    - The wound's strength is approximately **10% of normal unwounded skin** when sutures are typically removed around the 7th day.
	    - Further collagen deposition and remodelling occur.
	    - The wound progressively gains strength, reaching **70-80% of its maximal tensile strength by about 3 months**. The mature scar appears as acellular connective tissue.
	6. **Suture Tracks (Clinical Relevance)**:
	    
	    - Each suture introduces a small, separate wound that undergoes a similar healing process (haemorrhage, inflammation, epithelial and fibroblastic proliferation).
	    - Upon suture removal (around 7th day), much of the epithelialised track is avulsed, and the remaining tissue is absorbed.
	    - **Complications**: Suture tracks can sometimes become infected (stitch abscess) or lead to **implantation (epidermal) cysts** if epithelial cells persist.
3. Define chemotaxis and give examples for chemotactic agents
	 **Chemotaxis**
	
	**Definition**:
	
	- Chemotaxis is the process of **migration of leucocytes toward the inflammatory stimulus** in the direction of the **gradient of locally produced chemoattractants**.
	- It describes the movement of leukocytes after crossing several barriers (endothelium, basement membrane, perivascular myofibroblasts, and matrix) to reach the interstitial tissues, mediated by chemotactic factors.
	
	**Mechanism/Concept**:
	
	- Leukocytes migrate through the vessel wall (transmigration or diapedesis) by squeezing through intercellular junctions between endothelial cells.
	- They then penetrate the basement membrane by secreting collagenases.
	- The concept is illustrated by **Boyden's chamber experiment**, where leukocytes migrate through a filter towards a chemotactic agent.
	
	**Chemoattractants (Chemotactic Agents)**: These substances are produced by microbes and host cells in response to infections, tissue damage, and during immunological reactions. They selectively attract various leukocytes to the site of inflammation.
	
	- **Exogenous**:
	    
	    - **Bacterial products**, such as **formylated peptides** (e.g., N-formylmethionine terminal amino acid).
	- **Endogenous**:
	    
	    - **Leukotriene B4 (LT-B4)**: A product of the lipooxygenase pathway of arachidonic acid metabolites. It is chemotactic for neutrophils, eosinophils, and monocytes.
	    - **Components of the complement system**: Specifically **C5a** and **C3a**. C3a and C5a are also known as anaphylatoxins.
	    - **Cytokines (Chemokines)**: Small proteins that act as chemoattractants for inflammatory cells.
	        - **Interleukins**, particularly **IL-8** (C-X-C chemokine).
	        - **Monocyte Chemoattractant Protein-1 (MCP-1)**: Chemoattractant for monocytes/macrophages, NK cells, and T cells.
	        - **Eotaxin**: Chemoattractant for eosinophils and basophils.
	        - Other chemokines include C-C chemokines, C chemokines, and CX3C chemokines.
	    - **Platelet-activating Factor (PAF)**: Causes increased leukocyte adhesion to endothelium and chemotaxis.
	
	**Clinical Significance**:
	
	- Chemotaxis is a **fundamental process in acute inflammatory response**.
	- Polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) comprise the first line of body defense in acute inflammation, followed by monocytes and macrophages, all guided by chemotaxis.

5. Granuloma- define , types with examples and mircoscopic appearance
	**Granuloma**
	
	**I. Definition**
	
	- A **granuloma** is a **circumscribed, tiny lesion** (approx. 1 mm in diameter) composed predominantly of a **collection of modified macrophages called epithelioid cells**, often rimmed at the periphery by lymphoid cells.
	- The term 'granuloma' indicates a **localised inflammatory mass** or collection of macrophages, rather than a true tumour.
	- Older granulomas may also show a rim of fibroblasts and connective tissue as the outermost layer.
	
	**II. Pathogenesis/Mechanism of Formation**
		- Formation of granuloma is typically a **Type IV (granulomatous) hypersensitivity reaction**.
		- It's a protective defense reaction, but can cause tissue destruction due to the persistence of a **poorly digestible antigen** (e.g., _Mycobacterium tuberculosis_, suture material).
		- Macrophages and monocytes engulf the antigen but fail to digest it, transforming into epithelioid cells. Activated T cells produce cytokines (IL-2, IFN-$\gamma$) that further activate macrophages, perpetuating the response.
	
	**III. Composition/Microscopic Appearance** A granuloma generally has the following structural components:
	
	1. **Epithelioid Cells**:
	    - **Modified macrophages** (histiocytes) named for their epithelial-like appearance.
	    - Elongated cells with **slipper-shaped, vesicular, lightly-staining nuclei** and pale pink granular cytoplasm with indistinct cell borders.
	    - Often appear to merge into one another.
	2. **Giant Cells**:
	    - Formed by the fusion of epithelioid cells, containing many small nuclei (20 or more).
	    - **Langhans’ Giant Cells**: Nuclei arranged around the periphery in a **horseshoe or ring pattern**, or clustered at the two poles. Characteristic of tuberculosis and sarcoidosis.
	    - **Foreign Body Giant Cells**: Nuclei are **scattered haphazardly** throughout the cytoplasm. Seen in chronic infective granulomas, leprosy, and tuberculosis.
	3. **Lymphoid Cells**:
	    - Mainly **T lymphocytes** (as a cell-mediated immune response), forming a peripheral mantle.
	    - Plasma cells may also be present in some types of granulomas.
	4. **Necrosis**:
	    - May or may not be present in the centre.
	    - **Caseation Necrosis**: A distinctive form, resembling **dry, soft, granular, yellowish cheese** grossly. Microscopically, it's structureless, eosinophilic material with granular debris and complete obliteration of tissue architecture. **Characteristic of tuberculosis**.
	    - **Non-caseating (Hard) Granulomas**: Lack necrotic centres. Seen in Crohn's disease, sarcoidosis, and foreign body reactions.
	5. **Fibrosis**:
	    - A feature of healing, with proliferating fibroblasts at the periphery. Older granulomas may show extensive fibrosis.
	
	**IV. Types and Examples of Granulomatous Inflammation** Granulomatous inflammation is a typical reaction to poorly digestible agents.
	
	6. **Foreign Body Granulomas**:
	    - Caused by inert foreign bodies that are too large to be phagocytosed by a single macrophage (e.g., suture material, talc, starch, keratin, cholesterol, parasites).
	    - The foreign material can often be seen in the centre, sometimes refractile under polarized light.
	    - Examples: Exogenous lipid pneumonia (around lipid droplets).
	7. **Immune Granulomas**:
	    - Caused by agents/microbes that induce a persistent T-cell-mediated immune response and are difficult to eradicate.
	    - Examples (with key differentiating features):
	        - **Tuberculosis (TB)**:
	            - **Etiologic agent**: _Mycobacterium tuberculosis_ (acid-fast bacilli).
	            - **Microscopy**: Classic **tubercle** with **central caseation necrosis** surrounded by epithelioid cells, Langhans' giant cells, and a peripheral mantle of lymphocytes. AFB demonstration via Ziehl-Neelsen (ZN) stain is confirmatory.
	        - **Leprosy**:
	            - **Etiologic agent**: _Mycobacterium leprae_ (acid-fast bacilli).
	            - **Microscopy**: In **Tuberculoid Leprosy**, non-caseating granulomas with epithelioid cells, Langhans' giant cells, and lymphocytes; few bacilli. In **Lepromatous Leprosy**, lipid-laden foamy macrophages (**lepra cells/Virchow cells**) filled with aggregates of bacilli (**globi**) with a "cigarettes-in-a-pack" appearance. Perineural inflammation is characteristic. Fite-Faraco stain for bacilli.
	        - **Sarcoidosis**:
	            - **Etiology**: Unknown.
	            - **Microscopy**: **Non-caseating "naked" granulomas** (devoid/paucity of peripheral lymphocytes). May contain cytoplasmic inclusions in giant cells: **Asteroid bodies, Schaumann’s bodies (conchoid bodies), and birefringent crystals**.
	        - **Syphilis**:
	            - **Etiologic agent**: _Treponema pallidum_.
	            - **Microscopy**: Lesion termed **gumma**. Features **central coagulative necrosis** (less destructive than caseation, outlines of necrosed cells faintly visible), surrounded by palisaded macrophages, plasma cells, lymphocytes, and giant cells. Treponemes are scant.
	        - **Actinomycosis**:
	            - **Etiologic agent**: _Actinomyces_ species (bacterial colony, 'sulphur granule').
	            - **Microscopy**: Granuloma with **central suppuration (abscess formation)**, surrounded by chronic inflammatory cells, giant cells, and fibroblasts. The 'sulphur granule' shows radiating filaments.
	        - **Crohn's Disease**:
	            - **Microscopy**: **Non-caseating, sarcoid-like granulomas** in affected bowel wall (in 60% of cases) and regional lymph nodes.
	        - **Cat-scratch Disease**:
	            - **Etiologic agent**: Gram-negative bacillus.
	            - **Microscopy**: Rounded or stellate granuloma with central granular debris and neutrophils; giant cells are rare.
	        - **Idiopathic Granulomatous Myocarditis**:
	            - **Microscopy**: Non-caseating granulomas with macrophages, lymphocytes, plasma cells, and multinucleate giant cells (foreign body/Langhans' type). No acid-fast bacilli or spirochaetes.
	        - **Eosinophilic Granuloma (Langerhans cell histiocytosis)**:
	            - **Microscopy**: Closely-packed aggregates of macrophages admixed with variable number of **eosinophils**.
	        - **Granuloma Pyogenicum**:
	            - **Microscopy**: Proliferating capillaries separated by abundant oedema and inflammatory infiltrate, resembling inflammatory granulation tissue.
	
	**V. Clinical Significance**
	
	- Granulomas are a hallmark of **chronic inflammation** and a key diagnostic feature for several important infectious (e.g., TB, leprosy) and non-infectious diseases (e.g., sarcoidosis, Crohn's disease).
	- Identification and characterisation of granulomas by histopathology, often with special stains (e.g., ZN, Fite-Faraco, GMS), is crucial for diagnosis and guiding treatment.
6. Ghon's lesion (primary complex - components)
	**Ghon's Lesion (Primary Complex)**
	
	**I. Definition**
	
	- **Primary Tuberculosis (Primary Complex/Ghon's Complex/Childhood Tuberculosis)** is the initial infection in an individual not previously infected or immunised.
	- It refers to the **lesion produced at the portal of entry** (e.g., lung) along with **foci in the draining lymphatic vessels and regional lymph nodes**.
	- It is considered the **classical example of granulomatous inflammation** due to _Mycobacterium tuberculosis_.
	
	**II. Components of Ghon's Complex (in Lungs)** In the lungs (most common site), the Ghon's complex consists of three components:
	
	1. **Pulmonary Component (Ghon's Focus)**
	    
	    - The primary focus in the lung.
	    - Typically a **1-2 cm solitary area of tuberculous pneumonia**.
	    - Usually located **peripherally**, often subpleurally, in any part of the lung (more commonly upper part of lower lobe).
	2. **Lymphatic Vessel Component**
	    
	    - Refers to the **lymphatics draining the lung lesion**.
	    - These contain phagocytes with bacilli and may develop **beaded, miliary tubercles** along the path to the hilar lymph nodes.
	3. **Lymph Node Component**
	    
	    - Consists of **enlarged hilar and tracheo-bronchial lymph nodes** in the drained area.
	    - Affected lymph nodes are often **matted and show caseation necrosis**.
	    - Nodal lesions are a potential source of re-infection later.
	
	**III. Common Locations**
	
	- **Most commonly involved**: Lungs and hilar lymph nodes.
	- **Other sites for primary complex**: Tonsils and cervical lymph nodes (ingested bacilli); small intestine and mesenteric lymph nodes.
	
	**IV. Microscopic Appearance (of lymph node component)**
	
	- The necrotic foci are composed of **structureless, eosinophilic, granular debris** which may contain foci of dystrophic calcification.
	- The surrounding area shows a characteristic **granulomatous inflammatory reaction** consisting of **slipper-shaped epithelioid cells** with interspersed **Langhans’ giant cells** and a peripheral mantle of lymphocytes. This entire structure is often called a 'tubercle'.
	
	**V. Clinical Relevance**
	
	- Identification of a Ghon's complex indicates **primary exposure to _Mycobacterium tuberculosis_**.
	- The incidence of **disseminated progressive primary tuberculosis is particularly high in immunocompromised hosts**, such as patients with AIDS.

7. Tuberculoid leprosy-morphology
	**Tuberculoid Leprosy (TT)**
	
	**I. General Characteristics**
	
	- Represents the **polar form with maximal host immune response** (high resistance).
	- It is considered the **less severe form** of leprosy.
	- Characterized by a **strong cell-mediated immunity** mediated largely by **CD4+ T cells**.
	- Clinically, there is typically **distinct sensory impairment** in affected areas [61ii].
	
	**II. Gross Appearance**
	
	- **Skin Lesions**:
	    - Usually presents as **single or very few lesions**, which are asymmetrical [50, 61i, 422].
	    - Appear as **localized, well-demarcated, hypopigmented and erythematous skin patches** [50, 61i, 422].
	    - Often have **raised outer edges and depressed pale centers** (indicating central healing).
	    - Commonly found on the face, extremities, or trunk.
	    - The margins may be hyperpigmented.
	
	**III. Microscopic Appearance**
	
	- **Granulomas**:
	    - Features **well-formed, circumscribed, non-caseating granulomas** (absence of central necrosis) [423, 642i].
	    - These granulomas are described as resembling **"hard tubercles"** similar to those found in tuberculosis [56i, 642i].
	    - Composed predominantly of **epithelioid cells** (modified macrophages), often with **Langhans' giant cells** (multinucleated giant cells with nuclei arranged in a horseshoe or ring pattern), and a **peripheral mantle of lymphocytes** [56i, 423].
	- **Epidermal-Dermal Interface**:
	    - The granulomatous infiltrate in the dermis **extends to and erodes the basal layer of the epidermis** [56iii, 642ii].
	    - A key differentiating feature is the **absence of a clear subepidermal zone** between the granuloma and the epidermis [51, 56iii, 423, 642ii].
	- **Nerve Involvement**:
	    - A strong predilection for and characteristic **infiltration and destruction of dermal nerves** by epithelioid cells and lymphocytes [56ii, 642iii].
	    - **Perineural inflammation** by lymphocytes is a typical finding.
	- **Bacteriology (Acid-Fast Bacilli - AFB)**:
	    - **Lepra bacilli are few or almost never found** in tissue sections [51, 56iv, 423, 642i].
	    - This low bacterial load leads to its classification as **paucibacillary leprosy**.
	    - While **Ziehl-Neelsen (ZN) staining** and **Fite-Faraco staining** (a modification of ZN, considered better for tissue sections) can be used, bacilli are generally scant or absent.
	
	---
	
	**Key Differentiating Points (from sources):**
	
	- **No Clear Zone**: Granulomas erode the basal layer of the epidermis [51, 56iii, 423, 642ii].
	- **Non-caseating Granulomas**: Lack central necrosis [423, 642i].
	- **Paucibacillary**: Few to no bacilli on Fite-Faraco stain [51, 423, 642i].
	- **High Resistance**: Reflects strong cell-mediated immunity.
	- **Positive Lepromin Test**: Indicates good immune response.

	Based on the clinical presentation and ultrasound findings, this is a case of **Acute Appendicitis**.
	
	
	**a. What type of inflammation is seen in this condition?**
	
	The condition observed in acute appendicitis is a form of **acute inflammation**. More specifically, it is characterised as:
	
	- **Suppurative or Purulent Inflammation**: This involves the production of a large amount of pus or purulent exudate. In acute appendicitis, the serosa may be coated with a **fibrinopurulent exudate**, and microscopically, the most important diagnostic feature is **neutrophilic infiltration of the muscularis**. Pus itself is composed of liquefied necrotic cell debris, dead leukocytes, and macrophages.
	- **Gangrenous Inflammation**: In advanced cases, the inflammation can progress to **acute gangrenous appendicitis**, where there is necrosis and ulceration of the mucosa extending through the wall, leading to the appendix becoming soft, friable, and coated with greenish-black gangrenous necrosis.
	
	**b. Discuss the cellular events occurring in this condition.**
	
	The cellular events in acute inflammation, such as seen in acute appendicitis, primarily involve the rapid recruitment and activity of leukocytes. These include:
	
	- **Exudation of Leukocytes**: This is the most important feature of the inflammatory response.
	    - **Margination and Rolling**: Leukocytes (primarily polymorphonuclear neutrophils, PMNs) slow down and accumulate along the endothelial surface of microvasculature.
	    - **Adhesion**: Leukocytes adhere to the activated endothelial cells.
	    - **Transmigration (Diapedesis)**: Leukocytes actively move through the vessel walls into the interstitial tissue. PMNs are the first line of body defense, followed later by monocytes and macrophages.
	- **Chemotaxis**: The migrating leukocytes are attracted to the site of injury by chemical gradients.
	- **Phagocytosis**: Once at the site of inflammation, neutrophils, and later monocytes/macrophages, engage in phagocytosis to engulf and degrade the inciting microorganisms (bacteria) and cellular debris.
	- **Neutrophilic Infiltration and Abscess Formation**: In acute appendicitis, **neutrophilic infiltration of the muscularis** is a key diagnostic histological criterion. The resulting tissue necrosis from the bacterial infection produces a liquid viscous material called pus, leading to the formation of abscesses.
	
	**c. List the chemical mediators that facilitate the cellular events.**
	
	Chemical mediators play a crucial role in orchestrating the cellular events of acute inflammation, particularly leukocyte recruitment and phagocytosis. Key mediators include:
	
	- **Chemokines**: These are small proteins that act as chemotactic cytokines, selectively attracting various leukocytes (including neutrophils) to the site of inflammation and promoting their recruitment.
	- **Cytokines (e.g., Interleukin-1 (IL-1), Tumour Necrosis Factor (TNF))**:
	    - They induce the expression of **endothelial adhesion molecules**, which are essential for the margination and firm adhesion of leukocytes to the endothelium before transmigration.
	    - They are also **endogenous pyrogens**, contributing to systemic features like fever.
	- **Platelet-Activating Factor (PAF)**: This mediator has multiple inflammatory effects, including increasing **leukocyte adhesion to the endothelium** and promoting **chemotaxis**.
	- **Complement System Products**: Activated components of the complement system act as mediators of inflammation. Specifically, C5a is a potent chemotactic agent for leukocytes. Mannose-binding lectin and collectins also bind to microbes and promote phagocytosis.
	- **Leukotriene B4 (LTB4)**: While not explicitly detailed for its role in cellular events in the given sources, Leukotrienes are part of the lipooxygenase pathway and are known to be potent chemotactic agents for neutrophils (External information to provide context for a known mediator).
	- **Bacterial Products**: Components of bacterial cell walls (e.g., N-formyl methyl peptides) directly act as chemoattractants for leukocytes.
