1. Classify antihypertensives with examples for each group. Explain the MOA, therapeutic uses and adverse effects of ACE inhibitors. Mention the drugs used to treat hypertensive crisis
	 **Antihypertensive Drugs**
	
	**I. Classification of Antihypertensive Drugs (with Examples):** Antihypertensive drugs are categorized based on their mechanism of action and chemical structure to lower blood pressure (BP).
	
	1. **Diuretics**:
	    
	    - **Thiazides and related agents:** Hydrochlorothiazide, Chlorthalidone, Indapamide.
	    - **Loop diuretics:** Furosemide, Bumetanide, Torsemide.
	    - **Potassium-sparing diuretics (Aldosterone antagonists):** Spironolactone, Eplerenone.
	    - **Direct inhibitors of renal epithelial Na+ channels:** Amiloride, Triamterene.
	2. **Drugs Affecting Renin-Angiotensin System (RAS) Inhibitors**:
	    
	    - **Angiotensin Converting Enzyme (ACE) Inhibitors:** Captopril, Enalapril, Lisinopril, Ramipril, Perindopril, Fosinopril, Trandolapril.
	    - **Angiotensin Receptor Blockers (ARBs):** Losartan, Candesartan, Valsartan, Telmisartan, Irbesartan, Olmesartan, Eprosartan.
	    - **Direct Renin Inhibitor:** Aliskiren.
	3. **Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)**:
	    
	    - **Phenylalkylamine:** Verapamil.
	    - **Benzothiazepine:** Diltiazem.
	    - **Dihydropyridines (DHPs):** Nifedipine, Amlodipine, Cilnidipine, Nicardipine, Benidipine, Isradipine, Felodipine, Lacidipine, Lercanidipine.
	4. **Sympatholytics**:
	    
	    - **Centrally acting sympatholytics:** Clonidine, α-Methyldopa.
	    - **β-Adrenergic blockers (β-blockers):** Propranolol, Atenolol, Metoprolol, Esmolol, Bisoprolol, Timolol, Nadolol, Pindolol, Sotalol.
	    - **β-Adrenergic blockers with additional α-blocking activity:** Labetalol, Carvedilol, Nebivolol.
	    - **α-Adrenergic blockers:**
	        - **Selective α1-blockers:** Prazosin, Terazosin, Doxazosin, Silodosin.
	        - **Nonselective α-blockers:** Phenoxybenzamine, Phentolamine.
	5. **Vasodilators**:
	    
	    - **Arteriolar dilators:** Hydralazine, Minoxidil, Diazoxide, Fenoldopam.
	    - **Primarily venodilator:** Nitroglycerin.
	    - **Arteriolar and venodilator:** Sodium Nitroprusside.
	
	**II. Angiotensin Converting Enzyme (ACE) Inhibitors:**
	
	- **Mechanism of Action (MOA):**
	    
	    - ACE inhibitors **inhibit the conversion of Angiotensin I to Angiotensin II** by blocking the Angiotensin Converting Enzyme (ACE).
	    - This leads to a **decrease in Angiotensin II levels**, which has several downstream effects:
	        - **Vasodilation:** Reduced Angiotensin II leads to **dilatation of arterioles**, which **decreases peripheral vascular resistance (PVR)** and **afterload**, thereby **lowering BP** and **improving tissue perfusion**.
	        - **Reduced Aldosterone Production:** Decreased Angiotensin II leads to a **reduction in aldosterone production**, which in turn **reduces sodium (Na+) and water retention**, thereby **decreasing preload**.
	        - **Decreased Sympathetic Activity:** ACE inhibitors reduce sympathetic nervous system activity.
	    - They also **inhibit the degradation of Bradykinin**, a potent vasodilator, by ACE. The accumulation of bradykinin **stimulates the synthesis of vasodilating prostaglandins**, further contributing to their antihypertensive effect.
	    - ACE inhibitors **retard/reverse pathological ventricular hypertrophy and vascular remodelling** by decreasing Angiotensin II and aldosterone levels.
	- **Therapeutic Uses:**
	    
	    - **Hypertension (HTN):** ACE inhibitors are **first-line drugs for all grades of hypertension**, including essential and renovascular hypertension. They are particularly suitable for **diabetic hypertensives** (delay/prevent renal complications), patients with **Left Ventricular (LV) hypertrophy**, and those with **congestive heart failure (CHF)** or **post-myocardial infarction (MI)**. They reduce cardiovascular morbidity and mortality and can prevent type 2 diabetes in high-risk individuals.
	    - **Congestive Heart Failure (CHF):** They are **standard therapy for all grades of CHF** (NYHA class I to IV) and asymptomatic LV systolic dysfunction. They improve symptoms, decrease hospitalisation, slow disease progression, and reduce mortality.
	    - **Acute Myocardial Infarction (MI):** Administered early (within 24 hours) and continued for at least 6 weeks (or longer in high-risk patients), they **reduce early and long-term mortality, reinfarction**, and the risk of developing heart failure.
	    - **Diabetic Nephropathy:** They are preferred drugs in both hypertensive and normotensive diabetics to **decrease systemic BP, dilate renal efferent arterioles** (reducing intraglomerular pressure), and **decrease microalbuminuria**, thereby preventing or delaying end-stage renal disease.
	    - **Scleroderma Renal Crisis:** ACE inhibitors provide **dramatic improvement** and are life-saving in this condition by preventing Angiotensin II-mediated effects.
	- **Adverse Effects:**
	    
	    - **Cough:** A **dry, persistent cough** is the most common side effect, occurring in 5-20% of patients, and is due to bradykinin accumulation in the lungs.
	    - **Angioedema:** A serious but rare swelling of the face, lips, tongue, and larynx, also thought to be bradykinin-mediated.
	    - **Hypotension:** **First-dose hypotension** can occur, especially in volume-depleted patients or those with CHF.
	    - **Hyperkalaemia:** Risk increases in patients with renal insufficiency or when combined with potassium-sparing diuretics.
	    - **Teratogenicity:** **Contraindicated in pregnancy** due to risk of foetal hypotension, renal failure, and neonatal death.
	    - **Renal Dysfunction:** Can worsen renal function in patients with bilateral renal artery stenosis.
	    - Other less common effects include neutropenia, rashes, itching, and loss of taste sensation (dysgeusia).
	
	**III. Drugs Used to Treat Hypertensive Crisis (Emergencies and Urgencies):** Hypertensive emergency is a medical emergency with very high BP (systolic ≥180 and/or diastolic ≥120 mm Hg) and evidence of acute target organ damage, requiring rapid BP reduction (25% over minutes to 1-2 hours) usually via parenteral drugs. Hypertensive urgency lacks acute target organ damage and allows for a more gradual reduction over hours.
	
	**Parenteral Drugs (for Hypertensive Emergencies):**
	
	- **Nicardipine:** A DHP CCB, highly vasoselective, primarily dilates arterioles. Preferred due to rapid onset, titratable action, and less toxicity than nitroprusside.
	- **Sodium Nitroprusside:** A powerful arteriolar and venodilator with instantaneous onset and titratable action. Used in aortic dissection (combined with a β-blocker) and acute hypertensive heart failure.
	- **Labetalol:** A combined α and β-blocker. Efficacious hypotensive agent when given IV, with low risk of excessive hypotension. Useful in hyperadrenergic states (e.g., pheochromocytoma, clonidine withdrawal, cocaine abuse, eclampsia) and aortic dissection, MI, or ischaemic stroke.
	- **Esmolol:** A short-acting, selective β1-blocker. Useful when cardiac contractility and work need to be reduced, such as in aortic dissection, or MI/ACS with raised BP and tachycardia.
	- **Furosemide:** A high-ceiling diuretic, used as an adjunct, especially with vasodilators, if there is volume overload (e.g., acute LVF, pulmonary oedema, cerebral oedema).
	- **Phentolamine:** A non-selective α-blocker. An alternative for hyperadrenergic states like pheochromocytoma or cheese reaction, acts rapidly.
	- **Hydralazine:** An arteriolar dilator, occasionally used in hypertensive emergencies, especially for preeclampsia/eclampsia.
	- _Other drugs (not available in India per sources):_ Fenoldopam (dopamine agonist), Clevidipine (ultrashort-acting DHP), Enalaprilat (parenteral ACE inhibitor).
	
	**Oral Drugs (for Hypertensive Urgencies):**
	
	- **Labetalol:** Moderate potency, starts acting in 2-4 hours. Suitable for preeclampsia, pheochromocytoma, stroke, and ischaemic heart disease.
	- **Amlodipine:** A slow and long-acting DHP. Suitable for severe hypertension without impending target organ damage.
	- **Captopril:** A non-prodrug, rapidly acting ACE inhibitor, though its response is unpredictable.
	- **Clonidine:** Lowers BP within a few hours but causes sedation and can lead to rebound hypertension on stopping.
	- _Note: Oral/sublingual immediate-release nifedipine capsules are **abandoned** for hypertensive urgencies due to safety concerns._

2. Calcium channel blockers, their Cardiovascular actions, uses and adverse effects
	**Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)**
	
	**I. Classification of CCBs:** CCBs are classified based on their chemical structure and predominant site of action.
	
	1. **Phenylalkylamine:**
	    - **Verapamil** – **Predominant action on the heart**.
	2. **Benzothiazepine:**
	    - **Diltiazem** – **Balances cardiac and vascular effects**.
	3. **Dihydropyridines (DHPs):**
	    - **Nifedipine** (prototype), **Amlodipine**, Cilnidipine, Nicardipine, Felodipine, Isradipine, Lacidipine, Lercanidipine, Benidipine, Nitrendipine, Nisoldipine.
	    - **Potent arteriolar dilators** with **minimal direct cardiac effects at therapeutic doses**.
	
	**II. Mechanism of Action (MOA):**
	
	- CCBs **block voltage-sensitive L-type Ca2+ channels** by binding to their α1-subunit.
	- This prevents the entry of Ca2+ ions into cardiac and smooth muscle cells.
	- They primarily bind to the **open and inactivated states** of the channel, minimally to the resting state.
	- This interference with Ca2+ influx disrupts excitation-contraction coupling, leading to muscle relaxation.
	
	**III. Cardiovascular Actions (Pharmacological Actions):**
	
	CCBs primarily act on cardiac and smooth muscles, with little action on veins, thus **not significantly altering preload** directly.
	
	1. **On Vascular Smooth Muscle:**
	    
	    - All CCBs cause **relaxation of vascular smooth muscle**, especially arterioles.
	    - This leads to a **decrease in Peripheral Vascular Resistance (PVR)** and **afterload**, thereby **lowering Blood Pressure (BP)**.
	    - They also cause **coronary vasodilatation**, increasing blood flow to ischemic areas.
	    - **DHPs** (e.g., Nifedipine, Amlodipine) are the **most potent arteriolar dilators**.
	    - **Verapamil** and **Diltiazem** also dilate arterioles but are less potent vasodilators than DHPs.
	2. **On the Heart:**
	    
	    - **Verapamil & Diltiazem** (non-DHPs):
	        - **Negative Chronotropic Effect:** Decrease heart rate by depressing the SA node.
	        - **Negative Dromotropic Effect:** Slow AV conduction by prolonging the effective refractory period (ERP) of the AV node.
	        - **Negative Inotropic Effect:** Decrease the force of myocardial contractility.
	        - These effects reduce oxygen requirement of the myocardium.
	        - **Clinical Relevance:** Useful in arrhythmias involving the AV node, but **caution in patients with cardiac dysfunction/heart failure** due to cardiac depressant effects.
	    - **Dihydropyridines (DHPs):**
	        - At therapeutic doses, have **minimal direct depressant effects on the heart** (SA node, AV conduction, contractility).
	        - However, rapid-acting DHPs (e.g., immediate-release Nifedipine) can cause **reflex tachycardia** and palpitation due to marked peripheral vasodilation and subsequent sympathetic activation. This reflex can be minimized by sustained-release preparations or co-administration with a β-blocker.
	        - **Cilnidipine** is notable for inhibiting N-type calcium channels in addition to L-type, which is claimed to **reduce reflex tachycardia**.
	
	**IV. Therapeutic Uses:**
	
	3. **Hypertension:**
	    - CCBs (all classes) are **first-line drugs for all grades of hypertension**.
	    - **DHPs** are preferred, especially in **elderly patients** and those with **associated conditions** like angina, asthma, peripheral vascular disease, migraine, hyperlipidaemia, diabetes, and renal dysfunction.
	    - They reduce albuminuria and slow nephropathy progression in diabetics.
	    - **Intravenous clevidipine** (a DHP) is used in **hypertensive emergencies**. **Nicardipine** is also popular for hypertensive emergencies.
	4. **Angina Pectoris:**
	    - Effective in reducing frequency and severity of **classical (stable) angina** by reducing cardiac work (afterload reduction, decreased BP x HR product).
	    - **Variant (Prinzmetal's) angina** is effectively treated prophylactically as CCBs attenuate coronary vasospasm.
	    - Used in **unstable angina** when symptoms persist despite nitrates/β-blockers, or when β-blockers are contraindicated.
	5. **Cardiac Arrhythmias:**
	    - **Verapamil & Diltiazem** are highly effective in **Paroxysmal Supraventricular Tachycardia (PSVT)** and for **controlling ventricular rate in atrial flutter and fibrillation** due to their depressant action on SA and AV nodes.
	6. **Raynaud's Phenomenon:**
	    - DHPs (e.g., Nifedipine, Amlodipine, Felodipine, Diltiazem) are used to **reduce severity of vasospastic episodes**.
	7. **Premature Labour:**
	    - **Nifedipine** is an alternative drug used as a uterine relaxant.
	8. **Subarachnoid Haemorrhage (SAH):**
	    - **Nimodipine** specifically dilates cerebral blood vessels and is used to **prevent cerebral vasospasm** and subsequent neurological deficits following SAH.
	9. **Hypertrophic Obstructive Cardiomyopathy:**
	    - The negative inotropic action of **Verapamil** can be beneficial.
	
	**V. Adverse Effects:**
	
	- **Common effects (especially with DHPs) due to vasodilatation:**
	    
	    - **Headache**.
	    - **Flushing**.
	    - **Ankle Oedema** (common with DHPs).
	    - **Palpitation**.
	    - **Reflex Tachycardia** (more with rapid-acting DHPs, Nifedipine).
	    - Hypotension.
	    - Dizziness, Fatigue, Sedation.
	- **Effects specific to Verapamil/Diltiazem (non-DHPs) due to cardiac depression:**
	    
	    - **Bradycardia**.
	    - **AV Block**.
	    - **Precipitation of Congestive Cardiac Failure (CCF)**, especially in patients with low cardiac reserve or existing cardiac dysfunction.
	    - **Constipation** (more common with Verapamil).
	- **Contraindications/Cautions:**
	    
	    - Verapamil and Diltiazem should be **avoided in patients with cardiac dysfunction** or **severe heart failure**.
	    - **Combination of Verapamil/Diltiazem with β-blockers should be avoided** due to additive cardiac depressant effects (risk of heart block, severe bradycardia, CCF).
	    - Short-acting DHPs (e.g., immediate-release nifedipine) are **abandoned** for hypertensive urgencies due to safety concerns (e.g., increased mortality in MI patients).
3. Treatment of hypertensive emergency
	 **Treatment of Hypertensive Emergency**
	
	**I. Definition & Goals:**
	
	- **Hypertensive Emergency:** Severe elevation of blood pressure (BP), typically **systolic ≥180 mmHg and/or diastolic ≥120 mmHg**, accompanied by **acute, progressive target organ damage** (e.g., hypertensive encephalopathy, acute renal dysfunction, retinopathy, acute LVF). This is a **medical emergency** requiring immediate intervention.
	- **Distinction from Hypertensive Urgency:** Same BP elevation but _without_ acute target organ damage. Urgencies can often be managed with oral agents over hours to days.
	- **Treatment Goal:** **Gradual reduction of BP** to prevent vital organ hypoperfusion.
	    - Reduce **Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP) by no more than 25% within the first hour**.
	    - Then, gradually lower BP to approximately **160/100 mmHg over the next 2-6 hours**.
	    - Achieve normal BP over the subsequent 24-48 hours.
	    - **Clinical Relevance:** Rapid or drastic BP reduction can lead to severe consequences like renal impairment, myocardial ischaemia, cerebral infarction, or blindness.
	
	**II. Clinical Scenarios Requiring Parenteral Therapy:**
	
	Parenteral (preferably IV) drugs are essential for these life-threatening conditions:
	
	- **Cerebrovascular Events:** Haemorrhagic or ischaemic stroke, head injury with high BP.
	- **Hypertensive Encephalopathy:** Headache, confusion, disorientation, mental deterioration.
	- **Acute Cardiac Issues:** Acute Left Ventricular Failure (LVF) with pulmonary oedema, Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS) or Myocardial Infarction (MI) with raised BP.
	- **Vascular Catastrophes:** Dissecting aortic aneurysm.
	- **Renal Failure:** Acute renal failure with raised BP.
	- **Obstetric Emergencies:** Eclampsia.
	- **Hyperadrenergic States:** Hypertensive episodes in pheochromocytoma, "cheese reaction" (with MAO inhibitors), or clonidine withdrawal.
	
	**III. Parenteral Drugs of Choice for Hypertensive Emergencies:**
	
	These agents are selected for their **fast, predictable, titratable, and short-acting** profiles.
	
	1. **Nicardipine:**
	    
	    - **Class:** Dihydropyridine (DHP) Calcium Channel Blocker (CCB).
	    - **Action:** Highly vasoselective, primarily causes **arteriolar dilatation**, leading to decreased peripheral resistance and BP.
	    - **Clinical Relevance:** Has become the **most popular drug** for various hypertensive emergencies, often replacing nitroprusside.
	    - **Specific Uses:** Aortic dissection, acute heart failure, acute renal failure (due to high BP), **preeclampsia**, and hypertension during cardiac/neurosurgery.
	    - **Side Effects:** Generally non-serious, mostly due to vasodilation.
	    - **Caution:** Reflex tachycardia may occur; **co-administration with a β-blocker (e.g., esmolol or labetalol) is recommended** when used in MI or ACS emergencies to offset reflex tachycardia.
	    - **Dose:** Initial 5 mg/hour IV infusion, titrated up to 15 mg/hour.
	2. **Sodium Nitroprusside:**
	    
	    - **Class:** Balanced arteriolar and venodilator; releases Nitric Oxide (NO).
	    - **Action:** **Instantaneous and potent** action. Reduces total peripheral resistance (TPR) and cardiac output (CO).
	    - **Modern Use:** Now a **second-line drug** due to availability of less toxic alternatives. **Primary indication: aortic dissection (often combined with esmolol)**. Can be used with loop diuretics in acute hypertensive heart failure.
	    - **Cautions:** Requires **precise infusion pump and intra-arterial BP monitoring**.
	    - **Toxicity:** Metabolized to **cyanide and thiocyanate**, which can cause CNS and other toxicities (e.g., anorexia, nausea, disorientation, psychosis, convulsions, lactic acidosis with prolonged use).
	    - **Contraindications:** Better **avoided in ischaemic stroke** (may compromise cerebral blood flow) and **haemorrhagic stroke** (may increase intracranial pressure). Also, it is light-sensitive and requires the infusion bottle to be covered.
	3. **Glyceryl Trinitrate (GTN) / Nitroglycerin:**
	    
	    - **Class:** Organic Nitrate, primarily a **venodilator**.
	    - **Action:** Acts within 2-5 minutes. Reduces **preload** by venous pooling.
	    - **Potency:** Not a potent hypotensive on its own.
	    - **Specific Uses:** Particularly suitable for **MI or other ACS** and **acute heart failure** accompanied by a rise in BP.
	    - **Combination:** For substantial BP lowering, it usually needs to be **combined with IV labetalol or nicardipine**.
	    - **Cautions:** **Tolerance can develop** with continuous infusion lasting over 12 hours. **Contraindicated if the patient has taken sildenafil in the past 24 hours** due to dangerous potentiation of hypotensive effects. Avoid in severe hypertension with ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke.
	4. **Labetalol:**
	    
	    - **Class:** Combined **α and β adrenergic blocker** (non-selective β, selective α1).
	    - **Action:** Efficacious hypotensive agent with a low risk of causing excessive hypotension. Reduces TPR and acts faster than pure β-blockers.
	    - **Specific Uses:** Highly useful in **pheochromocytoma and other hyperadrenergic states** (e.g., cheese reaction, cocaine abuse). Safe in **aortic dissection, MI/ACS, ischaemic stroke, intracranial haemorrhage, and preeclampsia**. Beneficial for patients with altered mental function as it does not cause sedation or increase intracranial pressure.
	    - **Contraindications:** **Heart failure and bronchial asthma**.
	    - **Dose:** 20-40 mg IV every 10 minutes until response, or 20 mg/hour IV infusion (increased up to 120 mg/hour).
	5. **Esmolol:**
	    
	    - **Class:** **Ultra-short acting selective β1 blocker**.
	    - **Action:** Rapid onset (1-2 minutes) and very short duration (action disappears 10-20 minutes after stopping infusion). Reduces cardiac contractility and work.
	    - **Specific Uses:** Particularly useful when cardiac contractility and work need to be rapidly reduced, such as in **aortic dissection**. Also used for emergency control of ventricular rate in supraventricular arrhythmias (e.g., atrial flutter, atrial fibrillation, PSVT).
	    - **Combination:** Its BP-lowering action is weak, so it is often combined with nicardipine or nitroprusside. Can be combined with nicardipine or GTN in MI/ACS complicated by raised BP and tachycardia.
	    - **Contraindications:** **Systolic heart failure and bronchial asthma**.
	    - **Dose:** 0.5 mg/kg bolus IV, followed by 50-200 µg/kg/minute IV infusion.
	6. **Phentolamine:**
	    
	    - **Class:** Non-selective α1 and α2 adrenergic blocker.
	    - **Action:** Rapid acting (2 minutes onset) with short duration (5-15 minutes).
	    - **Specific Uses:** Alternative to labetalol for **hyperadrenergic states**, including hypertensive episodes in **pheochromocytoma, clonidine withdrawal, and cheese reaction**.
	    - **Side Effects:** Tachycardia and myocardial ischaemia can occur, necessitating the addition of a β-blocker.
	    - **Dose:** 5-10 mg IV.
	7. **Hydralazine:**
	    
	    - **Class:** Directly acting arteriolar vasodilator.
	    - **Action:** Slower onset (20-30 minutes) and longer duration (4-6 hours) than other IV agents, less predictable.
	    - **Specific Uses:** Historically and currently used as an alternative for **preeclampsia/eclampsia**.
	    - **Cautions:** Causes **tachycardia**; avoid in patients with myocardial ischaemia or aortic dissection. A β-blocker can be combined to mitigate tachycardia.
	8. **Furosemide:**
	    
	    - **Class:** High-ceiling (loop) diuretic.
	    - **Action:** **Not a primary hypotensive agent**. Acts as a useful adjunct. Causes prompt increase in systemic venous capacitance, reducing left ventricular filling pressure.
	    - **Specific Uses (Adjunct):** Indicated for **volume overload** (e.g., acute LVF, pulmonary oedema) or **cerebral oedema** (in encephalopathy). Enhances Ca2+ excretion in hypercalcaemia of malignancy.
	    - **Cautions:** Avoid if the patient is hypovolaemic (e.g., in eclampsia, pheochromocytoma).
	
	**IV. Abandoned / Less Preferred Agents:**
	
	- **Oral Immediate-Release Nifedipine Capsules:** Abandoned for hypertensive urgencies due to safety concerns (e.g., increased mortality in MI patients).
	- **Other Oral Antihypertensives** (e.g., oral labetalol, amlodipine, captopril, clonidine): While effective for hypertensive _urgencies_, they are **not suitable for emergencies** due to slower onset of action (hours to days).
4. 65 yo patient presented with headache, giddiness, palpitations, He had no other neurologic symptoms. BP = 280-148 mmHg, Discuss the drug treatment for hypertensive emergencies
	For a 65-year-old patient presenting with a blood pressure of **280/148 mmHg** along with symptoms like headache, giddiness, and palpitations, the diagnosis is a **hypertensive emergency**. This is defined by a very high blood pressure (systolic ≥ 180 mmHg and/or diastolic ≥ 120 mmHg) accompanied by acute, progressive end-organ damage, which in this case includes neurological symptoms indicative of hypertensive encephalopathy.
	
	The drug treatment for hypertensive emergencies requires **aggressive parenteral (preferably intravenous) therapy**. The primary goal is to achieve a **controlled reduction of mean arterial blood pressure by up to 25% over minutes to 1-2 hours**, followed by a more gradual reduction to around 160/100 mmHg over the next 2-6 hours, and then to normal over 48 hours. **Rapid or drastic BP reduction must be avoided** as it can impair perfusion to vital organs such as the kidneys, myocardium, and brain.
	
	### **Drug Treatment for Hypertensive Emergencies**
	
	**I. General Principles:**
	
	- Use **intravenous drugs** with **fast onset**, **predictable effects**, **titratable action**, and **short duration**.
	- The choice of drug is guided by the specific **target organ damage** and **patient comorbidities**.
	- **Continuous monitoring** of blood pressure is essential.
	
	**II. Specific Intravenous Drugs:**
	
	1. **Nicardipine:**
	    
	    - **Class:** Dihydropyridine Calcium Channel Blocker (DHP CCB).
	    - **Mechanism:** Primarily a potent **arteriolar dilator**, decreasing peripheral vascular resistance.
	    - **Key Features:** Rapid onset and offset of action (action lasts 3-4 hours). Has become the **most popular drug** for various hypertensive emergencies, often replacing nitroprusside.
	    - **Clinical Uses:** Beneficial in **ischaemic and haemorrhagic stroke**, **aortic dissection**, acute heart failure, and acute renal failure due to high BP. Used during cardiac surgery and neurosurgery.
	    - **Dose:** Initially **5 mg/hour i.v. infusion**, increasing as needed up to 15 mg/hour.
	    - **Adverse Effects:** Mostly **vasodilation-related** and non-serious, such as headache and flushing. **Reflex tachycardia** may occur, which can be countered by co-administration with a beta-blocker (e.g., esmolol or labetalol) in cases like MI or ACS.
	2. **Sodium Nitroprusside:**
	    
	    - **Class:** Balanced arteriolar and venodilator.
	    - **Mechanism:** Releases **nitric oxide (NO)**, causing relaxation of vascular smooth muscle.
	    - **Key Features:** **Instantaneous onset** and **brief duration of action** (2-5 minutes), making it highly titratable.
	    - **Clinical Uses:** A commonly used drug, especially in **aortic dissection** (often combined with esmolol). Can be used in severe heart failure.
	    - **Monitoring:** Requires a **precise infusion pump** and **intra-arterial BP monitoring** due to its potency and rapid action.
	    - **Adverse Effects:** Can produce **cyanide and thiocyanate toxicity** (psychosis, lactic acidosis, convulsions). May **increase intracranial pressure** in haemorrhagic stroke or compromise cerebral blood flow in ischaemic stroke. Headache, palpitation, vomiting, and sweating.
	    - **Caution:** Less favoured now due to availability of less toxic alternatives like nicardipine.
	3. **Glyceryl Trinitrate (GTN):**
	    
	    - **Class:** Predominant venodilator.
	    - **Mechanism:** Reduces preload on the heart.
	    - **Key Features:** Acts within 2-5 minutes. Not a potent hypotensive on its own.
	    - **Clinical Uses:** Particularly suitable for **MI or other Acute Coronary Syndromes (ACS)** and **acute heart failure** accompanied by raised BP. Relieves pulmonary oedema.
	    - **Dose:** Typically 5-20 µg/min i.v. infusion.
	    - **Adverse Effects:** Common side effects include **headache**, postural hypotension, and reflex tachycardia. **Tolerance** can develop with continuous infusion lasting over 12 hours.
	    - **Caution:** Avoid if systolic BP < 90 mmHg, heart rate < 50 or > 100 beats/min, suspected right ventricular infarction, or if sildenafil has been taken in the past 24 hours.
	4. **Labetalol:**
	    
	    - **Class:** Combined **alpha and beta adrenergic blocker**.
	    - **Mechanism:** Reduces total peripheral resistance.
	    - **Key Features:** Efficacious hypotensive with a low risk of excessive hypotension. Acts faster than pure beta-blockers.
	    - **Clinical Uses:** Very useful in **hyperadrenergic states** (e.g., phaeochromocytoma, clonidine withdrawal, cocaine abuse). Safely used in **aortic dissection**, MI/ACS, ischaemic stroke, intracranial haemorrhage, and **preeclampsia**. Suitable for patients with altered mental function as it does not cause sedation or increase intracranial pressure.
	    - **Dose:** 20-40 mg i.v. every 10 minutes until response, or 20 mg/hour i.v. infusion, increasing up to 120 mg/hour.
	    - **Adverse Effects:** Most important side effect is **postural hypotension**. Others include failure of ejaculation, rashes, and liver damage.
	    - **Contraindications:** **Heart failure** and **bronchial asthma**.
	5. **Esmolol:**
	    
	    - **Class:** Ultra-short acting **selective β1 blocker**.
	    - **Mechanism:** Primarily reduces cardiac contractility and work.
	    - **Key Features:** Very rapid onset (1-2 minutes) and short duration of action (10-20 minutes after infusion cessation). BP lowering action is weak.
	    - **Clinical Uses:** Particularly useful when cardiac contractility and work need to be reduced, such as in **aortic dissection**. Used in MI/ACS complicated by raised BP and tachycardia. Can terminate supraventricular tachycardia and control ventricular rate in atrial fibrillation/flutter.
	    - **Dose:** **0.5 mg/kg bolus i.v.** followed by 50-200 µg/kg/min i.v. infusion.
	    - **Adverse Effects:** Main side effects are hypotension, dizziness.
	    - **Contraindications:** **Systolic heart failure** and **asthma**.
	6. **Phentolamine:**
	    
	    - **Class:** Non-selective α1 + α2 adrenergic blocker.
	    - **Mechanism:** Rapidly acting alpha blocker.
	    - **Clinical Uses:** An alternative to labetalol for **hyperadrenergic states**, such as hypertensive episodes in **phaeochromocytoma**, clonidine withdrawal, or cheese reaction.
	    - **Dose:** 5-10 mg i.v..
