1. Drug synergism with examples
	**Drug Synergism**
	
	- **Definition:** When two or more drugs are given simultaneously or in quick succession, and the **action of one drug is facilitated or increased by the other**, they are said to be synergistic.
	    
	- In a synergistic pair, both drugs can have action in the same direction, or one drug may be inactive when given alone but still enhance the action of the other when given together.
	    
	- Synergism can manifest as:
	    
	    - Decrease in the **Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC)** of one or both Antimicrobial Agents (AMAs).
	    - A **more rapid lethal action** of the combination than either drug alone, potentially resulting in faster infection cure.
	    - Synergistic prolongation of the **Post-Antibiotic Effect (PAE)**.
	- **Types of Synergism:**
	    
	    - **Additive:** The effect of the two drugs in the same direction simply adds up: Effect of drugs A + B = Effect of drug A + Effect of drug B. This occurs when the component given alone produces no effect but enhances the effect of the other (this latter case is also referred to as potentiation).
	        - _Examples:_ Antihypertensives (e.g., enalapril + hydrochlorothiazide).
	    - **Supraadditive (Potentiation):** The combined effect is **greater than the sum of the individual effects**.
	        - _Examples:_
	            - Acetylcholine + physostigmine.
	            - Levodopa + carbidopa/benserazide.
	            - Adrenaline + cocaine/desipramine.
	            - Sulfamethoxazole + trimethoprim.
	            - Tyramine + MAO inhibitors.
	            - Penicillin + aminoglycoside (e.g., Penicillin/ampicillin + streptomycin/gentamicin; Ticarcillin + gentamicin for _Pseudomonas_) - Penicillins, by acting on the cell wall, may enhance aminoglycoside penetration.
	            - Vancomycin + gentamicin for enterococcal Subacute Bacterial Endocarditis (SABE).
	            - Ceftazidime + ciprofloxacin for _Pseudomonas_ infected orthopedic prosthesis.
	            - Rifampin + isoniazid in tuberculosis.
	            - Rifampin + dapsone in leprosy.
	            - Certain combinations for Visceral Leishmaniasis (VL) in India (e.g., Liposomal Amphotericin B + Miltefosine, L-AMB + Paromomycin, Miltefosine + Paromomycin).
	- **Clinical Significance & Relevance:**
	    
	    - **Treating Difficult Infections:** Synergistic combinations are used for infections that are normally difficult to cure. Examples include SABE and _Pseudomonas_ infections, where combinations aim for faster cure and reduced relapse by more complete eradication.
	    - **Preventing Resistance:** Combining drugs, especially in prolonged therapy (e.g., tuberculosis, HIV-AIDS), can prevent the emergence of resistance. The chance of a bacillus being resistant to two unrelated drugs is significantly lower than resistance to either drug alone. Combined formulations for TB and HIV-AIDS ensure a single drug is not administered.
	    - **Optimizing Drug Action:** For some combinations (e.g., $\beta$-lactams with aminoglycosides), synergistic prolongation of PAE has been shown. Full doses of individual drugs are typically given for optimum synergism.
	    - **Fixed Dose Combinations (FDCs):** Many FDCs are justified because they are synergistic. However, potential disadvantages exist if components are unnecessary, doses cannot be individualized, or pharmacokinetic profiles differ.
	    - **Drug Choice & Regimens:** Knowledge of synergism influences the selection of drug combinations and regimens in specific conditions.
2. Drug antagonism --define mention the types and mechanism with examples
	**Drug Antagonism**
	
	- **Definition:** When two or more drugs are given, and one drug **decreases or abolishes the action of another**, they are said to be antagonistic. The effect of drugs A + B is less than the sum of the individual effects (effect of drug A + effect of drug B). Usually, in an antagonistic pair, one drug is inactive alone but decreases the effect of the other.
	- Antagonism can occur at the **pharmacokinetic** or **pharmacodynamic** level. (This summary focuses on pharmacodynamic mechanisms).
	
	**Types and Mechanisms:**
	
	Antagonism can be classified based on the mechanism involved:
	
	1. **Physical Antagonism:**
	    
	    - **Mechanism:** Based on a **physical property** of the drugs. One drug prevents the other from reaching its site of action or reduces its concentration.
	    - **Example:** **Charcoal** adsorbs alkaloids in the gut, preventing their absorption. Used in alkaloidal poisoning.
	2. **Chemical Antagonism:**
	    
	    - **Mechanism:** A **chemical reaction** between the two drugs occurs outside the receptor/target molecule, producing an inactive product.
	    - **Example:** **Potassium permanganate** oxidizes alkaloids. **Chelating agents** bind heavy metals. **Antibodies** bind to drugs (e.g., anti-digoxin antibodies for digoxin toxicity).
	3. **Physiological/Functional Antagonism:**
	    
	    - **Mechanism:** The two drugs act on **different receptors or by different mechanisms** but produce **opposite overt effects** on the same physiological function.
	    - **Examples:**
	        - **Histamine** (bronchoconstriction, lowers BP) and **adrenaline** (bronchodilation, raises BP) on bronchial muscles and blood pressure.
	        - **Hydrochlorothiazide** (increases urinary K+ excretion) and **amiloride** (decreases urinary K+ excretion).
	        - **Glucagon** (increases blood sugar) and **insulin** (decreases blood sugar) on blood sugar level.
	4. **Receptor Antagonism:**
	    
	    - **Definition:** One drug (**antagonist**) blocks the receptor action of another drug (**agonist**). This is a very important mechanism as many physiological signal molecules act through receptors.
	    - Receptor antagonists are relatively **selective**, meaning they block actions mediated by a specific receptor type (e.g., an anticholinergic blocks effects of cholinergic agonists but not histamine/5-HT which use different receptors).
	    - Types of receptor antagonism:
	        - **Competitive (Equilibrium Type):**
	            - **Mechanism:** Antagonist is structurally **similar** to the agonist. It competes with the agonist for the **same binding site** on the receptor. The antagonist has affinity but **no intrinsic activity** (does not activate the receptor).
	            - **Characteristics:** Binding is **reversible** and depends on the relative concentrations of agonist and antagonist. Higher concentrations of the agonist can progressively **overcome the block**.
	            - **Effect on Dose-Response Curve (DRC):** Causes a **parallel rightward shift** of the agonist DRC. The **maximal response remains unchanged** (surmountable antagonism). The intensity of response depends on the concentration of both agonist and antagonist.
	            - **Note:** A **partial agonist** can also act as a competitive antagonist to a full agonist by occupying the receptor but producing only a submaximal effect.
	            - **Examples:**
	                - **ACh - Atropine** (Muscarinic receptors)
	                - **Morphine - Naloxone** (Opioid receptors)
	                - Methotrexate inhibits dihydrofolate reductase by binding to the **catalytic site** with very high affinity, competing with the natural substrate DHFA. This is an example of enzyme inhibition acting like competitive antagonism at the enzyme's active site.
	        - **Noncompetitive:**
	            - **Mechanism:** Antagonist is usually chemically **unrelated** to the agonist. Binds to a **different site** (allosteric site) on the receptor, altering it so it cannot bind the agonist. Alternatively, it may bind to the same site but irreversibly/pseudoirreversibly, or bind to downstream components, or prevent the receptor from transducing the signal.
	            - **Characteristics:** No competition between agonist and antagonist because they bind to different sites. Even high agonist concentration **cannot reverse the block completely**.
	            - **Effect on Dose-Response Curve (DRC):** Increasing concentrations of the antagonist progressively **flatten the agonist DRC**. The **maximal response is suppressed** (unsurmountable antagonism). Maximal response depends primarily on the concentration of the antagonist.
	            - **Examples:** Diazepam - Bicuculline (though the source lists this as noncompetitive, Bicuculline is often described as a competitive antagonist at the GABA binding site on the GABAA receptor. The source description suggests it might be acting differently). The source also mentions experimentally produced noncompetitive antagonists not in clinical use.
	        - **Non-equilibrium Antagonism:**
	            - **Mechanism:** Often involves **covalent or very tight binding** of the antagonist to the receptor. This makes the block prolonged and difficult to reverse by increasing agonist concentration.
	            - **Characteristics:** Considered a type of noncompetitive antagonism. The effect lasts much longer than the presence of free antagonist in the body. Recovery depends on synthesis of new receptors.
	            - **Effect on DRC:** Results in flattening of the agonist DRC.
	            - **Example:** **Phenoxybenzamine** (an alpha-adrenergic blocker) is a non-equilibrium antagonist of adrenaline.
	
	**Clinical Relevance:**
	
	- Understanding antagonism is crucial for predicting and managing **drug interactions**.
	- Competitive antagonism can be overcome by **increasing the dose of the agonist** (within safety limits). This is relevant in treating overdose (e.g., opioid overdose treated with naloxone).
	- Noncompetitive and non-equilibrium antagonism are generally **not surmountable** by increasing agonist dose.
	- Physiological antagonism is used therapeutically when the undesirable effect of one drug is counteracted by another acting via a different pathway (e.g., using adrenaline for bronchospasm caused by histamine release).
