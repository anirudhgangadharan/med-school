1. Define bioavailability of a drug. Describe various factors affecting bioavailability of a drug giving suitable examples by oral route. Name drugs which are subjected to first pass metabolism.
	**Bioavailability**
	
	- **Definition:** Refers to the **rate and extent** of absorption of a drug from a dosage form administered by any route.
	- Determined by its **concentration-time curve** in blood or excretion in urine.
	- Measures the **fraction (F)** of the administered dose that reaches the systemic circulation in the **unchanged form**.
	- Bioavailability of a drug injected i.v. is **100%**.
	
	**Factors Affecting Bioavailability (Oral Route)**
	
	Oral administration is the commonest but often results in bioavailability less than 100%. This is primarily due to:
	
	1. **Incomplete Absorption:** The drug may not be fully absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract.
	    - **Drug Solubility/Properties:**
	        - Drugs must dissolve in the aqueous biophase; rate of dissolution governs absorption for poorly water-soluble drugs (e.g., aspirin, griseofulvin, ketoconazole requires low pH).
	        - Highly ionized drugs are poorly absorbed (e.g., gentamicin, neostigmine).
	        - Lipid-soluble drugs are absorbed more readily, especially in the intestine.
	        - Ionization status depends on pKa and pH; acidic drugs are absorbed more from the stomach, basic drugs from the intestine.
	    - **Dosage Form:** Tablets/capsules require disintegration and dissolution; excipients and manufacturing can affect these rates (e.g., particle size reduction increases absorption rate for aspirin, griseofulvin).
	    - **GI Physiology:**
	        - Surface area: Small intestine has much larger surface area than the stomach.
	        - Gastric emptying rate: Faster emptying accelerates overall absorption by moving drug to the higher surface area of the intestine. Affected by food.
	        - Presence of food: Can dilute drug, retard absorption, form poorly absorbed complexes (e.g., tetracyclines with calcium in milk), or delay gastric emptying. Exception: Fatty food enhances absorption of some drugs (e.g., lumefantrine, griseofulvin).
	        - Vascularity: Blood circulation removes absorbed drug, maintaining gradient.
	2. **First-Pass (Presystemic) Metabolism:** Metabolism of the drug **during its passage from the site of absorption into systemic circulation**.
	    - Occurs mainly in the **intestinal wall** and **liver** via the portal vein after oral administration.
	    - Significantly **reduces the fraction of unchanged drug** reaching systemic circulation.
	    - Extent varies greatly between drugs.
	    - Can be avoided by using sublingual, transdermal, or parenteral routes.
	    - Affected by liver function; bioavailability of high first-pass drugs is **increased in liver disease**.
	    - Shows **marked individual variation** due to differences in metabolism extent.
	    - Oral bioavailability can be increased if another drug competes for the same metabolic enzymes during first pass.
	3. **Excretion into Bile:** Absorbed drug may be directly excreted into bile, reducing systemic bioavailability.
	
	**Clinical Relevance:**
	
	- Bioavailability variations are significant for drugs with **low safety margins** (e.g., digoxin) or where dosage requires **precise control** (e.g., oral hypoglycaemics, oral anticoagulants).
	- Explains why the **oral dose is often considerably higher** than the parenteral dose for drugs with high first-pass metabolism.
	- High first-pass metabolism means plasma concentration of metabolites will be much higher with oral dosing compared to parenteral for the same therapeutic level, potentially leading to **increased adverse effects** if metabolites are active/toxic.
	
	**Drugs Subjected to First-Pass Metabolism**
	
	- Many drugs undergo significant first-pass metabolism when given orally, leading to reduced oral bioavailability compared to i.v. or other routes bypassing the liver/gut wall.
	- Examples include:
	    - **High First-Pass Metabolism:**
	        - Glyceryl trinitrate (GTN)
	        - Lidocaine
	        - Morphine
	        - Propranolol
	        - Isosorbide mononitrate
	        - Metoprolol
	        - Pentazocine
	        - Testosterone
	        - Naloxone (inactive orally)
	        - Levodopa
	        - Hydralazine
	        - Chlorpromazine
	    - **Intermediate First-Pass Metabolism:**
	        - Aspirin
	        - Theophylline
	        - Quinidine
	        - Desipramine
	        - Nortriptyline
	        - Phenobarbitone
	        - Phenylbutazone
	        - Tolbutamide
	- Note: Some drugs are primarily destroyed by digestive juices (e.g., Penicillin G, Insulin) or simply not absorbed (e.g., Streptomycin) rather than undergoing metabolism in the gut wall/liver, although their oral bioavailability is also low.
2. Explain plasma binding protein of drugs with suitable examples. Give its clinical importance
	
	**Plasma Protein Binding of Drugs**
	
	- **Definition:** Most drugs exhibit **physicochemical affinity** for plasma proteins and bind to them, primarily in a **reversible** manner. This is a key pharmacokinetic process impacting distribution.
	- **Binding Proteins:**
	    - **Albumin:** Major binding protein, especially for **acidic drugs**. More abundant, quantitatively very important.
	        - Examples: Barbiturates, Benzodiazepines, NSAIDs, Valproic acid, Phenytoin, Penicillins, Sulfonamides, Tetracyclines, Tolbutamide, Warfarin, Diclofenac, Furosemide.
	    - **α₁-acid glycoprotein:** Primarily binds **basic drugs**.
	        - Examples: β-blockers, Bupivacaine, Lidocaine, Disopyramide, Imipramine, Methadone, Prazosin, Quinidine, Verapamil, Propranolol.
	- **Binding Extent:** Varies greatly between drugs; not predictable by pharmacological or chemical class alone. Can be saturated at high drug concentrations.
	
	**Clinical Importance (High-Yield Points):**
	
	- **Drug Activity:** Only the **free (unbound)** drug fraction is available to diffuse out of capillaries and elicit pharmacological effects at target sites. The bound fraction is **inactive**.
	- **Distribution:** Highly plasma protein bound drugs are largely **restricted to the vascular compartment** as they do not cross membranes (except through large capillary spaces). They tend to have **smaller volumes of distribution (Vd)**. Example: Streptomycin, Gentamicin (Vd ≈ extracellular fluid volume).
	- **Drug Storage/Duration of Action:** Plasma protein binding acts as a **temporary storage** mechanism. The bound fraction is in equilibrium with free drug and **dissociates** as free drug is eliminated, helping to maintain free drug concentration. High binding _generally_ makes a drug **longer acting** by limiting its availability for metabolism and excretion.
	- **Elimination:**
	    - Bound drug is **not filtered by glomeruli**.
	    - However, drugs that undergo **active tubular secretion** in the kidney or **active transport/metabolism** in the liver can be efficiently removed. In these cases, plasma protein binding can act as a carrier, promoting dissociation and elimination, potentially leading to a **higher clearance** than total renal or hepatic blood flow for highly extracted drugs (e.g., Penicillin).
	- **Drug Interactions (Displacement):** Drugs competing for the **same binding site** can displace each other, transiently increasing the **free concentration** of the displaced drug. While often minimal in impact due to redistribution/elimination, this can be clinically significant for drugs with **narrow therapeutic windows** or when combined with inhibited clearance. Note: Not all highly bound drugs displace each other (different binding sites). Acidic drugs generally don't displace basic drugs and vice versa.
	    - Example: Displacement of warfarin by other albumin binders.
	- **Impact of Disease States:**
	    - **Hypoalbuminemia (e.g., liver disease)**: Can **reduce binding** of acidic drugs (e.g., phenytoin, furosemide, diclofenac, warfarin), leading to potentially **higher free drug concentrations** and increased risk of toxicity at usual doses.
	    - **Renal Failure (Uraemia):** Can reduce binding of some drugs (e.g., phenytoin, pethidine).
	    - **Inflammatory Disease/Pregnancy:** Can **increase** levels of α₁-acid glycoprotein, potentially increasing binding of basic drugs (e.g., propranolol).
	- **Therapeutic Drug Monitoring (TDM):** Plasma concentration measurements usually reflect the **total drug concentration** (bound + free). Interpretation requires considering the degree of protein binding, especially in conditions altering binding (e.g., hypoalbuminemia), to estimate the clinically relevant **free drug concentration**. When relating plasma levels to in vitro activity (e.g., MIC for antimicrobials), binding must be accounted for.
