1. Tetracyclines-uses
	
	**TETRACYCLINES - USES**
	
	- **General Considerations**:
	    
	    - Broad-spectrum antibiotics.
	    - Mechanism: Primarily bacteriostatic; inhibit protein synthesis by binding to 30S ribosomes.
	    - Clinical use has significantly **declined** due to widespread **resistance** and the availability of safer/more effective alternative antimicrobials (AMAs).
	    - Should generally only be used when a more selective and less toxic AMA is unavailable.
	    - Not dependable for **empirical treatment of serious/life-threatening infections**.
	    - May be used for the initial treatment of mixed infections, although other combinations (e.g., β-lactam + aminoglycoside, 3rd gen cephalosporin, or fluoroquinolone) are now preferred.
	- **First-Choice Drugs** (Despite resistance by many organisms, tetracyclines are _still_ the preferred drugs for certain infections):
	    
	    - **Venereal Diseases**:
	        - **Chlamydial/nonspecific urethritis/endocervicitis**: Doxycycline for 7 days is effective.
	        - **Lymphogranuloma venereum**: Resolves in 2-3 weeks.
	        - **Granuloma inguinale** (due to _Calymmatobacterium granulomatis_): A 3-week course is the most effective treatment.
	    - **Atypical pneumonia** due to _Mycoplasma pneumoniae_: Therapy reduces illness duration.
	    - **Psittacosis**: Treated in 2 weeks.
	    - **Cholera**: Adjuvant value, reducing stool volume and duration of diarrhoea.
	    - **Brucellosis**: Highly efficacious with rapid symptomatic relief. **Therapy of choice**: Doxycycline (200 mg/day) + Rifampin (600 mg/day) for 6 weeks. Gentamicin may be combined in acute cases.
	    - **Plague**: Highly effective in bubonic and pneumonic plague. Preferred for blind/mass treatment during an epidemic (though streptomycin may act faster). Used alone or combined with streptomycin in confirmed cases.
	    - **Relapsing fever** due to _Borrelia recurrentis_: Responds adequately.
	    - **Rickettsial infections** (Typhus, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, Q fever, etc.): Respond dramatically.
	- **Second-Choice Drugs**:
	    
	    - Alternatives to penicillin/ampicillin for **tetanus, anthrax, actinomycosis, and listeria infections**.
	    - Alternatives to ceftriaxone, amoxicillin, or azithromycin for **gonorrhoea** in patients allergic to penicillin.
	    - Alternatives to ceftriaxone for **syphilis** in patients allergic to penicillin (early syphilis requires 2 weeks, late syphilis requires 1 month).
	    - Alternatives to penicillin for **leptospirosis**: Doxycycline (100 mg BD for 7 days) is curative. Weekly doxycycline (200 mg) can be used as prophylaxis in at-risk subjects during an epidemic.
	    - Alternatives to azithromycin for **pneumonia due to _Chlamydia pneumoniae_**.
	    - Oral as well as topical tetracycline has been used in **trachoma**.
	    - Alternatives to ceftriaxone/azithromycin for **chancroid**.
	    - Alternatives to streptomycin for **tularemia**.
	- **Other Specific Situations**:
	    
	    - **Urinary Tract Infections (UTI)**: Occasionally used based on sensitivity reports, particularly in _Chlamydia trachomatis_ cystitis. Note: Tetracyclines are seldom effective for UTI now due to resistance.
	    - **Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP)**: When a more selective antibiotic cannot be used.
	    - **Amoebiasis**: Used along with other amoebicides for chronic intestinal amoebiasis. Note: Metronidazole is the first-line drug for amoebiasis. Tetracyclines have no role as alternative luminal amoebicides.
	    - **Malaria**: Doxycycline is used as an **adjuvant** to quinine or artesunate for **chloroquine-resistant _P. falciparum_ malaria**. It has slow-acting, weak erythrocytic schizontocidal action but is never used alone for malaria treatment.
	    - **Acne vulgaris**: Prolonged low-dose therapy may be used in severe cases (due to sensitivity of _Propionibacterium acnes_), but simpler treatments are usually preferred.
	    - **Leprosy** (_M. leprae_):
	        - Many fluoroquinolones (ofloxacin, pefloxacin, moxifloxacin, sparfloxacin) are highly active; ciprofloxacin has poor activity. Ofloxacin is cidal to _M. leprae_.
	        - Moxifloxacin is the most potent FQ against _M. leprae_.
	        - Minocycline is active against _M. leprae_ and is being tried in alternative MDT regimens. It has less activity than rifampin but more than clarithromycin.
	        - These may be used in alternative regimens if rifampin cannot be used, or to shorten duration and reduce resistance.
	- **Tigecycline (Glycylcycline subclass)**:
	    
	    - Newer synthetic AMA. Administered only by slow i.v. infusion (poor oral absorption).
	    - Approved for **serious and hospitalized patients** with:
	        - Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP).
	        - Complicated skin and soft tissue infections (but **not diabetic foot**).
	        - Complicated intraabdominal infections caused by enterococci, anaerobes, and Enterobacteriaceae.
	    - **Not recommended** for hospital-acquired/ventilator-associated chest infections (higher mortality in comparative trials).
	    - **Not suitable** for urinary tract infections (low urine concentration).
	    - Use is restricted to prevent emergence of resistance; reserved for serious infections caused by **multidrug-resistant gram-positive bacteria** (VRE, vancomycin-resistant MRSA, multi-resistant _S. pneumoniae_, etc.).
	    - Used as a reserve drug for extensively drug-resistant (XDR) TB.
	- **Resistance**:
	    
	    - Resistance develops slowly, commonly by reduced intracellular concentration or efflux pumps.
	    - Doxycycline and minocycline are not effectively effluxed, so some resistant strains may respond to these.
	    - Plasmid-mediated resistance can occur via a protein that protects the ribosomal binding site.
	    - Widespread use has led to common resistance in gram-positive cocci, _E. coli_, Enterobacter, and others. Cross-resistance among classical tetracyclines is common and often complete. Partial cross-resistance may occur with chloramphenicol, erythromycin, and lincomycin.
2. Advantages of Doxycycline over oxytetracycline
	**Advantages of Doxycycline (Doxy) over Oxytetracycline (OxyT)**
	
	Drawing specifically from the comparative features presented in the sources:
	
	- **Potency:** Doxycycline is listed as having **Higher Potency** compared to Oxytetracycline which is listed as Low.
	- **Intestinal Absorption:** Doxycycline demonstrates **Much Better Absorption** (95-100%) which is also **Not Interfered by Food**, while Oxytetracycline has 60-80% absorption. This translates to more predictable and higher systemic levels of the drug.
	- **Plasma Half-life (t½):** Doxycycline has a **Longer Plasma Half-life** (12-18 hours) compared to Oxytetracycline (6-10 hours). A longer half-life generally supports less frequent dosing.
	- **Dosing Frequency:** As a consequence of its longer half-life, Doxycycline can often be given as a **Single Daily Dose** (after an initial loading dose of 200 mg, followed by 100-200 mg OD), unlike Oxytetracycline which requires dosing **multiple times a day** (250-500 mg QID or TDS). **Once-daily dosing** significantly enhances patient compliance.
	- **Gastrointestinal Side Effects:**
	    - **Alteration of Intestinal Flora:** Doxycycline causes **Less Marked Alteration** of intestinal flora compared to Oxytetracycline (Moderate vs Marked).
	    - **Incidence of Diarrhoea:** Doxycycline has a **Lower Incidence of Diarrhoea** compared to Oxytetracycline (Low vs High). This is because smaller amounts of Doxycycline reach the lower bowel in active form.
	
	**Considerations / Potential Disadvantages of Doxycycline (compared to OxyT):**
	
	The sources also note some aspects where Doxycycline may be less favourable:
	
	- **Phototoxicity:** Doxycycline is listed with **Higher Phototoxicity** compared to Oxytetracycline which is listed as Low.
	- **Specific Toxicity:** The comparative table lists "High renal toxicity, diabetes insipidus" under Doxycycline. Oxytetracycline is listed with "less tooth discolouration". However, it's noted that Doxycycline is primarily excreted in faeces, which might imply advantages in renal impairment, though the source also generally advises caution with tetracyclines in renal insufficiency. This specific toxicity listing warrants careful consideration in clinical practice.
