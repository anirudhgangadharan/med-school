1. Classify antiretroviral drugs with examples, explain the mechanism of action, uses and adverse effects of zidovudine

	Antiretroviral (ARV) drugs are a class of medications designed to combat human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, a retrovirus. These drugs do not cure the infection, but they are crucial for prolonging and improving the quality of life for patients and postponing complications associated with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) or AIDS-related complex (ARC). The effectiveness of ARV therapy is primarily monitored by assessing plasma HIV-RNA levels and CD4 lymphocyte counts at regular intervals.
	
	HIV is a single-stranded RNA retrovirus that uniquely uses a viral RNA-dependent DNA polymerase, known as **reverse transcriptase**, to transcribe proviral DNA from its viral RNA genome. This proviral DNA then integrates with the host's chromosomal DNA, leading to permanent cell infection, which is why eradication of the virus is currently impossible.
	
	Over the past 35 years, several virus-specific targets for anti-HIV drugs have been identified. The established targets include HIV reverse transcriptase and HIV protease, which cleaves the large virus-directed polyprotein into functional viral proteins. Newer targets being exploited include the fusion of the viral envelope with the plasma membrane of CD4 cells, chemokine coreceptor (CCR5) on host cells, and HIV-integrase. The first ARV drug, zidovudine, was introduced in 1987.

	### Mechanism of Action, Uses, and Adverse Effects by Class
	
	#### 1. Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NRTIs)
	
	- **Mechanism of Action**: NRTIs are nucleoside (or nucleotide, like Tenofovir) analogues. After entering host cells, they are **intracellularly phosphorylated** by host kinases to their active triphosphate forms. These active metabolites then **compete with natural deoxyribonucleotides for incorporation into the proviral DNA chain** during reverse transcription. Their incorporation **terminates the DNA chain elongation**, effectively inhibiting HIV reverse transcriptase and preventing the infection of new cells.
	- **Examples**: Zidovudine (AZT), Didanosine (ddI), Stavudine (d4T), Lamivudine (3TC), Abacavir (ABC), Emtricitabine (FTC), and Tenofovir (TDF).
	- **Uses**: NRTIs are a fundamental component of highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART), always used **in combination with at least two other ARV drugs** to prevent resistance. They are essential components of most first-line triple drug regimens recommended by organizations like WHO. Some NRTIs, like lamivudine and tenofovir, are also active against Hepatitis B virus (HBV). Emtricitabine in combination with tenofovir is advocated by WHO for pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) of HIV in high-risk adults.
	- **Adverse Effects**: Common side effects vary by drug but can include **nausea, abdominal pain, headache, fatigue, and diarrhoea**. More serious toxicities such as **anaemia and neutropenia (with zidovudine)**, **peripheral neuropathy (with didanosine and stavudine)**, **lactic acidosis (with stavudine and didanosine)**, **lipodystrophy (with stavudine, less with abacavir)**, and **hypersensitivity reactions (with abacavir)** have been reported. Renal toxicity can be a concern with tenofovir.
	
	#### 2. Non-nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NNRTIs)
	
	- **Mechanism of Action**: Unlike NRTIs, NNRTIs are chemically distinct compounds that **directly inhibit HIV reverse transcriptase without requiring intracellular phosphorylation**. They bind to a different site on the enzyme compared to NRTIs and are **non-competitive inhibitors**. They are more potent against HIV-1 but do not inhibit HIV-2.
	- **Examples**: Nevirapine (NVP), Efavirenz (EFV), Delavirdine (DLV), Etravirine (ETV), and Rilpivirine.
	- **Uses**: NNRTIs are key components of first-line ART regimens, typically combined with two NRTIs.
	- **Adverse Effects**: **Rapid development of viral resistance occurs if used alone**, necessitating combination therapy. **Cross-resistance between nevirapine and efavirenz is common**. Side effects can include **skin rashes**, and hepatic dysfunction is a concern with nevirapine. Efavirenz can cause neuropsychiatric symptoms. Etravirine is associated with nausea and skin rashes. Both can induce their own metabolism and interact with other drugs metabolized by CYP enzymes.
	
	#### 3. Protease Inhibitors (PIs)
	
	- **Mechanism of Action**: PIs inhibit the **HIV-encoded aspartic protease enzyme**, which is responsible for cleaving a large viral polyprotein into functional viral proteins and enzymes (like reverse transcriptase and integrase). By binding to the active site of the protease, PIs interfere with this cleavage function. This action occurs at a **late step in HIV replication (maturation of new virus particles)**, leading to the production of **immature, non-infectious viral progeny**, thereby preventing further rounds of infection. PIs do not require intracellular activation by phosphorylation.
	- **Examples**: Ritonavir (RTV), Atazanavir (ATV), Indinavir (IDV), Nelfinavir (NFV), Saquinavir (SQV), Lopinavir (LPV), Darunavir (DRV), and Fosamprenavir (FPV).
	- **Uses**: PIs are highly effective viral inhibitors and are used in combination therapy, typically with two NRTIs or one NRTI and one NNRTI. They are generally **reserved for second-line regimens or for cases of treatment failure** as per WHO and NACO guidelines, due to their potential for drug interactions and metabolic side effects.
	- **Adverse Effects**: Prominent adverse effects include **gastrointestinal intolerance** (nausea, diarrhoea, abdominal pain), **asthenia, headache, dizziness, limb/facial tingling, numbness, and rashes**. Of particular concern are **metabolic complications like lipodystrophy** (abdominal obesity, buffalo hump, wasting of limbs and face), **dyslipidaemia** (raised triglycerides and cholesterol), and **insulin resistance**, which can exacerbate diabetes. PIs are extensively metabolized, mainly by CYP3A4, and are potent inhibitors of this enzyme, leading to numerous and often unpredictable **drug interactions**. Rifampin, for example, can render PIs ineffective by inducing their metabolism. To mitigate issues with bioavailability and dosing, PIs are often "boosted" by combining them with a low, subtherapeutic dose of ritonavir, which acts as a CYP3A4 inhibitor.
	
	#### 4. Integrase Inhibitors
	
	- **Mechanism of Action**: HIV integrase is a viral enzyme that translocates to the host cell nucleus along with the proviral DNA after reverse transcription. It **nicks the host chromosomal DNA, integrates the proviral DNA with it, and reseals it**, permanently infecting the cell. Integrase inhibitors interfere with this crucial virus-specific function.
	- **Examples**: Raltegravir and Dolutegravir.
	- **Uses**: Integrase inhibitors are potent ARV drugs. Raltegravir is active against both HIV-1 and HIV-2 and has shown clinical efficacy in rapidly reducing HIV-RNA load and improving CD4 cell counts in both treatment-naive and previously treated patients. Dolutegravir is a second-generation integrase inhibitor that retains some activity against raltegravir-resistant viruses and is gaining popularity as a first-line regimen when combined with two NRTIs, offering good tolerability and once-daily dosing.
	- **Adverse Effects**: Generally, they have a good tolerability profile. Raltegravir can cause nausea, headache, diarrhoea, and weakness, with myopathy as a potential toxicity. Dolutegravir's adverse effects include rashes, hypersensitivity, and occasional hepatotoxicity. Both require caution with cationic medications (e.g., antacids containing Ca²⁺ or Mg²⁺) due to chelating properties.
	
	#### 5. Entry Inhibitors
	
	- **Examples**: Enfuvirtide (T-20).
	- **Mechanism of Action**: The sources classify Enfuvirtide as an entry inhibitor. No detailed mechanism or adverse effects for this drug are provided in the given sources beyond its classification.
	
	#### 6. CCR-5 Receptor Inhibitors
	
	- **Mechanism of Action**: The HIV envelope glycoprotein gp120 anchors to the CD4 site of the host cell by binding to a cell membrane receptor, often the **CCR5 chemokine receptor**. Maraviroc, a novel anti-HIV drug, targets and **blocks the host cell's CCR5 receptor**, thereby interfering with virus attachment and subsequent entry of the viral genome into the cell. It is important to note that it is ineffective against HIV strains that are CXCR4 receptor tropic or dual CCR5/CXCR4 tropic.
	- **Examples**: Maraviroc.
	- **Uses**: Maraviroc has demonstrated significant reduction in HIV-RNA load and improvement in CD4 counts when added to optimized background therapy in patients who have been previously treated and have CCR5-tropic HIV infection (tropism must be confirmed prior to use). There is no cross-resistance with other ARV drugs.
	- **Adverse Effects**: Side effects include cough, fever, abdominal pain, rashes, and postural hypotension, but generally, maraviroc's tolerability is satisfactory.
	
	### Focus on Zidovudine (AZT)
	
	**Classification**: Zidovudine (AZT) is a **Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitor (NRTI)** and is considered the **first and prototype NRTI**. It is a thymidine analogue.
	
	**Mechanism of Action**: Zidovudine is an inactive prodrug that requires **intracellular phosphorylation** in the host cell to form its active metabolite, **zidovudine triphosphate**. This triphosphate form selectively **inhibits viral reverse transcriptase** in preference to cellular DNA polymerase. It does so by **competing with ATP for incorporation into viral DNA**, and once incorporated, it **terminates the DNA chain elongation**. Zidovudine thus **prevents the infection of new cells by HIV**. However, it has no effect on proviral DNA that has already integrated into the host chromosome. Its action is specific to retroviruses.
	
	**Pharmacokinetics**: Zidovudine is **rapidly absorbed orally**, with a bioavailability of approximately 65%. It is quickly cleared by **hepatic glucuronidation**, resulting in a short plasma half-life (t½) of about 1 hour. Around 15-20% of the unchanged drug, along with its metabolites, is excreted in the urine. Zidovudine crosses the placenta and is found in breast milk.
	
	**Uses**: Historically, zidovudine was the first ARV drug introduced. Today, it is **used exclusively in combination with at least two other ARV drugs** due to the rapid development of resistance when used as monotherapy. Although it is no longer included in the preferred first-line WHO (2016) regimens for adults and adolescents, it remains a component of alternative regimens. Fixed-dose combinations containing zidovudine are available, such as Lamivudine + Zidovudine (e.g., COMBIVIR) or Lamivudine + Zidovudine + Nevirapine (e.g., DUOVIR-N). Zidovudine is also considered **relatively safe during pregnancy** for women undergoing ART.
	
	**Adverse Effects**:
	
	- The **most important and dose-related adverse effects are anaemia and neutropenia**.
	- Common side effects that often occur at the start of therapy but diminish later include **nausea, anorexia, abdominal pain, headache, insomnia, and myalgia**.
	- Less frequent but serious adverse effects can include **myopathy, pigmentation of nails, lactic acidosis, and hepatomegaly**.
	
	**Interactions**:
	
	- **Paracetamol can increase zidovudine toxicity**, likely by competing for glucuronidation.
	- **Azole antifungals** also inhibit zidovudine metabolism.
	- Other **nephrotoxic and myelosuppressive drugs**, as well as **probenecid**, can enhance its toxicity.
	- It is important to **avoid combining zidovudine with stavudine** due to pharmacodynamic antagonism, as they compete for the same activation pathway.
	
	In summary, current antiretroviral therapy for HIV emphasizes **highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART)**, which involves combining three or more drugs from different classes. This multi-drug approach is essential because HIV's reverse transcriptase and other enzymes are highly prone to copying errors, leading to a high rate of mutation and rapid development of drug resistance if monotherapy is used. Effective ART aims to maximally and durably inhibit viral replication to maintain a strong immune response, prevent opportunistic infections, and reduce the risk of HIV transmission.
3. HAART (Highly active antiretroviral therapy)
	**HAART (Highly Active Antiretroviral Therapy) / ART (Antiretroviral Therapy)**
	
	Highly Active Antiretroviral Therapy (HAART), now commonly referred to as Antiretroviral Therapy (ART), is the **cornerstone of HIV management**.
	
	**Core Principle:**
	
	- HAART involves the **combination of three or more anti-HIV drugs** administered concurrently.
	- **Monotherapy is strictly contraindicated** due to rapid emergence of drug resistance.
	
	**Goals of Therapy:**
	
	- To achieve **maximal and durable suppression of viral replication** (reducing plasma HIV-RNA to undetectable levels, typically <50 copies/µL).
	- To **restore and maintain effective immune response**, as evidenced by a rise in CD4 lymphocyte count.
	- To **improve the patient's quality of life**, mitigate neurological manifestations of AIDS, and prevent opportunistic infections and other complications.
	- To **prevent HIV transmission** (sexual and vertical).
	
	**Drug Targets & Classes:** Anti-HIV drugs target specific virus-directed processes essential for HIV replication.
	
	**1. Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NRTIs)**
	
	- **Mechanism of Action:** These are nucleoside or nucleotide analogues. After intracellular phosphorylation by host cell enzymes, they become active triphosphate metabolites. These metabolites **selectively inhibit viral reverse transcriptase** by competing with natural nucleotides for incorporation into the growing proviral DNA chain. Their incorporation results in **chain termination**, preventing further DNA elongation and thus replication of the HIV genome.
	    
	- **Key Concept:** They do not affect proviral DNA already integrated into the host chromosome.
	    
	- **Examples:** Zidovudine (AZT), Lamivudine (3TC), Abacavir (ABC), Tenofovir (TDF), Emtricitabine (FTC).
	    
	    - **Zidovudine (AZT) - _Prototype NRTI_**
	        
	        - **Mechanism:** A **thymidine analogue** (azidothymidine). Its triphosphate form selectively inhibits viral reverse transcriptase in preference to cellular DNA polymerase. It gets incorporated into the proviral DNA and terminates chain elongation.
	        - **Pharmacokinetics:**
	            - **Rapid oral absorption** (~65% bioavailability).
	            - Quickly cleared by **hepatic glucuronidation** (plasma t½ 1 hour).
	            - **Crosses placenta and is found in milk**.
	            - Mainly excreted in urine (15-20% unchanged).
	        - **Adverse Effects:**
	            - **Anaemia and neutropenia are the most important and dose-related adverse effects**.
	            - Common initial side effects (diminish later): Nausea, anorexia, abdominal pain, headache, insomnia, myalgia.
	            - Infrequent: Myopathy, pigmentation of nails, lactic acidosis, hepatomegaly.
	        - **Interactions:**
	            - **Paracetamol increases AZT toxicity** (competition for glucuronidation).
	            - Azole antifungals inhibit AZT metabolism.
	            - Other nephrotoxic and myelosuppressive drugs, and probenecid, enhance toxicity.
	            - Stavudine and zidovudine exhibit **mutual antagonism** (competing for same activation pathway).
	        - **Uses:** Always used **in combination with at least 2 other ARV drugs**. Not included in the preferred 1st line WHO (2016) regimen, but is a component of one alternative regimen.
	    - **Lamivudine (3TC)**
	        
	        - **Mechanism:** Deoxycytidine analogue, inhibits HIV reverse transcriptase and HBV DNA polymerase. Incorporation into viral DNA terminates chain elongation. Low systemic toxicity.
	        - **Resistance:** Point mutation in HIV-RT and HBV-DNA polymerase leads to rapid lamivudine resistance.
	        - **Pharmacokinetics:** High oral bioavailability, longer plasma t½ (6-8 hours), main elimination unchanged in urine.
	        - **Uses:** Essential component of most **first-line triple drug WHO and NACO regimens** for HIV, synergises with most other NRTIs. Also effective in chronic hepatitis B, but resistance limits its first-line use.
	        - **Side Effects:** Generally well tolerated; headache, fatigue, rashes, nausea, anorexia, abdominal pain. Pancreatitis and neuropathy are rare. No haematological toxicity.
	    - **Abacavir (ABC)**
	        
	        - **Mechanism:** Guanosine analogue, converted to carbovir triphosphate, incorporated into proviral DNA, terminates chain elongation.
	        - **Resistance:** Develops slowly, little cross-resistance with other NRTIs.
	        - **Adverse Effects:** **Hypersensitivity reactions (2-5% adults) are the major problem** (rashes, fever, abdominal pain, flu-like symptoms). Prompt discontinuation is crucial; re-administration can be fatal. Avoid in patients with cardiovascular risk factors. Lipodystrophy is least likely.
	        - **Uses:** Component of preferred 1st line WHO regimen for children.
	    - **Tenofovir (TDF)**
	        
	        - **Mechanism:** **Nucleotide analogue** (only one commonly used ARV in this class). Active against HIV and HBV.
	        - **Tolerability:** Good tolerability profile, less toxic than other first-line regimens.
	        - **Uses:** Included in the **preferred 1st line WHO regimen for adults and adolescents**.
	        - **Adverse Effects:** Renal toxicity is to be watched.
	    - **Emtricitabine (FTC)**
	        
	        - **Mechanism:** Fluorinated cytidine analogue, converted intracellularly to its triphosphate, which inhibits HIV reverse transcriptase.
	        - **Pharmacokinetics:** Well absorbed orally, largely excreted unchanged by kidney, long intracellular t½ (~40 hours) permits **once daily dosing**.
	        - **Resistance:** Some cross-resistance with lamivudine.
	        - **Uses:** One of the **first-line anti-HIV drugs**, component of preferred WHO regimen for adults and adolescents. In combination with tenofovir, it is advocated by WHO for **pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) of HIV** in high-risk adults. Active against HBV, but sudden stoppage can cause rebound exacerbation of hepatitis.
	        - **Side Effects:** One of the **least toxic ARV drugs**; fatigue, headache, nausea, diarrhoea, and discoloration of exposed skin.
	
	**2. Non-Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NNRTIs)**
	
	- **Mechanism of Action:** Unlike NRTIs, NNRTIs **directly bind to HIV-1 reverse transcriptase at a non-active site**, causing a conformational change that inhibits its function. They **do not require intracellular phosphorylation** for activation.
	    
	- **Resistance:** Resistance develops rapidly by point mutation of the enzyme if used alone. **Cross-resistance between Nevirapine (NVP) and Efavirenz (EFV) is common**.
	    
	- **Examples:** Efavirenz (EFV), Nevirapine (NVP), Etravirine.
	    
	    - **Efavirenz (EFV)**
	        
	        - **Pharmacokinetics:** Oral absorption ~50%, completely metabolized (t½ 48 hours), mainly by CYP2B6 and CYP3A4. It is an **enzyme inducer** (autoinduction) but also inhibits CYP3A4.
	        - **Uses:** Preferred over nevirapine as the NNRTI in **first-line WHO regimens**.
	        - **Interactions:** Rifampin induces its metabolism.
	    - **Nevirapine (NVP)**
	        
	        - **Pharmacokinetics:** Well absorbed orally, extensively metabolized (t½ ~30 hours), mainly by CYP3A4 and CYP2B6. It is an **enzyme inducer** (autoinduction), requiring a lower initial dose which is doubled after 2 weeks.
	        - **Uses:** Used as an alternative 1st-line regimen.
	        - **Contraindications/Precautions:** Should be avoided in patients with hepatic dysfunction and in those receiving rifampin.
	    - **Etravirine**
	        
	        - **Mechanism:** Second-generation NNRTI, active against HIV-1 mutants resistant to other NNRTIs.
	        - **Uses:** Indicated in adults and children >6 years who have been previously treated and are resistant/intolerant to other NNRTIs.
	
	**3. HIV Protease Inhibitors (PIs)**
	
	- **Mechanism of Action:** PIs inhibit the HIV protease enzyme, which is crucial for the **maturation of new virus particles**. The protease normally cleaves large polyproteins into functional viral proteins. By inhibiting this cleavage, PIs cause HIV-infected cells to produce immature, non-infectious viral progeny, thus preventing further rounds of infection. They **do not require intracellular activation by phosphorylation**.
	    
	- **Uses:** Not used as monotherapy (resistance develops). Generally **reserved for failure cases** in initial regimens as their use can restrict future treatment options. A **boosted PI regimen** (combining with a low, subtherapeutic dose of Ritonavir) is common to improve bioavailability and reduce clearance.
	    
	- **Adverse Effects (General):**
	    
	    - Gastrointestinal intolerance, asthenia, headache, dizziness, limb and facial tingling, numbness, rashes.
	    - **Metabolic complications are a prominent concern:** **Lipodystrophy** (abdominal obesity, buffalo hump, wasting of limbs/face), **dyslipidaemia** (raised triglycerides and cholesterol), **insulin resistance, and exacerbation of diabetes**.
	- **Examples:** Atazanavir (ATV), Lopinavir (LPV/r), Darunavir (DRV), Ritonavir (RTV).
	    
	    - **Atazanavir (ATV)**
	        
	        - **Pharmacokinetics:** Absorption improved with light meals; metabolized by CYP3A4. Bioavailability improved by boosting with Ritonavir.
	        - **Adverse Effects:** Minimal dyslipidaemia and other metabolic complications, but **jaundice occurs in some patients** (due to inhibition of hepatic glucuronyl transferase).
	    - **Lopinavir (LPV/r)**
	        
	        - **Pharmacokinetics:** Available only in combination with Ritonavir to improve bioavailability.
	        - **Adverse Effects:** Diarrhoea, abdominal pain, nausea, and dyslipidaemias are common. ECG abnormalities noted.
	    - **Darunavir (DRV)**
	        
	        - **Mechanism:** Potent newer PI, active against HIV-1 and HIV-2, including strains resistant to other PIs.
	        - **Pharmacokinetics:** Improved bioavailability when co-administered with Ritonavir.
	
	**4. HIV Integrase Inhibitors (INSTIs)**
	
	- **Mechanism of Action:** These drugs inhibit HIV integrase, a viral enzyme that integrates the proviral DNA (transcribed from viral RNA) into the host cell's chromosomal DNA. By blocking this step, they prevent the permanent infection of the host cell.
	    
	- **Examples:** Raltegravir, Dolutegravir (DTG).
	    
	    - **Raltegravir**
	        
	        - **Pharmacokinetics:** Rapid oral absorption, extensively glucuronide conjugated in the liver (plasma t½ ~12 hours).
	        - **Interactions:** Rifampin induces its metabolism (dose needs to be doubled). Cation-containing antacids (Ca2+, Mg2+) and iron supplements should be avoided or staggered due to chelating property.
	        - **Side Effects:** Nausea, headache, diarrhoea, weakness; myopathy is a potential toxicity.
	        - **Uses:** Used along with 2 NRTIs. In countries where cost is not a major constraint, it is preferred due to high efficacy and better tolerability.
	    - **Dolutegravir (DTG)**
	        
	        - **Mechanism:** Second-generation integrase inhibitor, active against both HIV-1 and HIV-2, retains some activity against raltegravir-resistant viruses.
	        - **Uses:** Combined with 2 NRTIs, it has shown superior results to raltegravir-containing regimens. Gaining popularity as a **1st line regimen for treatment-naive HIV patients** due to potent activity, good tolerability, and once-daily dosing (cost permitting).
	        - **Interactions:** Dose should be doubled when given with enzyme inducers like rifampin and efavirenz. Avoid combination with nevirapine and etravirine. Cationic medications should be avoided or staggered.
	
	**5. Entry Inhibitors**
	
	- **Mechanism of Action:** These drugs target the initial steps of HIV infection, such as viral entry into the host cell.
	    
	- **Examples:** Maraviroc (CCR5 receptor inhibitor).
	    
	    - **Maraviroc**
	        - **Mechanism:** A novel anti-HIV drug that targets the **host cell CCR5 chemokine receptor** and blocks it, interfering with viral attachment and subsequent entry of the viral genome into the cell.
	        - **Specificity:** Only effective against HIV strains that are CCR5-tropic; has no effect on CXCR4-tropic or dual CCR5/CXCR4-tropic strains.
	
	**Clinical Management Considerations:**
	
	- **Regimen Selection:** Initial 1st-line regimens universally include **2 NRTIs + 1 NNRTI**. Preferred NRTIs are lamivudine, abacavir, tenofovir, and emtricitabine. Efavirenz is preferred over nevirapine as the NNRTI. WHO (2016) preferred regimens include Tenofovir + Lamivudine + Efavirenz or Tenofovir + Emtricitabine + Efavirenz. Integrase inhibitors (raltegravir, dolutegravir) plus 2 NRTIs are increasingly preferred in settings where cost is not a barrier.
	- **Adherence (Compliance):** **Compliance is a major determinant of outcome** and durability of regimens. Use of fixed-dose drug combinations (FDCs) greatly improves convenience and adherence.
	- **Drug Resistance:** High mutation rate of HIV reverse transcriptase leads to rapid development of drug resistance if suboptimal regimens or monotherapy are used. Drug resistance studies are recommended before initiating ART, especially in developed countries. Viral resistance testing is recommended for selecting salvage (second-line) regimens, which almost always include a **boosted PI**.
	- **Drug Interactions:** Careful attention to drug interactions and toxicities is crucial, especially when multiple drugs (ARVs, anti-tuberculosis, anti-fungals, etc.) are used concurrently. Rifampin, a potent enzyme inducer, significantly enhances the metabolism of PIs and some NNRTIs, making them ineffective. Rifabutin (a less potent inducer) may be substituted for rifampin in HIV patients receiving these drugs.
	- **Special Populations:**
	    - **HIV-AIDS and TB:** TB treatment regimens are the same as in non-HIV cases, typically 4 drugs (HRZE) initially, followed by HRE. Thrice-weekly regimens should not be used in HIV-positive patients due to higher relapse/failure rates and increased risk of rifampin resistance. Pyridoxine is given with isoniazid to counteract neurological side effects, especially in AIDS patients.
	    - **Mother-to-Child Transmission:** Offspring of HIV-positive pregnant women can be protected by treating the woman with tenofovir + lamivudine + efavirenz during pregnancy, and the neonate with syrup nevirapine for 6 weeks after delivery.
	    - **Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP):** A 4-week regimen of at least 2 ARV drugs (mostly 2 NRTIs), preferably with an added 3rd drug of a different class, significantly reduces HIV infection risk if started soon after exposure.
4. Protease inhibitors-name and mechanism of action
	### Protease Inhibitors (PIs)
	
	**Names of Key Protease Inhibitors:**
	
	- **Atazanavir (ATV)**
	- **Indinavir (IDV)**
	- **Nelfinavir (NFV)**
	- **Saquinavir (SQV)**
	- **Ritonavir (RTV)**
	- **Lopinavir** (often combined with ritonavir, **LPV/r**)
	- **Fosamprenavir (FPV)**
	- **Darunavir (DRV)**
	
	**Mechanism of Action:**
	
	- PIs act at a **late step in HIV replication**, specifically the **maturation** of new virus particles.
	- They **competitively inhibit the HIV protease enzyme**.
	- HIV protease is essential for cleaving viral polyproteins into their final functional, structural, and enzymatic components.
	- By inhibiting this cleavage, PIs prevent the formation of mature, functional viral components.
	- This results in the production of **immature, non-infectious viral progeny**, thereby preventing further rounds of infection.
	- **Crucially, PIs do not require intracellular activation by phosphorylation**.
	- They are effective in both newly and chronically infected cells.
	
	**Clinical Relevance / Key Points:**
	
	- **Clinical Use:** PIs are anti-retroviral drugs primarily used in the treatment of Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection and Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS).
	- **Combination Therapy:** They are typically used **orally in combination with reverse transcriptase inhibitors** in patients with AIDS, and other effective drugs.
	- **Cross-Resistance:** Cross-resistance is common among different PIs, but generally not with reverse transcriptase inhibitors.
	- **Pharmacokinetics:**
		- Extensively **metabolized in the liver**.
		- Oral absorption can be variable; some need to be taken with meals, others on an empty stomach.
	- **'Boosted PI Regimen' (Clinical Significance):**
		- A common strategy involves combining PIs (except nelfinavir) with a **low, subtherapeutic dose of ritonavir**.
		- Ritonavir acts as a **potent CYP3A4 inhibitor**, reducing the first-pass metabolism and systemic clearance of the co-administered PI.
		- This boosting strategy **increases the bioavailability** and prolongs the plasma levels of the companion PI, allowing for **lower doses and/or reduced frequency of administration**, which improves patient compliance by reducing the tablet burden.
	- **Drug Interactions:** PIs are subject to many complex and often unpredictable drug interactions due to their effects on CYP isoenzymes (both induction and inhibition). For example, rifampin induces their metabolism, rendering them ineffective.
	- **Adverse Effects (High-Yield for Exams):**
		- Gastrointestinal: Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea.
		- Metabolic: Skeletal muscle wasting, **lipodystrophy**, insulin resistance, **diabetes**.
