 1. Biphosphonates (MOA, uses and adverse effects)
	Here’s a **high-yield, exam-focused summary** on **Bisphosphonates** suitable for medical school:
	
	---
	
	## **Bisphosphonates**
	
	**Class:** Anti-resorptive agents / Bone-modifying drugs
	
	---
	
	### **1. Mechanism of Action (MOA)**
	
	**Primary Action:**
	
	- Bisphosphonates are **structural analogs of pyrophosphate** → bind to **hydroxyapatite in bone**
	    
	- Inhibit **osteoclast-mediated bone resorption**
	    
	
	**Two Classes & MOA Details:**
	
	1. **Non-nitrogenous bisphosphonates** (e.g., Etidronate)
	    
	    - Metabolized into **cytotoxic ATP analogs** → induce **osteoclast apoptosis**
	        
	2. **Nitrogen-containing bisphosphonates** (e.g., Alendronate, Risedronate, Zoledronate)
	    
	    - Inhibit **farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase** in **mevalonate pathway**
	        
	    - Disrupt **prenylation of small GTP-binding proteins** → osteoclast apoptosis
	        
	
	**Net Effect:** ↓ bone resorption → ↑ bone mineral density → ↓ fracture risk
	
	**High-yield:** Nitrogen-containing bisphosphonates are **more potent** and widely used.
	
	---
	
	### **2. Clinical Uses**
	
	**Primary:**
	
	- **Osteoporosis** (postmenopausal, age-related, glucocorticoid-induced)
	    
	- **Paget’s disease of bone**
	    
	- **Hypercalcemia of malignancy**
	    
	
	**Other:**
	
	- Bone metastases (to reduce skeletal events)
	    
	- Multiple myeloma (to reduce bone lesions)
	    
	
	**High-yield clinical point:**
	
	- Oral bisphosphonates → first-line for **postmenopausal osteoporosis**
	    
	- IV forms → used for **malignancy-related hypercalcemia** or **patients intolerant to oral drugs**
	    
	
	---
	
	### **3. Adverse Effects (ADEs)**
	
	**Common:**
	
	- Gastrointestinal irritation: **esophagitis, gastritis** (oral forms)
	    
	- Musculoskeletal pain
	    
	
	**Serious:**
	
	- **Osteonecrosis of the jaw (ONJ)** – mostly IV, high-dose, oncology patients
	    
	- **Atypical femoral fractures** – prolonged therapy (>5 years)
	    
	- Hypocalcemia (especially in patients with low vitamin D or renal failure)
	    
	
	**Other:**
	
	- Acute phase reaction (fever, myalgia) with IV bisphosphonates
	    
	
	**High-yield caution:**
	
	- Take oral bisphosphonates **with a full glass of water, upright for 30–60 min**, to reduce esophageal irritation
	    
	- Monitor **serum calcium, renal function**
	    
	
	---
	
	### **Memory Tips / Mnemonics**
	
	1. **“BISPHOS = Bone Inhibitor Stops Phosphate Osteoclasts”** → MOA reminder
	    
	2. **Oral ADE mnemonic:** _“Heartburn & Pain”_ → esophagitis, gastritis, musculoskeletal pain
	    
	3. **Serious ADE mnemonic:** _“Jaw & Thigh”_ → Osteonecrosis of jaw, Atypical femur fracture

	### **Bisphosphonates (BPNs)**
	
	**1. Definition and Overview**
	
	- Bisphosphonates are **analogues of pyrophosphate** with a carbon atom replacing oxygen in the P-O-P skeleton.
	- They are the **most effective antiresorptive drugs** used to **inhibit bone resorption** and are valuable in preventing osteoporosis, as well as in treating metabolic bone diseases and hypercalcaemia.
	- BPNs are grouped into three generations based on their potency: first, second (e.g., Pamidronate, Alendronate, Ibandronate), and third (e.g., Risedronate, Zoledronate). The second and third-generation compounds are more potent and have higher efficacy.
	
	**2. Mechanism of Action (MOA)** BPNs interfere with bone resorption through a multifaceted mechanism:
	
	- **High Affinity for Calcified Tissue**: BPNs have a strong affinity for calcium phosphate and selectively localise in calcified tissue, particularly in the acidic zone under osteoclasts at resorptive pits.
	- **Internalisation by Osteoclasts**: When calcium ions are released from the bone surface due to high acidity, BPNs are also released and **internalised into osteoclasts by endocytosis**.
	- **Inhibition of Osteoclast Function**: Once inside osteoclasts, they cause:
	    - **Impaired vesicle fusion**.
	    - **Enhanced apoptosis** of osteoclasts.
	    - **Interference with the mevalonate pathway**, which may also contribute to anti-tumour activity in bony metastasis.
	
	**3. Uses** BPNs are effective in conditions characterised by enhanced bone turnover.
	
	- **Osteoporosis**:
	    - Effective in preventing and treating **postmenopausal, age-related, idiopathic, and steroid-induced osteoporosis** in both men and women.
	    - **Alendronate** has been shown to be as or more effective than hormone replacement therapy (HRT) or raloxifene in conserving bone mineral density.
	    - They **reduce the risk of vertebral and hip fractures** (e.g., Alendronate by 47-56%).
	    - BPNs **do not directly stimulate new bone formation**; they primarily check bone resorption.
	- **Paget's Disease**:
	    - BPNs effectively **arrest bone remodelling** and induce remission.
	    - They are generally **more convenient, effective, and cheaper than calcitonin**.
	    - Combined use of BPNs and calcitonin can further increase efficacy.
	    - Treatment with BPNs should generally **not exceed 6 months**, but courses may be repeated after a gap.
	- **Hypercalcaemia of Malignancy**:
	    - Severe hypercalcaemia is a medical emergency requiring rapid rehydration with intravenous (i.v.) saline infusion as the first step.
	    - **Pamidronate (i.v.)** or **Zoledronate (i.v.)** are the most effective drugs, though they take 24-48 hours to act. Zoledronate is now the **drug of choice** due to its higher efficacy and faster action, and can be infused over 15 minutes.
	    - They can be supplemented by intramuscular (i.m.) calcitonin for a rapid initial action (for up to 2 days).
	    - **Oral BPNs are not useful** in this acute setting.
	- **Osteolytic Bone Metastasis**:
	    - Parenteral Pamidronate or Zoledronate can **arrest osteolytic lesions and reduce bone pain**.
	    - Potent BPNs, especially Zoledronate, have shown **independent anti-tumour properties** and adjuvant value in reducing breast carcinoma recurrences and delaying skeletal events in women with bony metastasis.
	
	**4. Adverse Effects**
	
	- **Common/Administration-Related (Oral BPNs)**:
	    - **Gastric irritation** and **oesophagitis** are major side effects. This can manifest as **gastric erosion** and **retrosternal pain**.
	    - **Flatulence, headache, and bodyache** are also reported with Alendronate.
	    - Initial fall in serum Ca2+ level can occur.
	- **Common (Intravenous BPNs)**:
	    - **Thrombophlebitis** of the injected vein.
	    - **Bone pain**.
	    - **Fever** and **flu-like reactions** due to cytokine release.
	    - Nausea, vomiting, and dizziness.
	- **Serious Complications (Rare but Significant)**:
	    - **Renal toxicity** has been encountered with Zoledronate.
	    - **Osteonecrosis of the jaw (ONJ)** is a rare but serious complication, primarily associated with **i.v. high-dose BPN therapy**.
	    - Etidronate (a first-generation BPN) can interfere with bone mineralisation, causing **osteomalacia** with continuous therapy, leading to its replacement by newer agents.
	
	**5. Contraindications**
	
	- **Oral BPNs**:
	    - **Gastro-oesophageal reflux**.
	    - **Peptic ulcer**.
	    - **Renal impairment**.
	- **General**:
	    - **Hypocalcaemia** is a risk, and the dose of calcium-sensing receptor agonists (like Cinacalcet) needs careful adjustment to avoid it.
	    - **Gallium nitrate**, a potent inhibitor of bone resorption, is nephrotoxic and is reserved for resistant cases of malignancy-associated hypercalcaemia.
	
	**6. Clinical Relevance and Administration Notes**
	
	- **Oral Administration**: **Crucial for compliance and efficacy**.
	    - Must be taken on an **empty stomach** in the morning with a **full glass of water**.
	    - The patient must **not lie down or take food for at least 30 minutes** after taking the pill.
	    - The tablet/capsule **should not be chewed**.
	    - These measures are essential to **prevent contact of the drug with oesophageal mucosa**, which can cause oesophagitis, erosions, and ulcers.
	- **Drug Interactions Affecting Absorption**:
	    - Calcium, iron, antacids, mineral water, tea, coffee, and fruit juice interfere with BPN absorption by forming non-absorbable complexes.
	    - Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) can accentuate the gastric irritation caused by BPNs.
	- **Dosing Frequency**: Intravenous administration allows for reduced dosing frequency, sometimes as infrequently as once a year, due to the sequestration of about 50% of the absorbed/injected drug in the bone.

2. Vitamin D (calciferol)- preparations, uses and adverse effects
	### **Vitamin D (Calciferol)**
	
	**1. Overview & Physiology**
	
	- **Definition:** Collective name for antirachitic substances, primarily **Cholecalciferol (Vit D3)** (synthesized in skin via UV) and **Calciferol (Vit D2)** (from irradiated foods).
	- **Activation (Hormone-like):** Vit D is a pro-hormone, undergoing two hydroxylations:
	    - 1. In liver: to **25-hydroxyvitamin D (calcifediol)**.
	    - 2. In kidney: to **1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol)**, the **active form**. This kidney step is **rate-limiting** and regulated by calcium deficiency, PTH, and calcitriol feedback.
	- **Physiological Actions (Calcitriol)**:
	    - **↑ Intestinal Absorption:** Enhances **calcium (Ca²⁺) and phosphate (PO₄³⁻)** absorption by increasing synthesis of calcium channels and **Calcium Binding Protein (CaBP)**.
	    - **Bone Remodelling:** Enhances bone resorption (by promoting osteoclast precursors) and helps bone mineralization indirectly by maintaining normal Ca²⁺/PO₄³⁻ levels and inducing osteocalcin.
	    - **↑ Renal Reabsorption:** Increases tubular reabsorption of Ca²⁺ and PO₄³⁻ in kidney (less marked than PTH).
	
	**2. Preparations & Dosage**
	
	- **Unit Conversion:** 1 µg Cholecalciferol = **40 IU** of Vitamin D.
	- **Daily Requirement:** Varies; generally **400 IU/day** to prevent deficiency; up to **1000 IU/day** recommended.
	- **Available Forms:**
	    - **Cholecalciferol (Vit D3):** Granules, oily solution for IM inj (e.g., ARACHITOL 300,000-600,000 IU/ml). Oral sachets/caps/tabs (e.g., CALCIROL 60,000 IU) given every 3-4 weeks, then 2-6 months.
	    - **Calciferol (Vit D2):** Oral solutions/capsules (25,000-50,000 IU).
	    - **Calcitriol (Active form):** **0.25-1 µg orally daily or on alternate days**. Also IV (0.5-1 µg alternate days).
	    - **Alfacalcidol (1α-OHD3):** Prodrug converted to calcitriol in liver. Oral (1-2 µg/day; children <20kg 0.5 µg/day).
	    - **Dihydrotachysterol:** Synthetic D2 analogue, useful in hypoparathyroidism/renal bone disease (0.25-0.5 mg/day).
	    - **Calcipotriol:** Non-hypercalcaemic analogue, used topically for psoriasis.
	
	**3. Uses**
	
	- **1. Nutritional Vitamin D Deficiency:**
	    - **Prophylaxis:** 400 IU/day.
	    - **Treatment (Rickets in children, Osteomalacia in adults):** 3000-4000 IU/day orally, or large intermittent doses (300,000-600,000 IU) every 2-6 months IM/oral. Also for deficiency due to obstructive jaundice or steatorrhoea.
	- **2. Metabolic Rickets:**
	    - **Vit D-Resistant Rickets (X-linked):** High dose calcitriol/alfacalcidol + phosphate.
	    - **Vit D-Dependent Rickets:** Calcitriol/alfacalcidol in normal doses.
	    - **Renal Rickets (Chronic Kidney Disease):** Calcitriol/alfacalcidol or dihydrotachysterol (as renal hydroxylation is impaired).
	- **3. Senile/Postmenopausal Osteoporosis:**
	    - Vit D3 + Calcium can improve calcium balance.
	    - **Calcitriol/Alfacalcidol** suppress parathyroids and reduce bone remodelling, thus beneficial in established osteoporosis.
	    - **Clinical Note:** While Vitamin D is important, the benefit of **calcium ± Vit D supplements alone** in increasing bone mass or preventing fractures is **controversial/marginal**. **Bisphosphonates are the most effective drugs for osteoporosis**. Calcium supplements should be given only if dietary intake is low due to potential MI risk.
	- **4. Hypoparathyroidism:** Dihydrotachysterol, calcitriol, or alfacalcidol are preferred due to direct action without PTH-dependent kidney hydroxylation.
	- **5. Fanconi Syndrome:** Can help raise lowered phosphate levels.
	- **6. Psoriasis:** **Calcipotriol** (topical, non-hypercalcaemic).
	
	**4. Adverse Effects (Hypervitaminosis D / Toxicity)**
	
	- Caused by chronic ingestion of large doses (e.g., >50,000 IU/day) or increased tissue sensitivity.
	- **Primary Manifestation: Hypercalcaemia**.
	- **Symptoms:** Weakness, fatigue, vomiting, diarrhoea, sluggishness, polyuria, albuminuria.
	- **Ectopic Calcium Deposition:** In soft tissues, blood vessels, parenchymal organs, renal stones, nephrocalcinosis.
	- **Other Effects:** Hypertension, growth retardation in children, rarely coma.
	- **Management:** **Withholding Vitamin D**, low calcium diet, plenty of fluids, and **corticosteroids**. Recovery may be incomplete.
	
	**5. Drug Interactions**
	
	- **Reduced Absorption:** Cholestyramine and liquid paraffin.
	- **Reduced Responsiveness:** **Phenytoin and Phenobarbitone** (prolonged use can cause rickets/osteomalacia by diminishing calcitriol's effect on intestine and bone, not by enhancing degradation as previously thought).
