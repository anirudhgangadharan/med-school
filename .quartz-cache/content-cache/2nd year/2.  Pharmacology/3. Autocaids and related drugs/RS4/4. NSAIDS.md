1. A 62-year-old man presented with pain in both knees, worsened by walking. The doctor made a diagnosis of osteoarthritis and prescribed one of the nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs).
	a. Mention any four NSAIDs useful in this condition.
		b. Discuss the adverse effects of NSAIDS.
	Here's a concise, high-yield, note-style explanation of NSAIDs for osteoarthritis, optimized for medical school exams:
	
	---
	
	### NSAIDs in Osteoarthritis: Drug Choices & Adverse Effects
	
	**Clinical Context:** Osteoarthritis (OA) is a degenerative joint disease commonly affecting older individuals, characterised by pain worsened by activity. NSAIDs are frequently used for symptomatic relief.
	
	#### a. Four NSAIDs Useful in Osteoarthritis:
	
	1. **Diclofenac**: Widely used for rheumatoid and osteoarthritis, providing quick pain relief and reducing wound oedema.
	2. **Piroxicam**: A long-acting potent NSAID, suitable for long-term anti-inflammatory use in OA and rheumatoid arthritis.
	3. **Celecoxib**: A selective COX-2 inhibitor with low ulcerogenic potential, effective in OA and rheumatoid arthritis.
	4. **Etoricoxib**: A newer COX-2 inhibitor with high selectivity, suitable for once-daily treatment of OA, rheumatoid arthritis, and acute gouty arthritis.
	
	_Note:_ **Paracetamol** is often considered the first-choice analgesic for most OA cases, particularly for mild-to-moderate pain with little inflammation. Topical NSAIDs, such as **diclofenac gel**, are also recommended for mild-to-moderate OA of the knee and hand as initial or adjunctive therapy.
	
	#### b. Adverse Effects of NSAIDs:
	
	NSAIDs typically share a spectrum of adverse effects, though their quantitative incidence varies significantly among individual drugs and patients.
	
	5. **Gastrointestinal (GI) System:**
	    
	    - **Gastric mucosal damage:** Common with non-selective COX inhibitors, leading to peptic ulcer and bleeding. This is due to inhibition of protective prostaglandins (PGs) that maintain gastric mucus, bicarbonate secretion, and mucosal blood flow.
	    - Common GI side effects include dyspepsia, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhoea.
	    - **Activation of peptic ulcer** is a significant risk.
	6. **Renal System:**
	    
	    - **Sodium and water retention**, leading to **oedema** and **rise in blood pressure (BP)**.
	    - **Impairment of renal blood flow** and reduction in glomerular filtration rate (GFR), which can **worsen renal insufficiency**.
	    - Risk of **chronic renal failure**, nephropathy, and rarely papillary necrosis.
	    - **Comorbid renal insufficiency** is aggravated by NSAIDs.
	    - Some (e.g., indomethacin) augment AVP-induced antidiuresis by inhibiting renal PG synthesis.
	7. **Cardiovascular (CVS) System:**
	    
	    - **Rise in BP** and **risk of myocardial infarction (MI)**, particularly with COX-2 inhibitors.
	    - Can precipitate or aggravate **congestive heart failure (CHF)**.
	    - COX-2 inhibitors lack the cardioprotective property of aspirin, as they do not depress TXA2 production by platelets but reduce PGI2 production by vascular endothelium. Patients with hypertension or other risk factors for heart attack/stroke should avoid selective COX-2 inhibitors.
	8. **Haematological System:**
	    
	    - **Prolonged bleeding time** due to interference with platelet aggregation (e.g., aspirin irreversibly inhibits TXA2 synthesis by platelets, with effects lasting about a week).
	    - Long-term high-dose use can decrease synthesis of clotting factors in the liver, predisposing to bleeding.
	    - **Haemolytic anaemia** (e.g., with mephenamic acid, rare but serious).
	    - **Haemolysis** in G-6PD deficient individuals (e.g., aspirin, dapsone, sulfonamides, chloramphenicol, nalidixic acid, primaquine, nitrofurantoin, quinolone/quinidine).
	9. **Hypersensitivity Reactions:**
	    
	    - **Precipitation of aspirin-sensitive asthma** (more common with non-selective COX inhibitors).
	    - Rashes, pruritus, and serious skin reactions are possible.
	10. **Other Important Effects:**
	    
	    - **CNS effects:** Drowsiness, dizziness, headache, nervousness, mental confusion (more with older agents like indomethacin).
	    - **Liver dysfunction and jaundice** (e.g., with piroxicam, rare reports).
	    - **Hyperuricaemia** and gout (more common with thiazides, but also seen with furosemide, less so with newer low-dose thiazides).
	    - **Hyperglycaemia and dyslipidaemia** (minimal with low-dose thiazides now).
	    - **Delayed/prolongation of labour**.
	    - **Hearing loss** (infrequent, mainly with high-ceiling diuretics; risk increased with renal insufficiency).
	
	**Clinical Relevance & Precautions:**
	
	- Patients with **pre-existing conditions** like peptic ulcer, hypertension, CHF, renal insufficiency, or haemostatic disorders should be monitored closely or alternative drugs chosen.
	- **Elderly patients** are more susceptible to adverse effects; lower doses are recommended.
	- Combining NSAIDs with **warfarin** increases the risk of bleeding.
	- NSAIDs can **impair the efficacy of certain antihypertensives** and other drugs.

2. Aspirin (salicyllates) - mechanism of action, pharmacological actions, adverse effects and uses
	
	### Aspirin (Acetylsalicylic Acid): Pharmacology & Clinical Relevance
	
	Aspirin is a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) with analgesic, antipyretic, and anti-inflammatory actions. It is distinct from opioids due to its lack of CNS depression, physical dependence, or abuse liability, and its primary action on peripheral pain mechanisms.
	
	#### Mechanism of Action
	
	Aspirin is **acetylsalicylic acid**. In the body, it is rapidly converted to **salicylic acid**, which is responsible for most of its actions.
	
	- **Irreversible inhibition of Cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes:**
	    - Aspirin **acetylates and irreversibly inactivates COX-1 and TX-synthase**.
	    - This leads to the **suppression of TXA2 synthesis** by platelets. Platelets cannot synthesize fresh enzyme (lack nuclei), so this effect lasts for about 5-7 days until new platelets are formed.
	    - It also inhibits COX-1 and COX-2 enzymes involved in prostaglandin (PG) synthesis, particularly at inflammatory sites.
	    - At low doses, aspirin **selectively inhibits TXA2 production** (antiplatelet effect) while at high doses, it inhibits both TXA2 and PGI2 synthesis.
	- **Other mechanisms:** Quenching of free radicals may contribute to its anti-inflammatory action.
	
	#### Pharmacological Actions
	
	1. **Analgesic Action:**
	    
	    - **Weaker analgesic** than morphine-type drugs.
	    - Effective in **inflammatory, tissue injury-related, connective tissue, and integumental pain**.
	    - **Ineffective in severe visceral and ischaemic pain**.
	    - Primarily due to **obtunding of peripheral pain receptors** and prevention of PG-mediated sensitization of nerve endings.
	    - A central subcortical action also raises pain perception threshold.
	    - Produces **no sedation, subjective effects, tolerance, or dependence**.
	    - Maximal effect at ~1000 mg single dose.
	2. **Antipyretic Action:**
	    
	    - **Resets the hypothalamic thermostat** and rapidly reduces fever by promoting heat loss (sweating, cutaneous vasodilation).
	    - Does not decrease heat production.
	    - Inhibits COX-2 (and possibly COX-3) which mediates pyrogen-induced fever.
	3. **Anti-inflammatory Action:**
	    
	    - Requires **high doses** (3-6 g/day or 100 mg/kg/day).
	    - **Suppresses signs of inflammation** (pain, tenderness, swelling, vasodilation, leukocyte infiltration).
	    - Does **not affect the progression of underlying diseases** like rheumatoid arthritis or rheumatic fever.
	4. **Gastrointestinal (GI) System:**
	    
	    - **Irritates gastric mucosa**, causing epigastric distress, nausea, and vomiting.
	    - **"Ion trapping"**: Aspirin (pKa 3.5) remains unionized in acid gastric juice, diffuses into mucosal cells (pH 7.1), then ionizes and becomes indiffusible, enhancing gastric toxicity.
	    - Promotes **local back diffusion of acid**, causing focal necrosis, erosive gastritis, and microscopic hemorrhages.
	    - **Increased occult blood loss** in stools even with a single tablet; averages 5 mL/day at anti-inflammatory doses.
	    - May lead to **peptic ulceration and bleeding/perforation**. Soluble/buffered preparations do not significantly lower the incidence of ulceration and bleeding.
	5. **Blood System:**
	    
	    - **Prolongs bleeding time** (for about a week) due to irreversible inhibition of TXA2 synthesis by platelets, interfering with platelet aggregation.
	    - **Long-term high dose intake** can decrease synthesis of clotting factors in the liver, predisposing to bleeding.
	    - **Haemolysis** in G-6PD deficient individuals.
	6. **Metabolic and Other Effects (at high/toxic doses):**
	    
	    - Can induce **salicylism** (dizziness, tinnitus, vertigo, reversible impairment of hearing and vision, excitement, hyperventilation, electrolyte imbalance).
	    - May **increase cardiac output** indirectly by enhancing metabolic rate.
	    - Toxic doses can **depress vasomotor centre**, causing a fall in BP.
	    - Can precipitate **CHF** if cardiac reserve is low.
	    - Interferes with urate excretion; at high doses (>5g/day), may increase urate excretion, but not a reliable uricosuric.
	
	#### Adverse Effects
	
	- **Common (at analgesic doses):** Nausea, vomiting, epigastric distress, increased occult blood loss in stools, gastric mucosal damage, peptic ulceration.
	- **Hypersensitivity & Idiosyncrasy (infrequent but serious):** Rashes, fixed drug eruption, urticaria, rhinorrhoea, angioedema, **asthma precipitation (aspirin-sensitive asthma)**, anaphylactoid reaction. Profuse gastric bleeding can occur in rare instances.
	- **Salicylism (at anti-inflammatory/toxic doses):** Dizziness, tinnitus, vertigo, reversible hearing/vision impairment, excitement, hyperventilation, electrolyte imbalance.
	- **Liver damage:** Raised serum transaminases in children with rheumatoid arthritis.
	- **Reye's Syndrome:** A rare, but serious hepatic encephalopathy in children with viral infections (varicella, influenza). **Paediatric formulations are prohibited in India and UK due to this risk**.
	- **Renal:** Sodium and water retention, edema, chronic renal failure, nephropathy, papillary necrosis (rare).
	- **Cardiovascular:** Rise in BP, risk of myocardial infarction (especially with COX-2 inhibitors), CHF.
	- **Pregnancy:** Repeated use may cause low birth weight, delayed/prolonged labor, greater postpartum blood loss, and **premature closure of ductus arteriosus** if taken near term. Avoided by breastfeeding mothers.
	- **Other:** Haemolysis in G-6PD deficient individuals.
	
	#### Uses
	
	1. **Analgesic:** For headache (including mild migraine), backache, myalgia, joint pain, pulled muscle, toothache, neuralgias, and dysmenorrhoea.
	2. **Antipyretic:** Effective in fever of any origin, but **paracetamol is generally preferred due to better safety profile**.
	3. **Acute Rheumatic Fever:** First-line drug for symptomatic relief, though it does not alter the course of cardiac complications.
	4. **Rheumatoid Arthritis & Osteoarthritis:** Provides symptomatic relief, but large doses are poorly tolerated for long periods, so **less frequently used now; newer NSAIDs or paracetamol are preferred**.
	5. **Cardiovascular Prophylaxis (Antiplatelet):**
	    - **Post-myocardial infarction (MI) & post-stroke:** Reduces incidence of reinfarction and mortality; routinely prescribed.
	    - **Acute Coronary Syndromes (ACS):** Soluble aspirin is given at presentation (UA, NSTEMI, STEMI). Combined with clopidogrel/prasugrel/ticagrelor for high-risk patients undergoing PCI.
	    - **Transient Ischaemic Attacks (TIAs) & Stroke Prevention:** Reduces incidence of TIAs and stroke; given to patients not treated with anticoagulants.
	    - **Prosthetic Heart Valves & Arterio-venous Shunts:** Reduces microthrombi formation and embolism when used with warfarin.
	6. **Prevention of Preeclampsia:** Low-dose aspirin (80-100 mg/day from 12th week of gestation) reduces risk in predisposed pregnant women.
	7. **Familial Colonic Polyposis & Colon Cancer Prevention:** Suppresses polyp formation and lowers colon cancer incidence in regular users. (Note: Some COX-2 inhibitors like rofecoxib and celecoxib showed increased cardiovascular events in trials for this indication).
	8. **Prevention of Flushing with Nicotinic Acid:** Due to PGD2 release in the skin.
	
	**Clinical Precaution:**
	
	- **Contraindicated** in aspirin-sensitive patients, peptic ulcer, bleeding tendencies, children with chickenpox or influenza (Reye's syndrome risk).
	- Avoid in chronic liver disease (hepatic necrosis risk), diabetics, low cardiac reserve/CHF.
	- Stop 1 week before elective surgery due to prolonged bleeding time.
	- Drug interactions: Displaces warfarin, sulfonylureas, phenytoin, methotrexate from protein binding sites, increasing toxicity risk. Antagonizes uricosuric action of probenecid and blunts diuretic action of furosemide/thiazides.
3. Aspirin is contraindicated in patients with Peptic ulcer. Give reasons
	**Aspirin (Salicylates): Contraindications in Peptic Ulcer Disease**
	
	Aspirin is **contraindicated in patients with peptic ulcer disease** due to its multifaceted mechanisms that **aggravate gastric mucosal damage and increase the risk of bleeding/perforation**.
	
	**Key Reasons for Contraindication:**
	
	2. **Direct Mucosal Irritation & Local Damage:**
	    
	    - Both **acetylsalicylic acid (aspirin)** and its active metabolite **salicylic acid** directly irritate the gastric mucosa.
	    - Aspirin particles in contact with the gastric lining promote **local back diffusion of acid**, leading to **focal necrosis of mucosal cells and capillaries**.
	    - This direct damage can manifest as acute ulcers, erosive gastritis, and microscopic haemorrhages.
	    - Even a single tablet can increase **occult blood loss in stools** (averaging 5 mL/day at anti-inflammatory doses), and potentially cause haematemesis.
	3. **Ion Trapping:**
	    
	    - Aspirin (a weak acid, pKa 3.5) remains largely **unionized** in the highly acidic gastric lumen, allowing it to **diffuse readily into gastric mucosal cells**.
	    - Once inside the mucosal cell (which has a near-neutral pH of 7.1), aspirin **ionizes**, becoming trapped (i.e., "ion trapping") and **unable to easily escape**, thus concentrating within the cells and **enhancing its direct gastric toxicity**.
	4. **Inhibition of Gastroprotective Prostaglandin (PG) Synthesis:**
	    
	    - Aspirin is a **non-selective, irreversible inhibitor of Cyclooxygenase (COX-1 and COX-2) enzymes** [previous response, 337].
	    - **COX-1** is constitutively expressed in the gastric mucosa and is responsible for synthesizing **gastroprotective prostaglandins (PGE2, PGI2)**.
	    - These PGs play a crucial defensive role by:
	        - **Reducing gastric acid secretion**.
	        - **Increasing mucus and bicarbonate (HCO3-) secretion**, which form a protective barrier.
	        - **Improving mucosal blood flow**, which aids in rapid repair and removal of damaging agents.
	    - By **irreversibly inhibiting COX-1**, aspirin leads to a **deficiency of these protective PGs**.
	    - This **impairs the stomach's defensive mechanisms**, thereby promoting and worsening ulceration.
	5. **Increased Bleeding Risk:**
	    
	    - Aspirin **irreversibly inhibits platelet aggregation** by suppressing TXA2 synthesis (a COX-1 dependent process). This effect persists for the lifetime of the platelet (approx. 5-7 days).
	    - This **antiplatelet action prolongs bleeding time**, significantly **increasing the risk of gastrointestinal bleeding** from pre-existing ulcers or aspirin-induced erosions [previous response, 348].
	    - The risk of bleeding is **further enhanced** when aspirin is taken concurrently with alcohol.
	
	**Clinical Relevance:**
	
	- Peptic ulcer disease involves an imbalance between aggressive and defensive factors. Aspirin critically impacts both, tilting the balance towards worsening the disease.
	- While paracetamol, a very weak COX inhibitor, is generally safe for patients with gastric issues, and selective COX-2 inhibitors are relatively safer in terms of gastric toxicity, aspirin directly poses a high risk.
	- Due to these severe local and systemic effects on the GI tract and haemostasis, aspirin is **strictly contraindicated** in patients with a history of peptic ulcer or bleeding tendencies.
4. Paracetamol poisoning treatment
	Here's a concise, high-yield, note-style summary of Paracetamol (Acetaminophen) poisoning treatment for medical school exams:
	
	---
	
	### Paracetamol (Acetaminophen) Poisoning: Management
	
	Paracetamol, while widely used as an analgesic and antipyretic, can cause severe hepatotoxicity in overdose due to saturation of metabolic pathways and depletion of hepatic glutathione.
	
	#### Mechanism of Toxicity
	
	- **Overdose:** Occurs with large doses, typically **>150 mg/kg or >10g in adults**; fatality common with >250 mg/kg.
	- **Metabolic Pathway Saturation:** Paracetamol is primarily metabolised by conjugation with glucuronic acid and sulfate. In overdose, these pathways become **saturated**.
	- **Toxic Metabolite Formation:** More of the minor, highly reactive metabolite, **N-acetyl-p-benzoquinoneimine (NABQI)**, is formed.
	- **Glutathione Depletion:** NABQI is normally detoxified by conjugation with **glutathione**. When a very large dose is taken, hepatic glutathione stores are **depleted**.
	- **Cellular Damage:** Depleted glutathione leads to NABQI covalently binding to proteins in **liver cells** (and renal tubules), causing **necrosis**. This exhibits a **threshold effect** – toxicity only manifests when glutathione is depleted to a critical point.
	- **Increased Risk:**
	    - **Chronic alcoholics:** Even 5g in one day can cause hepatotoxicity due to **CYP2E1 induction**, which metabolises paracetamol to NABQI.
	    - **Premature infants (<2kg):** Not recommended due to fear of hepatotoxicity.
	
	#### Clinical Features/Manifestations
	
	- **Early (initial hours):**
	    - Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain.
	    - Liver tenderness (often without impaired consciousness).
	- **Later (after 12-18 hours):**
	    - **Centrilobular hepatic necrosis**.
	    - May be accompanied by **renal tubular necrosis** and **hypoglycaemia**.
	    - Jaundice typically starts after 2 days.
	    - Progression to coma is possible.
	    - **Fulminating hepatic failure and death** are likely if plasma levels are high.
	
	#### Treatment
	
	**I. Termination of Exposure (Decontamination)**
	
	- If patient presents early:
	    - **Induce vomiting** (e.g., syrup ipecac) or perform **gastric lavage**.
	    - Administer **activated charcoal** orally to prevent further absorption.
	
	**II. Specific Antidote: N-acetylcysteine (NAC)**
	
	- **Mechanism of Action:** Replenishes the **glutathione stores** of the liver, thereby preventing the toxic metabolite (NABQI) from binding to other cellular constituents.
	- **Administration:**
	    - **Intravenous (preferred for severe cases):**
	        - **Loading dose:** 150 mg/kg infused in 200 ml of 5% glucose solution over **15 minutes**.
	        - **Maintenance:** Followed by the same dose (150 mg/kg) given intravenously over the **next 20 hours**.
	    - **Oral (alternative, if IV not feasible or less severe):**
	        - 75 mg/kg every 4-6 hours for 2-3 days.
	- **Critical Timing:** The **ingestion-treatment interval is crucial**. NAC is **practically ineffective if started 16 hours or more** after paracetamol ingestion. **Earlier administration yields better outcomes**.
	
	**III. Supportive Measures**
	
	- **Resuscitation & Vital Functions:**
	    - Ensure **patent airway** and adequate ventilation. Provide artificial respiration/100% oxygen if needed.
	    - Maintain blood pressure and heart beat with fluids, pressor agents, etc..
	    - Prevent and manage seizures with intravenous lorazepam.
	    - Maintain body temperature.
	    - Maintain blood sugar level with dextrose infusion, especially in patients with altered sensorium.
	- **Enhanced Elimination:**
	    - **Haemodialysis** is efficacious in hastening the elimination of many drugs, including salicylates and theophylline, and can be considered in severe cases of paracetamol poisoning, especially if there's renal failure or very high plasma levels.
	
	**Clinical Relevance & Precautions:**
	
	- Given its widespread availability as an **over-the-counter (OTC)** drug, awareness of toxicity and prompt management is essential.
	- The US-FDA (2009) recommended reducing the maximum single dosage form to 650 mg and total daily dose to 2600 mg for adults to enhance safety.
	- Paracetamol is generally safer than aspirin for non-inflammatory conditions, causing less gastric irritation, ulceration, and bleeding, and not affecting platelet function or clotting. This makes it a preferred analgesic/antipyretic in many settings where aspirin is contraindicated.
