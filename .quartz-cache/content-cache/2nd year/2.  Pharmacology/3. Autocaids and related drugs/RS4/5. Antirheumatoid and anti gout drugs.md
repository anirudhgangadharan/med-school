1. A 32-year-old man presents with pain, redness and swelling of great toe. A  diagnosis of acute gout was made colchicine was prescribed for the patient.
		a. Explain the mechanism of action of colchicine's in acute gout.
		b. Mention the adverse effects of colchicine.
	
	**Colchicine in Acute Gout: Mechanism of Action & Adverse Effects**
	
	Colchicine is a specific drug used in the management of acute gout, acting distinctly from analgesics or anti-inflammatory drugs.
	
	**a. Mechanism of Action in Acute Gout:**
	
	Colchicine's action is primarily focused on **suppressing gouty inflammation**, rather than inhibiting uric acid synthesis or promoting its excretion.
	
	- **Inhibition of Granulocyte Migration:** Acute gout attacks are triggered by the precipitation of urate crystals in the synovial fluid. When these crystals are engulfed by synovial cells, they release mediators that initiate an inflammatory response. Chemotactic factors are produced, leading to the migration of **granulocytes into the joint**. Colchicine's key action is to **inhibit this granulocyte migration** into the inflamed joint.
	- **Microtubule Binding & Mitotic Inhibition:** Colchicine achieves this by **binding to tubulin**, a fibrillar protein, thereby **inhibiting mitosis** in rapidly dividing cells like granulocytes. This binding also disrupts microtubule formation, which is crucial for cell motility and various cellular functions.
	- **Suppression of Inflammatory Mediators:** It suppresses the subsequent inflammatory events by **inhibiting the release of chemotactic factors** and certain glycoproteins from granulocytes that would otherwise aggravate the inflammation. This interrupts the vicious cycle of inflammation.
	- **No Effect on Uric Acid:** It is important to note that colchicine **does not affect the synthesis or excretion of uric acid** itself. Its benefit is solely in addressing the inflammatory response to urate crystal deposition.
	
	**b. Adverse Effects of Colchicine:**
	
	Colchicine has a **high and dose-related toxicity**, which often limits its use.
	
	- **Gastrointestinal (GI) Disturbances (Most Common & Dose-Limiting):**
	    
	    - **Nausea**
	    - **Vomiting**
	    - **Watery or bloody diarrhoea**
	    - **Abdominal cramps**
	    - These symptoms are attributed to the drug's accumulation in the intestine and its inhibition of mitosis in the rapidly proliferating mucosal cells of the gut.
	- **Severe Toxicity/Overdose:**
	    
	    - **Kidney damage**
	    - **CNS depression**
	    - **Intestinal bleeding**
	    - **Muscular paralysis**
	    - **Respiratory failure (leading to death)**
	
	**Clinical Relevance:**
	
	- Colchicine is the **fastest-acting drug** to control an acute attack of gout, with control usually achieved within 6-12 hours.
	- Due to its higher toxicity, it is generally considered a **second-line option** after NSAIDs for acute gout.
	- The dramatic response to colchicine can sometimes be considered **diagnostic** for acute gout.
2. Rationale use of methotrexate in the rheumatoid arthritis
	**Rationale Use of Methotrexate (Mtx) in Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA)**
	
	Methotrexate (Mtx) is a cornerstone in the management of Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA), classified as a **Disease-Modifying Antirheumatic Drug (DMARD)**. It is chosen for its ability to suppress the disease process itself, rather than just offering symptomatic relief, thereby bringing about remission and retarding disease progression.
	
	**I. Rationale for Use & Mechanism of Action:**
	
	- **Primary Mechanism:** Mtx is a **dihydrofolate reductase inhibitor**. This action is linked to its prominent **immunosuppressant and anti-inflammatory properties** in RA.
	- **Cellular Effects:** Its beneficial effects in RA are believed to be related to:
	    - **Inhibition of cytokine production**.
	    - **Suppression of chemotaxis**.
	    - **Reduction of cell-mediated immune reactions**.
	    - **Inhibition of the proliferation of immune-inflammatory cells**.
	
	**II. Clinical Advantages & Efficacy:**
	
	- **First-Choice DMARD:** Mtx is currently the **DMARD of first choice and the standard treatment for most RA patients**, including cases of juvenile RA.
	- **Rapid Onset of Relief:** Symptom relief typically begins **relatively rapidly, within 3-6 weeks**. This quicker response makes it preferred for initial treatment compared to other non-biological DMARDs.
	- **Predictable and Sustained Response:** Mtx provides a **more predictable and sustained response over the long-term**.
	- **Combination Therapy:** It is frequently included in **combination regimens of 2 or 3 DMARDs** for enhanced efficacy. This approach aims to ameliorate pain, swelling, and joint stiffness, prevent articular cartilage damage and bony erosions, and preserve joint function.
	
	**III. Dosing and Administration:**
	
	- **Low-Dose Weekly Regimen:** The use of a **low-dose (7.5-15 mg) weekly Mtx regimen** has significantly improved the drug's acceptability in RA.
	
	**IV. Pharmacokinetics & Interactions:**
	
	- **Absorption & Excretion:** Oral bioavailability of Mtx can be variable and influenced by food. It is primarily excreted by the kidneys.
	- **Drug Interactions:**
	    - **Probenecid and aspirin** can **increase Mtx levels and toxicity**.
	    - **Trimethoprim** may add to the inhibition of dihydrofolate reductase, potentially depressing bone marrow.
	
	**V. Side Effects and Contraindications:**
	
	- **Common Side Effects (low dose):**
	    - **Oral ulceration**.
	    - **G.I. upset**.
	- **Serious Side Effects (prolonged therapy):**
	    - **Dose-dependent progressive liver damage, potentially leading to cirrhosis**.
	    - Increased incidence of chest infections.
	    - Mtx toxicity can also manifest as megaloblastic anaemia and other toxic symptoms.
	- **Contraindications:** Mtx is **contraindicated in pregnancy, breast-feeding, liver disease, active infection, leucopenia, and peptic ulcer**.
	- **Renal Impairment:** It is **not recommended for patients with renal disease** as its excretion is hindered, leading to potential accumulation and toxicity.
	
	**Clinical Relevance:**
	
	- Despite potential adverse effects, the substantial benefits of Mtx in controlling RA make it a **critical component of early and ongoing therapy**.
	- For Mtx toxicity, **folinic acid (leucovorin)** is used as an antidote because it bypasses the enzyme (DHFRase) inhibited by Mtx, unlike folic acid which is ineffective in this context.
3. Rational use of colchicines in acute gout
	### Colchicine in Acute Gout: Rational Use
	
	**Colchicine** is a specific pharmacological agent used in the management of gout, a crystal-induced arthropathy characterized by acute, severe inflammatory attacks.
	
	#### 1. Mechanism of Action (MOA) in Gout
	
	- **Not a typical Analgesic/Anti-inflammatory:** Colchicine is **neither an analgesic nor a conventional anti-inflammatory drug**. It does not directly reduce pain or generalized inflammation like NSAIDs.
	- **Specific Anti-Gouty Action:** It specifically **suppresses gouty inflammation**.
	- **No Uric Acid Effect:** It **does not inhibit the synthesis or promote the excretion of uric acid**.
	- **Tubulin Binding & Granulocyte Inhibition:** Its primary mechanism involves binding to the **fibrillar protein tubulin**, thereby **inhibiting granulocyte migration** (chemotaxis) into the inflamed joint.
	    - This action interrupts the vicious cycle initiated by urate crystal phagocytosis by synovial cells, which normally leads to the release of chemotactic factors and subsequent inflammatory responses.
	    - It also inhibits the release of a glycoprotein from phagocytosed urate crystals that aggravates inflammation.
	- **Other Actions:** Has antimitotic properties (metaphase arrest) and increases gut motility.
	
	#### 2. Clinical Use & Efficacy in Acute Gout
	
	- **Fastest Acting:** Colchicine is considered the **fastest acting drug** to control an acute attack of gout.
	- **Diagnostic Value:** Its **dramatic response** in an acute attack is often considered diagnostic for gout.
	- **Dosage for Acute Attack:**
	    - Typically 0.5 mg, 1-3 hourly, up to 4 doses in a day.
	    - **Maximum total dose:** 6.0 mg spread over 3-4 days.
	    - Control usually achieved in 6-12 hours; complete resolution in 3-5 days.
	    - A second course should not be started before 3-7 days.
	- **Abortive Use:** Small doses (0.5-1.5 mg) taken at the first symptom can **abort an acute attack**.
	- **Prophylaxis:** Can be used in **maintenance doses** (0.5-1 mg/day) for 4-8 weeks during the initiation of urate-lowering therapy to prevent acute attacks.
	
	#### 3. Adverse Effects & Toxicity
	
	- **High Toxicity:** Colchicine has a **high and dose-related toxicity**.
	- **Gastrointestinal (GI) Effects (Dose-limiting):**
	    - **Nausea, vomiting, watery or bloody diarrhoea, and abdominal cramps** are the most common dose-limiting adverse effects.
	    - These are due to accumulation of the drug in the intestine and inhibition of mitosis in its rapid-turnover mucosa.
	- **Overdose:** Can lead to kidney damage, CNS depression, intestinal bleeding, and potentially death from muscular paralysis and respiratory failure.
	- **Drug Interactions:** CYP3A4 inhibitors can retard colchicine metabolism and **enhance its toxicity**.
	
	#### 4. Place in Therapy (Rational Use)
	
	- **Second-Line to NSAIDs:** Due to its significant GI toxicity, colchicine is generally a **second-line option to NSAIDs** for acute gout.
	    - NSAIDs (e.g., naproxen, piroxicam, diclofenac, indomethacin, etoricoxib) are typically preferred as first-line due to better tolerability, though their onset of action may be slightly slower than colchicine.
	- **When Colchicine is Preferred/Considered:**
	    - When **NSAIDs are contraindicated** (e.g., history of peptic ulcer bleed, renal failure).
	    - In patients who **do not tolerate NSAIDs** or fail to respond adequately to them.
	    - As an **abortive measure** when symptoms first appear.
	    - For **prophylaxis** of acute attacks when initiating urate-lowering therapy.
	- **Comparison with Corticosteroids:** Systemic corticosteroids are also highly effective and produce a rapid response, but are typically reserved for patients with renal failure or a history of peptic ulcer bleed where NSAIDs are contraindicated, or when other options are ineffective.
	- **Monitoring:** Given its narrow therapeutic index and dose-related toxicity, careful **monitoring for GI side effects** is essential.
	
	**Clinical Relevance:** Despite its side effect profile, colchicine remains a valuable tool in specific clinical scenarios for acute gout due to its rapid and unique anti-inflammatory mechanism. Its role in aborting attacks and preventing flares during the initiation of chronic urate-lowering therapy is particularly important.
