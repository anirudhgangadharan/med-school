1. Rationale for using ethyl alcohol in methyl alcohol poisioning.
	**Rationale for Using Ethyl Alcohol (Ethanol) in Methyl Alcohol (Methanol) Poisoning**
	
	- **Problem:** Methyl alcohol is toxic largely because it is metabolized to **formaldehyde** and **formic acid**. Formic acid is responsible for the specific toxicity, including retinal damage, acidosis, and potentially death from respiratory failure.
	- **Mechanism of Toxicity:** Methanol is metabolized by the enzyme **alcohol dehydrogenase** (ADH).
	- **Ethanol's Role:** Ethanol is also metabolized by alcohol dehydrogenase.
	- **Competitive Inhibition:** Ethanol is **preferentially metabolized** by alcohol dehydrogenase over methanol.
	- **Enzyme Saturation:** By maintaining a sufficiently high blood concentration (e.g., 100 mg/dl), ethanol effectively **saturates alcohol dehydrogenase**.
	- **Outcome:** This saturation significantly **retards methanol metabolism**.
	- **Goal:** By slowing methanol metabolism, ethanol **reduces the rate of generation of the toxic metabolites** (formaldehyde and formic acid). This buys time for the body to excrete the unmetabolized methanol or for other interventions (like dialysis).
	- **Clinical Use:** Ethanol (typically 10% in water) can be administered, often via nasogastric tube, following a loading dose and subsequent hourly infusion.
	- **Challenges/Disadvantages of Ethanol Use:**
	    - Difficult to maintain a uniform blood alcohol concentration due to changing pharmacokinetics and lack of an intravenous formulation for regulated administration (in the context described for India).
	    - Requires repeated measurement of alcohol blood levels.
	    - The necessary concentration (100 mg/dl) causes **intoxication**.
	    - Can cause **hypoglycaemia**.
	    - Use is described as "tricky".
	    - Treatment needs to continue for several days due to the long sojourn of methanol in the body.
	- **Preferred Alternative:** Fomepizole, a specific inhibitor of alcohol dehydrogenase, is noted as the drug of choice, offering advantages like a longer half-life, lack of inebriating action, and an intravenous dosage form.
2. A 40-year-old man consults his family with complaints of difficulty to fall asleep since a month. He was diagnosed to be suffering from chronic insomnia. He was prescribed a benzodiazepine.

	b. Enumerate the advantage of benzodiazepines over barbiturates as sedative hypnotics.

	**Benzodiazepines (BZDs)**
	
	- **Mechanism of Action:**
	    
	    - BZDs are **selective CNS depressants**.
	    - They act by **enhancing presynaptic/post-synaptic inhibition**.
	    - They bind to a **specific BZD receptor**.
	    - This receptor is an **integral part of the GABA-A receptor-Cl⁻ channel complex**.
	    - Binding of BZDs facilitates the action of the primary neurotransmitter, **GABA**.
	    - Specifically, BZDs **increase the frequency of Cl⁻ channel opening** induced by GABA.
	    - This leads to increased influx of chloride ions, causing **neuronal hyperpolarization and inhibition** [Source does not explicitly state hyperpolarization, but it's the physiological consequence of increased Cl- influx via GABA-A receptors. Sticking strictly to sources, they say "potentiate GABAergic inhibition by increasing the lifetime of cl- channel opening induced by GABA"]. _Correction: Source states BZDs increase the frequency of Cl- channel opening, not lifetime. Barbiturates increase lifetime._ Let's refine: By increasing the **frequency of Cl⁻ channel opening** induced by GABA.
	    - This action occurs at a site on the GABA-A complex distinct from the GABA binding site, possibly on the interface of α and γ subunits.
	    - BZDs do **not** directly open the Cl⁻ channel (unlike high concentrations of barbiturates).
	- **Advantages over Barbiturates as Sedative-Hypnotics:**
	    
	    - Produce a **lower ceiling CNS depression**. Ingestion of even 20 hypnotic doses usually does not endanger life, and respiration is mostly not significantly depressed.
	    - Have a **high therapeutic index**.
	    - Hypnotic doses do **not markedly depress respiration, cardiac contractility, or BP**. Higher doses cause only mild depression.
	    - Cause **less distortion of sleep architecture**; rebound phenomena on discontinuation are less marked.
	    - **Do not alter the disposition of other drugs** by microsomal enzyme induction (unlike barbiturates).
	    - Have **lower abuse liability**; tolerance, psychological and physical dependence, and withdrawal syndrome are less marked.
	    - A **specific antagonist (Flumazenil) is available** to rapidly reverse their anaesthetic or overdose effects.
	    - **Do not provoke postoperative nausea or vomiting**.
	    - **Do not stimulate involuntary movements**.
	
	**Non-Benzodiazepine Hypnotics**
	
	- **Two Examples:**
	    
	    - **Zolpidem**
	    - **Eszopiclone** (Zopiclone is also mentioned, and Eszopiclone is its active enantiomer)
	- **Two Advantages (relative to some BZDs):**
	    
	    - Act selectively on the **α₁ subunit containing BZD receptors**, resulting predominantly in hypnotic-amnesic action with weaker antianxiety, muscle relaxant, and anticonvulsant effects. This selectivity can mean **fewer non-hypnotic side effects** at hypnotic doses.
	    - Generally have a **shorter duration of action** and/or minimal active metabolites, leading to **minimal residual day time sedation** or impairment. They are preferred for short-term use in sleep onset insomnia.
3. Mechanism of action and adverse effects, therapeutic uses and contraindications of Diazepam
	**Diazepam: Mechanism, Adverse Effects, Uses & Contraindications**
	
	**a. Mechanism of Action**
	
	- Diazepam is a **selective CNS depressant**.
	- It acts primarily by **enhancing inhibitory GABAergic transmission**.
	- It binds to a **specific Benzodiazepine (BZD) receptor**, which is an integral part of the **GABA-A receptor-Cl⁻ channel complex**.
	- Binding of Diazepam **facilitates the action of GABA**.
	- BZDs like Diazepam **increase the frequency of Cl⁻ channel opening** induced by GABA.
	    - _Clinical Note:_ This differs from barbiturates, which increase the _lifetime_ of channel opening.
	- This binding occurs at an **allosteric site** on the GABA-A complex, distinct from the GABA site.
	- BZDs **do not directly open the Cl⁻ channel** themselves.
	- Enhancing GABA-induced Cl⁻ influx leads to **neuronal hyperpolarization** and inhibition.
	- Diazepam acts preferentially on the **midbrain ascending reticular formation** (maintains wakefulness) and the **limbic system** (thought/mental functions).
	- Muscle relaxant effects involve a **medullary site of action**. Ataxia is due to action on the cerebellum.
	- Diazepam has **similar affinity for BZD receptor isoforms containing different α subunits (α₁, α₂, α₃, α₅)**, giving it a **broad spectrum activity** (anxiolytic, hypnotic, muscle relaxant, anticonvulsant).
	- Given intravenously, Diazepam (but not other BZDs) causes **analgesia**.
	
	**b. Adverse Effects**
	
	- **CNS Effects:**
	    - Sedation, drowsiness, light-headedness.
	    - Dizziness, vertigo, ataxia.
	    - Disorientation, mental confusion (especially in the elderly).
	    - Amnesia (anterograde amnesia is prominent, especially perioperatively).
	    - **Prolongation of reaction time** and impairment of psychomotor skills (patients should not drive).
	    - Sleep walking, sleep driving, and other abnormal sleep behaviours with no memory.
	    - Hangover (less common, more likely with larger doses or longer-acting drugs).
	    - Weakness.
	    - Psychotic symptoms possible with concurrent sodium valproate.
	    - Can aggravate seizures in epileptics.
	- **Respiratory Effects:**
	    - By itself, **does not markedly depress respiration** at hypnotic doses.
	    - **Respiratory depression can be marked when given with opioids**.
	    - Dangerous in patients with **respiratory insufficiency** (emphysema, COPD).
	    - Can **aggravate sleep apnoea**.
	    - Use during labour may cause **respiratory depression in the neonate**.
	    - IV injection for emergency seizure control can cause marked respiratory depression.
	- **Cardiovascular Effects:**
	    - By itself, **does not markedly depress cardiac contractility or BP** at hypnotic doses.
	    - IV injection can cause a **fall in BP** (may be marked occasionally) and decreased cardiac contractility.
	    - Fall in BP with Diazepam is due to **reduction in cardiac output**.
	    - Coronary arteries dilate on IV injection.
	    - When used with opioids, cardiac function can be considerably compromised.
	    - IV injection for emergency seizure control can cause marked fall in BP.
	- **Other Effects:**
	    - Dry mouth.
	    - Blurring of vision.
	    - Urinary incontinence, urinary hesitation (especially in elderly males).
	    - Constipation.
	    - Increased appetite and weight gain.
	    - Alterations in sexual function (some women may fail to ovulate).
	    - Rashes are uncommon.
	- **Administration Site Issues (IV):**
	    - Burning sensation in the vein.
	    - Incidence of **thrombophlebitis**. (Less common with Lorazepam).
	- **Dependence & Withdrawal:**
	    - Lower abuse liability than barbiturates.
	    - Tolerance, psychological and physical dependence, and withdrawal syndrome are less marked than with barbiturates.
	    - Withdrawal phenomena are mild.
	    - Long-term use for anxiety has potential to impair mental functions and produce dependence.
	    - Risk of tolerance and abuse is maximum among chronic insomniacs.
	
	**c. Therapeutic Uses**
	
	- **Hypnotic:** Can be used for **sleep onset difficulty** and **sleep maintenance** due to rapid oral absorption. Suitable for patients with frequent nocturnal awakenings. Less likely to cause rebound insomnia on discontinuation of chronic use.
	- **Anxiolytic:** Used as an **anxiolytic**. Effective in **acute panic states** and **anxiety associated with organic disease**.
	- **Muscle Relaxant:** Used as a **muscle relaxant**. Produces **centrally mediated skeletal muscle relaxation**. Effective for **muscle spasms and spasticity**.
	- **Anticonvulsant:** Has **anticonvulsant activity**. It is a **first line drug for emergency control of convulsions**, including status epilepticus, tetanus, eclampsia, convulsant drug poisoning. Also used for febrile convulsions in children (rectal instillation preferred). Its utility in long-term treatment of epilepsy is limited by tolerance.
	- **Premedicant/Anaesthetic:** Used for **preanaesthetic medication, i.v. anaesthesia and conscious sedation**. Relatively large IV doses produce sedation, amnesia, and then unconsciousness. Used for endoscopies, cardiac catheterization, angiographics, conscious sedation during local/regional anaesthesia, fracture setting, ECT, and minor procedures due to calming-amnesic-analgesic and muscle relaxant properties and relative safety.
	- **Other:** Used before ECT and electrical cardioversion of arrhythmias. Decreases nocturnal gastric secretion and prevents stress ulcers.
	
	**d. Contraindications (Specific mentions or strong cautions)**
	
	- Patients with **respiratory insufficiency** (emphysema, COPD).
	- Patients with **sleep apnoea**. All hypnotics aggravate sleep apnoea.
	- Use during **labour** (risk of neonatal respiratory depression).
	- Patients who need to **drive or operate machinery** due to impairment of psychomotor skills.
	- Concomitant use with **alcohol or other CNS depressants** due to synergy and excessive impairment.
	- Patients with **compromised cardiovascular status** should be used with caution, especially via IV injection which can cause significant falls in BP and decreased cardiac contractility.
	- Long-term daily use may be problematic in chronic insomnia due to risk of tolerance, abuse, and potential for cognitive impairment.
	- Should be used with caution in the **elderly**, who are more susceptible to psychomotor side effects, mental confusion, ataxia, urinary hesitation/incontinence, and next day sedation.
	- In **epileptics**, while anticonvulsant, there's a possibility of aggravating seizures. (Though primarily used for emergency control).
	- Use in patients with **pre-existing psychotic illness** is not mentioned as a contraindication in the BZD section, but antagonism of excessive DA action in the limbic system is noted as a reason why antidopaminergic drugs are antipsychotic, suggesting potential issues with BZDs in this context, although not explicitly stated for Diazepam. (Source discusses Levodopa contraindication in psychotic illness due to excessive DA action).
