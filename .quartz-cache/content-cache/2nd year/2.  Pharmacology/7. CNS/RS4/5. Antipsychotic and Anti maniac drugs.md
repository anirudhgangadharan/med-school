1. Atypical Antipsychotics, name them and also mention 4 advantages over conventional antipsychotics)
	**Atypical Antipsychotics (Second Generation)**
	
	- A group of **newer antipsychotics** (second generation).
	- Used primarily in the treatment of **psychoses**, especially **schizophrenia**.
	
	**Mechanism of Action**
	
	- Pharmacologically **distinct** from conventional antipsychotics.
	- Characterised by **weak Dopamine D2 receptor blocking action**.
	- Possess **potent 5-HT2 (Serotonin 2A) antagonistic activity**.
	- Also block other receptors (D1, D3, D4, 5-HT1A, α1, H1, muscarinic, etc.) to varying degrees. Relative selectivity for **D4 receptors** (sparse in basal ganglia) may contribute to differing profile.
	- Antipsychotic property likely depends on a **specific profile of action on several neurotransmitter receptors**.
	
	**Examples of Atypical Agents** (as listed in sources)
	
	- Clozapine
	- Risperidone
	- Olanzapine
	- Quetiapine
	- Aripiprazole
	- Ziprasidone
	- Amisulpiride
	- Zotepine
	- (Other examples mentioned: Levosulpiride, Asenapine, Iloperidone)
	
	**Advantages Over Conventional (Typical) Antipsychotics** (Key benefits for patients and clinical practice)
	
	1. **Minimal Extrapyramidal Side Effects (EPS)**: This is a defining feature and a **major advantage**. Atypical antipsychotics produce **few or no extrapyramidal symptoms**. EPS (like parkinsonism, dystonias, akathisia) are major dose-limiting side effects with typical neuroleptics. EPS are least with clozapine and _all other atypical antipsychotics_, except at higher doses of risperidone.
	2. **Lower Risk of Tardive Dyskinesia (TD)**: Tardive dyskinesia is a serious, potentially irreversible long-term EPS. TD is **rare with clozapine** and the class generally carries a **low risk** compared to typical neuroleptics. This makes them **preferable for long-term use** in chronic schizophrenia.
	3. **Greater Efficacy Against Negative Symptoms**: While efficacy against positive symptoms (e.g., hallucinations, delusions) is rated as similar to typical antipsychotics, atypical agents are considered **more effective in relieving negative symptoms** of schizophrenia (e.g., apathy, social withdrawal, emotional blunting). They also tend to **improve impaired cognitive function** in psychotics.
	4. **Utility in Mood Disorders (Mania & Bipolar)**: Several atypical antipsychotics (e.g., olanzapine, risperidone, aripiprazole, quetiapine, ziprasidone) are effective in treating **acute mania** and are used in the **maintenance therapy of bipolar disorder**. They are often the preferred choice for oral treatment of mania to **avoid extrapyramidal side effects**.
	5. **Better Tolerability Profile (Overall)**: In general, atypical antipsychotics are considered **better tolerated** and produce **fewer side effects and neurological complications** compared to older typicals. (Note: Some atypicals like olanzapine and clozapine carry a higher risk of significant metabolic side effects like weight gain, elevated blood sugar, and triglycerides).
	
	**Clinical Considerations Summary**
	
	- Often selected as **first-line drugs** for schizophrenia.
	- **Preferable for long-term use** in chronic schizophrenia due to low TD risk.
	- Choice depends on symptom profile, tolerance of specific side effects, and co-morbidities.
2. Lithium-mechanim of action and list adverse effects and indications
	**Lithium Carbonate**
	
	- A **small monovalent cation**.
	- Discovered to be effective for **excitement and mania** in 1949, with clinical use starting in the 1960s.
	- Has a **unique place in psychiatry**.
	- Established as the **standard antimanic and mood stabilising drug**.
	- Requires monitoring of **narrow range of serum concentrations**.
	
	**Mechanism of Action (Not fully known, several hypotheses proposed)**
	
	- Practically **no acute effects** in normal individuals or bipolar patients.
	- On prolonged administration, acts as a **mood stabiliser** in bipolar disorder.
	- Gradually **suppresses acute mania** over 1-2 weeks.
	- Prevents **cyclic mood changes** with continued treatment.
	- May affect **ionic fluxes** across brain cells or modify cellular membrane properties by partly replacing body Na+. However, therapeutic Li+ concentration is low relative to Na+ and K+.
	- Decreases **presynaptic release of NA and DA** in the brain (in treated animals) without affecting 5-HT release. This _may_ correct monoamine imbalance. (Doesn't explain lack of effect in non-manic individuals).
	- **Inositol Depletion Hypothesis**: Lithium inhibits hydrolysis of inositol-1-phosphate by inositol monophosphatase, reducing free inositol supply for phosphatidyl-inositide regeneration. This system generates IP3 and DAG. This may **selectively dampen signal transduction** in overactive receptors functioning through phosphatidyl inositol hydrolysis, preferentially affecting **hyperactive neurones** involved in the manic state. Valproate, with similar effects in mania/bipolar disorder, also reduces intraneuronal inositol concentration by inhibiting de novo synthesis.
	- May involve other neuronal signalling elements like PKc, glutamate, arachidonate.
	- Inhibits **ADH action** on renal tubules, causing a diabetes insipidus-like state.
	- Exerts an **insulin-like action** on glucose metabolism.
	- Inhibits **release of thyroid hormones**, stimulating thyroid through pituitary feedback.
	
	**Adverse Effects**
	
	- Side effects are **common**, but mostly tolerable.
	- **Toxicity occurs at levels marginally higher** than therapeutic.
	- **Initial/Common:**
	    - Nausea, vomiting, mild diarrhoea (initially, dose-dependent, minimised by low starting dose).
	    - Thirst and polyuria (experienced by most).
	    - Fine tremors (may occur even at therapeutic concentrations).
	- **CS Toxicity (Plasma concentration > 1.5 mEq/L)**:
	    - **Coarse tremors**, giddiness, **ataxia**, motor incoordination, nystagmus.
	    - Mental confusion, slurred speech, hyperreflexia.
	    - Overdose (> 2 mEq/L) causes **muscle twitchings, drowsiness, delirium, coma, convulsions**.
	- **Other Toxicity Features (> 2 mEq/L)**:
	    - Vomiting, severe diarrhoea, albuminuria.
	    - **Hypotension and cardiac arrhythmias**.
	- **Treatment of Toxicity**: Symptomatic. No specific antidote. Osmotic diuretics and sodium bicarbonate promote excretion. **Haemodialysis** indicated if serum levels are > 4 mEq/L.
	- **Long-term Use Effects**:
	    - **Renal diabetes insipidus** (in some patients).
	    - Weight gain.
	    - **Goitre** (in ~4%), due to interference with thyroid hormone release; may lead to hypothyroidism. Manageable with thyroid supplementation, does not warrant discontinuation.
	    - Dermatitis, worsening of acne.
	- **Cardiac Effects**:
	    - Reduction of T-wave amplitude at therapeutic levels.
	    - SA node and A-V conduction depression at higher levels.
	- **Contraindications**:
	    - **Pregnancy**: risk of fetal goitre and congenital abnormalities (especially cardiac). Newborn often hypotonic.
	    - **Sick sinus syndrome**.
	
	**Indications**
	
	- Treatment of **Mania and Bipolar Disorder**.
	- **Acute Mania**: Effective, but response is slow (1-2 weeks). Control of plasma levels is difficult acutely. Many psychiatrists prefer rapid-acting atypical antipsychotics (with or without BZD) and start Lithium after acute phase control.
	- **Bipolar Disorder (Maintenance Therapy)**: Proven efficacy. Gradually introduced and maintained at plasma concentration 0.5-0.8 mEq/L. Lengthens intervals between mood swings (mania and depression attenuated) in up to 70% patients.
	- Due to side effects and difficulty in maintaining narrow serum levels, **valproate and other alternatives are more frequently used now**.
	- Can be combined with **valproate or lamotrigine** in resistant cases or to improve efficacy.
	
	**Clinical Use Points**
	
	- Used as Carbonate salt (tablets, SR tablets) or Citrate (syrup).
	- Starting dose typically 300-600 mg/day, gradually increased to therapeutic levels.
	- Requires monitoring of serum levels.
	- Maintenance therapy often for 6-12 months to prevent recurrences.
	- Elimination is primarily renal (80% reabsorbed in proximal tubule, handled like Na+).
	- Risk of increased serum Li+ with Na+ restriction or use of **thiazide/furosemide diuretics, NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors**.
	- Excreted in breast milk, mothers on Lithium should not breastfeed.
