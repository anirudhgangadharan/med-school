1. A 20-year-old male presents to hospital with history of three episodes of seizures in the last two weeks. A diagnosis of generalized tonic clonic seizure was made 
	 a. Mention four groups of drugs that can be used in this patient giving one example for each group. 
	 b. Describe the mechanism of action, adverse effects and uses of any one drug.
	**Patient Presentation:** 20-year-old male with recent onset Generalized Tonic-Clonic Seizures (GTCS).
	
	**a. Four Drug Groups/Examples for GTCS**
	
	For Generalized Tonic-Clonic Seizures (GTCS), the first-line drugs include:
	
	1. **Carbamazepine**
	2. **Lamotrigine**
	3. **Valproate**
	4. **Diazepam** (primarily for emergency control of convulsions/status epilepticus, which can arise from GTCS)
	
	**b. Mechanism of Action, Adverse Effects, and Uses of Diazepam**
	
	**Diazepam**
	
	- **Mechanism of Action**
	    
	    - Selective **CNS depressant** [Mechanism of Action section from previous turn].
	    - Primary action: Enhances **inhibitory GABAergic transmission** [Mechanism of Action section from previous turn, 108].
	    - Binds to a specific **Benzodiazepine (BZD) receptor** site on the **GABA-A receptor-Cl⁻ channel complex** [Mechanism of Action section from previous turn]. This is an allosteric site, separate from the GABA binding site [Mechanism of Action section from previous turn].
	    - Binding **facilitates GABA action** [Mechanism of Action section from previous turn, 59, 46].
	    - Increases the **frequency of Cl⁻ channel opening** induced by GABA [Mechanism of Action section from previous turn].
	    - Does **not** directly open the Cl⁻ channel [Mechanism of Action section from previous turn].
	    - Increased Cl⁻ influx leads to **neuronal hyperpolarization** and inhibition [Mechanism of Action section from previous turn].
	    - Preferential action on **midbrain ascending reticular formation** and **limbic system** [Mechanism of Action section from previous turn].
	    - Muscle relaxant effects involve a **medullary site** [Mechanism of Action section from previous turn]. Ataxia linked to cerebellum [Mechanism of Action section from previous turn].
	    - Has **broad spectrum activity** (anxiolytic, hypnotic, muscle relaxant, anticonvulsant) due to similar affinity for various BZD receptor isoforms (α₁, α₂, α₃, α₅) [Mechanism of Action section from previous turn].
	    - Unlike Ketamine, it does **not** inhibit NMDA receptors.
	- **Adverse Effects**
	    
	    - **CNS:** Sedation, drowsiness, light-headedness. Dizziness, vertigo, **ataxia**. Disorientation, **mental confusion** (esp. elderly). **Amnesia** (anterograde prominent). **Impairment of psychomotor skills** (Prolongs reaction time; **patients should not drive**). Sleep walking/driving, abnormal sleep behaviours without memory. Hangover (less common). Weakness. May **aggravate seizures** in epileptics [94, Adverse Effects section from previous turn]. Psychotic symptoms possible with valproate [Adverse Effects section from previous turn].
	    - **Respiratory:** By itself, usually **does not markedly depress respiration** at hypnotic doses. Depression can be **marked when given with opioids** [10, Adverse Effects section from previous turn]. Dangerous in patients with **respiratory insufficiency** (emphysema, COPD) [Adverse Effects section from previous turn]. Can **aggravate sleep apnoea** [33, Adverse Effects section from previous turn]. Use during labour may cause **neonatal respiratory depression** [Adverse Effects section from previous turn]. **Marked respiratory depression** possible with **IV injection** for emergency seizure control [60, Adverse Effects section from previous turn].
	    - **Cardiovascular:** By itself, usually **does not markedly depress cardiac contractility or BP** at hypnotic doses. **IV injection** can cause a **fall in BP** (may be marked) and decreased cardiac contractility [10, 60, Adverse Effects section from previous turn]. Fall in BP is due to reduced cardiac output [Adverse Effects section from previous turn]. Coronary arteries dilate on IV injection [Adverse Effects section from previous turn]. Cardiac function compromised with opioids.
	    - **Other:** Dry mouth. Blurring of vision. Urinary incontinence, urinary hesitation (esp. elderly males). Constipation [Adverse Effects section from previous turn]. Increased appetite/weight gain [Adverse Effects section from previous turn]. Alterations in sexual function [Adverse Effects section from previous turn]. Rashes uncommon [Adverse Effects section from previous turn].
	    - **IV Site:** Burning sensation [Adverse Effects section from previous turn]. **Thrombophlebitis** is common [60, Adverse Effects section from previous turn]. Lorazepam causes less.
	    - **Dependence:** Lower abuse liability than barbiturates [Adverse Effects section from previous turn]. Tolerance, dependence (psychological/physical), withdrawal syndrome are less marked than with barbiturates [Adverse Effects section from previous turn]. Withdrawal phenomena are mild [32, Adverse Effects section from previous turn]. Long-term use for anxiety can impair mental function and cause dependence [Adverse Effects section from previous turn]. Risk of tolerance and abuse is maximum in chronic insomniacs [Adverse Effects section from previous turn]. Tolerance to anticonvulsant effect develops rapidly, limiting long-term use for epilepsy.
	- **Therapeutic Uses**
	    
	    - **Anticonvulsant:** **First line drug for emergency control of convulsions**, e.g., **status epilepticus**, tetanus, eclampsia, convulsant drug poisoning. Used for **febrile convulsions** in children (**rectal instillation** preferred). Limited utility in long-term epilepsy due to rapid tolerance to anticonvulsant effect.
	    - **Anxiolytic:** Used for anxiety. Effective in **acute panic states** and anxiety with organic disease.
	    - **Hypnotic:** Can be used for **sleep onset difficulty** and **sleep maintenance** due to rapid oral absorption. Useful for patients with frequent nocturnal awakenings. Less likely to cause rebound insomnia on chronic discontinuation.
	    - **Muscle Relaxant:** Produces centrally mediated skeletal muscle relaxation. Effective for **muscle spasms and spasticity**.
	    - **Premedicant/Anaesthetic:** Used for **preanaesthetic medication**, **i.v. anaesthesia**, and **conscious sedation**. IV doses produce sedation, amnesia, unconsciousness. Used for endoscopies, cardiac catheterization, angiographics, conscious sedation during local/regional anaesthesia, fracture setting, ECT, minor procedures due to calming, amnesic, analgesic, and muscle relaxant properties and relative safety.
	    - **Other:** Used before ECT and electrical cardioversion. Decreases nocturnal gastric secretion and prevents stress ulcers.
	
	**Contraindications/Cautions**
	
	- Patients with **respiratory insufficiency** (emphysema, COPD) [Adverse Effects section from previous turn, 116].
	- Patients with **sleep apnoea** [33, Adverse Effects section from previous turn].
	- Use during **labour** (risk of neonatal respiratory depression) [Adverse Effects section from previous turn].
	- Patients who need to **drive or operate machinery** due to impairment of psychomotor skills [33, Adverse Effects section from previous turn].
	- Concomitant use with **alcohol or other CNS depressants** (synergy leading to excessive impairment/depression) [Adverse Effects section from previous turn, 96].
	- Caution in patients with **compromised cardiovascular status**, especially with IV injection [10, 60, Adverse Effects section from previous turn].
	- Caution in the **elderly** (increased susceptibility to side effects: psychomotor, confusion, ataxia, urinary issues, next day sedation) [33, Adverse Effects section from previous turn].
	- Caution in **epileptics** (risk of aggravating seizures, though used for emergency control) [94, Adverse Effects section from previous turn].
	- Not for long-term daily use in chronic insomnia due to dependence risk.
	- Use in patients with **psychotic illness** is not explicitly contraindicated for BZDs in the provided sources on Diazepam, but the role of DA in the limbic system and effect of antipsychotics is discussed, suggesting potential complexities.
2. Phenytoin sodium/diphenyl hydantoin sodium-mechanism of action, adverse effects, drug interactions, therapeutic uses
	**Phenytoin sodium (Diphenylhydantoin sodium)**
		- Synthesized as a barbiturate analogue, but lacks significant sedative property.
		- First tested for anticonvulsant activity in 1938 using the electroshock seizure model.
		- Was a major antiepileptic drug until recently.
	
	**Mechanism of Action**
	
	- Primary action: Prevents repetitive detonation of normal brain cells.
	- Mechanism: **Prolongs the inactivated state of voltage-sensitive neuronal Na⁺ channels**.
	    - This action is noted at therapeutic concentrations.
	    - Governs the refractory period of the neurone.
	    - Result: **Inhibits high-frequency neuronal discharges** with little effect on normal low-frequency discharges.
	- Other proposed mechanisms (require higher concentrations, uncertain contribution):
	    - Depresses presynaptic release of **glutamate** (excitatory transmitter).
	    - Facilitates **GABA** (inhibitory transmitter) release.
	    - Reduces Ca²⁺ influx.
	    - Prevents intracellular accumulation of Na⁺ during repetitive firing.
	- Does not interfere with kindling, unlike phenobarbitone and valproate.
	- Confers efficacy in trigeminal neuralgia and cardiac arrhythmias due to selective suppression of high-frequency firing.
	- Does not raise the threshold for PTZ convulsions.
	
	**Adverse Effects**
	
	- **Dose-related Toxicity** (occurs with higher plasma concentrations, >10 µg/ml due to saturation kinetics):
	    - **Cerebellar & Vestibular:** **Ataxia**, vertigo, **diplopia**, **nystagmus**.
	    - **CNS:** Drowsiness, behavioural alterations, **mental confusion**, hallucinations, disorientation, rigidity.
	    - **Gastrointestinal:** Epigastric pain, nausea, vomiting.
	- **Non-dose-related Side Effects** (can occur at therapeutic levels):
	    - **Gingival hyperplasia**.
	    - Hirsutism.
	    - Acne.
	    - Coarsening of facial features.
	- **Long-term Use:**
	    - **Megaloblastic anaemia:** Due to interference with folate absorption.
	    - **Osteomalacia:** Due to interference with Vit. D activation.
	    - **Peripheral neuropathy**.
	    - Encephalopathy, cognitive impairment (rare).
	    - Cerebellar degeneration (rare).
	- **Intravenous (IV) Injection:**
	    - Local vascular injury, intimal damage, thrombosis, **thrombophlebitis**.
	    - Tissue necrosis if extravasates.
	    - **Fall in BP and cardiac arrhythmias** can occur; requires **continuous ECG monitoring**.
	- **Hypersensitivity Reactions:**
	    - Skin rashes (e.g., morbilliform rash, potentially severe Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis).
	    - Drug hypersensitivity syndrome (fever, rash, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, eosinophilia, facial edema).
	- **Teratogenicity:** Can cause **'foetal hydantoin syndrome'** when administered during pregnancy (hypoplastic phalanges, cleft palate, harelip, microcephaly). Probably caused by its arene-oxide metabolite.
	- Can **exacerbate absence and myoclonic seizures**.
	
	**Drug Interactions**
	
	- **Enzyme Induction:** Potent inducer of CYP2C8/9, CYP3A4/5 isoenzymes.
	    - Increases degradation (reduces efficacy) of:
	        - **Steroids** (e.g., oral contraceptives failure).
	        - Doxycycline.
	        - Theophylline.
	        - Carbamazepine (and vice versa).
	        - TCAs (e.g., imipramine).
	        - Methadone (can cause withdrawal symptoms).
	- **Enzyme Inhibition:** Competitively inhibits CYP2C9/19.
	- **Binding in GI tract:** Sucralfate binds phenytoin and decreases its absorption.
	- **Displacement from protein binding sites:** Phenobarbitone, phenylbutazone, aspirin, and CPZ can displace TCAs from protein binding sites and cause transient overdose symptoms, but also states that Phenytoin can be displaced by these.
	- **With Fosphenytoin:** Fosphenytoin can be injected with both saline and glucose drips, unlike phenytoin sodium which precipitates in glucose solutions.
	
	**Therapeutic Uses**
	
	- Was a standard drug for **Generalized Tonic-Clonic Seizures (GTCS)** and partial seizures.
	- Now used only when better tolerated newer drugs cannot be used, due to frequent side effects and numerous interactions.
	- **Status Epilepticus:** Phenytoin sodium IV is used only when **fosphenytoin** is not available.
	    - Must be injected slowly (25-50 mg/min).
	    - Causes more marked local vascular complications than fosphenytoin.
	    - Requires ECG monitoring.
	- **Trigeminal Neuralgia:** Second choice drug after carbamazepine. Selective action on high-frequency nerve impulses helps interrupt temporal summation of afferent impulses.
	- Can be used for cardiac arrhythmias.
	
	**Key Considerations**
	
	- **Nonlinear clearance:** Small dose increases cause disproportionately high plasma concentrations and toxicity due to capacity-limited metabolism.
	- **Therapeutic Drug Monitoring:** Monitoring plasma concentration is very helpful in adjusting dosage. Therapeutic range: 10-20 µg/ml.
	- Not effective in absence and atonic seizures.
	- Can exacerbate absence and myoclonic seizures.
	- Oral absorption is slow and variable due to poor aqueous solubility. Bioavailability of different preparations may differ.
	- Fosphenytoin is a water-soluble prodrug replacing IV phenytoin for status epilepticus due to easier IV administration and fewer local complications.
	
	**(Fosphenytoin is the preferred IV agent where available for emergency situations)**.

3. Newer Anti epileptic drugs
	
	**Newer Antiepileptic Drugs**
	
	- General trend: Newer drugs are often preferred due to being **less sedating**, producing **fewer side effects**, and having **insignificant drug interactions** compared to older agents. However, they are less well-studied.
	
	**Lamotrigine**
		- A newer anticonvulsant.
		- **Mechanism of Action:**
		    - Similar to carbamazepine profile.
		    - **Prolongation of Na⁺ channel inactivation**.
		    - **Suppression of high frequency firing**.
		    - May directly block voltage sensitive Na⁺ channels, stabilizing presynaptic membrane and preventing release of excitatory neurotransmitter, mainly **glutamate and aspartate**.
		    - Does not antagonize PTZ seizures or block NMDA glutamate receptors.
		- **Adverse Effects:**
		    - Significant side effects are less frequent compared to phenytoin and carbamazepine.
		    - Tolerability profile is favourable.
		- **Drug Interactions:**
		    - Plasma t½ (~24 hr) is reduced to ~16 hours when patients are receiving **phenytoin, carbamazepine, or phenobarbitone**.
		    - **Valproate inhibits glucuronidation of lamotrigine**, doubling its blood level. Lamotrigine dose should be reduced to half in patients taking valproate.
		    - Lamotrigine lowers valproate levels.
		    - Does not alter the metabolism of other anticonvulsants or oral contraceptives.
		- **Therapeutic Uses:**
		    - Broad-spectrum antiepileptic.
		    - **First line drug** now for **Partial seizures** (SPS, CPS) and **Generalized Tonic-Clonic Seizures (GTCS)**.
		    - Effective as monotherapy or as add-on therapy in refractory cases.
		    - Absence and myoclonic or akinetic epilepsy cases have also been successfully treated.
		    - Some cases of neuralgic pain respond.
		    - Strong evidence of efficacy for prophylaxis of **depression in bipolar disorder**.
		    - **Not effective for treatment/prevention of mania**.
		    - Extensively used in maintenance therapy of **type II bipolar disorder** (major depressive episodes ± hypomania).
		    - Can be combined with lithium to improve efficacy.
		    - Alternative for trigeminal neuralgia (less efficacious than carbamazepine).
	**Gabapentin**
		- Newer congener.
		- **Mechanism of Action:**
		    - Suppresses kindled seizures.
		    - Modifies maximal electroshock and inhibits PTZ induced clonic seizures.
		    - May modulate a subset of neuronal voltage sensitive **Ca²⁺ channels containing α₂δ-1 subunits**, potentially reducing glutamate release.
		    - May have a GABA enhancing action.
		    - Uncertain which mechanism is responsible for anticonvulsant/analgesic effect.
		- **Adverse Effects:** Not detailed in the sources.
		- **Drug Interactions:** Not detailed in the sources.
		- **Therapeutic Uses:**
		    - Add-on drug for **Partial seizures** (SPS, CPS) with or without generalization.
		    - Specific analgesic effect in **neuropathic pain**.
		    - Can be tried in non-responders for trigeminal neuralgia.
	
	**Levetiracetam**
		- A unique anticonvulsant.
		- **Mechanism of Action:**
		    - Suppresses kindled seizures.
		    - Ineffective against maximal electroshock or PTZ.
		    - Mechanism not fully known.
		    - Binds selectively to a **synaptic vesicular protein 'SV2A'**, possibly altering glutamate and/or GABA release.
		    - Inhibits N-type calcium channels.
		- **Adverse Effects:**
		    - Few side effects: sleepiness, dizziness, weakness.
		    - Rarely behavioural changes.
		    - Driving may be impaired.
		    - Good tolerability.
		    - Not approved for use in children below 4 years.
		- **Drug Interactions:**
		    - **Free of drug interactions**.
		    - Neither oxidized by CYP enzymes nor induces or inhibits them.
		- **Therapeutic Uses:**
		    - Demonstrated clinical efficacy as adjunctive medication and monotherapy in **refractory partial seizures** (with or without generalization).
		    - Increasingly used in partial seizures and myoclonic epilepsy (mainly add-on) due to good tolerability.
		    - Second-line adjunctive drug for **GTCS** and **absence seizures**.
	**Topiramate**
		- Weak carbonic anhydrase inhibitor.
		- Broad spectrum anticonvulsant activity.
		- **Mechanism of Action:** Multiple mechanisms.
		    - Phenytoin-like **prolongation of Na⁺ channel inactivation**.
		    - Suppression of repetitive neuronal firing.
		    - GABA potentiation by a postsynaptic effect.
		    - Antagonism of certain glutamate receptors.
		    - Neuronal hyperpolarization through K⁺ channels.
		- **Adverse Effects:** Not detailed in the sources.
		- **Drug Interactions:** Carbamazepine can reduce efficacy.
		- **Therapeutic Uses:**
		    - Indicated as **monotherapy** and for supplementing primary antiepileptic drugs in **refractory Partial seizures** (SPS, CPS) and **GTCS**.
		    - Promising results in myoclonic seizures.
	
	**Zonisamide**
		- Newer anticonvulsant with weak carbonic anhydrase inhibitory action.
		- **Mechanism of Action:**
		    - Modifies maximal electroshock seizures and inhibits kindled seizures.
		    - Does not antagonize PTZ.
		    - **Prolongation of Na⁺ channel inactivation**, suppressing repetitive neuronal firing.
		    - Suppresses **T-type Ca²⁺ currents** in certain neurones.
		- **Adverse Effects:**
		    - Somnolence, dizziness, headache, irritability, anorexia.
		    - Metabolic acidosis and renal stones.
		    - Avoided in patients sensitive to sulfonamides.
		- **Drug Interactions:** **No drug interactions** have been observed.
		- **Therapeutic Uses:**
		    - Indicated as both **monotherapy** and **add-on drug** in refractory **Partial seizures** (with or without generalization).
		    - Tried in absence seizures.
		    - Not to be given to children.
	
	**Lacosamide**
		- Recently introduced antiseizure drug.
		- **Mechanism of Action:**
		    - Enhancing **Na⁺-channel inactivation**.
		    - Suppressing repetitive firing of neurones.
		- **Adverse Effects:**
		    - **Ataxia, vertigo, diplopia, tremor**.
		    - Depression.
		    - Cardiac arrhythmia.
		- **Drug Interactions:**
		    - Metabolized by CYP2C19.
		    - **No alteration in dose of companion antiepileptic drug is needed**.
		    - Neither induces nor inhibits drug metabolizing enzymes.
		- **Therapeutic Uses:**
		    - Indicated in **adults only** for **add-on therapy of partial seizures** (with or without generalization).
	
	**Oxcarbazepine**
		- Newer congener of carbamazepine.
		- **Mechanism of Action:** Rapidly converted to an active metabolite (glucuronide conjugated, not oxidized). Action likely similar to carbamazepine (Na+ channel inactivation).
		- **Adverse Effects:**
		    - Toxic effects due to epoxide metabolite are avoided.
		    - Risk of hepatotoxicity is estimated to be lower than with carbamazepine.
		    - Risk of **hyponatremia is more**.
		    - Better tolerated than carbamazepine.
		- **Drug Interactions:**
		    - Drug interactions and autoinduction of own metabolism are less marked.
		    - Weak enzyme inducer.
		- **Therapeutic Uses:**
		    - Indications are the same as for carbamazepine.
		    - Alternative/Add-on drug for Partial seizures (SPS, CPS) with or without generalization.
		    - Alternative/Add-on drug for GTCS.
		    - Dose-to-dose is 1½ times less potent than carbamazepine.
	
	**Eslicarbazepine**
		- (S)+ enantiomer prodrug.
		- Very rapidly converted in liver to the same active metabolite as oxcarbazepine.
		- **Mechanism of Action:** Same range as oxcarbazepine (likely Na+ channel inactivation via metabolite).
		- **Adverse Effects:** Same range as oxcarbazepine.
		- **Drug Interactions:** Not detailed but implied to be similar to oxcarbazepine due to common metabolite.
		- **Therapeutic Uses:**
		    - Approved as **add-on drug for partial seizure** with or without generalization **only in adults**.
		    - Suitable for **once daily dosing**.
	
	**Other Newer/Add-on Drugs Mentioned (Minimal Detail):**
		- Perampanel, Retigabine, Stiripentol, Rufinamide: Introduced as second line/add-on drugs for refractory partial seizures. No further details on mechanism, effects, or interactions provided in the sources.
4. Sodium valproate-mechanism of action, adverse effects and therapeutic uses
	**Sodium Valproate (Valproic Acid, Divalproex)**
	
	- Branched chain aliphatic carboxylic acid with **broad spectrum anticonvulsant action**.
	- Effective in partial seizures (SPS, CPS) and GTCS, as well as absence, myoclonic, and atonic seizures.
	- Produces little sedation or other central effects at anticonvulsant doses.
	
	**Mechanism of Action**:
	
	- Appears to act by **multiple mechanisms**.
	- **Phenytoin-like frequency-dependent prolongation of Na⁺ channel inactivation**.
	    - Suppresses high frequency neuronal firing.
	- Weak attenuation of **Ca²⁺ mediated 'T' current** (ethosuximide like).
	- **Enhanced release of inhibitory transmitter GABA**.
	    - Inhibition of GABA degradation (by GABA-transaminase).
	    - Probably increases GABA synthesis from glutamic acid.
	- **Blockade of excitatory NMDA glutamate receptors**.
	- May also affect synaptic vesicular protein 'SV2A' and Collapsin-response mediator protein (CRMP2) indirectly.
	- Reduces intraneuronal concentration of inositol by inhibiting de novo inositol synthesis (similar to Lithium effect in mania).
	
	**Adverse Effects**:
	
	- Relatively low toxicity overall.
	- **Common/Mild**: anorexia, vomiting, loose motions, heartburn.
	- **Dose-dependent (Higher doses)**: drowsiness, ataxia, tremor. Cognitive and behavioural effects are generally not prominent.
	- **Less frequent**: Alopecia, curling of hair, weight gain, increased bleeding tendency, rashes, thrombocytopenia.
	- **Hepatic**: Asymptomatic rise in serum transaminase is often noted; **monitoring of liver function is advised**. A rare but serious adverse effect is **fulminant hepatitis**; occurs only in children (especially below 3 yr). Risk is greater in those with hepatic disease or receiving other anticonvulsants or hepatotoxic drugs.
	- **Teratogenicity**: When administered during pregnancy, it has produced **spina bifida and other neural tube defects** in the offspring. **Should be avoided in pregnancy**. Combination with carbamazepine doubles teratogenic frequency.
	
	**Therapeutic Uses**:
	
	- **Epilepsy**:
	    - **Drug of choice for absence seizures**.
	    - **Most effective drug for myoclonic seizures**.
	    - One of the **1st line drugs for partial seizures and GTCS** (due to good tolerability).
	    - Drug of choice for **atonic seizures** (control is often incomplete).
	- **Bipolar Disorder & Mania**:
	    - Very commonly used as an **alternative to lithium**.
	    - **First line treatment for mania** (relatively high dose oral).
	    - Acts faster than lithium but slower than antipsychotics ± a BZD.
	    - Has a **better safety profile than lithium**.
	    - Effective **prophylactic in bipolar disorder**.
	    - Can be useful in those not responding to lithium or not tolerating it.
	    - Patients with **rapid cycling pattern** may particularly benefit from valproate therapy.
	    - A combination of lithium and valproate may succeed in cases resistant to monotherapy with either drug.
	    - A combination of valproate with an atypical antipsychotic has high efficacy in acute mania. Divalproex is more commonly used due to better gastric tolerance.
	- **Other**:
	    - Has been used for **panic attacks**.
	    - Has some **prophylactic efficacy in migraine**.
	
	**Pharmacokinetics**:
	
	- Good oral absorption.
	- 90% bound to plasma proteins.
	- Completely metabolized in liver by oxidation (mainly CYP2C9 and 2C19) and glucuronide conjugation.
	- Plasma t½ is 10-15 hours, but anticonvulsant effects are longer lasting.
	
	**Drug Interactions**:
	
	- **Valproate inhibits glucuronidation of lamotrigine**, doubling its blood level. The dose of lamotrigine should be reduced to half in patients taking valproate.
	- Lamotrigine lowers valproate levels.
	- Carbamazepine can reduce efficacy of valproate.
	- Metabolism by CYP2C9 and 2C19. Risk of hepatitis increased when receiving other anticonvulsants or hepatotoxic drugs.
5. The drug used for treatment of trigeminal neuralgia
	1. Carbamazepine
