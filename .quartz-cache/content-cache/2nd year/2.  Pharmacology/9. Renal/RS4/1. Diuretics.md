1. High ceiling diuretics (loop diuretics)-mechanism of action uses, and adverse effects
	**High Ceiling (Loop) Diuretics: High-Yield Notes**
	
	- **Classification:** Diuretics (Natriuretics). Known as 'High Ceiling' or 'Loop' diuretics.
	- **Key Drugs:**
	    - **Furosemide** (Frusemide): The first and prototype drug.
	    - **Bumetanide**: Similar to furosemide, 40 times more potent.
	    - **Torasemide** (Torsemide): Similar properties to furosemide, 2-3 times more potent.
	    - _Ethacrynic acid_ and _Organomercurials_ are mentioned as historical agents.
	- **Mechanism of Action:**
	    - Primary site: **Thick ascending limb of loop of Henle (TAL)** (Site II, Fig 42.1).
	    - Inhibit the **Na+-K+-2Cl- cotransport** at the luminal membrane.
	    - Act from the **luminal side** after being secreted into the proximal tubule (PT) by organic anion transport.
	    - This inhibition **abolishes the corticomedullary osmotic gradient**.
	    - Block both positive and negative free water clearance.
	    - Minor component of action on PT also demonstrated.
	    - Binding involves attaching to the Cl- binding site of the cotransporter protein.
	- **Effects on Ions/Electrolytes:**
	    - Substantial **natriuresis** (Na+ loss). Maximal fractional Na+ loss can be up to ~25%.
	    - Increased **K+ excretion** (mainly due to high Na+ load reaching distal tubule).
	    - Increased **Ca2+ excretion**.
	    - Increased **Mg2+ excretion**.
	    - Weak Carbonic Anhydrase (CAse) inhibitory action, increases HCO3- excretion, but urine pH rises only mildly; predominant urinary anion is Cl-.
	- **Pharmacokinetics/Properties:**
	    - **High efficacy**: Maximal natriuretic effect much greater than other classes.
	    - **Dose-response**: Diuretic response increases with increasing dose (up to 10 L urine/day).
	    - **Rapid Onset**: i.v. 2-5 mins, oral 20-40 mins.
	    - **Short Duration of Action**: 3-6 hours (Furosemide). Torasemide is longer (4-8 hours).
	    - Effective even in **severe renal failure**.
	    - Oral bioavailability is about 60% for furosemide, 80-100% for bumetanide, and more rapid/complete for torasemide, even with bowel edema in CHF.
	    - Primarily eliminated by renal excretion. Plasma half-life is short (e.g., Furosemide ~1 hr, Bumetanide 60 min), but prolonged in renal/hepatic insufficiency. Torasemide t½ is 3.5 hrs.
	- **Clinical Uses (Indications):**
	    - **Edema**: Preferred for rapid mobilization of edema fluid irrespective of etiology (cardiac, hepatic, renal). Drugs of choice in **Nephrotic Syndrome** and other resistant edema. Effective in **Chronic Renal Failure** (with massive doses). May decrease need for dialysis in impending Acute Renal Failure.
	    - **Acute Pulmonary Edema (Acute LVF, following MI)**: Prompt relief with i.v. administration due to venodilator action preceding saluretic effect.
	    - Cerebral Edema (may be combined with osmotic diuretics).
	    - **Hypertension**: Indicated only in presence of **renal insufficiency, heart failure, resistant cases**, or hypertensive emergencies (thiazides preferred otherwise).
	    - Preventing volume overload during blood transfusion in severely anaemic patients.
	    - Hyponatraemia (infused with hypertonic saline).
	    - **Hypercalcaemia of malignancy**: Augments Ca2+ excretion after volume replacement.
	- **Adverse Effects & Complications:**
	    - **Electrolyte Disturbances**:
	        - **Hypokalaemia**: Most significant problem with brisk diuresis or prolonged use, potentially leading to **cardiac arrhythmias**. Less common than with thiazides at standard doses due to shorter duration.
	        - **Acute Saline Depletion**: Dehydration, marked fall in BP (especially postural), increased risk of peripheral venous thrombosis.
	        - Dilutional Hyponatraemia (rare with thiazides).
	        - **Hypocalcaemia**.
	        - **Magnesium Depletion**: May increase risk of **ventricular arrhythmias**, especially post-MI or in digitalised patients.
	    - **Other Side Effects**:
	        - GIT/CNS disturbances: Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, headache, giddiness, weakness.
	        - **Hearing Loss**: Infrequent, risk increases with renal insufficiency, direct toxicity to inner ear hair cells.
	        - Allergic Manifestations: Rashes, photosensitivity (especially in sulfonamide hypersensitivity), rare blood dyscrasias.
	        - Hyperuricaemia (lower incidence than thiazides).
	        - Hyperglycaemia and dyslipidemia (minimal with lower doses now).
	    - **Specific Risks**:
	        - Precipitate hepatic coma in cirrhotics (due to hypokalaemia, alkalosis).
	        - Avoided in toxaemia of pregnancy (risk to placental circulation).
	- **Interactions:**
	    - Potentiate other antihypertensives.
	    - Hypokalaemia **enhances digoxin toxicity** and increases risk of polymorphic ventricular tachycardia with Q-T prolonging drugs.
	    - Increased risk of hyperkalaemia with K+ supplements, ACE inhibitors/ARBs.
	    - Increased ototoxicity/nephrotoxicity with **aminoglycoside antibiotics**.
	    - **NSAIDs** diminish diuretic action (inhibit renal PG synthesis).
	    - Probenecid inhibits tubular secretion, decreasing action.
	    - Increase serum lithium level.
	- **Resistance:**
	    - Causes: Renal insufficiency, CHF (impaired absorption, decreased renal blood flow), Cirrhosis, Nephrotic Syndrome.
	    - Long-term use can cause distal nephron hypertrophy.
	    - Resistance is more common with thiazides but can occur with loops. Can be overcome by adding a diuretic acting on the distal tubule (e.g., thiazide, metolazone).
2. Thiazide diuretics (hydrochlorothiazide)-name four mechanism of action , therapeutic uses and adverse effects
	**Thiazide Diuretics (Hydrochlorothiazide): High-Yield Notes**
	
	- **Classification:** Diuretics (Natriuretics); Medium efficacy diuretics.
	    
	- **Key Drug:** **Hydrochlorothiazide**. Related drugs: Chlorthalidone, Indapamide, Metolazone, etc..
	    
	- **Site of Action:** Primary site is the **cortical diluting segment/early Distal Tubule (DT)** (Site III).
	    
	- **Mechanism of Action (Four Key Points):**
	    
	    1. Inhibit the **Na+-Cl- symport** at the luminal membrane of cells in the early DT. This is the main mechanism reducing NaCl reabsorption.
	    2. Gain access to their site of action via the organic acid secretory pathway in the proximal tubule.
	    3. Bind to specific receptors located on the luminal membrane in the early DT.
	    4. By a direct distal tubular action, they **decrease renal Ca2+ excretion**. (This is a key effect often discussed as part of their mechanism/pharmacodynamics, distinct from the main natriuretic mechanism). _(Note: Some also have weak Carbonic Anhydrase inhibitory action in the PT, and they reduce GFR, but inhibition of Na-Cl symport, binding, and reduced Ca excretion are more specific and prominent actions/effects)._
	- **Efficacy:** Medium efficacy. Maximal fractional Na+ loss is ~8-9%. They are moderately efficacious because ~90% of glomerular filtrate is reabsorbed before reaching the DT.
	    
	- **Pharmacokinetics (HCTZ):** Well absorbed orally; no injectable forms. Action starts within 1 hour. Duration of action is typically 6-12 hours. Eliminated mainly unchanged by the kidney. Plasma t½ of HCTZ is 3-4 hours. Metolazone is a related exception that is effective in severe renal failure.
	    
	- **Clinical Uses (Therapeutic Indications):**
	    
	    - **Hypertension:** One of the **first-line drugs** for hypertension, especially Chlorthalidone and Indapamide. Effective in lowering BP.
	    - **Edema:** Used for **mild-to-moderate cases**. Effective in cardiac edema. Less effective in hepatic or renal edema. Powerless in the presence of renal failure (except Metolazone). May be used for maintenance therapy of edema.
	    - **Diabetes Insipidus:** Paradoxically exert an antidiuretic effect. Effective in **nephrogenic diabetes insipidus**. Can reduce urine volume in pituitary-origin DI, though less effective than vasopressin analogues. Mechanism involves inducing electrolyte depletion and enhancing proximal reabsorption.
	    - **Hypercalciuria:** Used for hypercalciuria with recurrent calcium stones in the kidney, by reducing Ca2+ excretion.
	- **Adverse Effects & Complications:**
	    
	    - **Hypokalaemia:** **Most significant problem**. Risk increases with brisk diuresis, prolonged therapy, or low dietary K+. Can cause weakness, fatigue, muscle cramps, and serious **cardiac arrhythmias**. Less common with standard doses of loops than thiazides due to shorter duration of action of loops.
	    - Alkalosis: May occur with hypokalaemia.
	    - **Hyperuricaemia:** Can cause rise in blood urate level, especially with long-term higher doses. More common than with furosemide.
	    - **Hyperglycaemia & Dyslipidemia:** Minimal with the lower doses currently used for hypertension, but occurred with higher doses in the past. Hyperglycaemia possibly due to decreased insulin release secondary to hypokalaemia.
	    - **Hypomagnesaemia:** May develop after prolonged use and increase the risk of **ventricular arrhythmias**, particularly post-MI or in digitalised patients.
	    - **Hypocalcaemia:** **Thiazides _raise_ serum Ca2+**. (Contrast with loop diuretics which increase Ca excretion).
	    - Acute Saline Depletion: Less common than with high ceiling diuretics, but can occur with overzealous use, leading to dehydration and postural hypotension.
	    - GIT and CNS disturbances: Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, headache, giddiness, weakness, paresthesias, impotence.
	    - Allergic Manifestations: Rashes, photosensitivity (especially in sulfonamide-sensitive patients), rare blood dyscrasias.
	    - Aggravation of Renal Insufficiency: Occasionally, possibly by reducing GFR. They are generally ineffective in significant renal failure.
	    - Precipitation of Hepatic Coma: In cirrhotics, due to hypokalaemia, alkalosis, and increased blood ammonia.
	    - Contraindicated in Toxaemia of Pregnancy: Risk to placental circulation.
	    - Hearing loss: **NOT caused by thiazides** (only by loop diuretics).
	- **Interactions:**
	    
	    - Potentiate other antihypertensives.
	    - Hypokalaemia enhances **digoxin toxicity** and increases risk of polymorphic ventricular tachycardia with Q-T prolonging drugs.
	    - NSAIDs diminish action.
	    - Probenecid inhibits tubular secretion.
	    - Increase serum **lithium** level.
	    - Reduce sulfonylurea action.
3. Potassium sparing diuretics-enumerate the mechanism of action , therapeutic uses and side effects
	**Potassium Sparing Diuretics: High-Yield Notes**
	
	- **Classification:** Diuretics; indirectly conserve Potassium (K+) while causing mild natriuresis. Also called 'aldosterone antagonists' or 'renal epithelial Na+ channel inhibitors' based on mechanism.
	- **Key Drugs:**
	    - **Aldosterone Antagonists:** **Spironolactone**, Eplerenone.
	    - **Renal Epithelial Na+ Channel Inhibitors:** **Amiloride**, Triamterene.
	- **Mechanism of Action:**
	    - Primary site: **Late Distal Tubule (DT) and Collecting Duct (CD)** (Principal Cells).
	    - Action is independent of aldosterone for Amiloride/Triamterene, but dependent for Spironolactone.
	    - **Two main mechanisms:**
	        1. **Aldosterone Antagonists (Spironolactone, Eplerenone):**
	            - Competitively inhibit the **intracellular mineralocorticoid receptor (MR)** in late DT/CD cells.
	            - Prevent the formation of 'aldosterone-induced proteins' (AIPs).
	            - AIPs normally promote Na+ reabsorption and K+ secretion. By inhibiting AIP formation/action, Spironolactone reduces Na+ reabsorption and, importantly, **decreases K+ secretion**.
	            - Act from the interstitial side of the tubular cell. No effect in the absence of aldosterone.
	        2. **Renal Epithelial Na+ Channel Inhibitors (Amiloride, Triamterene):**
	            - Directly block the 'renal epithelial' or 'amiloride sensitive' **Na+ channel (ENaC)** located on the **luminal membrane** of principal cells in the late DT/CD.
	            - Blocking Na+ entry into the cell reduces the lumen-negative transepithelial potential difference that normally drives K+ secretion into the lumen via K+ channels.
	            - Thus, they **inhibit K+ secretion**.
	            - Also reduce H+ secretion by decreasing the lumen-negative potential, predisposing to acidosis (Amiloride).
	- **Efficacy:** Low efficacy; induce only mild natriuresis (maximal fractional Na+ loss ~3%). This is because only a small fraction of filtered Na+ reaches the CD.
	- **Effects on Ions/Electrolytes:**
	    - Mild natriuresis (Na+ excretion increased).
	    - Significantly **decrease K+ excretion** (conserve K+).
	    - Reduce Ca2+ and Mg2+ excretion (Amiloride/Triamterene). Thiazides also reduce Ca2+ excretion, loops increase it.
	    - Amiloride/Triamterene may slightly alkalinize urine initially (less H+ secretion), but Amiloride predisposes to acidosis.
	- **Clinical Uses (Therapeutic Indications):**
	    - **To counteract K+ loss** caused by thiazide or loop diuretics. Often used in combination for this purpose.
	    - **Edema:** More useful in edema associated with **high aldosterone levels**, such as cirrhotic edema and nephrotic edema. They potentiate thiazides/loop diuretics and can break resistance in **refractory edema** due to secondary hyperaldosteronism. Frequently added in ascites due to cirrhosis of liver.
	    - **Hypertension:** Used to augment thiazides in **resistant hypertension**. Also help prevent thiazide-induced hypokalaemia. (Spironolactone may attenuate renal fibrosis/hypertrophy).
	    - **Heart Failure:** Spironolactone is used as an additional drug in moderate-to-severe CHF to retard disease progression and lower mortality.
	    - **Primary Hyperaldosteronism (Conn's Syndrome):** Spironolactone is indicated.
	    - **Diabetes Insipidus:** **Amiloride is the drug of choice for lithium-induced nephrogenic DI**. This is because Amiloride blocks lithium entry through Na+ channels in CD cells. Can be combined with a thiazide ± indomethacin in nephrogenic DI.
	- **Adverse Effects & Complications:**
	    - **Hyperkalaemia:** **The most important adverse effect**. Risk is increased in patients with **renal insufficiency** or those taking **K+ supplements**. Also more likely with concurrent use of **ACE inhibitors/ARBs**, beta-blockers, or NSAIDs. Can be dangerous.
	    - **Acidosis:** More likely with Amiloride/Triamterene, as Amiloride reduces H+ secretion.
	    - **Gastrointestinal & CNS:** Nausea, diarrhoea, headache (common with Amiloride/Triamterene).
	    - **Spironolactone Specific:** Because it's a steroid, spironolactone can cause antiandrogen effects (gynecomastia, impotence, menstrual irregularities) - _This information is not in the provided sources but is a known side effect_.
	- **Interactions:**
	    - Dangerous hyperkalaemia with **K+ supplements**.
	    - More pronounced hyperkalaemia with **ACE inhibitors/ARBs**.
	    - Aspirin blocks spironolactone action.
	    - Increased risk of hyperkalaemia with beta-blockers or NSAIDs (with Amiloride/Triamterene).
	- **Contraindications:**
	    - **Renal insufficiency**.
	    - Hyperkalaemia.
	    - Concurrent use with K+ supplements.
