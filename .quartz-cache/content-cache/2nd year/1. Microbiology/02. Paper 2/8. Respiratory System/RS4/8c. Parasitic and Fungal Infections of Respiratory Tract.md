2. Mention 3 pulmonary parasites infecting man
		**Pulmonary Parasites Infecting Humans**
		Parasites can infect the respiratory tract and lungs, sometimes as their primary site or as part of their life cycle.
	
	- **_Paragonimus westermani_**:
	    
	    - The **oriental lung fluke**, a trematode.
	    - Considered a **primary pathogen of the lungs**.
	    - Causes **endemic hemoptysis**.
	    - Life cycle involves snails and crabs/crayfishes as intermediate hosts; humans are the definitive host [implied by life cycle details in sources].
	    - Infection from ingesting infective metacercaria larvae [implied by life cycle details in sources]. Larvae migrate to the lungs and develop into adult worms. Adult worms form cysts in the lungs.
	    - **Clinical features**: Productive cough with **brownish blood-tinged rusty sputum** often with an offensive fishy odor. Occasional frank hemoptysis. Chronic cases may lead to bronchitis, bronchiectasis, pneumonia, or lung abscess.
	    - **Laboratory diagnosis**:
	        - **Sputum microscopy**: **Saline mount** of early morning, deeply coughed sputum sample (particularly blood-tinged flecks) is examined for characteristic **operculated eggs**. Multiple samples (up to seven) may be needed if the egg burden is low.
	        - Peripheral blood **eosinophilia** is common.
	        - Radiological tests like Chest X-ray may show characteristic pulmonary lesions (patchy densities, cavities, pleural effusion).
	    - **Treatment**: **Praziquantel** (25 mg/kg/dose, three doses per day for 2 days) is the drug of choice. Surgical management may be needed for lesions.
	- **_Ascaris lumbricoides_**:
	    
	    - The largest nematode parasitizing the human intestine, commonly called **roundworm**.
	    - Intestinal nematode that **passes through the lungs during its life cycle**.
	    - Causes **Loeffler's pneumonia** during the migratory stage in the lungs.
	    - Life cycle involves larvae migrating through the lungs. Larval migration through the small bowel mucosa may also cause symptoms.
	    - **Eosinophilia** is prominent during the early lung stage. **Charcot-Leyden crystals** may be found in sputum and stool (though nonspecific).
	    - Diagnosis is typically made by finding characteristic eggs in stool [implied by chapter context on intestinal helminths and Ascaris life cycle].
	- **Filarial nematodes** (causing Tropical Pulmonary Eosinophilia - TPE):
	    
	    - Vector-borne parasites (implied by discussion in filarial nematodes context). Examples include _Wuchereria bancrofti_ and _Brugia malayi_, which cause lymphatic filariasis [implied by Table 69.1 referring to Classical Filariasis].
	    - Cause **hypersensitivity in the lungs**, resulting in **Tropical Pulmonary Eosinophilia (TPE)**.
	    - TPE is considered a form of occult filariasis, believed due to a hypersensitivity reaction to microfilaria antigens trapped in the lungs. Microfilariae are typically absent in the blood in TPE.
	    - **Clinical features**: Characteristic **nocturnal paroxysmal cough and wheezing**, weight loss, low-grade fever. Occasionally, hepatosplenomegaly and lymphadenopathy may occur (Meyers Kouwenaar Syndrome).
	    - **Laboratory diagnosis**:
	        - Marked **blood eosinophilia** (absolute eosinophil count >3000/µL).
	        - Chest X-ray showing diffuse infiltration.
	        - Elevated serum IgE levels.
	        - Pulmonary function test showing obstructive changes.
	        - Microfilariae are **absent in blood**.
	    - **Treatment**: Responds well to **diethylcarbamazine (DEC)** (4–6 mg/kg for 14 days). Relapse can occur.
	
	Other parasites can rarely infect the lungs, such as _E. histolytica_ (pulmonary amoebiasis - second most common site of extraintestinal amoebiasis after liver), _Toxoplasma_ (pulmonary toxoplasmosis, mainly causes encephalitis in HIV patients), and _Echinococcus granulosus_ (pulmonary hydatidosis - second most common site of hydatid disease after liver). Parasites of lower animals that rarely infect humans, like _Mammomonogamus_ (larynx and trachea), _Capillaria aerophila_ (trachea and bronchus), and _Ascaris suum_ (lungs and intestine), are also mentioned as rare causes.
3. Rhinocerebral Mucor mycosis
	**Rhinocerebral Mucormycosis (Zygomycosis)**
	
	- **Clinical Relevance/Context:**
	    
	    - Rare infection of the sinuses, eyes, orbits, oral cavity, and brain.
	    - Caused by **opportunistic saprophytic fungi** of the **Zygomycetes family**, primarily **_Mucor_** and **_Rhizopus_**.
	    - Most common form of mucormycosis.
	    - Occurs commonly in patients with **diabetic ketoacidosis**.
	    - Other risk factors include end-stage renal disease, patients taking iron therapy or deferoxamine, and defects in phagocytic functions.
	    - Clinically starts as eye and facial pain, may progress to cause orbital cellulitis, proptosis, and vision loss.
	- **Laboratory Diagnosis:**
	    
	    - **Specimen:** **Biopsy tissue** from the involved area.
	    - **Direct Microscopy:**
	        - **10% KOH mount** of biopsy tissue. Reveals **nonseptate broad branching hyphae**.
	        - **Histopathological staining** (e.g., **H&E or methenamine silver stain**) of tissue biopsies. Shows **broad aseptate hyaline hyphae with wide angle branching** (Fig 69.3 mentioned).
	    - **Culture:**
	        - Inoculated onto **Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA)**.
	        - Incubation at **25°C**.
	        - Colonies: Rapidly growing, **white cottony woolly**, with **tube filling growth** (hence called "lid lifters"). Colonies may become brown/black later due to sporulation ("salt and pepper appearance").
	        - Microscopic appearance of culture (LPCB mount): Reveals broad aseptate hyaline hyphae, sporangiophore, sporangium, sporangiospores. Different species (_Rhizopus_, _Lichtheimia_, _Mucor_) differentiated by presence and position of rhizoids.
	- **Prevention:**
	    
	    - Specific primary preventive measures (e.g., prophylaxis, environmental control) are **not detailed** in the provided sources.
	    - **Correction of underlying disease** (e.g., **diabetic ketoacidosis**) is listed as a treatment modality. This is a crucial aspect in managing the condition in susceptible patients.

	**Aspergillus species: Microscopic Features**
	
	- **General Morphology:** _Aspergillus_ is a **hyaline mold**. Microscopy reveals characteristic **septate hyphae** and structures associated with conidiation (asexual reproduction).
	- **Hyphae:**
	    - **Narrow, septate hyphae**.
	    - Characteristic **acute angle branching** (typically 45 degrees). This is a key feature seen in direct examination (e.g., tissue).
	- **Conidiation Structures (from culture):**
	    - **Conidiophores:** Arise from the hyphae.
	    - **Vesicles:** Swollen structures at the end of the conidiophore. Can be **tubular** or **globular** in shape.
	    - **Phialides (Sterigmata):** Finger-like projections arising from the vesicle. These are the conidia-producing cells. They can be arranged in **one or two rows**. If in two rows, the first row is called **metulae**.
	    - **Conidia:** Chains of conidia arise from the phialides.
	- **Key Differentiating Features (Based on Culture Morphology):** Species can be identified based on the macroscopic colony appearance and microscopic features of the conidiation structures:

|Feature|_A. fumigatus_|_A. flavus_|_A. niger_|
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
|**Colony Color**|**Smoky green**|**Yellow green**|**Black**|
|**Vesicle Shape**|**Conical-shaped**|**Globular-shaped**|**Globular-shaped**|
|**Phialide Rows**|**Single row**|**One or two rows**|**Two rows**|
|**Conidia Location**|Arise from **upper third** of vesicle|Arise from **upper two-third to entire** vesicle|Arise from **entire** vesicle|
|**Conidia Colour**|**Hyaline**|**Hyaline**|**Black**|

- **Staining:**
    - **Direct Examination (Tissue/Specimen):** **KOH (10%) mount** or **histopathological staining** (e.g., H&E or Methenamine silver stain) reveals the hyphae.
    - **Culture:** **LPCB (Lactophenol Cotton Blue)** mount is used to visualise the structures from the colony.

	A neat labelled diagram illustrating _Aspergillus_ morphology from a culture mount (like LPCB) would typically show:
	
	1. **Septate Hyphae:** Narrow filaments with visible cross-walls (septa).
	2. **Conidiophore:** An upright stalk arising from the hyphae.
	3. **Vesicle:** The swollen tip of the conidiophore.
	4. **Phialides (Sterigmata):** Small projections covering part or all of the vesicle surface.
	5. **Metulae:** If present (e.g., _A. flavus_, _A. niger_), these would be the first row of cells arising from the vesicle, with phialides arising from them.
	6. **Conidia:** Chains of spores budding off from the tips of the phialides.
	7. The diagram could optionally show the differences in vesicle shape, number/arrangement of phialides/metulae, and conidia origin on the vesicle for the major species (_A. fumigatus_, _A. flavus_, _A. niger_).
	
	**Clinical Relevance:**
	
	- **Invasive Aspergillosis:** Histopathology showing narrow septate hyphae with acute angle branching in tissue from immunocompromised patients (e.g., neutropenic, glucocorticoid use) is strongly suggestive.
	- **Aspergilloma (Fungal Ball):** A mass of hyphae found in pre-existing lung cavities.
	- **Allergic Bronchopulmonary Aspergillosis (ABPA):** Hypersensitivity reaction to _Aspergillus_ colonisation.
	
	These microscopic features are crucial for the laboratory diagnosis of aspergillosis, complementing clinical suspicion and other diagnostic methods like antigen detection.
