1. Name the viruses causing skin lesions
	
	Here is a concise, high-yield, note-style format answer regarding viruses causing skin lesions, optimized for medical school exams:
	
	---
	
	**Viruses Causing Skin Lesions: Etiology & Key Diagnostic Features**
	
	**I. Herpesviruses**
	
	2. **Herpes Simplex Viruses (HSV-1, HSV-2)**
	    
	    - **Clinical Manifestations:** Characterized by **vesicular lesions**.
	        - **Oral-facial:** Herpes labialis (painful vesicles near lips), gingivostomatitis, ulcerative stomatitis, tonsillitis, periocular vesicular lesions.
	        - **Cutaneous:** Herpetic whitlow, febrile blisters.
	        - **Genital:** Multiple, painful, bilateral, tiny **vesicular ulcers**.
	        - May also present as pustules or bullae.
	    - **Laboratory Diagnosis (Key):**
	        - **Cytopathology (Tzanck preparation):** Reveals **Cowdry type A intranuclear inclusion bodies** (Lipschultz body) & **multinucleated giant cells** (Tzanck cells). _Clinical Relevance: Rapid bedside test, though lacks specificity for HSV vs. VZV_.
	        - **Molecular Methods:** **PCR** and **real-time PCR** for HSV DNA, highly sensitive and specific, can differentiate types (HSV-1 vs. HSV-2) and quantify viral load.
	        - **Virus Isolation:** In cell lines (e.g., McCoy cell lines) demonstrating characteristic cytopathic effect (CPE) like diffuse rounding and ballooning.
	        - **Viral Antigen Detection:** Direct immunofluorescence (IF).
	3. **Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV)**
	    
	    - **Clinical Manifestations:** Produces **vesicular eruptions (rashes)** on skin and mucous membranes.
	        - **Chickenpox:** Generalized, diffuse, bilateral **vesicular rashes** (primary infection).
	        - **Zoster (shingles):** Unilateral and segmental **vesicular rashes** (reactivation of latent VZV).
	    - **Laboratory Diagnosis:**
	        - **Cytopathology (Tzanck smear):** Similar to HSV, revealing **multinucleated giant cells**.
	        - **Molecular Methods:** **PCR** for VZV-specific genes.
	        - **Specific Antigen/Antibody Detection:** Direct IF for antigen, ELISA for IgM/IgG antibodies.
	4. **Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV)**
	    
	    - **Clinical Manifestations:** Can cause **rashes**, particularly **following ampicillin therapy**. Primarily known for infectious mononucleosis.
	    - **Laboratory Diagnosis:**
	        - **Serology:** IgM antibodies to viral capsid antigen (VCA) indicate current infection; IgG indicates past infection. Heterophile antibody tests (Monospot test).
	5. **Human Herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6) and 7 (HHV-7)**
	    
	    - **Clinical Manifestations:** **Roseola infantum** (exanthem subitum or sixth disease), characterized by high fever followed by a rash.
	
	**II. Other DNA Viruses**
	
	6. **Human Papillomavirus (HPV)**
	    
	    - **Clinical Manifestations:**
	        - **Benign:** Various types of **warts** (common, flat, plantar, anogenital/condyloma acuminatum).
	        - **Malignant:** Associated with carcinomas (e.g., cervical, laryngeal, esophageal).
	    - **Laboratory Diagnosis:**
	        - **Molecular Methods:** **PCR** or hybrid capture assay for HPV DNA.
	        - **Cytology/Histopathology:** Papanicolaou smears, histopathological staining of biopsies.
	7. **Poxviruses**
	    
	    - **Smallpox Virus (Variola):**
	        - **Clinical Manifestations:** Highly contagious, severe **exanthematous disease**. Rashes are **deep seated**, appear in **one stage**, and have a **centrifugal distribution** (affecting palms, soles, extensor surfaces first).
	        - **Laboratory Diagnosis:** Direct detection of **intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies (Paschen bodies)** in skin scrapings. Electron microscopy for brick-shaped appearance.
	    - **Molluscum Contagiosum Virus:**
	        - **Clinical Manifestations:** Pink, pearly, **wart-like umbilicated lesions**.
	        - **Laboratory Diagnosis:** Detection of **Molluscum bodies** (intracytoplasmic eosinophilic inclusions) in skin scrapings.
	8. **Parvovirus (B19)**
	    
	    - **Clinical Manifestations:**
	        - **Erythema infectiosum (fifth disease):** Characteristic "**slapped cheek appearance**" rash on the face.
	        - Papular-purpuric gloves and socks syndrome.
	    - **Laboratory Diagnosis:**
	        - **Molecular Methods:** **PCR** for viral DNA (most sensitive).
	        - **Antibody Detection:** ELISA for IgM indicates recent infection.
	9. **Adenoviruses**
	    
	    - **Clinical Manifestations:** Can cause non-specific **macular viral rashes**. Primarily respiratory, ocular, and GI pathogens.
	
	**III. RNA Viruses**
	
	10. **Measles Virus**
	    
	    - **Clinical Manifestations:** Typical **maculopapular rash**. Characteristic **Koplik's spots** (bluish-white spots on buccal mucosa).
	    - **Laboratory Diagnosis:**
	        - **Antigen Detection:** Direct IF for measles antigens (nucleoprotein).
	        - **Molecular Methods:** **RT-PCR** for viral RNA.
	        - **Antibody Detection:** Measles-specific **IgM antibody** in serum is diagnostic.
	11. **Rubella Virus**
	    
	    - **Clinical Manifestations:** Characterized by **rashes**.
	    - **Laboratory Diagnosis:** **Serology (ELISA for IgM)** is the preferred method.
	12. **Coxsackieviruses (e.g., A16, A6, Enterovirus 71)**
	    
	    - **Clinical Manifestations:** **Hand-foot-and-mouth disease (HFMD)**, presenting with oral/pharyngeal ulcerations and **vesicular rashes on palms and soles**.
	13. **Viruses Causing Viral Hemorrhagic Fevers (VHF)**
	    
	    - **Clinical Manifestations:** Lead to vascular damage and hemorrhage, presenting with **petechiae**, **purpura**, and **ecchymosis**.
	        - **Dengue Virus:** **Petechial** or **maculopapular rashes**.
	        - **Ebola and Marburg Viruses:** Can cause **ecchymosis**.
	        - **Chikungunya Virus:** Maculopapular rashes.
	    - **Laboratory Diagnosis:**
	        - **Molecular Methods:** **RT-PCR** for viral RNA (e.g., for Dengue).
	        - **Serology:** IgM antibody detection (e.g., for Dengue, Chikungunya).
	14. **Cytomegalovirus (CMV)**
	    
	    - **Clinical Manifestations:** Can cause **petechiae** and **purpura**.
	    - **Laboratory Diagnosis:** Detection of characteristic "**Owl's eye appearance**" inclusion bodies. Molecular methods (PCR) for viral load quantification are key.

2. Tzank smear (cell)
	Here is a concise, high-yield, note-style format answer regarding Tzanck smear, optimized for medical school exams:
	
	---
	
	**Tzanck Smear (Cytopathology)**
	
	**I. Overview & Purpose**
	
	- **Definition:** A rapid **cytopathological examination** of scrapings obtained from the **base of a skin lesion**.
	- **Purpose:** Primarily used as a **bedside diagnostic aid** for **vesicular skin eruptions**, particularly those caused by herpesviruses [I. Herpesviruses, 1; 745].
	
	**II. Procedure**
	
	- **Specimen Collection:** Scrapings are taken from the base of the lesion.
	- **Staining:** Smears are typically stained with **Wright’s or Giemsa stain** (known as Tzanck preparation), or Papanicolaou stain.
	
	**III. Key Microscopic Findings (High-Yield)** Tzanck smears reveal characteristic cytopathological changes in infected cells:
	
	- **Multinucleated Giant Cells (Tzanck Cells):** These are a hallmark finding, characterised by **faceted nuclei and ground glass chromatin** [I. Herpesviruses, 1; I. Herpesviruses, 2; 745].
	- **Cowdry Type A Intranuclear Inclusion Bodies (Lipschultz Body):** Eosinophilic inclusions within the nucleus [I. Herpesviruses, 1; 745].
	- **Ballooning of Infected Cells:** Swelling of individual cells.
	- **Margination of Chromatin:** Chromatin pushed to the periphery of the nucleus.
	
	**IV. Clinical Relevance & Applications**
	
	- **Rapid Diagnosis:** Provides a quick, inexpensive bedside clue [I. Herpesviruses, 1].
	- **Aids in Diagnosing:**
	    - **Herpes Simplex Viruses (HSV-1, HSV-2):** Herpes labialis, genital herpes, herpetic whitlow [I. Herpesviruses, 1].
	    - **Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV):** Chickenpox, Zoster (shingles) [I. Herpesviruses, 2].
	
	**V. Limitations**
	
	- **Lack of Specificity:** Cannot differentiate between **HSV-1, HSV-2, and VZV**, as all produce similar cytopathological changes [I. Herpesviruses, 1; 745]. Further specific tests (e.g., PCR, viral culture, serology) are needed for definitive diagnosis and speciation [I. Herpesviruses, 1; I. Herpesviruses, 2; 749].
	- **Low Sensitivity:** Sensitivity can be low, particularly for mucosal swabs (<30%) or ulcerative lesions.
	- **Not a Gold Standard:** While helpful for initial suspicion, it is often followed by more sensitive and specific molecular methods (e.g., PCR) or viral culture for confirmation [I. Herpesviruses, 1; I. Herpesviruses, 2; 747, 749].
