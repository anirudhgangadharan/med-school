1. Extraintestinal manifestations of amoebiasis
	**EXTRAINTESTINAL MANIFESTATIONS OF AMOEBIASIS**
	
	Extraintestinal amoebiasis refers to infections caused by _Entamoeba histolytica_ outside the gastrointestinal tract, typically resulting from the invasion and dissemination of trophozoites from the large intestine.
	
	**I. Pathogenesis & Spread:**
	
	- **Trophozoite Invasion**: _E. histolytica_ trophozoites invade the intestinal mucosa and gain access to the **portal venous system**.
	- **Hematogenous Spread**: This is the **most common route** for spread to extraintestinal sites. Trophozoites can survive in the bloodstream due to resistance to complement-mediated lysis.
	
	**II. Organ Systems & Specific Manifestations:**
	
	1. **Liver (Amoebic Liver Abscess - ALA)**:
	    
	    - **Most Common Extraintestinal Site**.
	    - **Location**: Most frequently affects the **posterior-superior surface of the right lobe** of the liver; usually single, rarely multiple.
	    - **Pathology**: Trophozoites occlude hepatic venules, leading to **anoxic necrosis of hepatocytes**.
	    - **Pus**: Characterized by **"anchovy sauce" pus** – thick, chocolate-brown fluid composed of necrotic hepatocytes without pus cells, and occasionally trophozoites (found mainly in the last drops from the abscess wall).
	    - **Key Clinical Features**: Tender hepatomegaly and fever are most consistent.
	    - **Complications of Rupture**:
	        - **Pulmonary Amoebiasis**: Rupture into the lungs or right pleura (most common complication). May present with trophozoites in sputum.
	        - **Amoebic Pleuritis**.
	        - **Amoebic Pericarditis**: Rupture of left-sided liver abscess into the pericardial cavity.
	        - **Subphrenic Abscess & Generalised Peritonitis**: Rupture below the diaphragm.
	        - **Granuloma Cutis (Amoebiasis Cutis)**: Rupture externally to the skin, causing skin lesions on the abdominal wall.
	2. **Lungs (Pulmonary Amoebiasis)**:
	    
	    - Often occurs secondary to rupture of a liver abscess through the diaphragm.
	    - Trophozoites may be found in sputum.
	3. **Brain (Cerebral Amoebiasis)**:
	    
	    - Rare but serious complication.
	    - Results from **hematogenous spread from the liver**.
	    - Causes **brain abscess** and **secondary amoebic encephalitis**.
	4. **Spleen**:
	    
	    - Can be affected by hematogenous spread from the liver.
	5. **Genitourinary (GU) Tract**:
	    
	    - Infection via hematogenous spread from the liver, leading to **painful genital ulcers**.
	6. **Skin (Amoebiasis Cutis)**:
	    
	    - Can manifest as **perianal skin ulcers** (as an extension of amoebic colitis) or **skin lesions on the abdominal wall** (secondary to a ruptured amoebic liver abscess).
	    - May also be seen following anal intercourse.
2. Structure of hyatid cyst
	The hydatid cyst, also known as **cystic echinococcosis**, is the **larval form** of the cestode _Echinococcus granulosus_. It is the **pathogenic form** that creates cystic lesions primarily in the liver and other organs of humans and herbivores.
	
	Here's a breakdown of its structure:
	
	- **General Morphology**
	    
	    - **Fluid-filled, bladder-like cyst**.
	    - Typically **unilocular** and **subspherical in shape**.
	    - Size usually ranges from **5–8 cm**, but can be anywhere from a few millimetres to over 30 cm.
	- **Cyst Wall Layers**
	    
	    - **Outer Pericyst:** The outermost layer, which is **host-derived**. This layer can be demonstrated with histopathological stains like Giemsa, H&E, and PAS.
	    - **Middle Ectocyst:** A **non-cellular, laminated membrane**. Visible with histopathological stains.
	    - **Inner Endocyst (Germinal Layer):** The innermost, **nucleated** layer that lines the ectocyst. This layer is responsible for giving rise to **brood capsules** and secreting **hydatid fluid**. It is also visible with histopathological stains.
	- **Contents within the Cyst**
	    
	    - **Brood Capsules:** Formed from the inner endocyst, these are small structures that contain numerous **protoscolices**. They can be seen in histopathological sections.
	    - **Protoscolices:** These are the **future heads** of the adult tapeworm. They are found within the brood capsules and are a component of hydatid sand.
	    - **Hydatid Fluid:** A **clear, pale yellow fluid** that fills the cyst. It is notable for being **antigenic, toxic, and anaphylactic**. Diagnostic aspiration of this fluid is generally not recommended due to the risk of anaphylaxis or dissemination.
	    - **Hydatid Sand:** This consists of **broken-off brood capsules and protoscolices** that settle at the bottom of the cyst. It can be detected by direct microscopy of aspirated hydatid fluid, where it feels like "grating of sand grains" when rubbed between two slides.
	- **Fate and Clinical Relevance**
	    
	    - Hydatid cysts can persist for years or even decades without symptoms.
	    - Rupture or leakage of the cyst can lead to the formation of **secondary cysts** (due to dissemination of protoscolices) or **severe anaphylactic reactions** to the hydatid fluid antigens.
	    - Cyst activity can be classified using imaging, such as ultrasonography (USG), which can show characteristic features like the "water lily sign" for transitional cysts (CE3 lesions) where detached laminar membranes float within the cyst cavity.
3. Lab diagnosis of Hyatid disease
	The laboratory diagnosis of Hydatid disease primarily involves imaging techniques, serology, and direct parasite detection, with molecular methods also playing a role.
	
	Here's a concise, high-yield overview:
	
	**Laboratory Diagnosis of Hydatid Disease**
	
	1. **Imaging Methods:** These are crucial for detecting and characterizing hydatid cysts.
	    
	    - **Ultrasonography (USG):**
	        - Allows **anatomic confirmation** of cysts and their location.
	        - Can detect both single and multiple cysts.
	        - WHO classifies USG images of cysts into six types (CL and CE1 to CE5) based on their activity, including the characteristic **"water lily sign"** for CE3 lesions (detached laminar membranes floating within the cyst).
	        - Useful for **monitoring treatment response** and epidemiological studies.
	    - **Computed Tomography (CT Scan):**
	        - Highly sensitive, detecting **90–100% of cases**.
	        - More accurately identifies the number and location of cysts, and complications.
	        - Superior to USG for detecting **calcified lesions**, smaller cysts, and extrahepatic cysts.
	        - Can be used as a **prognostic marker**.
	    - **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):**
	        - Offers **higher contrast resolution**, making cysts clearer.
	        - Used as an alternative to CT scan but **poorly detects calcified cysts**.
	2. **Serological Tests (Antibody Detection):** These tests are generally performed in a two-step approach: a sensitive screening test followed by a more specific confirmatory test.
	    
	    - **ELISA:**
	        - Uses **crude _Echinococcus granulosus_ cyst fluid antigen** or **recombinant B2t antigen** (the latter provides better results).
	        - Also used as an **indicator of cure** in surgically treated patients.
	    - **Dot Immunogold Filtration Assay (DIGFA):**
	        - Can simultaneously detect serum antibodies against **four native antigens** (three from _E. granulosus_ and one from _E. multilocularis_).
	    - **Immunoblot (Western Blot):**
	        - Considered the **most specific serological method**.
	        - Detects antibodies to hydatid cyst fluid antigen or antigen B fragment.
	        - Useful for sero-epidemiological studies but **cannot differentiate between recent and past infection**. Antigen B is the antigen of choice for sero-epidemiological studies.
	    - **Clinical Correlation:** Liver cysts yield better results than extrahepatic cysts, while pulmonary cysts often do not produce detectable antibodies. Alveolar echinococcosis generally shows better serological results than cystic echinococcosis.
	3. **Microscopy and Histopathology:**
	    
	    - **Hydatid Fluid Microscopy:**
	        - Aspirated hydatid fluid can be examined by direct microscopy or acid-fast stain to detect **hydatid sand**, which consists of protoscolices and brood capsules.
	        - However, **diagnostic aspiration is generally not recommended** due to the risk of fluid leakage, which can lead to anaphylaxis or dissemination of infection.
	    - **Histological Examination:**
	        - Surgically removed cysts can be stained with Giemsa, Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E), and Periodic Acid–Schiff (PAS) stains to demonstrate the **three layers of the cyst wall** and **attached brood capsules**.
	4. **Molecular Methods:**
	    
	    - **PCR** targeting mitochondrial DNA has been developed.
	    - **PCR-RFLP** can be used to detect genotypes of _E. granulosus_.
	5. **Skin Test (Casoni Test):**
	    
	    - This test, which detects an immediate hypersensitivity reaction, is **now considered obsolete**.
