1. Poliomyelitis-pathogenesis, clinical manifestation, laboratory diagnosis, immunoprophylaxis (vaccine against poliomyelitis/polio vaccines).
	
	**Poliomyelitis**
	
	- Caused by **Polioviruses**, which are type of enteroviruses belonging to the family Picornaviridae.
	- Simple, very small (28–30 nm), nonenveloped, spherical viruses with icosahedral symmetry.
	- Possess single-stranded positive sense linear RNA.
	- Three wild poliovirus strains exist: WPV1, WPV2, and WPV3. All produce similar manifestations and severity.
	- They are genetically and immunologically-distinct, particularly in the VP1 region. Antibody response is type-specific.
	- As of 2020, WPV2 and WPV3 were globally eradicated. Currently, all natural cases are caused by WPV1.
	
	**Pathogenesis**
	
	- **Transmission:** Primarily by the **feco-oral route**, followed by respiratory droplets (inhalation), or rarely conjunctival contact.
	- **Initial Multiplication:** Viruses multiply locally in intestinal epithelial cells, submucosal lymphoid tissues, tonsils, and Peyer’s patches.
	- **Cell Entry:** Viral entry into host cells is mediated by binding to **CD155 receptors** present on the host cell surface.
	- **Spread:** Poliovirus spreads from the initial sites of multiplication to the central nervous system (CNS).
	- **Target Site:** The final target site for poliovirus is the **motor nerve ending**, specifically the anterior horn cells of the spinal cord.
	- **Outcome:** Involvement of anterior horn cells leads to **muscle weakness and flaccid paralysis**.
	- **Neuron Degeneration:** Virus-infected neurons undergo degeneration. Earliest change is degeneration of Nissl body (aggregated ribosomes in neuron cytoplasm).
	- Pathological changes are always more extensive than the distribution of paralysis.
	
	**Clinical Manifestations**
	
	- Incubation period usually 7–14 days.
	- Manifestations range from asymptomatic to severe paralytic stage.
	- In children, the disease progression is typically **biphasic**:
	    - **Aseptic meningitis** occurs first.
	    - Recovery, followed by a return of fever with **paralytic features** 1–2 days later.
	- Sites involved in paralysis can be spinal, bulbospinal, and bulbar, leading to variations in paralysis nature (e.g., respiratory insufficiency or dysphagia in bulbar involvement).
	- Cranial nerves are occasionally involved.
	- There is **no sensory loss**.
	- **Risk factors** for paralytic disease include older children and adults, pregnant women, following heavy muscular exercise, trauma at the time of CNS symptoms, tonsillectomy (predisposes to bulbar poliomyelitis), and IM injections (increase risk of paralysis in involved limb).
	
	**Laboratory Diagnosis**
	
	- Diagnosis is based on clinical signs and symptoms, plus laboratory confirmation.
	- **Specimen:** Poliovirus may be recovered from **throat swabs** (up to 3 weeks) and **rectal swabs or stool samples** (up to 12 weeks). Virus isolation from CSF or blood is very rare. Specimens should be kept frozen during transport.
	- **Virus Isolation:**
	    - Primary monkey kidney cells are the most recommended cell lines.
	    - Viral growth can be identified by Cytopathogenic effects (CPE) (cell sheet crenation and degeneration) appearing in 3–6 days.
	    - Isolated virus can be identified and serotyped by neutralization with specific antiserum or by immunofluorescence test.
	- **Molecular Methods:**
	    - PCR assay targeting the VP1 region of poliovirus is highly useful, rapid, more sensitive, and serotype-specific.
	    - Real-time multiplex reverse-transcriptase PCR targeting the VP1 region can detect and differentiate between wild and vaccine polioviruses (VAPP and VDPV strains) directly from stool specimen.
	    - **BioFire FilmArray**, an automated nested multiplex PCR, can simultaneously detect agents of pyogenic and viral meningitis, including poliovirus, from CSF in 1 hour. While CSF is less likely to yield the virus, the FilmArray can be part of a diagnostic panel for meningitis.
	- **Antibody Detection:**
	    - A rise in antibody titer in paired sera collected at 1–2 weeks interval suggests poliomyelitis.
	    - **Neutralization test** is the recommended method.
	    - Only the first infection with poliovirus produces strictly type-specific responses. Subsequent infections induce antibodies against group-specific antigen common to all three serotypes.
	- **Sewage Testing:** Routine screening of sewage is conducted under the polio eradication program to verify transmission.
	
	**Immunoprophylaxis (Polio Vaccines)**
	
	Both inactivated and live attenuated polio vaccines are available.
	
	1. **Injectable Polio Vaccine (IPV, Salk Vaccine):**
	    - Prepared by inactivating poliovirus (grown in monkey kidney cells) with formalin.
	    - Contains antigens for all three serotypes (1, 2, and 3).
	    - Can be given IM (full dose) or ID (fractional dose).
	    - **Advantages:** Much safer than OPV, especially in immunocompromised people. Does not cause vaccine-associated paralytic polio (VAPP). More stable, does not require stringent storage.
	    - **Disadvantages:** Does **not provide herd immunity** (not spread by feco-oral route). Not useful during epidemics. More expensive than OPV.
	    - In India's National Immunization Schedule (2020), two fractional doses (f-IPV) are given intradermally at 6th and 14th weeks along with bivalent OPV.
	2. **Oral Polio Vaccine (OPV, Sabin Vaccine):**
	    - Contains live attenuated poliovirus strains.
	    - Available as trivalent (types 1, 2, 3) or bivalent (types 1 and 3). Trivalent OPV was replaced by bivalent OPV since 2015 due to eradication of serotype 2 and risk of vaccine-induced paralysis. Monovalent OPV (any one serotype) is also available.
	    - Recommended vaccine under national immunization schedules in India and most other countries.
	    - Administered orally.
	    - **Advantages:** Cheaper. Easier to administer. Provides **mucosal immunity**, eradicating carriage from nasopharynx. Can spread by feco-oral route, providing **herd immunity**. Useful during epidemics.
	    - **Disadvantages:** Can cause **Vaccine-Associated Paralytic Poliomyelitis (VAPP)** and Vaccine-Derived Polioviruses (VDPV). Requires stringent cold chain storage. Efficacy decreased by other enteroviruses or diarrhea. Breastfeeding should be avoided for 30 min before/after administration as it may wash away OPV.
	
	- **VAPP and VDPV:** VAPP denotes paralytic poliomyelitis cases following OPV administration. VAPP strains are OPV-like with limited genetic divergence (<1%). VAPP can occur in recipients and close contacts via feco-oral spread.
	- **Polio Eradication:** Attributed to extensive global immunization programs like Pulse Polio Immunization (PPI). PPI involves administering bivalent-OPV to all children under 5 years annually, irrespective of routine status; these are extra doses. Acute flaccid paralysis (AFP) surveillance is conducted to monitor progress.
