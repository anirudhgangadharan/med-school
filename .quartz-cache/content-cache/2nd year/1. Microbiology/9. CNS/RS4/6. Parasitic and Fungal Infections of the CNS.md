1. Toxoplasmosis-life cycle, pathogenesis, clinical features and laboratory diagnosis (serological tests).
	
	**Toxoplasmosis**
	
	- Caused by the intracellular parasite, _**Toxoplasma gondii**_.
	
	**Life Cycle**
	
	- **Definitive Hosts:** Cats and other felines. Sexual cycle occurs here. Oocysts are excreted in their feces.
	- **Intermediate Hosts:** Man and other animals.
	- **Infective Forms:**
	    - **Oocysts:** Mature quadrinucleated cysts are infective (Note: Source refers to _E. histolytica_ specifically, but the concept of mature cysts as infective form is generally applicable to protozoa in this context and _T. gondii_ oocysts are mentioned as infective in). Sporulated oocysts from contaminated soil, food, or water.
	    - **Tissue Cysts:** Contain bradyzoites. Found in undercooked meat of infected animals.
	- **Transmission:**
	    - Ingestion of contaminated **uncooked or under-cooked meat** (most common route).
	    - Ingestion of sporulated oocysts from contaminated soil, food, or water.
	    - **Transplacental transmission** from mother to fetus.
	    - Blood transfusion and organ transplantation.
	
	**Pathogenesis**
	
	- Depends on the host's immune status.
	- Infection involves **direct invasion** by the parasite.
	- **Tachyzoites** (rapidly multiplying trophozoites) are seen in acute infection. They can spread to various tissues like lymphatic tissue, skeletal muscle, myocardium, retina, placenta, and CNS.
	- Host immune response controls acute infection, leading to the formation of **tissue cysts (pseudocysts)** containing **bradyzoites**. These cysts persist, indicating the presence of infection but not necessarily acute disease.
	- In **immunocompromised hosts** (especially HIV with CD4 < 100/µL), **reactivation** of latent infection occurs. Bradyzoites transform back into tachyzoites, leading to **necrotizing encephalitis** and secondary pressure effects.
	
	**Clinical Features**
	
	- Vary depending on the patient population.
	- **Immunocompetent Patients:**
	    - Usually **asymptomatic** and self-limited.
	    - Most common manifestation is **lymphadenopathy**.
	    - Less commonly, can cause chorioretinitis, myocarditis, or encephalitis.
	- **Immunocompromised Patients (especially HIV/AIDS):**
	    - **Toxoplasmic Encephalitis (TE)** is the most common manifestation.
	    - Occurs when CD4+ T cell count falls below 100/µL.
	    - Symptoms: altered mental status, seizures, sensory abnormalities, cerebellar signs, focal neurologic findings (motor deficits, cranial nerve palsies, visual-field loss).
	- **Congenital Toxoplasmosis:**
	    - Due to transplacental transmission.
	    - Can cause congenital malformations: **chorioretinitis, hydrocephalus, and intracranial calcifications**.
	
	**Laboratory Diagnosis (Serological Tests)**
	
	- Serology is a key diagnostic modality.
	- **ELISA:** Most widely used assay for detecting **IgG and IgM antibodies** (Note: Source refers to mumps ELISA, but ELISA is a general method used for _Toxoplasma_ antibodies according to).
	    - **IgM positive (+):** Usually indicates **recent infection**. Can persist for several months or be false-positive.
	    - **IgG seroconversion** (negative to positive) or **≥4-fold rise** in IgG titre in paired sera (taken 2-3 weeks apart) indicates **acute infection**.
	    - **IgG positive (+), IgM negative (-):** Usually indicates **past infection**.
	    - **IgG positive (+), IgM positive (+):** May indicate acute infection or chronic infection with persisting IgM. Further testing needed.
	    - **IgG Avidity Test:** Differentiates recent vs. past infection in cases with positive IgG and IgM. Low avidity IgG suggests recent infection (<3-4 months). High avidity IgG suggests infection occurred >4 months prior.
	    - **IgG negative (-), IgM negative (-):** Unlikely infection.
	    - **IgG negative (-), IgM positive (+):** Usually indicates **false-positive IgM**.
	- **Western blot:** Used to **confirm** positive IgG or IgM results.
	- **Sabin-Feldman Dye Test:**
	    - Historically the **gold standard antibody detection method**.
	    - A complement-mediated neutralization test requiring **live tachyzoites**.
	    - **Seldom used nowadays** in routine diagnostics due to technical difficulty and inability to differentiate recent vs. past infection.
	
	**Other Diagnostic Modalities** (Beyond serology, mentioned in sources)
	
	- **Direct Microscopy:** Detection of tachyzoites (acute infection) or tissue cysts (presence of infection) in blood, body fluids, tissue biopsies (lymph node, bone marrow, brain, muscle) stained with Giemsa, H&E, PAS, or peroxidase stain.
	- **Molecular Diagnosis (PCR):** Highly sensitive and specific. Detects _Toxoplasma_-specific DNA in blood, **CSF**, bronchoalveolar lavage, amniotic fluid. Can detect genotypes. Used for TE or congenital infections.
	- **Imaging:** CT or MRI scan of the brain can show multiple ring enhancing lesions in basal ganglia or corticomedullary junction in TE in HIV patients.
	- **Animal Inoculation:** Isolation from mice by intraperitoneal inoculation of samples.
	- **Tissue Culture:** Isolation by inoculating into cell lines like human foreskin fibroblast, HeLa, Vero.
2. Neurocysticercosis (laboratory diagnosis).
	**Neurocysticercosis (NCC) - Laboratory Diagnosis**
	
	- **NCC** is CNS infection by the larval stage of _Taenia solium_. Diagnosis involves a combination of methods.
	
	**Key Diagnostic Modalities:**
	
	1. **Imaging Studies (Radiodiagnosis)**:
	    
	    - **CT Scan and MRI** are the two **important imaging methods** used to detect cysticerci in the brain.
	    - Provide crucial information about the:
	        - Number of cysts (single or multiple).
	        - Location (parenchymal or extraparenchymal).
	        - Size (small _Cysticercus cellulosae_ or large _Cysticercus racemosus_).
	        - **Morphological Stage** (vesicular, necrotic, nodular, calcified).
	        - Extent of the lesion.
	        - **Activity:** Ring-like enhancement surrounding cysts with associated inflammation and edema indicates **acute infection**.
	    - **CT Scan** is useful for detecting **calcified cysts** (appears as hyperdense dots).
	    - **MRI** has **higher contrast resolution** and is superior to CT for detecting:
	        - Extraparenchymal cysts in ventricles and cisterns.
	        - Inflammatory changes.
	        - Vesicular and necrotic lesions.
	        - Noncystic lesions.
	2. **Immunodiagnosis (Antibody Detection)**:
	    
	    - Advantages include lower cost than CT and MRI and confirmation of etiology.
	    - **ELISA** is used to detect **antibodies in serum and CSF**.
	    - Highly sensitive (75–90%) in serum.
	    - CSF ELISA also gives better results.
	3. **Histopathology and Microscopy**:
	    
	    - Cysticerci can be detected in tissues (muscles, eyes, subcutaneous tissues, brain) by **biopsy** following surgical removal or **fine needle aspiration (FNAC)** of the cyst.
	    - Samples are stained (e.g., Giemsa, Ryan’s modification of trichrome stain, H&E stain).
	    - Microscopy can differentiate between viable, necrotic, and calcified cysticerci.
	    - Cholesterol crystals may be seen. Charcot-Leyden crystals are characteristically absent.
	4. **Fundoscopy**:
	    
	    - Used for **ocular cysticercosis** to visually identify the movements and morphology of the larval worm.
	
	**Comprehensive Diagnostic Criteria:**
	
	- **Revised Del Brutto’s Diagnostic Criteria** (updated in 2016) is widely used in endemic countries.
	- It is based on a combination of **clinical, imaging, immunological, and epidemiological data**.
	
	**Note on CSF analysis:**
	
	- General CSF parameters (cell count, protein, glucose) are not specifically detailed for NCC in these sources, unlike for other CNS infections like bacterial meningitis, viral meningitis, or tuberculous meningitis.
	- However, detection of **anti-cysticercal antibodies in CSF by ELISA** is listed as an immunological diagnostic method for NCC.
	- One source notes that CSF analysis contributes nothing to diagnosis or therapy in focal intracranial infections such as brain abscess, and LP increases the risk of herniation. While NCC lesions are often cystic and space-occupying, which could be considered focal, the sources explicitly list CSF antibody detection as a diagnostic method for NCC.
3. Cryptococcus neoformans/cryptococosis (cryptococcal/fungal meningitis) morphology cultural characters and laboratory diagnosis.
	**Cryptococcus neoformans / Cryptococcosis (Cryptococcal Meningitis)**
	
	- **Causative Agent:** _**Cryptococcus neoformans**_, a **capsulated yeast**.
	- **Clinical Relevance:** Primary CNS pathogen. Causes potentially **fatal meningitis** in **HIV-infected individuals**, especially with **CD4 T cell counts less than 200/µL**. Presents as **chronic meningitis**. _C. gattii_ can cause infection in immunocompetent individuals.
	
	**Morphology**
	
	- Seen as **round budding yeast cells**.
	- Possesses a prominent **polysaccharide capsule**.
	- Capsule is visualised as a clear, **refractile delineated space** surrounding the yeast cell in negative stains like **India ink**.
	- Capsule can be twice as thick as the diameter of the yeast cell.
	- Appears as gram-positive round budding yeast cells on Gram staining.
	
	**Cultural Characters**
	
	- Grows on standard media: **SDA (without antibiotics)**, blood agar, or chocolate agar.
	- Incubation temperature: **37°C**.
	- Colony appearance: **Mucoid creamy white** and yeast-like colonies. The mucoid appearance is due to the capsule.
	- Specific media for identification:
	    - **Niger seed agar** or **bird seed agar** used to demonstrate **melanin production** (colonies turn brown/black).
	- Biochemical tests: **Urease positive**. Assimilates inositol and nitrate.
	
	**Laboratory Diagnosis**
	
	- **Specimens:** Primarily **CSF**. Also blood, skin scrapings.
	- **Direct Detection:**
	    - **CSF Microscopy:**
	        - **India ink stain (or Nigrosin):** Used for **negative staining** to visualise the capsule as a halo around the yeast cells. **Sensitivity is 60-70%**.
	        - Gram stain: May show gram-positive round budding yeast cells.
	        - Other stains: **Mucicarmine stain** (stains cell wall), **Masson-Fontana stain** (demonstrates melanin).
	    - **Antigen Detection:**
	        - Detection of **capsular antigens** in CSF or serum using the **Latex Agglutination Test**.
	        - This is a **rapid, highly sensitive (95%), and specific** method.
	- **Culture:**
	    - Inoculate CSF onto SDA (without antibiotics), blood agar, or chocolate agar and incubate at 37°C.
	    - Blood culture uses biphasic bottles.
	    - Colony morphology aids identification (mucoid, creamy white).
	    - Culture allows for confirmation and further identification tests.
	- **Identification:**
	    - Based on colonial morphology, microscopic appearance, and biochemical tests.
	    - Key tests: **Melanin production** on Niger seed/bird seed agar, **positive urease test**, growth at 37°C.
	    - Automated systems (MALDI-TOF) can also be used.
