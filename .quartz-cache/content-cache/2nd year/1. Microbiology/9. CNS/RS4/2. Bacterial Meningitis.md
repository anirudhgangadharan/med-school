1. A 2-year-old boy was brought to the hospital with history of high fever with vomiting and drowsiness. On examination the child has neck rigidity and other signs of meningitis. CSF examination was suggestive of bacterial meningitis:

	a. List the bacteria causing acute meningitis.
	
	b. Describe the pathogenesis of meningococcal meningitis.
	
	c. Describe in detail sample collection and laboratory diagnosis of meningitis.
	
	d. Discuss prevention of meningococcal meningitis
	
	**Case Scenario Analysis: Suspected Bacterial Meningitis in a 2-Year-Old Boy**
	
	**a. Bacteria Causing Acute Meningitis**
	
	Based on the clinical presentation and CSF findings suggestive of bacterial meningitis, the potential bacterial etiological agents include:
	
	- **Overall Common Causes:**
	    - _Streptococcus pneumoniae_ (most common cause overall, ~50%)
	    - _Neisseria meningitidis_ (~25%)
	    - _Group B Streptococcus_ (_Streptococcus agalactiae_) (~15%)
	    - _Haemophilus influenzae_ (<10%). Note: _H. influenzae_ serotype b (Hib) is a major cause of pyogenic meningitis mainly in children less than 2 years of age.
	    - _Listeria monocytogenes_ (~10%)
	- **Other Potential Agents:**
	    - Gram-negative bacilli such as _Escherichia coli_. Note: _E. coli_ and _Klebsiella_ are common causes in neonates.
	    - _Pseudomonas_ (can cause meningitis in postoperative or post-traumatic patients).
	    - _Acinetobacter_ (associated with post-neurosurgical meningitis).
	    - _Elizabethkingia meningosepticum_ (can cause meningitis in neonates).
	    - _Staphylococcus aureus_ and coagulase-negative staphylococci (can cause meningitis following neurosurgical procedures, particularly shunting).
	    - _Corynebacterium jeikeium_ (can cause meningitis, especially in immunocompromised hosts).
	**b. Pathogenesis of Meningococcal Meningitis**
	
	- Meningococcal meningitis is caused by _Neisseria meningitidis_ (meningococcus), a capsulated gram-negative diplococci.
	- Humans are the only natural host for meningococci.
	- The most common source of infection is nasopharyngeal carriers.
	- Transmission occurs from person-to-person through droplets of respiratory secretions. Close and prolonged contact facilitates spread.
	- Organisms gain access to the meninges primarily by **hematogenous spread**, where they enter the subarachnoid space through the choroid plexus or other blood vessels of the brain.
	- Other routes include direct spread from an infected site close to the meninges (e.g., otitis media, sinusitis) or through anatomical defects.
	
	**c. Sample Collection and Laboratory Diagnosis of Meningitis in Detail**
	
	Laboratory diagnosis is crucial for confirming bacterial meningitis and identifying the agent.
	
	- **Specimen Collection:**
	    
	    - **Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF):** The most ideal specimen for pyogenic meningitis.
	        - Obtained by **lumbar puncture** under strict aseptic conditions.
	        - Divided into **three sterile containers**: one each for cell count, biochemical analysis, and bacteriological examination.
	        - CSF should be examined **immediately** as it is a precious specimen.
	        - CSF for bacterial culture should **never be refrigerated**; if delayed, keep at 37°C (e.g., in an incubator or bedside in blood culture bottles).
	        - CSF for molecular diagnosis can be kept inside the freezer.
	    - **Blood Culture:** Another useful specimen. Collected in conventional or automated blood culture bottles.
	    - **Other Specimens (for suspected meningococcal meningitis):** Nasopharyngeal swabs, pus or scrapings from rashes (transported in media like Stuart’s medium).
	- **CSF Analysis (Cytological and Biochemical):** Gives preliminary clues.
	    
	    - **CSF Pressure:** Highly elevated (>180 mm of water).
	    - **Total Leukocyte Count:** Highly elevated, typically 100–10,000/µL (usually >1000/µL).
	    - **Predominant Cell Type:** **Neutrophils** (polymorphonuclear cells), 90–95%. Note: _Listeria_ meningitis is an exception with predominantly lymphocytes.
	    - **Glucose Level:** Markedly diminished or even absent (<40 mg/dL).
	    - **Total Protein Content:** Markedly increased (>45 mg/dL, usually >250 mg/dL).
	- **CSF Microscopy (Gram Staining):** Provides a preliminary clue for early empirical therapy.
	    
	    - Smear is made from CSF.
	    - **Gram stain** reveals bacterial morphology. Finding **gram-negative diplococci** is highly suggestive of _Neisseria meningitidis_.
	    - "Heaped smear" technique can increase sensitivity due to potentially low bacterial load.
	    - Other morphologies can suggest different agents (e.g., gram-positive cocci in chains for _S. agalactiae_, gram-positive short bacilli for _Listeria_, gram-negative coccobacilli for _H. influenzae_).
	- **Culture:** Allows isolation and identification of the etiological agent.
	    
	    - Specimens (CSF, Blood) are cultured on enriched media like **chocolate agar and blood agar**. MacConkey agar can also be used. Selective media (Thayer Martin/NYC) for meningococci from non-sterile sites.
	    - Culture plates are incubated at 37°C, preferably in a candle jar (provides 5% CO2) for 48 hours.
	    - _Neisseria meningitidis_ produces non-hemolytic colonies on blood agar.
	    - Culture of CSF and blood is positive in 70-85% of patients, but sensitivity drops with prior antibiotic therapy or delay in processing.
	- **Rapid Diagnostic Tests:** Considered to determine bacterial etiology, especially with prior antibiotics.
	    
	    - **Antigen Detection:** Capsular antigen detection in CSF (e.g., by latex agglutination).
	    - **Molecular Methods (NAATs):** Highly sensitive, detect even few bacteria in CSF, faster than culture.
	        - Multiplex PCR or multiplex real-time PCR can simultaneously detect common agents.
	        - **BioFire FilmArray** is an automated nested multiplex PCR that can detect 14 common agents in CSF in 1 hour.
	        - Specific genes targeted for meningococcus include _ctrA_ and _sodC_.
	- **Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (AST):** Essential to guide definitive therapy. Done by disk diffusion or preferably automated MIC-based methods (e.g., VITEK).
	    
	
	**d. Prevention of Meningococcal Meningitis**
	
	Prevention strategies include chemoprophylaxis and vaccination.
	
	- **Chemoprophylaxis:**
	    
	    - Indicated for **close contacts** of primary cases, regardless of their vaccination status.
	    - Close contacts include household contacts and others directly exposed to patient's oral secretions in the 7 days before symptom onset.
	    - **Drug of choice is Ceftriaxone** (single dose, IM).
	    - Alternatively, rifampicin or ciprofloxacin can be given.
	- **Vaccine Prophylaxis:**
	    
	    - Meningococcal polysaccharide vaccines are available.
	    - Current formulations include bivalent (serogroups A and C) or quadrivalent (serogroups A, C, Y, and W135) vaccines.
2. A 7-month-old baby was admitted to the hospital with complaints of high-grade fever, vomiting, seizure and neck rigidity. CSF sample was collected by lumbar puncture in a sterile container and sent to the laboratory for biochemical analysis, cell count and culture and sensitivity testing. Gram's stain revealed, gram-negative diplococci:

	a. What is probable clinical diagnosis?
	
	b. What are etiological agents?
	
	c. Pathogenesis of this disease.
	
	d. Describe the laboratory diagnosis in detail.
	
	e. What are the treatment modalities in this case?
	**Case Scenario Analysis: Suspected Meningitis in a 7-Month-Old**
	
	**a. Probable Clinical Diagnosis** Based on the clinical presentation of high-grade fever, vomiting, seizure, and neck rigidity, the probable clinical diagnosis is **Acute Bacterial Meningitis** (also called pyogenic meningitis). This is further supported by the CSF analysis showing gram-negative diplococci. Meningitis is an inflammation of the leptomeninges surrounding the brain and spinal cord.
	
	**b. Etiological Agents** Given the finding of **gram-negative diplococci** in the CSF Gram's stain, the most likely etiological agent in this case is **Neisseria meningitidis** (meningococcus). While _Streptococcus pneumoniae_ is the overall most common cause of pyogenic meningitis, the specific Gram stain morphology in this case points strongly towards meningococcus. Other agents of pyogenic meningitis include Group B Streptococcus, _Haemophilus influenzae_, _Listeria monocytogenes_, and occasionally gram-negative bacilli like _Escherichia coli_. _H. influenzae_ appears as gram-negative coccobacilli, not typically diplococci, and _E. coli_ are bacilli.
	
	**c. Pathogenesis** Acute bacterial meningitis is an acute purulent infection within the subarachnoid space, characterised by elevated polymorphonuclear cells in CSF. While the sources do not detail the pathogenesis specific to _N. meningitidis_, in general, bacterial meningitis occurs when bacteria colonise the nasopharynx and invade the bloodstream, subsequently crossing the blood-brain barrier to infect the meninges and subarachnoid space. The resultant inflammatory response leads to the observed clinical features.
	
	**d. Laboratory Diagnosis in Detail** The laboratory diagnosis is crucial for confirming the clinical suspicion and identifying the specific etiological agent:
	
	- **Specimen Collection:** **CSF** is the most ideal specimen for pyogenic meningitis. It is obtained by **lumbar puncture** under strict aseptic conditions and divided into sterile containers for cell count, biochemical analysis, and bacteriological examination. CSF should be examined **immediately** as it is a precious specimen. **Blood culture** is another useful specimen, collected in conventional or automated blood culture bottles.
	- **CSF Analysis:**
	    - **Cell Count:** Expected to show **highly elevated leukocyte count** (typically >1000/µL) with a predominance of **neutrophils** (90–95%). (Note: _Listeria_ meningitis is an exception with predominantly lymphocytes).
	    - **Biochemical Analysis:** **Total protein content** is expected to be **elevated**, and the **glucose level** is expected to be **markedly diminished or even absent**. CSF pressure is typically **highly elevated**.
	- **CSF Microscopy (Gram Staining):** Direct microscopic examination of a Gram-stained smear of CSF provides a preliminary clue. In this case, the finding of **gram-negative diplococci** is highly suggestive of _Neisseria meningitidis_. To increase sensitivity, a "heaped smear" technique can be used.
	- **Culture:** Specimens (CSF, Blood) are cultured. Culture plates (blood agar and chocolate agar) are incubated at 37°C, preferably in a candle jar (provides 5% CO2) for 48 hours. _Neisseria meningitidis_ appears as gram-negative cocci. Culture allows for isolation and further identification. Culture of CSF and blood is positive in 70-85% of patients with bacterial meningitis, but sensitivity drops with prior antibiotic therapy.
	- **Rapid Diagnostic Tests:** These should be considered to determine bacterial etiology, especially if antibiotics were given prior.
	    - **Antigen Detection:** Capsular antigen detection in CSF, e.g., by latex agglutination.
	    - **Molecular Methods:** Nucleic Acid Amplification Tests (NAAT) like **PCR** are highly sensitive. Automated multiplex PCR systems like BioFire FilmArray can simultaneously detect common meningitis agents, including meningococcus (targeting _ctrA_ and _sodC_ genes).
	- **Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (AST):** Essential to guide definitive therapy. It is done by disk diffusion or preferably automated MIC-based methods.
	
	**e. Treatment Modalities** Early initiation of treatment is crucial due to the high mortality associated with pyogenic meningitis. Treatment is initially empirical and modified based on laboratory results.
	
	- **Empirical Therapy:** Based on the strong suspicion of _Neisseria meningitidis_ from the Gram stain, an antibiotic with good CSF penetration covering this organism would be initiated.
	- **Definitive Therapy:** Once _Neisseria meningitidis_ is confirmed and AST results are available, therapy is modified.
	    - The **drug of choice** for meningococcal meningitis is **third-generation cephalosporins**, such as **ceftriaxone or cefotaxime**, given for 7 days. Penicillin can also be used, but reduced sensitivity has been reported.
	- **Adjunctive Therapy:** **IV dexamethasone** is added to the regimen to reduce intracranial pressure.
	- **Symptomatic Treatment:** Includes measures such as aggressive fluid resuscitation (if needed for shock) and measures to decrease intracranial pressure.
	
	**Important Note:** The empirical treatment mentioned for adults and neonates in the source gives context, but the specific regimen for a 7-month-old would follow national/local guidelines, typically using third-generation cephalosporins which are explicitly mentioned as the drug of choice for confirmed meningococcal meningitis.
