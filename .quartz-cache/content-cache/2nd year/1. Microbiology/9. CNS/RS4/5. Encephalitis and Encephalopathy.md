1. Japanese B encephalitis-etiology, pathogenesis, laboratory diagnosis and prophylaxis.
	**Japanese B Encephalitis (JE)**
	
	- **Etiology:**
	    
	    - Caused by **Japanese Encephalitis virus (JEV)**.
	    - Belongs to the family **Flaviviridae**.
	    - It is an **enveloped virus**, containing **single-stranded RNA (ssRNA)**.
	    - Leading cause of vaccine-preventable viral encephalitis in Asia, including India.
	- **Pathogenesis:**
	    
	    - Transmitted by **arthropods**.
	    - JEV is an arbovirus, specifically transmitted by **Culex mosquitoes**.
	    - Reservoir hosts include **birds** and **pigs**.
	    - Man is considered a **dead-end host**.
	    - Incubation period is typically **5–15 days** in persons who develop symptoms.
	    - JE typically shows an **iceberg phenomenon**, with much less overt cases compared to subclinical/inapparent infections (ratio of 1:300 to 1:1000). Even during an epidemic, the number of cases may be only 1–2 per village.
	    - Clinical course can be divided into stages: Prodromal, Acute encephalitis, and Late stage/sequelae.
	    - Pathogenesis involves the virus invading the central nervous system (CNS), causing acute inflammation of the brain parenchyma (encephalitis).
	- **Clinical Manifestations (Implicitly included in the pathogenesis description of stages):**
	    
	    - **Prodromal stage:** A febrile illness, with onset varying from abrupt to subacute (2–5 days).
	    - **Acute encephalitis stage:** Characterized by acute onset of **fever**, **mental confusion**, disorientation, delirium, **seizures** (among children), or coma. It is the most common cause of acute encephalitis syndrome (AES) in India.
	    - **Late stage and sequelae:** Convalescent stage where the patient may recover fully or retain permanent neurological deficits (up to 50%).
	    - Case fatality rate is about **20–40%**.
	    - Specific laboratory diagnosis of viral encephalitis is discussed under respective agents. CSF analysis typically shows **lymphocytic pleocytosis**, mildly elevated protein, and normal glucose, indistinguishable from viral meningitis.
	- **Laboratory Diagnosis:**
	    
	    - CSF analysis: Characteristic CSF profile includes lymphocytic pleocytosis, mildly elevated protein, and normal glucose. CSF findings in viral meningitis (similar to encephalitis) are normal or slightly elevated protein (20–80 mg/dL), normal glucose (rarely low), normal or mildly elevated pressure, and cell count typically 25–500/μL (predominantly lymphocytes, though neutrophils may predominate in the first 48 hours). Organisms are not seen on Gram staining.
	    - **IgM capture antibody (MAC) ELISA:** Recommended method for diagnosis. Supplied by NIV, Pune. Uses JERA (JE recombinant antigen) to detect JE-specific IgM antibody in serum.
	    - **Molecular methods:** Reverse-transcriptase (RT) PCR and real-time RT-PCR have been developed to detect JE virus-specific envelope (E) gene in blood.
	- **Immunoprophylaxis (Vaccine):**
	    
	    - Vaccine prophylaxis is recommended for children residing in rural areas and individuals living in endemic areas.
	    - Licensed JE vaccines available in India include:
	        - **Live attenuated Sa 14-14-2 vaccine:** Prepared from the SA 14-14-2 strain. Cell-line derived, commonly using primary hamster kidney cell lines. Manufactured in China but licensed in India. Given to children under the National Immunization Program.
	        - **Combined vaccine:** A genetically engineered JE vaccine combining the attenuated SA14-14-2 strain and yellow fever vaccine strain 17D (YF 17D) virus as a vector. Has been tested in clinical trials.
	- **Treatment:**
	    
	    - Treatment of JE is only by **supportive measures**; no specific antiviral drugs are available.
2. Pathogenesis and laboratory diagnosis of rabies.
	**Rabies**
	
	- Caused by **Rabies virus**, a member of the **Rhabdoviridae** family.
	- It is an **enveloped virus** with a characteristic **bullet shape**. Contains single-stranded, negative-sense RNA.
	- Almost always fatal once clinical symptoms appear.
	
	**Pathogenesis**
	
	- **Transmission:** Usually by a deep **bite or scratch** from an infected animal with rabies.
	    - **Rabid dogs** account for **99%** of cases globally.
	    - **Bats** are a major source in America.
	    - Other sources: foxes, raccoons, skunks, jackals, mongooses, wild carnivores.
	    - Can also occur via **non-bite exposures** like direct contact of infected saliva with open wounds or mucous membranes.
	    - **Viremia does not occur**. Human-to-human transmission via saliva shedding is documented in rabid animals but **not documented in humans**.
	- **Initial Replication:** Virus multiplies locally at the **site of inoculation** (muscle or connective tissue).
	- **Neuronal Entry:** Virus binds to **nicotinic acetylcholine receptors** present at neuromuscular junctions, gaining entry to peripheral neurons.
	- **Spread to CNS:** Rabies virus spreads **centripetally** along the **peripheral motor nerves** via **retrograde fast axonal transport**.
	    - Spreads at a rate of up to **250 mm/day**.
	    - Reaches the dorsal root ganglia of the spinal cord and ascends towards the CNS.
	    - Incubation period is variable and prolonged (average 20–90 days, ranges from 1 week to 19 years), related to the distance from inoculation site to CNS.
	- **CNS Infection:** Rapidly disseminates within the CNS.
	    - Most common sites: **hippocampus and cerebellum**.
	    - Causes acute infectious disease of the CNS.
	- **Centrifugal Spread:** From the CNS, the virus spreads along sensory and autonomic nerves to various tissues, including **salivary glands** (important for transmission).
	- **Pathological Changes:** Presence of **Negri bodies** in brain parenchyma is an important finding.
	    - These are **intracytoplasmic eosinophilic inclusions** composed of viral proteins and RNA.
	
	**Laboratory Diagnosis**
	
	- Laboratory diagnosis provides **supportive evidence** for confirmation, with treatment often started based on clinical suspicion due to the high mortality.
	- **Specimens:** Excised tissue bits from necrotic depths of wounds are more reliable than wound swabs. Other important specimens include hair follicle at the nape of the neck, corneal impression smear, saliva, brain tissue (postmortem), and CSF.
	- **Rabies Antigen Detection:**
	    - **Direct Immunofluorescence Test (direct-IF) / Direct Fluorescent Antibody (DFA) test:** Detects rabies nucleoprotein antigens using fluorescent-tagged antibodies.
	    - Considered the "**gold standard**" due to high sensitivity and specificity.
	    - Best specimen: **Hair follicle of the nape of the neck** (most sensitive).
	    - Corneal impression smear can be used but is less sensitive (30%) and usually positive in the late stage.
	- **Viral Isolation:**
	    - **Mouse inoculation:** Intracerebral inoculation into suckling mice can cause encephalitis and death. Brain examined for Negri bodies and antigen. Technically challenging and slower.
	    - **Cell lines:** Mouse neuroblastoma and Baby Hamster Kidney (BHK) cell lines are preferred. Faster yield (2-4 days) than mice. Viral growth detected by direct-IF.
	- **Antibody Detection:**
	    - Detection of **CSF antibodies is more significant** than serum antibodies.
	        - Serum antibodies appear late and can be due to vaccination.
	        - CSF antibodies appear early and are produced _only_ in rabies-infected individuals, not post-vaccination.
	    - Tests include Mouse Neutralization Test (MNT), Rapid Fluorescent Focus Inhibition Test (RFFIT), Fluorescent Antibody Virus Neutralization test (FAVN), and Indirect Fluorescence Assay (IFA).
	- **Viral RNA Detection:**
	    - **Reverse Transcription-Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR):** Amplifies rabies virus genes (e.g., nucleoprotein, large structural protein) from brain tissue, saliva, skin biopsy.
	    - Considered the **most sensitive and specific assay** available at present for diagnosis.
	- **Negri Body Detection:**
	    - Useful to confirm **postmortem diagnosis**.
	    - Detected in brain tissue sections (commonly hippocampus and cerebellum) using histological stains (H&E, Sellers) or immunohistochemistry.
	    - **Immunohistochemistry is more sensitive and specific** than histological staining.
	    - **Pathognomonic** of rabies.
	    - However, Negri bodies may **not be detected in 20% of cases**; their absence does not rule out the diagnosis.
3. Mention three viruses causing encephalitis.
	
	**Viral Encephalitis - Etiological Agents**
	
	- Viral agents predominantly cause encephalitis.
	- Encephalitis is an acute inflammation of the brain parenchyma caused by infectious agents, most often **viruses**.
	
	**Common Viral Causes of Acute Encephalitis:**
	
	- **Herpesviruses**:
	    - **Herpes simplex virus (HSV)**: Most common cause of acute **sporadic viral encephalitis**. HSV-1 is more common (>95%) than HSV-2.
	    - **Cytomegalovirus (CMV)**: Particularly in **immunocompromised hosts**.
	    - **Varicella-zoster virus (VZV)**: Can cause meningitis and encephalitis, sometimes without rash.
	    - **Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)**: Can cause meningitis and encephalitis, sometimes associated with infectious mononucleosis.
	    - Human herpesvirus 6.
	- **Arboviruses**: Arthropod-transmitted viruses.
	    - **Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV)**: **Most common cause of epidemic encephalitis in Asia, including India**. Transmitted by _Culex_ mosquitoes.
	    - **West Nile virus (WNV)**: **Most common cause of epidemic encephalitis in USA**. Endemic in Americas, Africa, West Asia, and highly prevalent in India. Mainly transmitted by _Culex_ mosquitoes.
	    - Other arboviruses causing encephalitis listed include: Saint Louis encephalitis virus (_Culex_), Tick-borne encephalitis viruses (Powassan virus, Colorado tick fever virus), California encephalitis virus (_Aedes triseriatus_), Murray Valley encephalitis virus (_Culex annulirostris_), Rocio virus (_Culex_), Russian spring-summer encephalitis virus (Tick), Louping-ill virus (Tick), Chandipura virus (Sandfly).
	- **Rabies virus**: Causes encephalitis secondary to dog bite. Belongs to the Rhabdoviridae family.
	- **Nipah and Hendra viruses**: Zoonotic paramyxoviruses that mainly cause encephalitis.
	- **Rare causes**:
	    - Enteroviruses.
	    - Mumps virus.
	
	**Viral Agents Causing Chronic Encephalitis/Encephalopathy (Slow Virus Diseases)**:
	
	- Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (caused by **JC virus**).
	- Subacute sclerosing panencephalitis (caused by defective **Measles virus**).
	- Progressive rubella panencephalitis (caused by **Rubella virus**).
	- Visna virus encephalitis (in sheep).
	- Borna virus disease (human infection not established).
	
	**Note:** The term 'encephalopathy' is a general term for diffuse brain disease, and while it usually refers to non-infective causes, it is used for certain chronic viral infections like slow virus and prion diseases. Prions are not viruses, as they lack nucleic acid.
4. Laboratory diagnosis of Rabies:
	
	**Laboratory Diagnosis of Rabies**
	
	- Laboratory diagnosis provides **supportive evidence** for confirmation. Treatment is often started immediately based on clinical suspicion due to high mortality.
	
	**Specimens**
	
	- Excised tissue bits from the necrotic depths of wounds (more reliable than wound swabs).
	- Hair follicle of the nape of the neck (**most sensitive** for antigen detection).
	- Corneal impression smear.
	- Saliva.
	- Brain tissue (for postmortem diagnosis, histopathology, PCR).
	- CSF (for antibody detection, RNA detection).
	- Skin biopsy.
	
	**Diagnostic Methods**
	
	1. **Rabies Antigen Detection**
	    
	    - **Direct Immunofluorescence Test (direct-IF) / Direct Fluorescent Antibody (DFA) test**.
	    - Detects rabies nucleoprotein antigens using fluorescent-tagged antibodies.
	    - Considered the "**gold standard**" due to high sensitivity and specificity.
	    - Performed on hair follicle at the nape of the neck (best specimen) or corneal impression smear. Corneal smear is less sensitive (30%) and usually positive late.
	2. **Viral Isolation**
	    
	    - **Mouse inoculation:** Intracerebral inoculation into suckling mice causes encephalitis and death; brain examined for Negri bodies and antigen.
	    - **Cell lines:** Mouse neuroblastoma and Baby Hamster Kidney (BHK) cell lines are preferred. Yields virus faster (2–4 days) than mice. Viral growth detected by direct-IF.
	3. **Antibody Detection**
	    
	    - Detection of **CSF antibodies is more significant** than serum antibodies.
	        - Serum antibodies appear late and can be due to vaccination.
	        - CSF antibodies appear early and are produced _only_ in rabies-infected individuals, not post-vaccination.
	    - Tests include Mouse Neutralization Test (MNT), Rapid Fluorescent Focus Inhibition Test (RFFIT), Fluorescent Antibody Virus Neutralization test (FAVN), and Indirect Fluorescence Assay (IFA).
	4. **Viral RNA Detection**
	    
	    - **Reverse Transcription-Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR):** Amplifies genes of rabies virus (e.g., nucleoprotein or large structural protein).
	    - Performed on brain tissue, saliva, skin biopsy samples.
	    - It is the **most sensitive and specific assay** available at present for diagnosis.
	5. **Negri Body Detection**
	    
	    - Useful to confirm **postmortem diagnosis** of rabies.
	    - **Intracytoplasmic eosinophilic inclusions** composed of viral proteins and RNA.
	    - Commonly found in Purkinje cells of the cerebellum and pyramidal neurons of the hippocampus.
	    - Detected in brain tissue using histological stains (H&E, Sellers stains) or immunohistochemistry.
	    - **Immunohistochemistry is more sensitive and specific** than histological staining.
	    - Negri body detection is **pathognomonic** of rabies.
	    - **However, may not be detected in 20% of cases**; absence does not rule out the diagnosis.
5. Postexposure prophylaxis of rabies
	**Rabies Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP)**
	
	- **PEP** is a crucial measure to prevent human rabies after exposure to a potentially rabid animal. It is administered during the early incubation period.
	- It provides **prophylactic measures** to prevent rabies.
	- **Components of PEP** (WHO Guideline 2018): PEP consists of three components:
	    1. **Local wound care**.
	    2. **Rabies vaccine**.
	    3. **Rabies immunoglobulin (RIG)**.
	- Inclusion of these components depends on the **risk of exposure**, classified by WHO into three categories.
	
	**Local Wound Care**
	
	- Wash all bite wounds and scratches thoroughly with **soap and water for 15 minutes**.
	- Use antiseptics like **povidone iodine or alcohol** to inactivate residual viruses.
	- **Avoid suturing** if possible; deeper wounds requiring suturing should be sutured loosely and only after RIG infiltration.
	- Debridement of devitalized tissues.
	- Tetanus prophylaxis.
	- Antibiotic treatment to prevent secondary bacterial infection.
	- Do **not** touch the wound(s) with bare hands.
	- Do **not** apply irritants like soil, oil, lime, herbs, or chalk.
	
	**Rabies Vaccine**
	
	- A series of rabies vaccine injections should be administered **promptly** after exposure.
	- **Type of vaccines**: **Cell line derived non-neural vaccines** are recommended (Purified chick embryo cell (PCEC), Purified Vero cell (PVC), Human diploid cell (HDC) vaccine). Older neural vaccines derived from animal brain are no longer in use.
	- **Routes**: Can be administered by **intradermal (ID)** or **intramuscular (IM)** routes.
	- **Site**: Preferred site is the **deltoid area** for adults and the anterolateral area of the thigh for children < 2 years; **never** in the gluteal region.
	- **Schedule**: Varies based on route (examples provided, but refer to current guidelines).
	    - ID: 2-site ID vaccine on days 0, 3, 7, 28.
	    - IM: 1-site IM vaccine on days 0, 3, 7, 14, 28.
	    - IM (Accelerated): 2-site IM on day 0, 1-site IM on days 7 and 21. (Note: The specific schedules may vary slightly in different guidelines; the principle is multiple doses over a defined period).
	- Changes in vaccine product or route during the course are acceptable if unavoidable.
	- If a dose is delayed, **resume** the regimen (do not restart).
	- **Vaccine may be discontinued** if the animal (dogs and cats) remains healthy for 10 days after the bite. For other animals, the tissue should be examined for rabies antigen/Negri body.
	
	**Rabies Immunoglobulin (RIG)**
	
	- Provides **passive immunization** by neutralizing the virus.
	- **Timing**: Administered **only once**, as soon as possible, and **not beyond day 7** after the first vaccine dose. Vaccines should never be withheld regardless of RIG availability.
	- **Preparations**: Human RIG (hRIG) and equine RIG (eRIG) are available and effective.
	- **Dose**: Maximum dose is 20 IU (hRIG) or 40 IU (eRIG) per kg body weight; there is no minimum dose.
	- **Administration**: Infiltrate **as much as possible into the wound**. The leftover dose is not necessarily administered IM at a different site.
	- When correctly administered, RIG neutralizes the virus at the wound site within a few hours.
	
	**WHO Risk Categorization & Recommended Prophylaxis**
	
	- **Category I (No risk)**:
	    - Exposure: Touching or feeding an animal, licks on intact skin.
	    - Recommendation: **No treatment needed** if history is reliable.
	- **Category II (Minor risk)**:
	    - Exposure: Minor scratches or abrasions without bleeding, nibbling of uncovered skin.
	    - Recommendation: **Wound management + Rabies vaccine**. RIG is **not** required, except for immunodeficient individuals who need RIG in addition. Observe the dog for 10 days.
	- **Category III (Major risk)**:
	    - Exposure: Single or multiple transdermal bites with bleeding, licks on broken skin (fresh wounds) or mucous membrane, direct contact with bats or wild animals.
	    - Recommendation: **Wound management + Rabies immunoglobulin + Rabies vaccine**. Observe the dog (if applicable and safe) for 10 days.
	
	**Important Notes**
	
	- In India, PEP is indicated following exposure to any animal bite **except rodents and bat bite**.
	- Human-to-human transmission is rare, but PEP is offered as a precautionary measure following exposure.
	- Immunodeficient individuals must receive both Ig and vaccine for Category II and III exposures and should be checked for serum antibody after 14 days of vaccination if possible.
	- There are **no contraindications to PEP**, including in pregnancy, lactation, infancy, old age, or in association with concurrent illness.
