1. Monoclonal antibody-principles production and its applications
	
	 
	
	### Monoclonal Antibodies (mAbs)
	
	**I. Definition & Principle**
	
	- **Definition**: Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) are **antibodies derived from a single clone of plasma cells**. This means they all have the **same antigen specificity**, being produced against a **single epitope** of an antigen.
	- **Contrast with Polyclonal Antibodies**: When an antigen with multiple epitopes enters the body, each epitope can stimulate a different B cell clone, leading to a mixture of antibodies (polyclonal). In contrast, mAbs originate from stimulating only one B cell clone by a single antigen epitope.
	
	**II. Production: Hybridoma Technique** The hybridoma technique was developed by G. Kohler and C. Milstein in 1975, for which they received the Nobel Prize in 1984.
	
	**A. Principle**
	
	- A **clone of B cells** (stimulated against a single epitope) is **fused with an immortal cell** (e.g., myeloma cell).
	- This fusion produces a **hybridoma cell** with two key properties:
	    1. **Produces monoclonal antibody** of the same antigen specificity (from B cell component).
	    2. **Multiplies indefinitely** (from immortal myeloma cell component).
	
	**B. Procedure**
	
	1. **Immunization & B Cell Collection**: A mouse is injected with the desired antigen. Splenic B cells, activated against the epitope, are then obtained.
	2. **Myeloma Cells**: **Cancerous plasma cells (myeloma cells)** are used for their immortal property.
	    - They are **genetically modified** (double mutated) to lose their ability to produce their own antibodies but retain immortality.
	3. **Fusion**: Mouse splenic B cells and mutated myeloma cells are fused using **polyethylene glycol**. This creates:
	    - Unfused myeloma cells.
	    - Unfused mouse splenic B cells.
	    - Fused hybridoma cells.
	4. **Purification/Selection on HAT Medium**: The cells are subcultured in a **HAT (Hypoxanthine-Aminopterin-Thymidine) medium** to select for hybridoma cells.
	    - Unfused splenic B cells: Die off as they are not immortal.
	    - Unfused myeloma cells: Cannot grow because they lack the HGPRT enzyme needed for purine synthesis via the salvage pathway (which HAT medium relies on).
	    - **Hybridoma cells**: Can grow and survive indefinitely.
	5. **Selection of Individual Hybridoma Cells**: The medium is diluted into multi-well plates (e.g., one cell per well) to obtain single clones. Hybridoma cells producing the desired mAb are identified (e.g., by radioimmunoassay or ELISA) and selectively proliferated.
	6. **Maintenance**: Selected hybridoma cells can be maintained by:
	    - **In vitro culture**: Producing pure mAbs at 10–60 µg/mL.
	    - **In vivo injection**: Injecting into the peritoneal cavity of a mouse to produce mAbs in ascitic fluid at 1–10 mg/mL (may require purification).
	
	**C. Types of Monoclonal Antibodies (Recombinant Technology)** Modifications are made to reduce the **Human Anti-Mouse Antibody (HAMA)** response, which can cause rapid elimination of foreign mAbs from the body.
	
	- **Mouse mAb**: 100% mouse-derived proteins. Suffix: `-umab` or `-onab`.
	- **Chimeric mAb**: 34% mouse proteins (variable region) + 66% human proteins (constant region). Suffix: `-ximab`.
	- **Humanized mAb**: Only the antigen-binding site (Complementarity Determining Region - CDR) is mouse-derived (10%), with the rest being human. Suffix: `-zumab`.
	- **Human mAb**: 100% human-derived amino acids. Best accepted in humans. Suffix: `-mumab`.
	
	**III. Applications of Monoclonal Antibodies**
	
	**A. Diagnostic Uses**
	
	- **Isolation & Purification**: Used to purify individual molecules (e.g., interferon, coagulation factor VIII).
	- **Identification of Cells & Clones**: For identifying specific cell types (e.g., **CD4 and CD8 T cells** using anti-CD4 and anti-CD8 mAbs).
	- **Diagnostic Reagents**: Widely used in antigen detection kits (e.g., **ELISA, rapid tests**), such as:
	    - Detection of **Hepatitis B surface antigen**.
	    - **Serogrouping of streptococci**.
	    - HIV diagnostic kits.
	
	**B. Therapeutic Uses & Immunoprophylaxis**
	
	- **Passive Immunity**: Administered for **post-exposure prophylaxis** against various infections, providing immediate, but temporary, protection. Examples include immunoglobulins against:
	    - **Hepatitis B**.
	    - **Rabies**.
	    - **Tetanus**.
	- **Therapeutic Agents**: Used in the treatment of a wide range of conditions, including:
	    - **Inflammatory and Allergic Diseases**:
	        - **Adalimumab** and **Infliximab** (target TNF-α) for **Rheumatoid Arthritis, Crohn's disease**.
	        - **Omalizumab** (target IgE) for **Asthma**.
	        - **Daclizumab** (target IL-2 receptor) for **rejection of kidney transplants**.
	    - **Cancers**:
	        - **Trastuzumab** (target HER-2) for **Breast Cancer**.
	        - **Rituximab** and **Tositumomab** (target CD20) for **Lymphoma, CLL**.
	        - **Bevacizumab** (target VEGF) for **Colorectal Cancers** (inhibits angiogenesis).
	    - **Immunotoxins**: mAbs conjugated with bacterial/chemical toxins (e.g., diphtheria toxin) to selectively kill target cells like cancer cells.
	- **Enzymes (Abzymes)**: Monoclonal antibodies with catalytic activity.

	### Immunoglobulin M (IgM)
	
	**I. Diagrammatic Representation**
	
	- In its **secreted form**, IgM is typically represented as a **pentamer**, comprising five monomeric IgM units joined together.
	- A **J chain** joins these five monomeric units to form the complete pentamer [11, Fig. 11.5A].
	- When present as a **membrane-bound antibody** on B cells, it exists in a **monomeric form**.
	
	**II. Key Characteristics & Structure**
	
	- **Molecular Weight & Sedimentation**: Among all immunoglobulins (Ig), IgM has the **highest molecular weight** (900 kDa) and maximum sedimentation coefficient (19S).
	- **Heavy Chain**: It possesses **µ (mu) heavy chains**.
	    - These µ heavy chains have **four constant domains (C$_{H}$1 to C$_{H}$4)** [11, Fig. 11.3B].
	    - Unlike IgG, IgA, and IgD, the µ heavy chain **does not have a hinge region**; instead, it has an additional constant domain.
	- **Valency**: Due to its pentameric structure, secreted IgM has **10 Fab regions and 10 valencies**. The monomeric B cell receptor form has a valency of 2.
	- **Distribution**: Primarily present in the **intravascular compartment**; it is not found in body fluids or secretions in significant amounts (80% intravascular distribution).
	- **Half-life**: It has a relatively **short half-life of 5 days**.
	
	**III. Functions & Clinical Relevance**
	
	1. **Acute Infection Marker**: IgM is the **first antibody produced following an infection**, making it a key indicator of **acute or recent infection** (primary immune response antibody).
	    - _Clinical Relevance_: Detection of IgM antibodies is crucial in diagnosing acute phases of various infections (e.g., dengue, Japanese encephalitis, West Nile virus, scrub typhus, leptospirosis, toxoplasmosis).
	2. **Potent Complement Activator**: It is the **most potent activator of the classical complement pathway** due to the presence of multiple complement binding sites (5 Fc regions) in its pentameric form.
	    - _Mechanism_: Its binding to antigen triggers the classical pathway, leading to the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC), which causes lysis of target cells.
	3. **B Cell Receptor**: It is present on the B cell surface in monomeric form and serves as a **B cell receptor for antigen binding**.
	4. **Opsonisation**: IgM acts as an **opsonin**, binding to antigens, which facilitates their recognition and removal by phagocytes. It is 500–1000 times more potent in opsonisation than IgG.
	5. **Fetal Immunity**: It is the **first antibody synthesized in fetal life** (around 20 weeks of gestation).
	    - _Clinical Relevance_: Its presence in a fetus or newborn indicates an **intrauterine infection**, as IgM **cannot cross the placenta**. This is useful in diagnosing congenital infections (e.g., TORCH infections).
	6. **Protection Against Intravascular Organisms**: Being primarily intravascular, IgM is responsible for protection against blood invasion by microorganisms. IgM deficiency is often associated with septicaemia.
	7. **Agglutination**: IgM is about **20 times more effective in bacterial agglutination** than IgG. This high avidity (due to multivalency) compensates for its lower individual affinity compared to IgG.
	8. **Avidity**: While its initial affinity is low, IgM's multivalency provides high avidity, allowing for effective antigen binding.
