1. Autoimmunity
	**Autoimmunity** is a condition where the body's own **immunologically competent cells** or **antibodies** act against its **self-antigens**, leading to structural or functional damage. Paul Ehrlich first introduced this concept, terming it "**horror autotoxicus**". Normally, the immune system avoids reacting to self-antigens through a protective mechanism called **immunological tolerance**. Any breach in these tolerance mechanisms can predispose an individual to autoimmune diseases.
	
	**I. Immunological Tolerance** The immune system employs both central and peripheral tolerance mechanisms to prevent self-reactivity:
	
	- **Central Tolerance**: Develops during lymphocyte maturation in primary lymphoid organs (bone marrow for B cells, thymus for T cells).
	    - **B Cells (Bone Marrow/Spleen)**: Immature B cells encountering self-antigens can undergo **receptor editing** (rearrangement of Ig genes to produce a different B cell receptor that no longer recognizes the self-antigen) or **negative selection** (apoptosis of self-reacting immature B cells).
	    - **T Cells (Thymus)**: Developing T cells undergo **negative selection**, where those that react strongly to self-antigens are eliminated by apoptosis.
	- **Peripheral Tolerance**: Deals with self-reactive lymphocytes that escape central tolerance and enter peripheral lymphoid organs.
	    - **Sequestration of Self-Antigens**: Certain self-antigens (e.g., from corneal proteins, testicular antigens, brain antigens) are "hidden" from the immune system in immunologically privileged sites.
	    - **Anergy, Phenotypic Skewing, Apoptosis**: Mechanisms by which self-reactive cells in the periphery become non-responsive or are eliminated.
	    - **Regulatory T (Treg) Cells**: These cells play a crucial role in suppressing self-reactive lymphocytes.
	
	**II. Mechanisms of Autoimmunity (Breakdown of Tolerance)** Autoimmunity arises from the failure of one or more of these tolerance mechanisms. Key mechanisms include:
	
	- **Loss of Regulatory T (Treg) Cells**: Impaired function or absence of Treg cells can lead to uncontrolled self-reactive lymphocyte activity.
	- **Providing T Cell Help to Stimulate Self-Reacting B Cells**: Antibody responses to self-antigens can occur when self-reactive B cells receive help from T cells. For example, drug-induced alterations in red blood cell (RBC) surfaces can create antigens recognized by helper T cells, leading to autoimmune hemolytic anemia.
	- **Molecular Mimicry**: Microorganisms may possess antigenic determinants (epitopes) that resemble self-antigens. An immune response against these microbial antigens can then cross-react with host self-antigens, causing tissue damage. A classic example is **acute rheumatic fever**, where antibodies against streptococcal M protein cross-react with cardiac antigens.
	- **Genetic Predisposition**: Host genetic factors, particularly **HLA alleles** (MHC molecules), are strongly associated with increased susceptibility to certain autoimmune diseases (e.g., **HLA B27** with ankylosing spondylitis, carrying a 90 times higher risk).
	
	**III. Classification and Clinical Examples of Autoimmune Diseases** Autoimmune diseases can be broadly classified based on their target:
	
	- **Single Organ or Cell Type Autoimmune Diseases**:
	    
	    - **Autoimmune Anemias**:
	        - **Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia**: Autoantibodies target RBC membrane proteins, triggering complement-mediated lysis or antibody-mediated opsonization.
	        - **Pernicious Anemia**: Autoantibodies target gastric parietal cells (leading to vitamin B12 deficiency) or intrinsic factor.
	    - **Graves’ Disease**: Autoantibodies stimulate the thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) receptor, leading to hyperthyroidism.
	    - **Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis**: Autoantibodies target thyroid peroxidase and thyroglobulin, causing hypothyroidism.
	    - **Myasthenia Gravis**: Autoantibodies bind to acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction, impairing nerve impulse transmission and causing muscle weakness. (This is an example of Antibody-dependent cellular dysfunction or ADCD).
	    - **Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus**: Autoantibodies and T cells destroy pancreatic beta cells, leading to insulin deficiency.
	    - **Goodpasture Syndrome**: Autoantibodies bind to basement membrane antigens in the kidney glomeruli and lung alveoli.
	- **Systemic Autoimmune Diseases**:
	    
	    - **Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)**: Autoantibodies target nuclear antigens (e.g., DNA, histones) and other cellular components, affecting multiple organs like skin, joints, kidney, and serosa.
	    - **Rheumatoid Arthritis**: Autoantibodies, including **rheumatoid factor** (an antibody against the Fc region of IgG) and anti-citrullinated peptide antibodies (ACPA), cause chronic inflammation of synovial joints.
	    - **Sjögren Syndrome**: Autoantibodies against ribonucleoprotein (RNP) antigens (SS-A (Ro) and SS-B (La)) target salivary and lacrimal glands, causing dry eyes and mouth.
	    - **Scleroderma (Systemic Sclerosis)**: Autoantibodies against nuclear antigens (e.g., DNA topoisomerase, centromere) lead to excessive fibrosis of the skin and internal organs.
	    - **Multiple Sclerosis**: Self-reactive T cells produce inflammatory lesions in the brain, destroying the myelin sheath of nerve fibers, leading to neurological dysfunction.
	
	**IV. Laboratory Diagnosis of Autoimmune Diseases** Diagnosis primarily involves detecting **autoantibodies** in patient serum:
	
	- **General Methods**:
	    - **ELISA** (Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay): Widely used for detecting specific autoantibodies.
	    - **Immunofluorescence Assays (IFA)**: Used to detect antibodies bound to cell surface antigens or other cellular components.
	    - **Western Blot**: Offers excellent specificity and is used as a confirmatory test for autoantibodies (e.g., in HIV, Lyme's disease, but also for detecting antibodies to tissue components).
	- **Specific Tests for Selected Autoimmune Diseases**:
	    - **Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia**: **Coombs test** (direct or indirect antiglobulin test) detects IgG autoantibodies on RBCs, leading to agglutination.
	    - **Goodpasture Syndrome**: Biopsies (e.g., kidney) stained with fluorescent-labeled anti-IgG and anti-C3b reveal **linear deposits** along basement membranes.
	    - **Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)**:
	        - **Lupus Band Test**: A direct immunofluorescence test that detects deposits of immunoglobulins and complement proteins in the patient's skin.
	        - **LE Cell Test**: Previously used, but now largely obsolete due to low sensitivity (positive in only 50-75% of cases).
	    - **Rheumatoid Arthritis**: Detection of **rheumatoid factor (RF)** and **anti-citrullinated peptide antibodies (ACPA)**.
	1. Mechanism of autoimmune disease
	In the context of medical school exams, understanding the mechanisms of autoimmune diseases is crucial for both diagnosis and management. Autoimmunity fundamentally arises from a **breakdown in the body's natural immunological tolerance**.
	
	### Mechanisms of Autoimmune Disease
	
	**I. Core Concept: Breakdown of Immunological Tolerance**
	
	- **Autoimmunity** occurs when the body's own **immunologically competent cells** or **antibodies** act against its **self-antigens**, leading to tissue damage.
	- Normally, the immune system avoids self-reactivity through **immunological tolerance**. A breach in these protective mechanisms predisposes to autoimmune diseases.
	
	**II. Immunological Tolerance (Brief Overview)** To appreciate the breakdown, a quick review of normal tolerance:
	
	- **Central Tolerance**: Occurs during lymphocyte maturation (thymus for T cells, bone marrow for B cells).
	    - **T Cells**: Self-reactive T cells are eliminated by **negative selection (apoptosis)** in the thymus.
	    - **B Cells**: Immature B cells encountering self-antigens undergo **receptor editing** or **negative selection (apoptosis)**.
	- **Peripheral Tolerance**: Addresses self-reactive lymphocytes that escape central tolerance and enter peripheral tissues.
	    - **Anergy**: Lymphocytes become non-responsive to self-antigens.
	    - **Phenotypic Skewing**: Self-reactive T cells secrete non-pathogenic cytokines.
	    - **Apoptosis (Activation-Induced Cell Death - AICD)**: Self-reactive T cells are eliminated via Fas-Fas ligand interaction.
	    - **Regulatory T (Treg) Cells**: Suppress self-reactive lymphocytes.
	    - **Sequestration of Self-Antigens**: Certain antigens are "hidden" from the immune system in immunologically privileged sites (e.g., corneal proteins, testicular antigens, brain antigens).
	
	**III. Specific Mechanisms of Autoimmunity (How Tolerance is Broken)**
	
	1. **Loss/Failure of Regulatory T (Treg) Cells**:
	    
	    - Impaired function or absence of **Treg cells** leads to uncontrolled activity of self-reactive lymphocytes, a key mechanism of autoimmunity.
	2. **Breakdown of T Cell Anergy**:
	    
	    - Normally non-immunogenic **self-antigens** may become immunogenic if their **APCs** (antigen-presenting cells) are induced to express **costimulatory molecules (B7)**, which are usually absent on normal cells. This can occur during **tissue necrosis** or **local inflammation**.
	    - **Clinical Relevance**: Postulated mechanism in diseases like **Multiple Sclerosis**, **Rheumatoid Arthritis**, and **Psoriasis**.
	3. **Failure of Activation-Induced Cell Death (AICD)**:
	    
	    - Self-reactive activated T cells fail to undergo programmed cell death (apoptosis) via the **Fas-Fas ligand pathway**.
	    - **Clinical Relevance**: Observed in patients with **Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)**.
	4. **Providing T Cell Help to Self-Reactive B Cells**:
	    
	    - Antibody responses to self-antigens can occur when self-reactive B cells receive **help from T cells**.
	    - **Clinical Example**: In **Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia**, certain drugs can alter **red blood cell (RBC) surfaces**, creating new antigens that are recognized by **helper T cells**. These T cells then help self-reactive B cells to produce autoantibodies against RBCs.
	5. **Molecular Mimicry**:
	    
	    - Microorganisms may possess **antigenic determinants (epitopes)** that resemble **self-antigens**. An immune response against these microbial antigens can then **cross-react with host self-antigens**, causing tissue damage.
	    - **Clinical Example**: **Acute Rheumatic Fever**, where antibodies formed against **streptococcal M protein** cross-react with **cardiac antigens** due to antigenic similarity.
	6. **Genetic Predisposition (e.g., HLA Association)**:
	    
	    - Host **genetic factors**, particularly specific **HLA alleles** (MHC molecules), are strongly associated with increased susceptibility to certain autoimmune diseases. While not a direct mechanism of tolerance breakdown, they **predispose** to such a breakdown.
	    - **High-Yield Examples**:
	        - **HLA B27**: Highly associated with **Ankylosing Spondylitis** (90 times higher risk) and Reiter's Syndrome (now Reactive Arthritis).
	        - **HLA DR-2**: Associated with **Multiple Sclerosis** and **Goodpasture's Syndrome**.
	        - **HLA DR-3**: Associated with **Myasthenia Gravis** and **Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)**.
	        - **HLA DR-4**: Associated with **Rheumatoid Arthritis**.
	        - **HLA DR-3/DR-4**: Associated with **Insulin-dependent Diabetes Mellitus**.
	7. **Release of Sequestered Antigens**:
	    
	    - Antigens normally hidden in immunologically privileged sites (e.g., lens antigens of the eye) can become exposed due to trauma or infection, triggering an immune response. (This is implicit from the 'sequestration of self-antigen' mechanism in peripheral tolerance).
	
	By understanding these mechanisms, clinicians can better appreciate the pathogenesis of autoimmune conditions and consider targeted therapies.
