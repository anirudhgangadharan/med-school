1. Mr. Michel, a 25-year-old Australian visited his local doctor complaining of difficulty in swallowing liquids, loss of appetite and restlessness. He had a travel history to India one month back and did mention being bitten by a street dog in Puducherry.
	
	a. What is the probable diagnosis of this clinical condition? (1)  
	b. Describe the pathogenesis and clinical manifestations of this condition. (3)  
	c. Describe the laboratory diagnosis of the condition. (4)  
	d. Add a note on vaccination to prevent this condition. (2)
	**Clinical Case: Mr. Michel (Rabies)**
	
	**a. Probable Clinical Diagnosis** Based on Mr. Michel's symptoms of **difficulty in swallowing liquids (hydrophobia)**, **loss of appetite**, **restlessness**, and a history of a **street dog bite** in India one month prior, the probable clinical diagnosis is **Rabies**.
	
	**b. Pathogenesis and Clinical Manifestations** Rabies is an acute, almost invariably fatal, viral encephalomyelitis.
	
	- **Pathogenesis**: The rabies virus spreads from the site of the bite to the Central Nervous System (CNS) via a **neuronal route**.
	- **Clinical Manifestations**: The clinical spectrum is typically divided into three phases:
	    1. **Prodromal Phase**: Lasts for 2–10 days. Characterized by non-specific symptoms such as **fever, malaise, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, photophobia, sore throat, and abnormal sensation (paresthesia, pain, or pruritus) around the wound site**.
	    2. **Acute Neurologic Phase**: This phase may be either encephalitic (furious) or paralytic (dumb).
	        - **Encephalitic (Furious) Rabies** (80% of cases): Lasts for 2–7 days. Key features include **hyperexcitability (anxiety, agitation, hyperactivity, bizarre behavior, hallucinations)**, followed by periods of lucidity. **Autonomic (sympathetic) dysfunction** manifests as increased lacrimation, salivation (**leading to foaming at the mouth**), perspiration, gooseflesh, cardiac arrhythmia, and priapism. The most characteristic symptoms are **hydrophobia (fear of water)** and **aerophobia (fear of air)**, which are involuntary, painful spasms of the respiratory, laryngeal, and pharyngeal muscles precipitated by attempts to swallow or by air currents.
	        - **Paralytic (Dumb) Rabies** (20% of cases): Characterized by **flaccid paralysis**, often beginning in the bitten limb and progressing to quadriparesis with facial paralysis. Hydrophobia and other encephalitic features are typically absent.
	    3. **Coma and Death**: Following the acute neurological phase, the patient progresses to coma, which invariably leads to death, usually within 14 days. Patients with paralytic rabies may survive slightly longer, up to 30 days, but **recovery and survival are extremely rare**.
	
	**c. Laboratory Diagnosis** Laboratory diagnosis is crucial for confirming rabies, although treatment should be initiated based on clinical suspicion due to the disease's high mortality.
	
	- **Gold Standard Test**: **Direct Fluorescent Antibody (DFA) test** on clinical specimens.
	    - **Specimens**: The **hair follicles at the nape of the neck** are considered the most sensitive specimen. A **corneal impression smear** can also be used, typically positive in the late stages with about 30% sensitivity.
	- **Viral Isolation**:
	    - **Mouse Inoculation**: Intracerebral inoculation into suckling mice can induce encephalitis and death. Brain biopsies from these animals are then examined for **Negri bodies** and rabies antigen.
	    - **Cell Lines**: Mouse neuroblastoma and BHK cell lines can also be used for viral isolation.
	- **Molecular Methods**: **Reverse-transcriptase PCR (RT-PCR)** can detect rabies viral RNA.
	- **Histopathological Examination**: Postmortem diagnosis can be made by detecting characteristic **Negri bodies** (pathognomonic inclusion bodies) in histopathological staining of brain tissue. However, Negri bodies may not be detected in 20% of cases, so their absence does not rule out the diagnosis.
	- **Antibody Detection**: While serology can detect antibodies, it is generally less direct for acute diagnosis in symptomatic patients.
	
	**d. Vaccination to Prevent Rabies** Prevention of rabies primarily relies on **post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP)** after a suspected exposure.
	
	- **Components of PEP**:
	    1. **Local Wound Management**: The wound should be **immediately and thoroughly washed with soap and water**.
	    2. **Rabies Immunoglobulin (RIG)**: Provides **passive immunity** and should be administered as soon as possible after exposure, infiltrating as much as possible directly into and around the wound site.
	    3. **Rabies Vaccine**: Provides **active immunity**.
	- **Types of Vaccines (Commercially available for human use)**:
	    - **Purified Chick Embryo Cell Vaccine (PCEC)**.
	    - **Human Diploid Cell Vaccine**.
	    - **Vero Continuous Cell Vaccine**.
	    - Rabies vaccines are prepared from a **fixed virus**.
	- **Vaccine Administration**:
	    - The vaccine can be administered via **intramuscular or intradermal** routes.
	    - Preferred sites are the **deltoid area for adults** and the **anterolateral area of the thigh for children (<2 years)**.
	    - It is crucial that the vaccine is **never administered in the gluteal region**.
	- **Indications**: In India, PEP is indicated following exposure to **any animal bite except rodents and bat bite**. Mr. Michel's case (dog bite) would be classified as a Category III exposure (major risk), which necessitates wound management, rabies immunoglobulin, and rabies vaccine.
	- **Discontinuation of Vaccine**: The vaccine course may be discontinued if the biting animal (dogs and cats) remains healthy after 10 days of observation.

2. Enumerate the agents of atypical pneumonia. Describe the pathogenesis, clinical manifestations and laboratory diagnosis of any one of them. _(2+2+2+4)_
	**Atypical Pneumonia**
	
	Atypical pneumonia refers to an infection primarily affecting the **interstitial space of the lungs**, differing from typical lobar pneumonia, which involves the alveoli. Clinically, it is often characterised by a **non-productive cough**. The antibiotics used for its treatment generally differ from those for typical pneumonia.
	
	**1. Agents of Atypical Pneumonia:**
	
	- **Bacterial Agents**:
	    - _**Mycoplasma pneumoniae**_
	    - _Chlamydophila pneumoniae_
	    - _Legionella species_ (e.g., _Legionella pneumophila_)
	    - Less common: _Coxiella burnetii_ (Q fever), _Francisella tularensis_ (pulmonary tularemia), and _Orientia tsutsugamushi_ (scrub typhus).
	- **Viral Agents**:
	    - **Influenza viruses**
	    - **Coronaviruses** (e.g., SARS-CoV-2 causing COVID-19)
	    - **Respiratory Syncytial Virus** (RSV)
	    - Others: Epstein-Barr virus, Adenoviruses.
	- **Fungal Agents** (more common in immunocompromised individuals):
	    - _**Pneumocystis jirovecii**_ (causes Pneumocystis pneumonia, PCP)
	    - _Aspergillus_ species
	    - Agents of **systemic mycoses**: _Histoplasma capsulatum_, _Blastomyces dermatitidis_, _Coccidioides immitis_, _Paracoccidioides brasiliensis_.	
	**2. Pathogenesis, Clinical Manifestations, and Laboratory Diagnosis of _Mycoplasma pneumoniae_**
	
	_**Mycoplasma pneumoniae**_ is a common bacterial agent causing atypical pneumonia, often referred to as "walking pneumonia" due to milder symptoms.
	
	- **Pathogenesis:**
	    
	    - The **most important step** in pathogenesis is the **attachment of _M. pneumoniae_ to the respiratory mucosa**.
	    - This attachment is mediated by its membrane-bound **adhesion proteins**, such as **cytadhesin P1 protein**.
	    - Following attachment, the organism **induces injury to the host respiratory tissue**.
	- **Clinical Manifestations:**
	    
	    - **Epidemiology**: Transmitted by **respiratory droplets**, especially in close contacts (e.g., families, military bases). Infections are often endemic with periodic epidemics every 4–7 years.
	    - **Incubation period**: Approximately **2–4 weeks**.
	    - **Upper Respiratory Tract Infections (URTI)**: _M. pneumoniae_ can cause pharyngitis, tracheobronchitis, or rarely otitis media. URTI symptoms are 20 times more common than pneumonia.
	    - **Pneumonia**: Causes an "atypical" community-acquired interstitial pneumonia. It is also known as Eaton agent pneumonia or primary atypical pneumonia.
	        - Onset is usually **gradual**.
	        - Characterised by **wheeze or rales, dry cough**, and peribronchial pneumonia with thickened bronchial markings and streaks of interstitial infiltration on **chest X-ray**.
	        - Typically mild and self-limited, but can progress to severe disease with **Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS)**.
	- **Laboratory Diagnosis:**
	    
	    - **Specimen Collection**: Ideal specimens include **throat swabs, nasopharyngeal aspirates, bronchial brushing, bronchoalveolar lavages (BAL), and lung biopsies**.
	        - Specimens must be placed immediately into transport media (e.g., standard Mycoplasma fluid medium or viral transport medium with antibiotics) to prevent drying.
	        - Transport should be immediate; if delayed, store at 4°C for up to 48 hours, or at -70°C for longer periods.
	    - **Culture**:
	        - Inoculated onto **PPLO agar (solid) or PPLO broth (liquid)**.
	        - Incubation is at 37°C for **5–7 days, or sometimes up to 1–3 weeks** due to slow growth.
	        - On solid media, colonies appear very small (200–500 µm) and are described as having a characteristic "**fried egg appearance**".
	        - In liquid media, growth is indicated by turbidity and a color change.
	        - Colonies can be examined using a hand lens or **Dienes' staining**.
	    - **Antigen Detection**: Direct Immunofluorescence (IF) and antigen capture ELISA are available methods.
	    - **Antibody Detection (Serology)**:
	        - **Specific antibody tests**: Indirect IF, latex agglutination assays, and ELISA using protein P1 antigens. Antibodies typically appear about 1 week after illness, peak at 3–6 weeks, and then decline.
	        - **Nonspecific antibody tests**: **Cold agglutination test** and Streptococcus MG test.
	        - Clinical relevance: Serological assays are **less popular** nowadays due to the time required to obtain results from convalescent-phase samples.
	    - **Molecular Methods**:
	        - **PCR** targeting _M. pneumoniae_ specific **16S rRNA gene and P1 adhesion gene** is available, offering high sensitivity and specificity.
	        - **Multiplex PCR** and **real-time PCR** formats are used for quantitative and simultaneous detection of atypical pneumonia agents.
	        - **BioFire FilmArray** is an automated multiplex PCR system that can detect _M. pneumoniae_ from respiratory samples.

3. Enumerate the differences between gonococcal and non-gonococcal urethritis. Write a note on laboratory diagnosis
**Urethritis: Gonococcal vs. Non-Gonococcal**

Urethritis refers to inflammation of the urethra, often manifesting with discharge and dysuria. It is broadly classified based on the presence or absence of _Neisseria gonorrhoeae_.

**1. Differences between Gonococcal and Non-Gonococcal Urethritis (NGU):**

|Features|Gonococcal Urethritis|Non-Gonococcal Urethritis (NGU)|
|:--|:--|:--|
|**Onset**|**Rapid (48 hours)**|**Longer (>1 week)**|
|**Urethral Discharge**|**Purulent** (flow of seed-resembling semen)|**Mucous to mucopurulent**|
|**Complications**|Disseminated Gonococcal Infection (DGI) (polyarthritis and endocarditis)|Water-can perineum, Reiter's syndrome (conjunctivitis, urethritis, arthritis, mucosal lesions)|
|**Diagnosis (Initial)**|Gram stain, Culture on Thayer Martin media|For _Chlamydia_: culture on McCoy and HeLa cell lines; for _Trichomonas_: detection of trophozoite; for _Candida_: detection of budding yeast cells; for PCR: HSV or _Chlamydia_|
|**Treatment (Initial)**|Ceftriaxone|For _Chlamydia_: Doxycycline; for _Trichomonas_: Metronidazole; for _Candida_: Clotrimazole (vaginal cream or tablet)|

**2. Laboratory Diagnosis of Urethritis:**

Laboratory diagnosis aims to identify the causative agent to guide appropriate treatment, as the treatment regimens differ significantly between gonococcal and non-gonococcal etiologies.

**A. Gonococcal Urethritis (caused by _Neisseria gonorrhoeae_)**

- **Specimen Collection:**
    - **Urethral swab in men** and **cervical swab in women** are preferred. Vaginal swabs are not satisfactory.
    - Dacron or rayon swabs are preferred; cotton and alginate swabs can be inhibitory to gonococci.
    - For chronic urethritis with minimal discharge, prostatic massage or collection of the morning drop of secretion may be necessary.
- **Transport:**
    - Specimens should be **transported immediately**.
    - If delay is expected, use charcoal-coated swabs in **Stuart’s transport medium** or Amies medium. Commercial devices like JEMBEC or Gono-Pak systems are available.
- **Microscopy (Gram Staining):**
    - Gram staining of urethral exudates reveals **gram-negative intracellular kidney-shaped diplococci** [782, Fig. 77.7].
    - This method is **highly specific and sensitive in symptomatic men**.
    - However, in females, sensitivity is only ~50% due to the presence of commensal _Neisseria_ species, which can confound interpretation; thus, **culture is recommended for diagnosis in women**.
- **Culture:**
    - Endocervical culture shows 80–90% sensitivity.
    - **Selective media** are preferred due to the presence of normal flora, such as **Thayer Martin medium** (chocolate agar with antibiotics).
    - Blood culture (using automated systems) and synovial fluid cultures should be considered in suspected cases of disseminated gonococcal infection (DGI).
- **Identification:**
    - Gonococci are **catalase and oxidase positive**.
    - They **ferment only glucose**, but not maltose and sucrose.
    - **Automated systems like MALDI-TOF** can be used for species identification.

**B. Non-Gonococcal Urethritis (NGU)**

NGU refers to chronic urethritis where gonococci cannot be demonstrated. It is more common than gonococcal urethritis.

- **Etiological Agents:**
    
    - **Most common:** _**Chlamydia trachomatis**_.
    - **Other bacterial agents:** _Ureaplasma urealyticum_ and _Mycoplasma hominis_.
    - **Viral agents:** Herpes simplex virus (HSV).
    - **Fungal agents:** _Candida albicans_.
    - **Parasitic agents:** _Trichomonas vaginalis_.
- **Laboratory Diagnosis for _**Chlamydia trachomatis**_ (Most Common NGU Agent):**
    
    - **Specimen Collection:**
        - Scrapings or swabs from infected sites must contain cells, as _Chlamydia_ are obligate intracellular bacteria.
        - Recommended specimens: urethral swab for NGU, endocervical swab for cervicitis, conjunctival swabs for ocular infections.
        - **First-catch urine samples in the morning** are preferred for urethritis or cervicitis, as they contain the greatest amount of urethral secretions.
    - **Microscopy:**
        - Chlamydiae are **poorly Gram-stained**.
        - Routine Gram staining often reveals **sterile pyuria** (elevated neutrophils without any visible organisms, including gonococci), suggesting a need for further chlamydial testing.
        - Other stains (Castaneda, Machiavello, Gimenez) can detect cytoplasmic **inclusion bodies**.
        - **Lugol’s iodine stains the inclusion bodies of _C. trachomatis_** due to the presence of glycogen matrix.
    - **Antigen Detection:** Enzyme immunoassays detecting **LPS antigens** are available.
    - **Culture:**
        - Historically, culture was the gold standard.
        - _C. trachomatis_ can be grown on **McCoy or HeLa cell lines**.
        - Viral growth is detected by the presence of inclusions in the cell lines after staining [794, Fig. 77.10].
    - **Molecular Methods (Nucleic Acid Amplification Tests - NAAT):**
        - NAATs (e.g., PCR, real-time PCR) have **revolutionized diagnosis**.
        - They are **highly sensitive and specific**, can detect even few copies of DNA, and can differentiate species and serovars.
        - **NAATs are currently the diagnostic assays of choice** for chlamydial infection, as recommended by the CDC, replacing culture.
    - **Serology:**
        - Complement fixation test (CFT) or ELISA using group-specific LPS antigen.
        - Micro-immunofluorescence (MIF) test detects antibodies against species and serovar-specific MOMP antigen.
- **Laboratory Diagnosis for Other NGU Agents:**
    
    - **_Urogenital Mycoplasmas_ (_Ureaplasma urealyticum_, _Mycoplasma hominis_):** Culture and **PCR** are appropriate diagnostic methods. _Ureaplasma_ forms very tiny colonies (T-form).
    - **_Trichomonas vaginalis_:**
        - **Wet (saline) mount of fresh samples** (within 10–20 minutes) can demonstrate **jerky motile trophozoites** and pus cells; sensitivity is variable but specificity is high.
        - **Culture is the gold standard method** for diagnosis, offering high sensitivity (75–96%) and specificity (100%).
        - Antigen detection (ELISA, DFA) and PCR are also available.
    - **_Candida albicans_:**
        - **Direct microscopy** of vaginal discharge/scrapings reveals **Gram-positive oval budding yeast cells with pseudohyphae**.
        - Culture on Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) produces creamy white and pasty colonies.
        - **Germ tube test is specific for _C. albicans_**. Molecular methods (PCR) are also used.
    - **Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV):**
        - Scrapings from lesions stained with Wright’s, Giemsa (Tzanck preparation), or Papanicolaou stain can detect **multinucleated giant cells with faceted nuclei and ground glass chromatin (Tzanck cells)** and Cowdry type A intranuclear inclusion bodies.
        - **Viral DNA (by PCR)** is highly sensitive and can be detected in scrapings. Multiplex PCR platforms are available. Viral antigen detection by direct immunofluorescence (IF) is also used.

4. Pathogenesis and laboratory diagnosis of COVID-19
	**COVID-19 (Coronavirus Disease 2019)**
	
	COVID-19 is a severe respiratory tract infection caused by **SARS-CoV-2** (Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2), which rapidly spread globally after its emergence in 2019-2020, leading to a catastrophic pandemic.
	
	**1. Pathogenesis**
	
	- **Viral Morphology & Entry**:
	    
	    - SARS-CoV-2 is an **enveloped, spherical virus** (approx. 120 nm), possessing helical symmetry and a positive-sense ssRNA genome.
	    - Its **Spike (S) protein** is crucial for entry, binding to **ACE-2 receptors** on host cells.
	    - ACE-2 receptors are highly expressed on **type-II alveolar cells** in the lungs, epithelial cells of the oral mucosa, and cells of the heart, kidney, endothelium, and intestine.
	- **Transmission**:
	    
	    - Primarily via **respiratory droplets and direct contact**.
	    - **Aerosol transmission** (spread beyond one meter) is possible in specific settings like **aerosol-generating procedures** (e.g., endotracheal intubation).
	    - **Pre-symptomatic transmission** occurs from infected individuals shedding virus 1-3 days before symptom onset, with peak viral loads around symptom onset.
	- **Disease Progression**:
	    
	    - **Initial Stage**: Infection of pharyngeal epithelium leads to **influenza-like illness (ILI)**.
	    - **Pulmonary Involvement**: Damage to **type-II alveolar cells** in the lungs reduces **pulmonary surfactants**, causing alveolar collapse.
	    - **Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS)**: This is the **leading cause of mortality**.
	        - A **dysregulated host immune response** can lead to a **cytokine storm**, with excessive release of cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α).
	        - This causes increased vascular permeability, resulting in **pulmonary edema** (seen as **'ground-glass' opacities on chest CT**) and eventual **lung fibrosis**.
	
	**2. Laboratory Diagnosis**
	
	- **Indications for Testing**:
	    
	    - Patients with **ILI or Severe Acute Respiratory Infection (SARI)** symptoms.
	    - Asymptomatic direct and high-risk contacts of confirmed cases (tested between day 5 and 10 of contact).
	    - History of international travel in the last 14 days.
	- **Specimen Collection and Transport**:
	    
	    - **Preferred specimens**: **Throat (oropharyngeal) and nasal swabs**.
	        - Use **Dacron or polyester flocked swabs** dipped in **viral transport media (VTM)**.
	    - **Alternative specimens**: Nasopharyngeal swab, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), or endotracheal aspirate (for ventilated patients).
	    - **Safety**: **Appropriate PPE** (gloves, gown, N95 respirator, face shield) must be used during collection.
	    - **Biosafety Levels**: NAAT requires BSL-2 facility; viral culture requires BSL-3 facility.
	- **Nucleic Acid Amplification Testing (NAAT)** – **Gold Standard**:
	    
	    - **Real-time Reverse Transcriptase PCR (RT-PCR)**:
	        - **Principle**: Qualitative method amplifying viral RNA, detected by fluorescence.
	        - **Target Genes**: Screening (E, N, M genes) and confirmatory (RdRp, N2 genes) are used.
	        - **Sensitivity**: Can detect viral RNA as early as **day 1 of symptom onset**.
	        - **Turnaround Time**: Approximately **4-5 hours**.
	    - **Automated Formats**: **CBNAAT** (targets E and N2 genes) and **Truenat** (targets E and RdRp genes) are available.
	        - **Advantages**: Fully automated, minimal handling, lower biosafety hazard.
	        - **Disadvantages**: Suitable for lower sample loads (1-4 samples per run).
	- **Antigen Detection**:
	    
	    - **Principle**: Rapid chromatographic immunoassay detecting **nucleocapsid protein antigen**.
	    - **Specimen**: Nasopharyngeal swab.
	    - **Performance**: **Highly specific (99-100%)**, but **moderately sensitive (50-84%)**.
	    - **Clinical Relevance**: A **point-of-care test** with results within one hour. **Symptomatic patients with negative antigen tests should be confirmed by real-time RT-PCR**.
	- **Antibody (IgG) Detection**:
	    
	    - **Timing**: IgG antibodies typically appear **after two weeks of onset** and persist for several months.
	    - **Utility**: **NOT used for clinical diagnosis** of active infection. Primarily useful for **serosurveillance** and surveys in high-risk populations.
	    - **Formats**: ELISA, chemiluminescence, and immunochromatographic test (ICT) formats are available.
	- **Other Methods**:
	    
	    - **Sequencing**: Used for initial viral identification and determining **mutations** in the viral genome, not for routine diagnosis.
	    - **Viral Culture**: Primarily for **research purposes** (e.g., understanding viral properties, vaccine development), **not recommended for routine diagnostic use** due to time, cost, and biosafety requirements.
	    - **Non-specific Tests**:
	        - **Radiology**: Chest CT scan often shows **ground-glass appearance** and/or consolidation.
	        - **Biomarkers**: Elevated IL-6, D-dimer, serum ferritin, severe lymphopenia, and elevated C-reactive protein can indicate severe inflammation and are used as prognostic markers.
5. Enumerate the various stages of diagnosis of human plague
	**Diagnosis of Human Plague**
	
	Plague, caused by _Yersinia pestis_, is a fulminant systemic zoonosis. It is classified as a **Category A bioterrorism agent** due to its highly infectious nature. Early diagnosis and prompt antibiotic treatment are crucial to reduce the high mortality rates (up to 50% if untreated).
	
	**Stages of Laboratory Diagnosis:**
	
	**1. Specimen Collection**:
	
	- **Clinical Relevance**: Specimen type depends on the clinical form of plague. **Safety precautions at Biosafety Level III (BSL-3)** are essential due to the high risk of laboratory-acquired infection.
	- **Bubonic Plague**: Pus or fluid aspirated from **buboes** (swollen lymph nodes).
	- **Pneumonic Plague**: **Sputum and blood**.
	- **Septicemic Plague**: **Blood** and splenic aspirate (post-mortem).
	- **Transport**: If transport delay is expected, **Cary–Blair medium** can be used.
	
	**2. Direct Microscopy**:
	
	- **Gram Staining**: Reveals **pus cells** and **gram-negative oval coccobacilli with rounded ends**, often surrounded by a capsule.
	- **Wayson Stain or Methylene Blue Staining**: Demonstrates bacilli with a typical **bipolar or "safety pin" appearance**, where the two ends are darkly stained with a clear central area [493, 499f].
	
	**3. Culture**:
	
	- _Y. pestis_ is **aerobic and facultatively anaerobic**.
	- **Blood Agar**: Colonies are **non-hemolytic** and **dark brown pigmented** due to hemin absorption.
	- **MacConkey Agar**: Forms **lactose non-fermenting, colorless colonies**.
	- **Culture Smear**: Gram staining shows **pleomorphism** (coccid, coccobacillary, bacillary, filamentous, and giant forms); involution forms may be seen in older cultures.
	- **Motility**: _Y. pestis_ is **non-motile at both 25°C and 37°C**, which helps differentiate it from other _Yersinia_ species that are motile at 25°C.
	
	**4. Identification**:
	
	- Can be done by **automated systems like MALDI-TOF** for rapid and accurate identification, including differentiation of its three biotypes.
	- **Conventional Biochemical Tests**:
	    - **Catalase positive** and **Oxidase negative**.
	    - **ICUT tests**: Indole negative, Citrate negative, Urease negative.
	    - **TSI (Triple Sugar Iron Agar) test**: Alkaline/acid (K/A) with no gas and no H2S.
	
	**5. F1 Antigen Detection**:
	
	- The **Fraction 1 (F1) capsular protein antigen** is a principal virulence factor and an immunodiagnostic marker.
	- It can be detected from **bubo aspirate or sputum** using direct immunofluorescence test, ELISA, or immunochromatographic test (ICT) with monoclonal antibodies.
	
	**6. Antibody Detection**:
	
	- Antibodies to F1 antigen can be detected by ELISA or passive agglutination.
	- **Clinical Relevance**: Antibodies typically appear late in the disease course, so they have **limited value for acute clinical diagnosis** but are useful **epidemiological markers** for seroprevalence studies as they can remain positive for several years.
	
	**7. Molecular Methods**:
	
	- **PCR assays** targeting the F1 antigen gene and pesticin genes are available. These methods are rapid and specific.
	
	**Treatment Considerations (Clinical Relevance)**:
	
	- **Prompt administration of appropriate antibiotics** is crucial for reducing mortality.
	- **Gentamicin or streptomycin** are the drugs of choice.
	- **Chemoprophylaxis** (e.g., doxycycline or levofloxacin for 7 days) is recommended for contacts of pneumonic plague.
	- A **vaccine** (formalin-killed) is available but primarily for anticipated outbreaks, not general use.
6. Define opportunistic infections and describe predisposing factors involved in opportunistic diseases
	**Opportunistic Infections (OIs)**
	
	**Definition**:
	
	- Opportunistic infections are illnesses that occur **more frequently or more severely** in individuals with **weakened immune systems** compared to those with healthy immune systems.
	
	**Predisposing Factors Involved in Opportunistic Diseases**:
	
	1. **Immunocompromised States**:
	    
	    - **HIV/AIDS**: The most significant predisposing clinical condition for acquiring a wide range of OIs. Common OIs in HIV patients include _Pneumocystis jirovecii_ pneumonia, extrapulmonary cryptococcosis (meningitis), disseminated non-tubercular mycobacterial infection, recurrent septicemia, toxoplasmosis, chronic intestinal cystoisosporiasis, atypical disseminated leishmaniasis, strongyloidiasis hyperinfection syndrome, and candidiasis (oral thrush, esophageal). _Penicillium marneffei_ also causes opportunistic infections in HIV-infected individuals.
	    - **Organ Transplantation**: Patients undergoing organ transplantation experience profound immunosuppression, making them highly susceptible to various OIs.
	    - **Primary Immunodeficiency Disorders**: Defects in immune components (T cell, B cell, combined immunodeficiencies, phagocytosis disorders) lead to increased risk.
	        - Those with **humoral (B cell) defects** are prone to **pyogenic bacterial infections**.
	        - Those with **cell-mediated (T cell) defects** are prone to infections by **viruses, fungi, and intracellular bacteria**.
	    - **Malignancies**: Patients with cancer are at increased risk for OIs. Specific examples:
	        - **Multiple myeloma & Chronic lymphocytic leukemia**: Predispose to infections by _S. pneumoniae_, _H. influenzae_, _N. meningitidis_ due to hyper/hypogammaglobulinemia.
	        - **Acute myeloid/lymphocytic leukemia**: Associated with granulocytopenia, increasing risk for extracellular gram-positive/negative bacteria and fungi.
	        - **Hodgkin’s lymphoma/leukemia**: Impaired T cell function leads to intracellular pathogen infections (e.g., _M. tuberculosis_, _Listeria_, _Salmonella_, _Cryptococcus_, herpesviruses).
	    - **Steroid Therapy**: Prolonged systemic corticosteroid therapy significantly increases susceptibility to various OIs, including aspergillosis and mucormycosis.
	2. **Chronic Diseases/Conditions**:
	    
	    - **Diabetes Mellitus**: Increases susceptibility to infections, especially in lower extremities (diabetic foot ulcers caused by _S. aureus_, Group B streptococci, Enterobacteriaceae, _P. aeruginosa_, anaerobes), and also risk for mucormycosis (diabetic ketoacidosis) and melioidosis.
	    - **Chronic Renal Failure**: Predisposes to OIs like melioidosis and invasive candidiasis.
	    - **Liver Disease/Cirrhosis**: Increases risk for infections, including _Vibrio vulnificus_ primary sepsis and _Capnocytophaga_ infections.
	    - **Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD)**: _Chlamydophila pneumoniae_ can cause exacerbations of COPD.
	    - **Anatomical or Functional Asplenia**: Increases incidence of bacterial sepsis, especially by capsulated bacteria like _Streptococcus pneumoniae_, _Neisseria meningitidis_, and _Haemophilus influenzae_.
	    - **Malnutrition**: Lowers host immunity and predisposes to various infections.
	    - **Old Age**: Increased risk for OIs, notably _Clostridioides difficile_ infection (>65 years) and candidiasis.
	3. **Invasive Procedures / Breaches in Anatomical Barriers**:
	    
	    - **Hospitalisation**: Patients in healthcare settings are at increased risk due to exposure to multi-drug resistant organisms and various invasive procedures.
	    - **Catheters/Central Lines**: Major source of healthcare-associated infections (e.g., Catheter-related Bloodstream Infection, catheter-associated UTI).
	    - **Surgery**: Surgical site infections are common healthcare-associated infections. Trauma (including surgical) is also a risk factor for mucormycosis and Melioidosis.
	    - **Dental Procedures/Minor Trauma in Mouth**: Can lead to transient bacteremia (e.g., by viridans streptococci) potentially causing bacterial endocarditis.

7. Viral zoonotic diseases
	**Viral Zoonotic Diseases**
	
	**Definition**:
	
	- A **zoonosis** is any disease or infection that is naturally transmissible from **vertebrate animals to humans**. Animals thus play an essential role in maintaining zoonotic infections in nature.
	
	**Clinical Relevance**: Zoonotic infections are a significant public health concern due to their potential for outbreaks and their impact on global health.
	
	**Key Viral Zoonotic Diseases & Predisposing Factors**:
	
	1. **Rabies**:
	    
	    - **Agent**: Rabies virus (Rhabdoviridae family, bullet-shaped, enveloped RNA virus).
	    - **Transmission**: Primarily by **bite or scratch from an infected animal**, especially rabid dogs (99% of cases); bats are major source in America. Non-bite exposures (e.g., direct contact with saliva on broken skin) are rare.
	    - **Reservoir**: Dogs, bats, foxes, raccoons, skunks, jackals, mongooses, and other wild carnivore hosts.
	    - **Pathogenesis**: Virus replicates locally at inoculation site, then spreads centripetally along peripheral motor nerves to CNS. **No viremia in brain infection**.
	    - **Clinical Manifestations**: Acute, rapidly progressive CNS disease. **Encephalitic form** presents as hyperexcitability, autonomic dysfunction, and **hydrophobia**; leads to coma and death. Recovery is extremely rare.
	    - **Diagnosis**:
	        - **Gold Standard (Post-mortem)**: **Negri bodies** (cytoplasmic inclusion bodies) in brain tissue by histological staining (e.g., H&E). Note: may be absent in 20% of cases.
	        - **Antigen Detection**: **Direct Immunofluorescence (DFA) test** from hair follicles at nape of neck or corneal smear (highly sensitive & specific).
	        - **Molecular Methods**: **RT-PCR** (most sensitive and specific) from brain tissue, saliva, skin biopsy.
	        - **Antibody Detection**: CSF antibodies appear early and are diagnostic; serum antibodies appear late and can be due to vaccination.
	    - **Prevention/Treatment**: **Post-exposure prophylaxis** (local wound care, rabies immunoglobulin, and vaccine) is crucial. Vaccine is a **fixed virus**.
	2. **Viral Hemorrhagic Fevers (VHFs)**:
	    
	    - Defined as a group of illnesses caused by different families of viruses that cause **vascular damage leading to symptomatic bleeding**.
	    - **Agents**: Arboviruses (Dengue, Yellow Fever), Filoviruses (Ebola, Marburg), Rodent-borne viruses (Hantaviruses, Arenaviruses).
	    - **Transmission**: Often by **arthropod vectors** (e.g., mosquitoes, ticks) or **contact with infected rodent body fluids/excretions**.
	    - **Diagnosis**: Typically **RT-PCR** for viral RNA (early phase) or **IgM antibody detection by ELISA** (later phase). **Biosafety Level 4 (BSL-4) facilities** are required for virus isolation due to high infectivity.
	    - **Treatment**: Mainly **supportive care**; definitive treatments are limited (e.g., Ribavirin for Lassa fever). Vaccines exist for Yellow Fever and Dengue.
	    - **Specific VHFs**:
	        - **Dengue**:
	            - **Vector**: _Aedes aegypti_ (principal), _Aedes albopictus_.
	            - **Clinical Manifestations**: Dengue fever, dengue hemorrhagic fever, dengue shock syndrome.
	            - **Diagnosis**: NS1 antigen detection, IgM ELISA, RT-PCR.
	        - **Yellow Fever**:
	            - **Agent**: Flavivirus (arbovirus).
	            - **Vector**: _Aedes aegypti_.
	            - **Endemic**: West Africa.
	            - **Vaccine**: Live attenuated **Yellow Fever 17D vaccine** (chick embryo derived).
	        - **Ebola & Marburg Viruses**:
	            - **Agent**: Filoviruses (filamentous shaped).
	            - **Transmission**: Direct contact with blood/body fluids.
	            - **Diagnosis**: Electron microscopy (shows filamentous viruses), virus isolation in Vero cell lines (BSL-4). RT-PCR for viral RNA.
	        - **Hantaviruses**:
	            - **Transmission**: Rodent-borne (contact with infected body fluids/excretions).
	            - **Clinical Manifestations**: Hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS) or hantavirus pulmonary syndrome (HPS).
	        - **Arenaviruses**:
	            - **Transmission**: Rodent-borne.
	            - **Clinical Manifestations**: South American hemorrhagic fever (Junin, Machupo, Guanarito, Sabia viruses) or Lassa fever (Old World viruses).
	        - **Kyasanur Forest Disease (KFD)**:
	            - **Agent**: Flaviviridae.
	            - **Vector**: **Hard ticks** (_Haemaphysalis spinigera_).
	            - **Endemic**: Shimoga and nearby districts of Karnataka, India.
	3. **Japanese B Encephalitis (JE)**:
	    
	    - **Agent**: Flavivirus (arbovirus).
	    - **Vector**: **Culex mosquito** (_C. tritaeniorhynchus_ major vector).
	    - **Reservoir**: Pigs (amplifier host).
	    - **Clinical Manifestations**: Leading cause of **viral encephalitis in Asia**.
	    - **Diagnosis**: **IgM capture antibody (MAC) ELISA** from serum, **RT-PCR** for viral RNA.
	    - **Treatment**: Supportive only.
	    - **Vaccine**: Live-attenuated and inactivated vaccines available.
	4. **Zika Virus**:
	    
	    - **Agent**: Flaviviridae (ssRNA arbovirus).
	    - **Vector**: _Aedes aegypti_.
	    - **Reservoir**: Monkeys.
	    - **Clinical Manifestations**: Mild febrile illness in adults, but can cause **congenital infection** (microcephaly).
	    - **Diagnosis**: RT-PCR (early stage), IgM antibody detection (later stage, cross-reacts with dengue).
	5. **Influenza**:
	    
	    - **Agent**: Influenza viruses (Orthomyxoviridae family, segmented RNA).
	    - **Reservoir**: Birds (primary), swine. Some subtypes can transmit to mammals.
	    - **Transmission**: Respiratory droplets.
	    - **Clinical Relevance**: Responsible for epidemics and pandemics (e.g., H1N1 in 2009). Avian flu strains can be highly virulent.
	    - **Diagnosis**: **Real-time RT-PCR** is gold standard (detects specific HA and NA genes, quantifies viral load).
	6. **Nipah and Hendra Viruses**:
	    
	    - **Agent**: Paramyxoviruses.
	    - **Clinical Manifestations**: Mainly cause **encephalitis**. Hendra virus can also cause pneumonia.
	    - **Diagnosis**: Real-time PCR (early), ELISA (later), immunohistochemistry. Electron microscopy shows filamentous viruses (Hendra).
	    - **Biosafety**: BSL-4 pathogens.
	7. **Monkeypox**:
	    
	    - **Agent**: Poxvirus.
	    - **Reservoir**: Monkeys.
	8. **Prion Diseases (variant Creutzfeldt-Jakob Disease - vCJD)**:
	    
	    - **Agent**: **Prion protein (PrPSc)**, not a virus, but a misfolded protein.
	    - **Transmission**: Ingestion of beef contaminated with **BSE prions** ("mad cow disease").
	    - **Clinical Manifestations**: Neurodegenerative condition with long incubation period.
	
	**General Diagnostic Principles for Viral Zoonoses**:
	
	- **Molecular Methods (RT-PCR)**: Often the **gold standard** for early, rapid, and specific diagnosis, especially for RNA viruses and for quantifying viral load.
	- **Serology (ELISA)**: Useful for detecting IgM (acute/recent infection) and IgG (past infection/immunity), but IgM may appear late or show cross-reactivity. Useful for epidemiological studies.
	- **Virus Isolation**: Time-consuming, expensive, and often requires high-level biosafety (BSL-3 or BSL-4) due to infectivity. Primarily for research or reference labs.
	- **Direct Microscopy**: Limited utility for most viruses due to small size; electron microscopy for morphology. Light microscopy for inclusion bodies (e.g., Negri bodies for rabies).
	
	**General Treatment & Prevention**:
	
	- **Treatment**: Often **supportive care** as specific antivirals are limited.
	- **Prevention**: Vaccination (where available), vector control, proper animal husbandry, and avoiding contact with infected animals.

	**Laboratory Diagnosis of Tuberculosis (TB)**
	
	**I. Diagnosis of Active Tuberculosis**
	
	2. **Specimen Collection**:
	    
	    - **Pulmonary TB**: **Sputum** (spot & early morning samples are crucial), gastric aspirate (for children).
	    - **Extrapulmonary TB (EPTB)**: Specimen varies by site (e.g., CSF, lymph node aspirate, tissue biopsy, pleural fluid, urine).
	    - **Processing**: Sputum and non-sterile specimens require digestion, decontamination, and concentration to liquefy pus, inhibit normal flora, and increase yield. This step is **not required for molecular methods or sterile site specimens**.
	    - **Key Consideration**: Samples for culture **must never be collected in formalin**.
	3. **Direct Microscopy (Acid-fast Staining)**:
	    
	    - **Principle**: Detects **acid-fast bacilli (AFB)** due to mycolic acid in cell wall. Provides **presumptive diagnosis**.
	    - **Ziehl-Neelsen (ZN) Technique (Hot Method)**:
	        - Appearance: **Long, slender, beaded, red-coloured AFB**. Background appears blue.
	        - Sensitivity: Low (detection limit ~**10,000 bacilli/mL of sputum**).
	        - Disadvantage: Cannot differentiate _M. tuberculosis_ from saprophytic mycobacteria.
	    - **Fluorescent (Auramine) Staining**:
	        - Appearance: Bacilli appear **brilliant yellow** (auramine-phenol) or **brilliant green** (acridine orange) against a dark background.
	        - Advantages: **More sensitive** than ZN and faster for screening (screened using 20x/25x objective). Recommended screening method by RNTCP.
	4. **Culture Methods**:
	    
	    - **Gold Standard**: Culture is the **gold standard** for TB diagnosis.
	    - Advantages: More sensitive than microscopy (detects 10-100 viable bacilli), confirms viability, and allows **Drug Susceptibility Testing (DST)**.
	    - **Conventional Solid Media (Lowenstein-Jensen/LJ)**:
	        - Incubation: **Prolonged (6-8 weeks)** due to slow generation time (10-15 hours).
	        - Colonies: Typically **rough, tough, and buff-coloured**.
	    - **Automated Liquid Culture Systems (e.g., MGIT)**:
	        - Endorsed by WHO and RNTCP.
	        - Advantages: **Faster (3-4 weeks)**, higher yield, and can perform **DST against first- and second-line anti-tubercular drugs (ATDs)**. Uses fluorometric principle.
	        - Clinical Relevance: Particularly useful for paucibacillary samples (e.g., EPTB).
	5. **Molecular Methods**:
	    
	    - **Revolutionary Impact**: Highly sensitive, rapid, and specific, overcoming limitations of culture and microscopy.
	    - **GeneXpert (Cartridge-based Nucleic Acid Amplification Test / CBNAAT)**:
	        - Type: Automated real-time PCR system. **Endorsed by WHO and used under RNTCP in India**.
	        - Speed: **Rapid, with a turnaround time of 2 hours**.
	        - Detection: Simultaneously detects **MTB complex DNA and rifampicin resistance (mutations in the _rpoB_ gene)**.
	        - Clinical Application: **Preferred initial diagnostic option for EPTB** specimens (CSF, lymph nodes, tissue) due to paucibacillary nature.
	        - Sensitivity: 88% for TB bacilli, 95% for rifampicin resistance.
	    - **Truenat**:
	        - Type: Chip-based real-time PCR system. Validated by ICMR, endorsed by WHO.
	        - Detection: Targets E gene (screening) and N2 gene (confirmation) for COVID-19 diagnosis, but implied similar function for TB.
	    - **Line Probe Assay (LPA)**:
	        - Method: Non-amplification molecular test using nucleic acid probes.
	        - Detection: Detects **resistance to both first-line (e.g., isoniazid) and second-line ATDs**.
	        - Turnaround Time: 2-3 days.
	        - Clinical Use: Performed on positive cultures or smear-positive clinical specimens. Useful for isoniazid mono-resistant cases.
	
	**II. Diagnosis of Latent Tuberculosis**
	
	6. **Tuberculin Skin Test (TST) / Mantoux Test**:
	    
	    - Antigen: Purified protein derivative (PPD-23).
	    - Reading: Induration measured after 48-72 hours. **≥10 mm induration is considered positive**.
	    - Interpretation: Indicates **prior exposure to _M. tuberculosis_** but does not confirm active disease in adults. Used as an epidemiological marker.
	    - Limitations: False positives due to BCG vaccination or non-tuberculous mycobacteria.
	7. **Interferon Gamma Release Assay (IGRA)**:
	    
	    - Principle: **In vitro test** where sensitised T lymphocytes from the patient are exposed to highly specific _M. tuberculosis_ antigens (CFP10, ESAT6). Release of IFN-γ is measured (e.g., by ELISA).
	    - Advantage: **Highly specific**, as the antigens are not shared by BCG vaccine strains or most non-tuberculous mycobacteria, leading to **no false-positive results**.
	
	**III. Resistance in _Mycobacterium tuberculosis_**
	
	8. **Mechanism of Resistance**:
	    
	    - Primarily due to **point mutations in the _M. tuberculosis_ genome**.
	    - Occurs at a rate of approximately once in 10^8 cell divisions.
	    - Mutational resistance is typically low-level and can be overcome by using a combination of different drug classes. This is why **multidrug therapy** is essential for TB treatment.
	9. **Key Drug-Resistant Genes**:
	    
	    - **Isoniazid**: _inhA_, _katG_, _AhpC_.
	    - **Rifampicin**: **rpoB** (RNA polymerase subunit B).
	    - **Pyrazinamide**: _pncA_.
	    - **Ethambutol**: _rpsL_.
	    - **Streptomycin**: _rpsL_, _rrs_, _strA_.
	    - **Fluoroquinolones**: _gyrA_ and _gyrB_.
	10. **Types of Drug Resistance**:
	    
	    - **Mono-resistance**: Resistance to **one first-line ATD only**.
	    - **Poly-resistance**: Resistance to **more than one first-line ATD**, excluding the combination of both isoniazid and rifampicin.
	    - **Rifampicin Resistance (RR)**: Resistance to rifampicin, with or without resistance to other ATDs. All MDR and XDR cases are also RR.
	    - **Multidrug-resistant Tuberculosis (MDR-TB)**:
	        - Definition: Resistance to **both isoniazid and rifampicin**.
	        - Clinical Relevance: A significant global public health concern with high prevalence in some regions, including India (e.g., 6.19% of all TB patients in India in 2018).
	    - **Pre-XDR-TB**:
	        - Definition: Resistance to **either fluoroquinolones (FQs) or second-line injectable (SLI) agents, but not both**, in addition to MDR-TB.
	    - **Extensively Drug-resistant Tuberculosis (XDR-TB)**:
	        - Definition: **MDR-TB that is also resistant to FQs AND at least one SLI agent** (amikacin/capreomycin/kanamycin).
	        - Clinical Relevance: Extremely difficult to treat, often rapidly progressive, and associated with high mortality.
	11. **Factors Contributing to Resistance**:
	    
	    - **Failure to adhere to multidrug regimens** is the most important reason.
	    - Other factors include prolonged duration of treatment, poor patient compliance, drug toxicities, and inadequate supervision and follow-up.
	12. **Management and Surveillance**:
	    
	    - **Universal-DST (U-DST)**: Testing all TB patients for drug resistance, at least to rifampicin (via CBNAAT), is now implemented in many areas.
	    - Treatment regimens are tailored based on the specific drug resistance patterns identified by DST.
	    - Newer ATDs (e.g., bedaquiline, delamanid) have been developed specifically to combat drug-resistant TB.

	- **Causative Agent**: Primarily _**Cryptococcus neoformans**_. Other species include _**C. gattii**_. Most laboratories typically report all isolates as _C. neoformans_.

	- **Habitat**:
	    - _C. neoformans_ is commonly found in **soil contaminated with avian excreta and pigeon droppings**.
	    - _C. gattii_ inhabits **eucalyptus trees**.
	- **Geographical Distribution**:
	    - _C. neoformans_ var. _grubii_ (serotype A) strains are found **worldwide**.

	    - Skin lesions (common with _C. neoformans_ var. _neoformans_).
	    - Osteolytic bone lesions.

	**Slow Virus Diseases**
	
	**I. Definition & Key Characteristics** Slow virus diseases are a group of **neurodegenerative conditions** affecting both humans and animals. They are characterised by:
	
	- **Long incubation period**, ranging from months to years.
	- **Predilection for the Central Nervous System (CNS)**.
	- **Invariably fatal** outcome.
	- Strong genetic predisposition.
	- **Lack of antigenicity**, resulting in a lack of immune response against viral proteins and associated inflammation.
	- Do not produce cytopathologic effect _in vitro_.
	- They cause a chronic form of encephalitis/encephalopathy, described as chronic, progressive demyelinating disease of the CNS.
	
	**II. Enumerate Slow Virus Diseases**
	
	1. **Progressive Multifocal Leukoencephalopathy (PML)**
	    
	    - **Causative Agent**: **JC virus** (a polyomavirus).
	    - **Host**: Human.
	    - **Pathology**: Characterised by multifocal areas of **demyelination** distributed throughout the brain, along with deformation of both astrocytes and oligodendrocytes.
	2. **Subacute Sclerosing Panencephalitis (SSPE)**
	    
	    - **Causative Agent**: **Measles** (or measles virus).
	    - **Host**: Human.
	    - **Clinical Relevance**: This is the **most important CNS complication** of measles.
	3. **Progressive Rubella Panencephalitis**
	    
	    - **Causative Agent**: Rubella.
	    - **Host**: Human.
	    - **Clinical Relevance**: An SSPE-like condition, but extremely rare, primarily affecting males with congenital rubella syndrome after a latent period.
	4. **Visna virus encephalitis**
	    
	    - **Host**: Sheep.
	    - **Pathology**: Causes demyelination of the CNS in sheep.
	5. **Maedi virus progressive pneumonia**
	    
	    - **Host**: Sheep.
	    - **Note**: This agent **does not produce CNS disease** but causes a slow progressive fatal hemorrhagic pneumonia of sheep.

4. DPT vaccine
	 **DPT Vaccine (Diphtheria, Pertussis, Tetanus)**
	
	**I. Definition & Purpose**
	
	- A **combined vaccine** used for **active immunoprophylaxis** against Diphtheria, Pertussis (whooping cough), and Tetanus.
	- Aims to stimulate the immune system to produce **active immunity** (protective antibody and/or T cell response).
	- It is part of the **National Immunization Schedule (NIS)** in India and many other countries.
	
	**II. Composition**
	
	- Consists of:
	    - **Diphtheria toxoid (DT)**.
	    - **Pertussis (whole cell killed preparation)**.
	    - **Tetanus toxoid (TT)**.
	- The pertussis component acts as an **adjuvant**, increasing the immunogenicity of both diphtheria and tetanus toxoids.
	- Commercial preparations are available (e.g., Glaxo, Kasauli), with varying Lf (Limit of flocculation) units for DT and TT, and bacterial counts for pertussis killed bacilli.
	
	**III. Mechanism of Action**
	
	- **Toxoids** (Diphtheria and Tetanus) induce neutralizing antibodies against their respective toxins, which are capable of neutralizing the toxin moiety produced during an infection.
	- The **whole-cell pertussis component** induces immunity against _Bordetella pertussis_.
	
	**IV. Administration**
	
	- **Route**: **Deep intramuscular (IM)**.
	- **Site**: Anterolateral aspect of the thigh in children; gluteal region is not preferred as fat may inhibit DPT absorption.
	- **Dosage**: Usual dose for children contains 25 Lf units of diphtheria toxoid.
	- **Storage**: Should be kept at **2–8°C**; must be discarded if accidentally frozen.
	- **Preservative**: Thiomersal (0.01%) is used.
	
	**V. Immunization Schedule (as per NIS 2020, India)**
	
	- **Primary Immunization**: Three doses of **pentavalent vaccine** (DPT + Hepatitis B + _H. influenzae_ type b) are given at **6, 10, and 14 weeks of birth**.
	    - _Note_: The interval between two doses of pentavalent vaccine should never be less than 1 month.
	- **Booster Doses**:
	    - First booster: **DPT at 16–24 months**.
	    - Second booster: **DPT at 5 years**.
	    - Further Td (tetanus and adult diphtheria toxoid) boosters are given at 10 and 16 years, and in pregnancy to reduce diphtheria resurgence in adults.
	
	**VI. Efficacy & Protection**
	
	- A protective antitoxin titer of **≥0.01 IU/mL** is considered protective against tetanus and diphtheria.
	- Efficacy of whole-cell pertussis vaccine is generally good (average 85%).
	
	**VII. Adverse Effects & Contraindications**
	
	- **Whole-cell pertussis vaccine** is associated with adverse effects:
	    - **Common**: Fever, injection-site pain, erythema, swelling, irritability.
	    - **Uncommon/Serious**: Can be **encephalitogenic**, associated with **neurological complications** (encephalitis, prolonged convulsion) and hypotonic hyporesponsive syndrome. The estimated risk is 1:1,70,000 doses.
	- **Absolute Contraindications**:
	    - **Hypersensitivity** to a previous dose of the vaccine.
	    - **Progressive neurological disorder**.
	- For older children (>7 years) who missed primary vaccination, **DaPT (Diphtheria, acellular Pertussis, Tetanus) vaccine** is recommended, as acellular pertussis is not associated with neurological complications.

	**Oncogenic DNA Viruses**
	
	Oncogenic viruses are those capable of inducing the transformation of normal cells into cancer cells [Preamble from prior turns]. Viral oncogenesis is a **complex, multistep process** that typically requires prolonged time, spanning years to decades. While viruses contribute to a portion of these oncogenic events, host immunity and genetic susceptibility are also crucial factors.
	
	DNA tumor viruses, when infecting permissive cells, are normally released via host cell lysis. Therefore, they are not oncogenic to permissive cells unless their replicative cycle is blocked, allowing for indefinite growth. The **retention of viral nucleic acid** inside host cells, often through integration of viral DNA into the host cell chromosome, is essential to maintain stable genetic changes in a tumor cell.
	
	**I. Key Mechanisms of Oncogenesis** (relevant to DNA viruses)
	
	- **Oncogenes**: Encode proteins (oncoproteins) that trigger cell transformation.
	    - **Proto-oncogenes**: Normal host genes that promote cell growth; their overactivation can lead to transformation.
	- **Interference with Tumor Suppressor Genes**: Inactivation of anti-oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes allows abnormal cellular proliferation.
	- **Modulation of Apoptosis-regulatory Genes**: Mutations in these genes can accelerate cellular transformation by altering programmed cell death.
	- **Impact on DNA Repair Genes**: Failure of these host genes to repair damaged DNA can lead to persistent mutations.
	- **Immune Evasion**: Viruses can contribute to oncogenesis by restricting viral gene expression (evading immune detection), infecting immune-inaccessible sites, undergoing mutations to escape immune responses, or suppressing essential immune cells.
	- **Host Cell Susceptibility**: Oncogenicity risk is heightened when non-permissive cells are infected by DNA tumor viruses, as the virus attempts to ensure its survival within these cells, potentially leading to cell immortalization.
	
	**II. Examples of Oncogenic DNA Viruses & Associated Malignancies**
	
	1. **Papillomaviridae / Polyomaviridae**
	    
	    - **Human Papillomaviruses (HPVs)**
	        - **Malignancies**: **Cervical carcinoma** is a primary association. Also linked to other genital tract carcinomas (anal, vulval/vaginal, penile), esophageal carcinoma, laryngeal carcinoma, and oropharyngeal carcinoma.
	        - **Mechanism**: The products of early genes **E6 and E7** possess oncogenic potential.
	            - **E6 protein**: Facilitates the degradation of the **p53 tumor-suppressor protein**.
	            - **E7 protein**: Binds to the **retinoblastoma gene product** and related proteins.
	        - **Clinical Relevance**: HPV types **16 and 18** exhibit high malignant potential. Prevention is available through HPV vaccines, which are subunit vaccines consisting of HPV L1 proteins produced by DNA recombinant technology.
	    - **Merkel Cell Virus**:
	        - **Malignancy**: Merkel cell carcinoma of the skin.
	2. **Herpesviridae**
	    
	    - **Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV)**
	        - **Malignancies**: **Burkitt’s lymphoma**, Hodgkin’s disease, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and B-cell lymphoma.
	        - **Mechanism**: **Latent membrane protein-1 (LMP-1)** is a crucial viral oncogene. It acts as an active CD40 receptor, stimulating B cell growth, activating NF-κβ and JAK/STAT signalling pathways, preventing apoptosis by activating BCL2, and inducing pro-angiogenic factors like VEGF. Viral **EBNA-2** also activates host cell cyclin-D and the proto-oncogene src.
	        - **Clinical Relevance**: EBV is associated with **90% of African and 20% of non-African cases of Burkitt’s lymphoma**. Nasopharyngeal carcinoma is noted among Chinese populations with specific dietary histories (e.g., salted fish, herbal snuff). EBV DNA is present in Reed-Sternberg cells in at least 50% of Hodgkin’s lymphoma cases.
	    - **Human Herpesvirus-8 (HHV-8) / Kaposi's Sarcoma-Associated Herpesvirus (KSHV)**
	        - **Malignancies**: **Kaposi’s sarcoma**, Castleman’s disease, and primary effusion lymphoma.
	        - **Mechanism**: Expression of early lytic genes (e.g., viral G protein-coupled receptor K1, viral interleukin-6 (vIL-6), K15) induces host cells to secrete angiogenic, inflammatory, and proliferative factors (e.g., VEGF, PDGF-β, angiopoietin 2, IL-6, IL-8).
	3. **Hepadnaviridae**
	    
	    - **Hepatitis B Virus (HBV)**
	        - **Malignancy**: **Hepatocellular carcinoma**.
	        - **Mechanism**: Chronic liver cell injury and compensatory regeneration are primary mechanisms. The **Hepatitis B X gene (HBx)**, a regulatory viral gene, can activate the transcription of cellular and viral genes. Integration of viral DNA into the host genome can lead to chromosomal rearrangements and the deletion of tumor suppressor genes.

7. Polio vaccine
	**Polio Vaccine**
	
	**I. Introduction**
	
	- Poliomyelitis (Polio) is caused by **Poliovirus**, an enterovirus of the Picornaviridae family.
	- It is transmitted by the **faeco-oral route** and can spread to the central nervous system (CNS) or spinal cord via the haematogenous route.
	- While most infections are asymptomatic, it can rarely progress to aseptic meningitis and **paralytic poliomyelitis**.
	- **Vaccines** are crucial for prevention, and both inactivated and live oral polio vaccines are available.
	
	**II. Types of Polio Vaccines**
	
	There are two main types of polio vaccines:
	
	**A. Injectable Polio Vaccine (IPV) / Salk Vaccine**
	
	- **Discovery**: Prepared by Jonas Salk in 1952.
	- **Preparation**: Virus is grown in **monkey kidney cell line** and inactivated by **formalin**.
	- **Composition**: Each 0.5 mL dose contains antigens from all three poliovirus serotypes (40 units of type 1, 8 units of type 2, and 32 units of type 3).
	- **Dosage/Route**: Can be given as full dose (0.5 mL/dose) intramuscularly (IM) or fractional dose (f-IPV) (0.1 mL) intradermally (ID).
	    - Under the National Immunization Schedule (NIS) India 2020, two **fractional doses (f-IPV)** are given ID at the 6th and 14th weeks of age along with bivalent Oral Polio Vaccine (OPV).
	- **Efficacy**: Around 80–90% protection is achieved after the full course of vaccination.
	- **Advantages**:
	    - **Safer** than OPV, even for immunocompromised individuals and in pregnancy.
	    - **Does not cause vaccine-associated paralytic polio (VAPP)**.
	    - More **stable** and does not require stringent storage conditions.
	- **Disadvantages**:
	    - **Does not provide herd immunity** as it is an inactivated vaccine and cannot spread by the faeco-oral route.
	    - **Not useful during epidemics** for community protection.
	    - Does not induce mucosal IgA production, thus **local immunity is absent**.
	    - Relatively **more expensive** than OPV.
	    - **Requires booster doses periodically** for long-lasting protection.
	
	**B. Oral Polio Vaccine (OPV) / Sabin Vaccine**
	
	- **Discovery**: Developed by Albert Sabin, Koprowski, and Cox around 1955.
	- **Formulations**: Available as trivalent OPV (serotypes 1, 2, and 3), bivalent OPV (serotypes 1 and 3), and monovalent OPV (any one serotype).
	    - **Trivalent OPV was replaced by bivalent OPV** in India since 2015, as serotype 2 was eradicated globally since 1999, and type 2 in OPV had a high risk of causing vaccine-induced paralysis.
	- **Preparation**: Contains live attenuated mutant strains (Ty21a lacks UDP-galactose-4-epimerase, _Gal E mutant_). On ingestion, it multiplies, initiates immune response, but self-destructs after 4-5 cell divisions.
	- **Dosage/Route**: Administered **orally** (two drops/dose).
	    - **National Immunization Schedule (NIS) India 2020**: Total five doses:
	        - **Zero dose**: At birth (if missed, within first 15 days).
	        - **1st, 2nd, 3rd doses**: At 6, 10, and 14 weeks (f-IPV given along with bivalent OPV at 6th and 14th week).
	        - **Booster dose**: At 16–24 months of birth.
	- **Efficacy**: About 90–100%, achieved much faster than IPV (with one or two doses).
	- **Advantages**:
	    - **Induces herd immunity**: Live OPV strains shed in faeces and can spread in the community by the faeco-oral route, providing both individual and community protection.
	    - **Vaccine of choice during epidemics**.
	    - **Induces local/mucosal immunity** by stimulating mucosal IgA production.
	    - **Cheaper** than IPV.
	    - **Easy to administer** (oral route).
	- **Disadvantages**:
	    - **Safety concerns**: Risky for immunocompromised individuals, pregnant women, and the elderly.
	    - **Unstable vaccine**: Requires stringent storage conditions (e.g., at –20°C, stabilized in MgCl2, pH < 7).
	    - **Efficacy decreases** due to interference by other enteroviruses, diarrhoea (OPV gets washed away), and breastfeeding (avoid 30 min before/after administration).
	    - Can cause **Vaccine-Associated Paralytic Poliomyelitis (VAPP)** and **Vaccine-Derived Polioviruses (VDPV)**.
	        - **VAPP**: Paralytic poliomyelitis occurring after OPV administration, due to limited genetic divergence from parental OPV strains (<1%). Can occur in recipients and close contacts due to faeco-oral spread.
	        - **VDPV**: Vaccine strains that have regained neurovirulence and can cause disease.
	
	**III. Polio Eradication Efforts**
	
	- Poliomyelitis is **at the verge of eradication** due to extensive global immunization programs.

	    - **Unstable vaccine**: Requires stringent storage conditions (e.g., at –20°C, stabilized in MgCl2, pH < 7).
	    - **Efficacy decreases** due to interference by other enteroviruses, diarrhoea (OPV gets washed away), and breastfeeding (avoid 30 min before/after administration).
	    - Can cause **Vaccine-Associated Paralytic Poliomyelitis (VAPP)** and **Vaccine-Derived Polioviruses (VDPV)**.
	        - **VAPP**: Paralytic poliomyelitis occurring after OPV administration. VAPP strains are OPV-like isolates showing limited genetic divergence from parental OPV strains (<1%). Can occur in OPV recipients as well as their close contacts due to faeco-oral spread.
	        - **VDPV**: Vaccine strains that have regained neurovirulence and are capable of producing disease in humans.
	
	**III. Polio Eradication Efforts**
	
	- Poliomyelitis is **at the verge of eradication** globally due to extensive immunization programs.
	- India achieved polio-free status, as mentioned by the context of specific National Immunization Schedules. The global effort aims to certify countries for eradication of wild poliovirus and contain all polioviruses, including from laboratories.
8. Emerging infections
	**Emerging Infections**
	
	**I. Definition & Significance**
	
	- **Definition**: Infectious diseases whose **incidence in humans has increased** in the past two decades or **threatens to increase** in the near future.
	- **Public Health Relevance**: Represent a significant and dynamic challenge to global health, necessitating continuous monitoring and adaptive strategies.
	- **Clinical Relevance**: Medical professionals, particularly Indian Medical Graduates (IMGs), must be trained to **recognize, diagnose, investigate, treat, and prevent** these newly emerging diseases that can lead to outbreaks, epidemics, or pandemics.
	
	**II. Characteristics & Contributing Factors**
	
	- **Novel or Newly Recognized Pathogens**: Introduction of entirely new microbial agents into human populations.
	- **Re-emerging/Resurgent Pathogens**: Known pathogens whose incidence significantly increases after a period of decline.
	- **Antimicrobial Resistance**: A major factor contributing to the emergence of challenging infections. Development of resistance by microorganisms to antimicrobial agents (acquired or intrinsic) renders previously effective treatments useless, creating new threats. This leads to the emergence of highly resistant strains, even if the pathogen itself is not novel.
	    - **Examples of Drug Resistance Impact**:
	        - **Multidrug-resistant (MDR) _E. coli_**: Frequently hospital-acquired, necessitating higher-spectrum antimicrobials like carbapenems.
	        - **Multidrug-resistant (MDR) _K. pneumoniae_**: Treatment is a global challenge due to high drug resistance.
	        - **Multidrug-resistant (MDR) _P. aeruginosa_**: Its ability to develop widespread resistance makes it a significant pathogen, especially in hospital environments.
	        - **Multidrug-resistant (MDR) _Acinetobacter baumannii_**: Notorious for resistance to multiple drugs, including carbapenems, leading to hospital outbreaks.
	- **Virulence Changes**: Evolution of existing pathogens to become more virulent or capable of causing more severe disease.
	- **Environmental & Host Factors**:
	    - **Immunosuppression**: Compromised host immunity increases susceptibility to various invasive infections.
	    - **Invasive Procedures/Extensive Surgeries**: Provide bacteria access to the bloodstream.
	    - **Globalisation & Travel**: Rapid movement of people and goods facilitates the swift spread of pathogens across geographical boundaries.
	    - **Climate Change & Ecological Disturbances**: Can alter vector ranges or bring humans into contact with novel reservoirs.
	    - **Healthcare Settings**: Can be major sites for the emergence and spread of resistant strains due to close contact and unhygienic practices.
	
	**III. Clinical Examples of Emerging Threats (as per sources)**
	
	- **Coronavirus (COVID-19)**: Described as the "most catastrophic and enigmatic disease," leading to the introduction of a pandemic management module in medical education.
	- **Influenza A Subtypes**: Novel influenza A subtypes cause global pandemics every 10–40 years, associated with much higher mortality than seasonal flu.
	- _**Candida auris**_: An **emerging species of _Candida_**, increasingly reported in healthcare settings due to its **multidrug resistance** and ability to cause outbreaks. It is difficult to identify with standard laboratory methods.
	- **Hypervirulent _Klebsiella pneumoniae_ (hvKp)**: Recently emerged as a **pathogen of global concern**.
	
	**IV. Preparedness & Management**
	
	- **Surveillance**: Robust surveillance systems are essential for early detection and monitoring of new or re-emerging threats.
	- **Diagnostic Capacity**: Rapid and sensitive diagnostic tools (e.g., **Real-time PCR, multiplex PCR, automated systems like BioFire FilmArray**) are critical for timely identification and response.
	- **Treatment Guidelines**: Continuous updates to antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) and treatment guidelines are necessary to combat evolving resistance patterns.
	- **Vaccine Prophylaxis**: Development and implementation of new vaccines or updated strategies are vital for prevention.
	- **Infection Control Practices**: Strict adherence to infection control measures (e.g., hand hygiene, PPE, isolation precautions) is crucial, especially in healthcare facilities, to prevent spread.
	- **Interdisciplinary Collaboration**: Essential for effective outbreak management, including public health, administration, and international cooperation.
