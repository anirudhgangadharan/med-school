1. Lab diagnosis of Syphilis
	**Syphilis**
	
	- **Aetiology:**
	    
	    - Caused by _**Treponema pallidum**_.
	    - _T. pallidum_ is extremely thin and delicate. It is **not cultivable** in vitro; it is maintained in rabbit testes.
	- **Laboratory Diagnosis:** As culture methods are not available, and microscopy is difficult, **antibody detection methods are of paramount importance** in the diagnosis of syphilis.
	    
	    - **Specimen Collection:**
	        
	        - Depends on the stage/manifestation: Exudate or swab from the edge of the ulcer, tissue sections.
	        - For **Neurosyphilis**: **CSF**,,.
	        - For **Congenital Syphilis**: Umbilical cord, placenta, nasal discharge, or skin lesion material.
	    - **Direct Microscopy:**
	        
	        - **Dark Ground Microscopy (DGM):** Used to directly visualize _T. pallidum_ from fresh lesions,,. Sensitivity approaches 100% when smear is made from fresh lesions.
	        - **Direct Fluorescent Antibody Staining for _T. pallidum_ (DFA-TP):** Smear/tissue section stained with fluorescent-labelled monoclonal antibody targeting surface antigens. _T. pallidum_ appears as distinct, sharply outlined, **apple green fluorescent colored bacilli**. Sensitivity approaches 100% with fresh lesions.
	        - **Silver Impregnation Methods:** Used for staining due to the thinness of treponemes. Includes Levaditi stain (for tissue sections) and Fontana stain (for smears),,.
	    - **Serology (Antibody Detection):**
	        
	        - **Non-Treponemal Tests (STS - Standard Tests for Syphilis):** Detect non-specific **reagin antibodies** induced against lipid haptens released from damaged host tissues, using cardiolipin antigen,.
	            - Examples: **VDRL** (Venereal Disease Research Laboratory),,, **RPR** (Rapid Plasma Reagin), TRUST (Toluidine Red Unheated Serum Test), USR (Unheated Serum Reagin Test).
	            - **VDRL** when reactive, is considered diagnostic of neurosyphilis, but has low sensitivity for this purpose.
	            - Can give **false positive results** in various conditions (e.g., pregnancy, autoimmune diseases).
	            - May show **prozone phenomenon** (false negative with high antibody titer).
	        - **Treponemal Tests:** Detect **specific antibodies** against _T. pallidum_ antigens. Used for confirmation after a reactive non-treponemal test.
	            - Examples: **TPHA** (Treponema pallidum Hemagglutination Assay), **TPPA** (Treponema pallidum Particle Agglutination Assay) (**more specific** than TPHA), **FTA-ABS** (Fluorescent Treponemal Antibody Absorption Test) (**most sensitive** treponemal test), ELISA, Western Blot.
	            - ELISA is widely used and automated.
	    - **Molecular Methods:**
	        
	        - A **multiplex PCR** assay has been developed for simultaneous detection of _T. pallidum_, _Haemophilus ducreyi_, and herpes simplex virus in genital ulcers,.
	    - **Specific Diagnosis:**
	        
	        - **Neurosyphilis:** Diagnosed by CSF analysis showing **increased lymphocytes** (>5/μL) and **increased protein** level (>45 mg/dL). A reactive **CSF VDRL** is highly specific but has low sensitivity.
	        - **Congenital Syphilis:**
	            - **Definitive:** Demonstration of _T. pallidum_ by DGM of umbilical cord, placenta, nasal discharge, or skin lesion material.
	            - **Presumptive:** Infant born to a mother with syphilis at delivery _plus_ reactive treponemal test in infant _plus_ one additional criterion.
	    - **Testing Algorithm (CDC Recommendation):**
	        
	        - Typically, **non-treponemal test (screening) followed by treponemal test (confirmation)**.
	        - In high prevalence areas, **reverse algorithm** (treponemal test first, then non-treponemal) may be cost-effective.
	- **Clinical Relevance:** Laboratory diagnosis is crucial for identifying infection across various stages and manifestations, including primary chancre, secondary rash/condylomata lata/mucosal patch, neurosyphilis,, and congenital syphilis. Accurate diagnosis guides treatment (e.g., Penicillin G).
2. Lymphogranuloma venerum-etiology, lab diagnosis and treatment
	**Lymphogranuloma Venereum (LGV)**
	
	- **Aetiology:**
	    
	    - Caused by certain serovars of _**Chlamydia trachomatis**_, the same organism associated with non-gonococcal urethritis.
	    - _C. trachomatis_ inclusion bodies can be stained by **Lugol's iodine**.
	- **Laboratory Diagnosis:**
	    
	    - **Molecular Methods:** **Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAAT)** are available and are the **most sensitive and specific test** for _Chlamydia_ diagnosis.
	    - **Antibody Detection:**
	        - ELISA can detect antibodies.
	        - **Microimmunofluorescence (MIF)** test detects antibodies using species and serovar specific MOMP antigen. Serovar and species-specific antigens are spotted onto slides.
	    - **Direct Detection:**
	        - Direct IF can detect inclusion bodies.
	        - Inclusion bodies are stained by **Lugol's iodine**.
	    - **Culture:**
	        - Used for culture confirmation.
	        - Culture on **McCoy cell line** is the **most commonly used method** for isolation of _Chlamydia_.
	    - _Historical/Obsolete:_ **Frei test** (a skin test) was used in the past but is now obsolete.
	- **Treatment:**
	    
	    - A **longer treatment course is necessary**.
	    - **Drug of choice:** **Doxycycline for 21 days**.
	    - **Alternative:** **Azithromycin (weekly once for 3 weeks)** can be given.
3. Gonococcal urethritis
	**Gonococcal Urethritis**
	
	- **Aetiology:**
	    
	    - Caused by _**Neisseria gonorrhoeae**_, a sexually transmitted bacterium.
	    - _N. gonorrhoeae_ is a **noncapsulated, gram-negative kidney-shaped diplococcus**.
	    - Pili (fimbriae) are principal virulence factors, aiding adhesion and preventing phagocytosis.
	- **Clinical Manifestations (Urethritis Focus):**
	    
	    - Gonorrhea commonly manifests as urethritis, cervicitis, and conjunctivitis.
	    - Onset of symptoms is typically **48 hours**.
	    - Characteristic urethral discharge is **purulent**.
	    - In males, symptoms often include dysuria and discharge.
	    - Can be a cause of acute urethral syndrome with low bacterial count and pyuria.
	    - Nonulcerative gonorrhea can enhance HIV transmission.
	- **Laboratory Diagnosis:**
	    
	    - **Specimen Collection:**
	        - **Urethral swab** in men is the preferred specimen. Cervical swab in women.
	        - Vaginal swab is not satisfactory.
	        - For urethral swab in men, discharge is collected after cleaning and pressing the base of the penis.
	        - For chronic urethritis with minimal discharge, prostatic massage secretion or morning drop can be collected.
	        - Dacron or rayon swabs are preferred over cotton/alginate.
	    - **Transport:**
	        - Specimens should be transported **immediately**.
	        - If delay is expected, use charcoal-based media (Stuart's, Amies) or commercial systems (JEMBEC, Gono-Pak).
	    - **Microscopy:**
	        - **Gram staining** of urethral exudates reveals **gram-negative intracellular kidney-shaped diplococci**.
	        - Gram staining is **highly specific and sensitive in symptomatic men**.
	        - In females, sensitivity is only ~50% due to commensal _Neisseria_; **culture is recommended** for diagnosis in women.
	        - Routine Gram staining may show **sterile pyuria** (elevated neutrophils without visible organisms) in non-gonococcal urethritis, prompting other tests.
	    - **Culture:**
	        - Required for confirmation, especially in females and for antibiotic susceptibility testing.
	        - Selective media are preferred for genital swabs due to normal flora, such as **Thayer Martin medium** (chocolate agar + antibiotics).
	        - Incubate in a candle jar (provides 5% CO2).
	        - Blood culture and synovial fluid cultures are done for suspected Disseminated Gonococcal Infection (DGI).
	    - **Identification:**
	        - Crucial to differentiate from commensal _Neisseria_ species.
	        - **Catalase positive** and **oxidase positive**.
	        - Ferment **only glucose**.
	        - Automated systems like MALDI-TOF can be used.
	    - **Molecular Methods:**
	        - **Nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT)** of throat swab targeting _N. gonorrhoeae_ is the test of choice for pharyngeal infections.
	        - NAAT can potentially be used for genital specimens, though the source focuses on NAAT for _Chlamydia_. NAATs can also detect _N. gonorrhoeae_.
	- **Treatment:**
	    
	    - Notes mention historical susceptibility and emergence of resistance to various antibiotics (sulfonamides, penicillins, quinolones).
	    - Source indicates **Ceftriaxone** is used for Gonococcal urethritis, Ophthalmia neonatorum (IV single dose), and Acute Bacterial Prostatitis (IM single dose, followed by doxycycline).
	    - For Gonococcal ON, topical bacitracin is also given.
	    - _(Note: Current guidelines often recommend dual therapy for uncomplicated gonorrhoea, typically a cephalosporin plus azithromycin or doxycycline, due to resistance concerns. This specific dual therapy recommendation for uncomplicated urethritis is not detailed in the provided sources, although doxycycline is mentioned for subsequent treatment of Acute Bacterial Prostatitis possibly caused by N. gonorrhoeae, and added for co-existing chlamydial infection in ocular cases)._ This is information outside the provided sources and may need independent verification.
	    - Treatment of all sexual partners is essential.
	- **Prophylaxis/Prevention:**
	    
	    - **No vaccine** available.
	    - General measures include early case detection, treatment of partners, contact tracing, and safe sex practices like condom use.
	- **Clinical Relevance:**
	    
	    - LGV is caused by _Chlamydia trachomatis_, not _N. gonorrhoeae_.
	    - NGU (Non-gonococcal urethritis) is typically caused by _Chlamydia trachomatis_, _Mycoplasma_, _HSV_, _Candida_, or _Trichomonas_, and is more common than gonococcal urethritis. NGU has a longer onset (>1 week), mucous/mucopurulent discharge, and is treated differently (e.g., Doxycycline for _Chlamydia_).
	    - _N. meningitidis_, another pathogenic _Neisseria_ species, causes meningitis. They differ in various aspects.
	    - Accurate diagnosis (especially differentiating from NGU) is crucial for appropriate treatment and preventing complications like DGI (polyarthritis, endocarditis) and pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) in women.
	    - Gonococcal ON in newborns from infected mothers requires prompt recognition and treatment.
4. Lab diagnosis of bacterial vaginosis
	**Bacterial Vaginosis (BV)**
	
	- **Aetiology:**
	    
	    - Caused by _**Gardnerella vaginalis**_ and various anaerobic bacteria.
	- **Laboratory Diagnosis:**
	    
	    - **Specimen:** Vaginal discharge.
	    - **Microscopy:**
	        - **Wet mount:** Demonstrates **clue cells** (vaginal epithelial cells coated with coccobacilli, granular appearance, indistinct borders) and **few leukocytes**. Note: BV is characterised by no associated inflammation.
	        - **Gram stain:** Used for **Nugent's score**, a scoring system counting _G. vaginalis_, _Mobiluncus_, and lactobacilli. A score of **≥7 is diagnostic**. _G. vaginalis_ appears **gram-variable** in smears.
	    - **Culture:**
	        - _G. vaginalis_ requires **enriched media** such as chocolate agar or BHI broth with serum.
	        - Produces minute **hemolytic colonies** on blood agar, incubated aerobically under 5% CO2.
	        - Identification by conventional biochemical tests or automated systems (MALDI-TOF or VITEK).
	        - Culture is listed as a diagnostic method and under "Other laboratory findings".
	    - **Molecular Methods:**
	        - **Broad-range PCR** amplification of 16S rRNA in vaginal fluid, with subsequent identification of specific bacterial species.
	    - **Basic Tests (often performed clinically/point-of-care but included in lab diagnosis discussion):**
	        - **Vaginal discharge pH:** Usually **>4.5**.
	        - **Whiff test:** Accentuation of a distinct **fishy odour** immediately after mixing vaginal secretions with 10% KOH. Positive in >75% of cases.
	- **Clinical Diagnosis Note:** BV is clinically diagnosed by Amsel's criteria, which requires 3 of 4 findings, including thin discharge, pH >4.5, positive Whiff test, and presence of clue cells on wet mount.
	    
	- **Clinical Relevance:** Associated risk factors include coexisting infections such as _Chlamydia trachomatis_ and _Neisseria gonorrhoeae_.
5. Trichomoniasis-pathogenesis and lab diagnosis
	**Trichomoniasis**
	
	- **Aetiology:**
	    
	    - Caused by _**Trichomonas vaginalis**_.
	    - A **sexually transmitted parasite**.
	- **Pathogenesis & Clinical Relevance:**
	    
	    - Primarily causes **urethritis**.
	    - In females, causes various manifestations, most common being **mucopurulent cervicitis**.
	    - Can progress to endometritis, salpingitis, PID, pelvic peritonitis.
	    - Complications include pyosalpinx, endometritis, infertility, low birth weight, and cervical erosions.
	    - Increases the risk of transmission of HIV and HSV-2 infections.
	- **Laboratory Diagnosis:**
	    
	    - **Specimens:** Vaginal discharge, urethral discharge, urine sediment, prostatic secretions. Fresh samples are important for wet mount.
	    - **Direct Microscopy:**
	        - **Wet (saline) mount:** Examination of fresh samples (within 10-20 minutes of collection). Demonstrates **jerky motile trophozoites** and pus cells. Sensitivity variable (40-80%), specificity up to 100%.
	        - **Staining:** Permanent stains (Giemsa, Papanicolaou), acridine orange fluorescent stain, **direct fluorescent antibody test (DFA)**. DFA is more sensitive (70-90%) than wet mount.
	        - **Trophozoite Morphology:** Pear shaped, 7-23 µm, exhibits jerky motility.
	    - **Culture:** Appropriate method. More sensitive than microscopy. Media include **Diamond medium** and **Trichosel medium**.
	    - **Molecular Methods:** Appropriate method. Includes PCR and real-time PCR. More sensitive than microscopy.
