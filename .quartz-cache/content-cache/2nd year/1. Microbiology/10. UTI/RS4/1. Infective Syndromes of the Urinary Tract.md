1. UTI causative organisms and lab diagnosis
	**Urinary Tract Infections (UTI)**
	
	- Infections of the urethra, bladder, kidney, and ureter.
	- Can be **community-acquired** or **healthcare-associated (HA-UTI)**. HA-UTI is often associated with indwelling urinary catheters and multidrug-resistant organisms.
	
	**Causative Organisms**
	
	- **Bacteria** are the major cause of UTI.
	    - **Escherichia coli (Uropathogenic E. coli - UPEC)**: By far the **most common cause** (70% of total cases) of all forms of UTIs (community-acquired, healthcare-associated, upper, and lower).
	    - **Other common endogenous flora**: Gram-negative bacilli (e.g., **Klebsiella**, **Proteus**) and **enterococci**.
	    - **Healthcare-associated UTIs**: Agents are often **multidrug resistant** and include members of Enterobacteriaceae, **staphylococci**, **Pseudomonas**, and **Acinetobacter**.
	    - **Specific mentions:**
	        - _Proteus mirabilis_ and _P. vulgaris_.
	        - _Staphylococcus saprophyticus_.
	        - _Staphylococcus aureus_ (especially via descending route).
	        - _Enterococcus faecalis_ and _E. faecium_, including Vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE).
	        - _Citrobacter_ (often MDR).
	- **Less frequent causes**: Viruses, parasites, and fungi infrequently infect the urinary tract.
	    - **Parasites**: _Schistosoma haematobium_ (urinary schistosomiasis), _Dioctophyme renale_. _Trichomonas vaginalis_ causes urethritis (sexually transmitted).
	    - **Fungi**: **Candida** is a common fungal cause, especially in critically ill or low birth weight infants. _Candida albicans_ is most common (80-90%).
	    - **Others**: _Salmonella_, _Mycobacterium tuberculosis_, _Leptospira_ (via descending route). _Neisseria gonorrhoeae_, _Chlamydia trachomatis_, Herpes simplex virus (associated with acute urethral syndrome).
	
	**Laboratory Diagnosis**
	
	- **Specimen Collection**:
	    - **Urine** is the most common specimen. Collect in a wide mouth screw capped sterile container.
	    - **Clean voided midstream urine**: Most common method. Collected after cleaning urethral meatus/glans.
	    - **Suprapubic aspiration**: **Most ideal specimen**, recommended for infants or patients in coma.
	    - **Catheterized patients**: Collect from the catheter tube (after clamping distally and disinfecting), **not** the uro bag.
	- **Direct Microscopy**:
	    - May show **pus cells**.
	    - **Gram staining** of culture smear reveals morphology (gram-negative bacilli, gram-positive cocci in clusters, etc.).
	    - Wet mount: For detection of _Trichomonas vaginalis_ trophozoites in urine sediment or discharge.
	    - Microscopy of urine sediment for **parasitic eggs** (_S. haematobium_).
	- **Culture**:
	    - **Gold standard** method for bacterial diagnosis.
	    - **Media**: Inoculate onto **CLED agar** (preferred) or MacConkey agar + blood agar.
	    - **Quantitative culture**: Essential to count colony forming units (cfu).
	    - **Significant Bacteriuria (Kass concept)**:
	        - **≥10^5 cfu/mL** of urine is considered **significant** and indicates infection.
	        - Low counts (<10^4 cfu/mL) usually indicate contamination.
	        - **Low counts can be significant** in certain conditions: patient on antibiotics/diuretics, infection with _S. aureus_, pyelonephritis, acute urethral syndrome, sample from suprapubic aspiration. **≥10^3 cfu/mL is significant in symptomatic catheterized patients**.
	    - Culture for _Candida_: Sabouraud dextrose agar.
	- **Identification**:
	    - Based on colony appearance on culture media.
	    - Conventional **biochemical tests** (e.g., ICUT for Enterobacteriaceae) or **automated identification systems** such as **MALDI-TOF** or **VITEK**.
	    - Key features: _E. coli_ (Catalase +, Oxidase -, Indole +), _Klebsiella_ (Urease +, Citrate +), _Proteus_ (Swarming on BA, Urease +).
	- **Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (AST)**:
	    - **Essential to guide appropriate treatment**.
	    - Methods: Disk diffusion (Mueller-Hinton agar) or automated MIC detection (VITEK).
	- **Other Diagnostic Tests**:
	    - **Antibody Coated Bacteria Test**: Differentiates upper (bacteria coated with antibodies) vs. lower UTI (bacteria not coated). Detected by immunofluorescence.
	    - **Molecular methods (PCR/NAAT)**: Used for agents difficult to culture like _Chlamydia trachomatis_, HSV, VRE (van gene). NAAT is the diagnostic assay of choice for chlamydial infection.
	    - Rapid tests (e.g., biochemical for _E. coli_).
2. Significant bacteriuria
	**Significant Bacteriuria**
	
	- Concept developed by Kass.
	- Basis: Normal urine is sterile, but can be contaminated by urethral flora during voiding. Bacterial count is lower in contaminated urine compared to that caused by an infection.
	- **Definition (Kass concept)**:
	    - A count of **≥10⁵ colony forming units (cfu)/mL** of urine is considered **significant** and indicates infection.
	- **Interpretation of Counts**:
	    - ≥10⁵ cfu/mL: **Significant** (indicates infection).
	    - 10⁴ to 10⁵ cfu/mL: Doubtful significance, should be clinically correlated.
	    - <10⁴ cfu/mL: Usually due to contamination from commensal bacteria and is of no significance.
	- **Conditions where Low Counts are Significant**:
	    - Patient on **antibiotic** or on **diuretic** treatment.
	    - Infection with some **gram-positive organisms** such as _S. aureus_.
	    - **Pyelonephritis** and **acute urethral syndrome**.
	    - Sample taken by **suprapubic aspiration**.
	    - In **symptomatic catheterized patients**: A count of **≥10³ CFU/mL** is considered significant.
	- **Methodology**:
	    - Quantitation is done by quantitative culture methods such as standardized loop technique (semi-quantitative) or pour plate method (quantitative). Each colony corresponds to one bacterium in the urine sample.
3. A 46-year-old man from Africa came to the OPD with abdominal pain, hematuria and dysuria. Urine culture showed no growth. Urine wet mount examination revealed oval non-operculated eggs with terminal spine:
	
	a. What is the etiological diagnosis?
	
	b. What is the definitive host and intermediate host?
	
	**Clinical Scenario Analysis**
	
	- **Patient:** 46-year-old male from Africa.
	- **Symptoms:** Abdominal pain, hematuria (blood in urine), dysuria (painful urination).
	- **Laboratory Findings:**
	    - Urine culture: No growth (sterile urine culture).
	    - Urine wet mount: Oval, non-operculated eggs with a terminal spine.
	
	**Diagnosis & Etiology**
	
	a. **Etiological Diagnosis:** * Based on the symptoms (hematuria, dysuria), geographical origin (Africa), sterile urine culture (ruling out common bacterial UTI), and the presence of specific eggs in urine, the probable diagnosis is a **parasitic infection of the urinary tract**. * The characteristic **oval, non-operculated eggs with a terminal spine** seen in the urine wet mount are diagnostic of _**Schistosoma haematobium**_ infection. * This condition is known as **urinary schistosomiasis**.
	
	b. **Definitive Host and Intermediate Host:** * The sources indicate that _S. haematobium_ infects humans and lays eggs that cause pathology in the urinary tract. Prevention involves the eradication of snails. * Based on general parasitology knowledge (not explicitly detailed in the provided sources): * **Definitive Host:** **Man** (Humans) - where the adult worms live and reproduce [Source text implies this through symptoms in humans and egg deposition in human tissues like the bladder]. * **Intermediate Host:** **Freshwater Snails** of the genus _Bulinus_ [Source text mentions snail eradication as a preventive measure].
	
	**Clinical Relevance & Key Points**
	
	- **Sterile urine culture** in the presence of symptoms (especially hematuria and dysuria) should raise suspicion for non-bacterial causes like _Schistosoma haematobium_ in endemic areas.
	- **Microscopy** of urine sediment for characteristic eggs is the primary laboratory diagnostic method. Urine should ideally be collected between 12 noon and 3 pm and concentrated for examination.
	- Chronic infection can lead to significant pathology, including bladder fibrosis, obstructive uropathies (hydroureter, hydronephrosis), and is a risk factor for squamous cell carcinoma of the bladder.
	- Antibody detection is useful for sero-epidemiology, while antigen detection can indicate recent infection and monitor treatment response.
