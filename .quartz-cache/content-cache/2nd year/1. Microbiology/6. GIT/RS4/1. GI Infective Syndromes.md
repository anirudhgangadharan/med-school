1. Traveler's diarrhea
	**Traveler's Diarrhea (TD)**
	
	- **Definition:** The most common travel-related infectious illness.
	- **Epidemiology:**
	    - Occurs in about **20–50%** of people traveling from temperate industrialized countries to tropical regions of Asia, Africa, and Central/South America.
	- **Clinical Presentation:**
	    - Typically begins within the first **3–5 days** of travel.
	    - Characterized by **sudden onset** of abdominal cramps, anorexia, and **watery diarrhea**.
	    - Generally **self-limited**, lasting for **1–5 days**.
	- **Microbial Agents:**
	    - Primarily **bacterial (50–75%)**.
	    - **Enterotoxigenic _Escherichia coli_ (ETEC)** is the **single most important agent** (10–45%).
	        - Pathogenesis involves attachment via **Colonization Factor Antigen (CFA)** and toxin production (heat-labile toxin increasing cAMP, heat-stable toxin increasing cGMP).
	    - **Enteroaggregative _E. coli_ (EAEC)** is another common agent (5–35%).
	    - _**Campylobacter jejuni**_ is more common in Asia (5–25%).
	    - Other bacterial agents include _Shigella_, Enteroinvasive _E.coli_, Non-typhoidal salmonellae, _Aeromonas_, _Plesiomonas_, and _Vibrio cholerae_.
	    - **Viruses (<20%)**.
	        - **Norovirus** is associated with traveling on cruise ships (<10%).
	    - Parasites like _Cryptosporidium_ and _Giardia_ are also mentioned as causing diarrhea, and immunocompromised individuals (e.g., AIDS) are at higher risk for invasive diarrheal illness like salmonellosis and cryptosporidiosis.
	- **Laboratory Diagnosis:**
	    - **Specimen:** Fecal specimen (containing mucus flakes) is collected. Rectal swab may be collected from carriers.
	    - Transport immediately or use transport media like Cary-Blair medium.
	    - **Microscopy:** Wet mount for pus cells, RBCs, parasitic forms. Gram stain not routinely done but may show comma-shaped bacilli (_V. cholerae_) or budding yeast cells (_Candida_) in immunocompromised hosts. Modified acid-fast staining for _Cryptosporidium_, _Isospora_, _Cystoisospora_.
	    - **Bacterial Culture:** Inoculate onto enrichment broth and selective media. Various media mentioned for general enteric pathogens include MacConkey agar, Blood agar, CLED agar, TCBS agar, Skirrow's, Butzler's, Campy BAP for _Campylobacter_. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing is done to guide treatment.
	    - **Antigen Detection:** Various formats available for microbial antigens in stool.
	    - **Molecular Methods:** PCR assays for specific genes. **BioFire FilmArray** is a commercial multiplex PCR that can detect common bacterial, viral, and parasitic diarrheal pathogens directly from stool.
	- **Treatment:**
	    - Mainstay is **fluid replacement**. Oral Rehydration Solution (ORS) is used for mild cases.
	    - Antimicrobials are generally avoided for mild to moderate cases.
	    - Antibiotics may be considered only for **severe Traveler's Diarrhea** (ETEC or EAEC), with **Azithromycin** being indicated. Rifaximin, a non-absorbable antibiotic, can also be given.
	    - Anti-motility agents (loperamide) and adsorbents (bismuth subsalicylate) may be used for moderate diarrhea.
	    - Empirical antibiotics (Ciprofloxacin or levofloxacin) are indicated for severe diarrhea (≥6 unformed stools/day ± fever, tenesmus, blood/fecal leukocytes). Azithromycin is an alternative for _Campylobacter_.
2. Viruses causing diarrhea (gastroenteritis)
	**Viral Gastroenteritis**
	
	- **Definition:** Inflammation of the stomach and intestine caused by viral infection, typically resulting in diarrhea, vomiting, and abdominal pain.
	- **Epidemiology:**
	    - Viruses account for the **majority** of acute infectious gastroenteritis cases worldwide.
	    - Most common among **children**, but can affect all ages.
	- **Clinical Presentation:**
	    - Acute diarrhea usually lasts <14 days.
	    - Abrupt onset, characterized by **vomiting followed by watery diarrhea**, fever, and abdominal pain. Generally self-limited.
	- **Key Viral Agents:**
	    - **Rotavirus:** **Most common cause of severe endemic diarrheal illness in children worldwide**. Damages enterocytes resulting in secretory diarrhea; NSP4 acts as an enterotoxin. Group A is most common in children, Group B causes outbreaks in adults in China. Wheel-shaped morphology on electron microscopy.
	    - **Norovirus (Calicivirus):** Important cause of **epidemic viral gastroenteritis in adults**. Causes outbreaks of vomiting and diarrheal illness in all ages, especially older children and adults. Associated with **travel on cruise ships**.
	    - **Adenovirus:** Specifically serotypes **40 and 41** are listed as common agents causing diarrhea.
	    - **Sapovirus (Calicivirus):** Causes sporadic cases and occasional outbreaks in infants, young children, and the elderly.
	    - Other viruses mentioned include Astrovirus (star-like morphology on EM), Toroviruses, and Picobirnaviruses (role in humans unclear).
	    - Certain **respiratory viruses** (e.g., SARS coronaviruses, Influenza A/H5N1, Influenza A/H1N1) can also cause diarrhea as part of their clinical manifestations.
	- **Laboratory Diagnosis:**
	    - **Specimen:** Feces collected early in the illness is ideal. Stool specimens contain mucus flakes.
	    - **Microscopy:**
	        - Electron microscopy can detect viral particles and morphology (e.g., Rotavirus, Astrovirus, Coronavirus).
	        - Immunoelectron microscopy (IEM) can demonstrate Rotaviruses.
	    - **Antigen Detection:** ELISA is available for detecting **rotavirus antigen** in stool.
	    - **Molecular Methods:** **Gold standard** for diagnosing viral meningitis (and more sensitive than viral cultures).
	        - **PCR-based methods** amplify specific viral DNA or RNA from CSF for definitive diagnosis of viral meningitis. For gastroenteritis, PCR assays can detect specific genes of enteric pathogens.
	        - **Multiplex PCR** (like BioFire FilmArray) can simultaneously detect common viral diarrheal pathogens directly from stool. BioFire FilmArray can detect 14 common agents of meningitis (pyogenic and viral) in CSF.
	    - **Culture:** Viral cultures may not be routinely performed for diagnosis. Caliciviruses are **not cultivable**. Isolation methods mentioned for Coxsackieviruses (tissue culture, though inoculating mice is obsolete).
	- **Treatment:**
	    - **Symptomatic management** is the mainstay.
	    - **Fluid and electrolyte replacement** is crucial. Oral Rehydration Solution (ORS) is used for mild cases.
