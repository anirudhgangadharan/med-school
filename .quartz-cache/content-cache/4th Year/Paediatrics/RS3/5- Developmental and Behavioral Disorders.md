1. Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)—clinical features and management.
**ATTENTION DEFICIT HYPERACTIVITY DISORDER (ADHD)**

**I. DEFINITION AND EPIDEMIOLOGY** Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is defined as a syndrome of hyperactivity, impulsiveness, and inattentiveness that is inappropriate for the child's age.

- **Prevalence:** The prevalence of ADHD in India is estimated to be 1.3 per 1000 children.
- **Gender predilection:** Boys suffer from this disorder thrice as commonly as girls.

**II. CLASSIFICATION** ADHD is clinically classified into three distinct types:

- **Class I:** Hyperactivity, impulsiveness, and inattentiveness combined (the most common type).
- **Class II:** Hyperactivity and impulsiveness only.
- **Class III:** Mainly inattentiveness (an uncommon presentation).

**III. CLINICAL FEATURES AND DIAGNOSTIC CRITERIA** Diagnosis is made clinically, and the DSM-5 criteria require the fulfillment of predefined characteristics in the domains of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity for a minimum of six months. The onset of symptoms must occur up to 12 years of age. A crucial diagnostic requirement is that the symptoms must be present in at least two different social settings and significantly interfere with social, academic, and occupational functioning.

**A. Inattention Domain** (Requires at least six of the following):

- Often fails to give close attention to details or makes careless mistakes in schoolwork or other activities.
- Often has difficulty sustaining attention in tasks or play activities.
- Often does not seem to listen when spoken to directly.
- Often fails to follow through on instructions and fails to finish schoolwork or chores (not due to oppositional behavior).
- Often has difficulty organizing tasks and activities.
- Often avoids, dislikes, or is reluctant to engage in tasks that require sustained mental effort (e.g., homework).
- Often loses things necessary for tasks or activities (e.g., pencils, books, toys).
- Often easily distracted by extraneous stimuli and is forgetful in daily activities.

**B. Hyperactivity and Impulsivity Domain** (Requires at least six of the following):

- _Hyperactivity:_ Often fidgets with hands or squirms in their seat. Often leaves their seat in the classroom or situations where remaining seated is expected. Often runs about or climbs excessively in inappropriate situations. Often has difficulty playing or engaging in leisure activities quietly. Often acts "on the go" or as if "driven by a motor" and talks excessively.
- _Impulsivity:_ Often blurts out answers before questions have been completed. Often has difficulty awaiting their turn. Often interrupts or intrudes on others by bursting into conversations or games.

**C. Associated Comorbidities** Children with ADHD frequently present with behavioral and medical comorbidities. These include sleep problems, enuresis, encopresis, obesity, specific learning disabilities, autism spectrum disorder, tics, conduct disorder, juvenile delinquency, anxiety, and bipolar illness.

**IV. MANAGEMENT** The management of ADHD requires a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach involving the pediatrician, child psychologist, teachers, and parents.

**A. Psychosocial and Behavioral Therapy**

- The cornerstone of management is psychotherapy tailored specifically for each individual child and their family.
- Management aims at tutoring the child to learn acceptable behavior and training them with patience and understanding through behavioral and psychosocial therapy.
- The therapy must be directed not only at the child but also at the parents and the school, requiring close coordination among parents, teachers, and psychologists.

**B. Pharmacotherapy** Medications are utilized in patients who show an inadequate response to psychological interventions.

- **Stimulants:** Drugs such as methylphenidate, dextroamphetamine, and magnesium pemoline are highly efficacious and strongly recommended. Methylphenidate is the primary drug used to address inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity.
- **Non-stimulants:** Atomoxetine is recommended as a preferred drug for adolescent ADHD and in cases of ADHD with comorbidities or contraindications to stimulants. It acts by increasing norepinephrine and dopamine levels, particularly in the prefrontal cortex.
- **Alpha-2 Agonists:** Drugs like clonidine and atomoxetine are specifically indicated to control hyperactivity.
- **Antipsychotics:** Agents such as risperidone or olanzapine are considered for managing associated anxiety, aggression, and repetitive behaviors.
- **Other Medications:** Tricyclic antidepressants (e.g., imipramine, desipramine) and phenothiazines (e.g., thioridazine) can be efficacious. Melatonin is used for sleep-related problems, and iron supplements are administered if an iron deficiency is documented.

**PICA**

**DEFINITION** Pica (derived from the Latin word _magpie_) is defined as the persistent ingestion of non-nutritive, non-edible substances for a period of at least 1 month.

- **Common ingested substances:** Earth, dust, clay, sand, plaster, charcoal, paint flakes from walls, fabrics, and ice (pagophagia).
- **Developmental context:** The behaviour is considered inappropriate to the child's developmental level and cultural practices. While an inclination for mouthing and tasting objects is considered a normal developmental variation up to 2 years of age, its persistence beyond this age constitutes a deviant behaviour that requires medical evaluation.

**ETIOLOGY AND PREDISPOSING FACTORS** The exact aetiology of pica often involves a complex interplay of nutritional, environmental, and psychological factors.

- **Nutritional Factors:** It is strongly associated with malnutrition, iron deficiency, and other specific vitamin and mineral deficiencies.
- **Psychosocial Factors:** Pica is frequently encountered in children from lower socioeconomic strata and is associated with poor parental supervision, parental neglect, family conflict, and psychosocial stress (e.g., maternal deprivation or child abuse and neglect).
- **Developmental and Psychiatric Factors:** Children with developmental delay, mental retardation, and other behavioural or psychosomatic disorders are highly predisposed to developing pica.

**CLINICAL FEATURES AND COMPLICATIONS (HIGH YIELD)** Children with pica often present with the following manifestations and secondary complications:

- **Anaemia:** Patients are frequently anaemic, primarily due to iron-deficiency anaemia (IDA), which may act as both a cause and an effect of the condition.
- **Parasitosis:** There is a high incidence of concurrent intestinal parasitic and worm infestations.
- **Lead Poisoning (Plumbism):** Ingestion of lead-based paint flakes or plaster puts the child at a significant risk for chronic lead poisoning, which can lead to dangerous neurological sequelae such as lead encephalopathy.
- **Bezoar Formation:** In severely disturbed children, pica may manifest as pulling out and swallowing their own hair (trichotillomania). This hair collects in the stomach to form a massive lump known as a _trichobezoar_ (hairball), which becomes palpable in the upper abdomen and causes dyspepsia.
- **Neurological Signs:** Some children may develop pseudotumour cerebri as a complication.

**MANAGEMENT** The management of pica requires a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach addressing the underlying nutritional and psychosocial deficits.

**1. Nutritional Rehabilitation and Medical Therapy**

- **Mineral and Vitamin Replacement:** Prompt treatment of associated mineral and vitamin deficiencies is essential. Iron supplementation must be initiated to correct iron deficiency anaemia.
- **Deworming:** The child must undergo routine stool microscopy and receive appropriate anthelmintic therapy to eradicate intestinal parasitic infestations.
- **Screening for Toxins:** Mandatory screening for blood lead levels must be conducted to rule out lead poisoning.

**2. Psychosocial and Behavioural Interventions**

- **Behaviour Modification:** Therapy is directed at behaviour modification to extinguish the habit.
- **Alleviating Stress:** Identifying and alleviating psychosocial stress within the home environment, improving the parent-child relationship, and ensuring adequate parental supervision are critical steps.
- **Psychotherapy:** In cases where pica is deeply rooted in psychosomatic problems or emotional deprivation, formal psychotherapy for the child and counselling for the parents are of significant value.

3. Temper tantrums
**TEMPER TANTRUMS**

**DEFINITION AND CONCEPT** Temper tantrums are a child's behavioral response to physical or emotional challenges, primarily utilized as an attention-seeking tactic.

**AGE OF OCCURRENCE**

- **Onset:** They typically begin between 18 to 36 months of age. They are recognized as a common behavioral problem in the infancy and preschool age groups.
- **Resolution:** The episodes usually gradually subside by the age of 3 to 6 years.

**CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS** The child exhibits disruptive and aggressive behaviors during an episode, which classically include:

- Crying and yelling.
- Kicking, hitting, and pushing.
- Biting.
- Throwing objects.
- Head banging.

**ASSOCIATED CLINICAL CONDITIONS** While tantrums are often a developmental phase in normal children, they can be prominently associated with certain conditions:

- **Breath-Holding Spells:** Children with breath-holding spells have a high incidence of temper tantrums and other behavioral disorders.
- **Emotional Deprivation (Psychosocial Dwarfism):** Sudden tantrums, along with crying spasms, enuresis, and insomnia, can be seen in children subjected to severe emotional deprivation.
- **Autism Spectrum Disorders:** Severe temper tantrums and aggression can be a comorbid behavioral manifestation in autistic children.

**MANAGEMENT** The cornerstone of managing temper tantrums revolves around behavioral modification and parental counseling.

- **Parental Attitude and Response:** Parents must be counseled to handle the behavior strategically by remaining calm, firm, and consistent. The primary goal is to ensure that the child is unable to take advantage of the behavior to fulfill their demands.
- **Safety Precautions:** During a violent tantrum, the immediate priority is to protect the child from injuring himself or others.
- **Behavioral Techniques:** The use of "time out" techniques and distraction are highly useful and effective strategies.
- **Pharmacotherapy:** Medications are generally not indicated for routine temper tantrums. However, if the tantrums and aggression are part of a severe underlying pervasive developmental disorder like autism, specific medications (such as neuroleptics like risperidone or olanzapine, or dopamine antagonists like haloperidol) may occasionally be warranted to relieve the comorbidity.
4. Breath holding spells (cyanotic spells)—management.
**BREATH-HOLDING SPELLS (CYANOTIC SPELLS)**

**INTRODUCTION** Breath-holding spells (BHS) are a common situational and psychosomatic disorder in the pediatric age group, typically with an onset between 6 and 18 months of age. The spells are broadly classified into two types: cyanotic and pallid, with the cyanotic type being thrice as frequent. Cyanotic spells are classically provoked by a disciplinary conflict, anger, or frustration, where the child uses the attack or its threat to assert themselves or express protest.

**MANAGEMENT** The management of breath-holding spells requires a systematic approach focusing primarily on parental counseling, behavioral modification, and addressing any underlying medical triggers.

**1. Immediate Care During the Spell**

- **Positioning:** The child should be immediately turned sideways; this allows oral secretions to drain properly and prevents aspiration.
- **Avoid Upright Posture:** Parents must be strictly instructed _not_ to pick the child up during a spell. Lifting the child decreases blood flow to the brain, which can exacerbate cerebral anoxia.
- **Composure:** Caregivers must remain calm during the event and allow the spell to pass without panic.

**2. Psychosocial and Behavioral Intervention (The Cornerstone)**

- **Parental Reassurance:** The most essential component of management is reassuring the family that the condition, though frightening to witness, is benign and self-limiting. Parents should be informed that the frequency of spells decreases as the child grows, and almost all children become symptom-free by 5 to 6 years of age.
- **Behavioral Modification:** Therapy must be directed at identifying the causative and precipitating factors (e.g., disciplinary conflicts) and treating these through counseling or psychotherapy.
- **Avoiding Secondary Gain:** The family must be advised to be highly consistent in handling the child. They should avoid exhibiting undue concern and must _never_ give in to the child's demands if the spell was provoked by anger or frustration. Yielding to the child reinforces the behavior.

**3. Medical and Pharmacological Therapy**

- **Treatment of Iron Deficiency Anemia (High Yield):** There is a well-known, strong association between breath-holding spells and iron deficiency anemia (IDA). Anemia is believed to adversely affect the autonomic nervous system's compensatory functions, directly contributing to cerebral anoxia during a spell. Evaluating for and promptly treating any coexisting IDA is mandatory, as iron therapy alone may promptly and fully stop the spells in many patients.
- **Routine Drug Therapy:** In general, routine pharmacological therapy is of insignificant value for typical cyanotic breath-holding spells.
- **Piracetam:** In refractory cases, therapeutic response to piracetam has been noted to be quite gratifying, although it is an expensive agent.
- _(Note for completeness):_ While cyanotic spells do not typically require vagolytic drugs, very frequently occurring _pallid_ breath-holding spells (which are mediated by cardiac asystole/vagal tone) may respond to oral atropine sulfate at a dose of 0.01 mg thrice daily.
