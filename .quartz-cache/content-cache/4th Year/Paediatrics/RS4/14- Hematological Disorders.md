1. Classify anemia and write a short note on the investigation of megaloblastic anemia.

**CLASSIFICATION OF ANEMIA**

Anemia is defined as a quantitative or qualitative deficiency of red cells or hemoglobin concentration in circulation. It can be classified based on etiology/pathogenesis or based on red blood cell (RBC) morphology.

**1. Etiological and Pathogenetic Classification** This classification divides anemia into two broad categories: impaired RBC production and increased RBC destruction.

- **I. Disorders of Impaired RBC Production**
    
    - **a. Deficiency Anemias:**
        - Iron deficiency anemia.
        - Nutritional megaloblastic anemia (Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency).
        - Mixed deficiency states (dimorphic anemia).
    - **b. Bone Marrow Failure:**
        - Aplastic anemia: Congenital (e.g., Fanconi anemia) and acquired (idiopathic or secondary).
        - Selective red cell aplasia: Congenital (Diamond-Blackfan anemia) and acquired (Transient erythroblastopenia of childhood).
        - Marrow replacement: Myelofibrosis, osteopetrosis, and malignancies (e.g., leukemia).
    - **c. Impaired Erythropoietin Production:**
        - Chronic renal failure.
        - Hypothyroidism and hypopituitarism.
        - Chronic malnutrition.
    - **d. Miscellaneous:**
        - Congenital dyserythropoietic anemias.
        - Erythropoietic porphyria.
- **II. Disorders of Increased RBC Destruction (Hemolytic Anemias)**
    
    - **a. RBC Membrane Defects:** Hereditary spherocytosis.
    - **b. Defects of Hemoglobin Synthesis:**
        - Quantitative defects (Thalassemias): Beta, alpha, and delta-beta thalassemias.
        - Qualitative defects (Hemoglobinopathies): Sickle cell disease, HbE disease.
        - Combined defects: HbS-beta thalassemia.
    - **c. Defects of RBC Enzymes:** Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G-6-PD) deficiency, pyruvate kinase deficiency.
    - **d. Acquired Defects:**
        - Immune hemolysis: Warm and cold antibody types, ABO and Rh-incompatibility.
        - Infections: Malaria, kala-azar, acute bacterial infections.

**2. Morphological Classification (Based on Red Cell Indices)** Using the Mean Corpuscular Volume (MCV), anemias are classified into three distinct categories based on red cell size.

- **Microcytic Anemia (MCV < 80 fl):** Characterized by small red blood cells. Common causes include iron deficiency anemia, thalassemia trait, lead poisoning, and anemia of chronic disease.
- **Normocytic Anemia (MCV 80-100 fl):** Characterized by normal-sized red blood cells. Causes include acute hemorrhage, anemia of chronic disease, renal failure, and transient erythroblastopenia of childhood.
- **Macrocytic Anemia (MCV > 100 fl):** Characterized by enlarged red blood cells. Common causes include Vitamin B12 deficiency, folic acid deficiency (megaloblastic anemias), and bone marrow failure syndromes.

---

**INVESTIGATION OF MEGALOBLASTIC ANEMIA**

Megaloblastic anemia is a distinct type of macrocytic anemia characterized by impaired DNA synthesis, leading to delayed nuclear maturation and the presence of large, nucleated red cell precursors (megaloblasts) in the bone marrow. It is most commonly caused by Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) or folic acid deficiency.

**1. Complete Blood Count (CBC) and Red Cell Indices**

- **Hemoglobin & RBC Count:** Both are typically reduced.
- **MCV & MCH:** The Mean Corpuscular Volume is elevated (MCV > 110 fl, often 100-120 fl), which is highly suggestive of megaloblastic anemia. The Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin (MCH) is usually elevated, but MCHC remains normal.
- **Other Cell Lines:** Because the deficiency affects all hematopoietic cell lines, leukopenia and thrombocytopenia are frequently observed. Pancytopenia is reported in 40-70% of cases.
- **Reticulocyte Count:** Typically normal or reduced, indicating ineffective erythropoiesis.

**2. Peripheral Blood Smear Examination**

- **Erythrocytes:** Reveals macrocytic oval red cells (macrocytes), marked anisocytosis, and poikilocytosis.
- **Leukocytes:** The presence of **hypersegmented neutrophils** (neutrophils with >5 nuclear lobes) in more than 5% of neutrophils is a hallmark and highly characteristic finding of megaloblastic anemia.

**3. Biochemical Studies (Vitamins and Metabolites)** To differentiate between folate and Vitamin B12 deficiency, specific serum assays are necessary before starting any supplementation.

- **Vitamin B12 and Folate Levels:** Serum cobalamin and folate levels will be reduced in their respective deficiency states.
- **Serum Homocysteine:** Elevated in both folic acid and Vitamin B12 deficiency.
- **Serum Methylmalonic Acid (MMA):** This is a highly specific diagnostic differentiator. High serum levels of MMA are seen **only** in Vitamin B12 deficiency, while they remain normal in isolated folate deficiency.
- **Other Chemistry:** Serum chemistry often shows elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and mild jaundice due to the destruction of fragile, abnormal red cells in the bone marrow (ineffective erythropoiesis).

**4. Bone Marrow Examination**

- **Indications:** A bone marrow aspirate should be performed in any child presenting with more than one abnormal hematological cell line (e.g., pancytopenia) to definitively rule out leukemia, myelodysplastic syndrome, and aplastic anemia.
- **Findings:** The marrow is hypercellular (hyperplastic). The classic finding is the presence of megaloblasts. The erythroid and granulocytic cell precursors show striking **nuclear-cytoplasmic asynchrony** (the cytoplasm matures, but the nucleus remains large and immature due to impaired DNA synthesis).

**5. Special Tests**

- **Schilling Test:** Once used to measure Vitamin B12 absorption from the gut. A tracer dose of radioactive Vitamin B12 is given to diagnose intrinsic factor deficiency (pernicious anemia) or ileal malabsorption (an excretion of < 5% indicates defective absorption).
- **FIGLU Test:** Formiminoglutamic acid (FIGLU) excretion in urine was historically used for folate deficiency, but it is currently rarely performed.

2. Thalassemia - major / trait - clinical features, investigations (describe and interpret) management

**THALASSEMIA**

**Definition and Pathophysiology** Thalassemia is an autosomal recessive inherited hemoglobinopathy caused by a mutation in the globin gene, leading to an alteration in the rate of globin chain production. In $\beta$-thalassemia, there is decreased ($\beta^+$) or absent ($\beta^0$) production of beta-globin chains. This leads to an excess of alpha chains that precipitate and destroy red blood cell precursors in the marrow (ineffective erythropoiesis) and shorten the lifespan of mature red blood cells (hemolysis).

---

**THALASSEMIA MAJOR (Homozygous)**

**Clinical Features** Infants are usually normal at birth due to the protective effect of fetal hemoglobin (HbF, $\alpha_2\gamma_2$). Symptoms begin to manifest between 6 to 12 months of age when $\gamma$-chain production turns off and the defective $\beta$-chain synthesis fails to take over.

- **General:** Progressive severe anemia, severe pallor, irritability, poor feeding, intolerance to exercise, and profound growth retardation/failure to thrive.
- **Hepatosplenomegaly:** Massive enlargement of the spleen (which may reach into the pelvis) and liver due to extramedullary hematopoiesis and increased destruction of defective RBCs.
- **Thalassemic (Hemolytic) Facies:** Due to marked erythroid hyperplasia expanding the bone marrow cavity. Characterized by frontal bossing, prominent facial bones/maxilla exposing the upper teeth, depressed nasal bridge, and malocclusion of teeth.
- **Skin:** Icterus (mild to moderate jaundice) and increased skin pigmentation (slate/bronze color) due to melanin deposition and hemosiderin (iron overload).
- **Cardiac:** Progressive myocardial iron deposition and chronic anemia can lead to cardiomegaly, heart failure, pericarditis, and fatal arrhythmias by the second decade of life.

**Investigations (Description & Interpretation)**

- **Complete Blood Count (CBC) & Indices:**
    - _Finding:_ Severe microcytic hypochromic anemia with Hemoglobin usually ranging between 2 to 8 g/dL. Mean Corpuscular Volume (MCV) < 80 fl and Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin (MCH) < 27 pg. Reticulocyte count is elevated (5–8%) indicating a hemolytic process.
- **Peripheral Blood Smear (PBS):**
    - _Finding:_ Marked anisocytosis and poikilocytosis, microcytic hypochromic red blood cells, target cells, fragmented erythrocytes (schistocytes), polychromatophilia, and basophilic stippling.
    - _Interpretation:_ Characteristically shows a large number of nucleated red blood cells (normoblasts), reflecting intense but ineffective marrow effort.
- **Hemoglobin Electrophoresis or HPLC (High Performance Liquid Chromatography):**
    - _Finding (Diagnostic):_ Absence or profound reduction of adult hemoglobin (HbA). Striking elevation of Fetal Hemoglobin (HbF), which always exceeds 40% and is usually >70%. HbA2 levels are variable and not needed for the diagnosis of major.
- **Radiology (X-rays):**
    - _Skull X-ray:_ "Hair-on-end" appearance due to vertical striations from the widening of the diploic space and atrophy of the outer table.
    - _Long Bones/Hands:_ Thinning of the cortex, widening of the medullary cavity, and coarsening of trabeculations.
- **Iron Studies:**
    - _Finding:_ Markedly elevated serum iron and serum ferritin (ferritin > 1000 µg/L indicates significant iron overload requiring chelation). Total Iron Binding Capacity (TIBC) is saturated.

**Management**

- **Blood Transfusion Therapy:** The mainstay of medical management. Regular administration of packed red blood cells (leukocyte-depleted to prevent alloimmunization) every 3-5 weeks.
    - _Goal:_ Maintain pre-transfusion hemoglobin levels between 9–10 g/dL. This promotes normal growth, suppresses excessive endogenous ineffective erythropoiesis, and prevents bone deformities.
- **Iron Chelation Therapy:** Essential to prevent fatal iron overload (hemochromatosis) from repeated transfusions. Initiated when serum ferritin is > 1000 µg/L or after 2–3 years of transfusions.
    - _Deferoxamine (DFO):_ The gold standard. Given as a slow subcutaneous infusion (40–60 mg/kg/day) over 8–12 hours for 5–6 days a week.
    - _Deferasirox:_ Oral chelator given once daily (20–30 mg/kg/day).
    - _Deferiprone:_ Oral chelator (50–75 mg/kg/day), highly effective for cardiac iron removal but carries a risk of neutropenia and arthropathy.
- **Supportive Therapy:** Regular folic acid supplementation. **Strict avoidance of iron preparations.** Vitamin C (small doses) can be given to enhance iron excretion, and drinking tea with meals reduces dietary iron absorption.
- **Splenectomy:** Indicated if there is severe hypersplenism (manifested by a rapidly increasing transfusion requirement > 200–250 mL/kg/year) or massive splenomegaly causing mechanical discomfort. Delayed until >5 years of age. Requires pre-splenectomy vaccines (Pneumococcal, Meningococcal, Hib) and post-splenectomy prophylactic oral penicillin to prevent overwhelming sepsis.
- **Curative Therapy:** Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation (HSCT) or allogeneic bone marrow transplant from an HLA-matched sibling is the only definitive cure. Best outcomes are achieved if done early before the onset of iron-induced organ damage.

---

**THALASSEMIA TRAIT / MINOR (Heterozygous)**

**Clinical Features**

- Patients are carriers with a single gene mutation and are typically entirely asymptomatic.
- May present with mild anemia and mild icterus, usually discovered incidentally on routine blood counts.

**Investigations (Description & Interpretation)**

- **CBC & Indices:**
    - _Finding:_ Mild anemia (Hemoglobin 10–12 g/dL) or normal.
    - _Interpretation:_ Marked microcytosis (MCV < 80 fl) and hypochromia (MCH < 27 pg) that is out of proportion to the degree of anemia. Uniquely, the **Red Blood Cell (RBC) count is elevated** and the Red Cell Distribution Width (RDW) is normal or only mildly increased (differentiating it from Iron Deficiency Anemia where RBC count is low and RDW is high).
- **Peripheral Blood Smear:** Mild hypochromia, microcytosis, and the presence of target cells. Significant anisopoikilocytosis is usually absent.
- **Hemoglobin Electrophoresis / HPLC:**
    - _Finding (Diagnostic):_ Elevated levels of Hemoglobin A2 (HbA2 > 3.5%). HbF may be normal or slightly increased. Concomitant iron deficiency can falsely lower HbA2 levels, leading to a missed diagnosis.
- **Iron Profile:** Serum iron and ferritin levels are normal or mildly elevated (unlike Iron Deficiency Anemia where they are low).

**Management**

- **No specific medical treatment or follow-up is required** for the trait itself.
- **Contraindication:** Iron therapy should _not_ be prescribed unless a definitive concomitant iron deficiency is biochemically confirmed, to avoid iatrogenic iron overload.
- **Genetic Counseling:** Crucial step. Adolescents and adults should be made aware of their carrier status. Antenatal screening and prenatal diagnosis (via chorionic villus sampling at 10–12 weeks) must be offered if both partners are identified as thalassemia carriers, as there is a 25% risk of having a child with Thalassemia Major.

3. Hemophilia - clinical features , treatment and prevention
**HEMOPHILIA**

**Definition and Classification** Hemophilia is the most common hereditary clotting disorder, accounting for 90-95% of such bleeding diatheses. It is characterized by a deficiency of specific plasma coagulation factors, leading to prolonged bleeding.

- **Hemophilia A (Classical Hemophilia):** Caused by a deficiency of Factor VIII (Antihemophilic factor). It is an X-linked recessive disorder occurring almost exclusively in males, while females act as carriers.
- **Hemophilia B (Christmas Disease):** Caused by a deficiency of Factor IX (Plasma thromboplastin component).
- **Hemophilia C:** Caused by a deficiency of Factor XI.

Based on the level of the deficient clotting factor, hemophilia is classified by severity:

- **Severe:** < 1% factor level.
- **Moderate:** 1–4% factor level.
- **Mild:** 5–40% factor level.

**Clinical Features** Manifestations directly depend on the extent of the clotting factor deficiency.

- **Neonatal Period:** The earliest presentation may be prolonged bleeding from the umbilical cord within a few days of birth, or excessive hemorrhage following circumcision.
- **Infancy and Childhood:** Patients exhibit a tendency for excessive bleeding, large contusions, or hematomas following minor trauma. Epistaxis and prolonged bleeding after tooth extraction or tonsillectomy are common.
- **Hemarthrosis (Hallmark Sign):** Deep bleeding into the joints (most frequently the knee, ankle, and elbow) is a characteristic and severely debilitating feature. The affected joint becomes swollen, tender, and painful on passive movements. Recurrent bleeding into the same joint leads to synovial thickening, degenerative changes, and eventually a crippled, immobile "fixed joint" (hemophilic arthropathy).
- **Internal/Deep Bleeds:** Bleeding can occur anywhere, including the gastrointestinal tract, genitourinary tract (hematuria), liver, spleen, or the iliopsoas muscle. Spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage is a severe, life-threatening complication that can lead to permanent disability or death.
- **Skin:** While widespread ecchymosis and deep hematomas occur, superficial petechiae do _not_ occur in hemophilia.

**Treatment** **1. Specific Replacement Therapy** The primary goal is the rapid replacement of the missing clotting factor.

- **Factor Concentrates:** Recombinant factor VIII or IX concentrates are the treatment of choice as they carry no risk of transfusion-transmitted infections.
- **Dose Calculation (Hemophilia A):** Dose of Factor VIII (units) = Desired rise (%) × body weight (kg) × 1.3 to 1.5. Because the half-life of Factor VIII is 8-12 hours, it is administered every 12 hours. For life-threatening bleeds (e.g., intracranial), the target is 100% correction, requiring 50 U/kg every 12 hours for 7–10 days.
- **Alternatives:** If specific concentrates are unavailable, Cryoprecipitate (rich in Factor VIII, fibrinogen, and von Willebrand factor) or Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP) can be used. For Hemophilia B, FFP or specific Factor IX concentrates (half-life of 24 hours) are utilized.

**2. Management of Inhibitors**

- The most serious complication of therapy is the development of alloantibodies (inhibitors) that neutralize the clotting factors.
- These patients require "bypassing agents" such as Recombinant Activated Factor VIIa (Novoseven) or Activated Prothrombin Complex (FEIBA).
- **Emicizumab**, a newer monoclonal antibody, acts as a non-factor product bypassing Factor VIII and can be given subcutaneously for prophylaxis in Hemophilia A with or without inhibitors. Immune tolerance induction therapy may also be attempted.

**3. Adjunctive Pharmacotherapy**

- For minor mucosal bleeding or dental extractions, antifibrinolytic agents like Epsilon-aminocaproic acid or Tranexamic acid are effective adjuncts.
- Desmopressin (DDAVP) and Danazol may be used in mild cases to transiently raise Factor VIII levels.

**4. Local and Supportive Measures (RICE Therapy)**

- **R**est and immobilization of the affected part.
- **I**ce (local cold application to induce vasoconstriction).
- **C**ompression via pressure bandages.
- **E**levation of the bleeding part.
- Local application of thrombin powder or foam.
- Following acute hemarthrosis, physiotherapy is crucial to prevent joint ankylosis and contractures.

**Prevention** **1. Secondary Prevention and Prophylaxis**

- **Routine Prophylaxis:** Routine prophylactic factor replacement (e.g., 15–20 IU/kg of Factor VIII, 2 to 3 times a week) is highly recommended to prevent spontaneous bleeds, avoid joint deformities, and allow a normal quality of life.
- **Trauma Avoidance:** Protective padding of cribs, avoiding contact sports, and maintaining a healthy body weight to reduce joint stress.
- **Contraindicated Medications:** Strict avoidance of intramuscular injections, aspirin, and other NSAIDs that impair platelet function.
- **Immunization:** Hepatitis B vaccination is mandatory due to lifetime exposure to blood products; all vaccines must be given via the subcutaneous route to prevent deep muscle hematomas.

**2. Primary Prevention (Genetic)**

- **Carrier Detection:** Female carriers can be identified through genetic DNA mutation studies or by demonstrating a Factor VIII C to FVIII Ag ratio of < 0.6.
- **Antenatal Diagnosis:** Can be offered to families with a known history. It is performed via chorionic villus sampling in the first trimester, amniocentesis (at 17 weeks), or fetal blood sampling via fetoscopy (at 18-20 weeks) utilizing DNA probes and PCR techniques. Genetic counseling is essential.
