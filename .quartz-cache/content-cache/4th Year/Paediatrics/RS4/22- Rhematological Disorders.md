**A. MOST PROBABLE DIAGNOSIS**

**Acute Rheumatic Fever (ARF) with Carditis and Migratory Polyarthritis**

- **Reasoning:** The child presents with a classic history of antecedent sore throat (streptococcal pharyngitis) 3 weeks prior. This is followed by a classic migratory polyarthritis (moving from the knee to the ankle to the wrist) and clinical evidence of active carditis (tachycardia and a pansystolic murmur in the mitral area, indicating mitral regurgitation). Rheumatic fever is a hypersensitivity reaction to beta-hemolytic streptococci of the Lansfield group.

---

**B. CRITERIA TO DIAGNOSE ACUTE RHEUMATIC FEVER**

The diagnosis is established using the **Revised Jones Criteria** _(information from outside the provided sources)_. Diagnosis requires evidence of a preceding Group A Streptococcal (GAS) infection **PLUS** either **2 Major Criteria** OR **1 Major + 2 Minor Criteria**.

- **Essential Requirement:**
    - Evidence of antecedent GAS infection (e.g., elevated ASO titer, positive throat swab culture, or positive rapid streptococcal antigen test).
- **Major Criteria (JONES):**
    1. **J - Joint involvement:** Migratory polyarthritis involving large joints (present in this patient).
    2. **O - (Heart shape): Carditis:** Involving endocardium, myocardium, or pericardium (present in this patient as a mitral murmur).
    3. **N - Nodules:** Subcutaneous, firm, painless nodules over extensor surfaces.
    4. **E - Erythema Marginatum:** Macular, non-pruritic, serpiginous rash with central clearing.
    5. **S - Sydenham Chorea:** Purposeless, involuntary, rapid neurological movements.
- **Minor Criteria:**
    1. **Clinical:** Fever, Arthralgia (arthralgia cannot be counted if polyarthritis is used as a major criterion).
    2. **Laboratory:** Elevated acute phase reactants (High ESR, elevated CRP).
    3. **ECG:** Prolonged PR interval (cannot be counted if carditis is used as a major criterion).

---

**C. MANAGEMENT OF THE CHILD**

**1. Eradication of Streptococcal Infection (Primary Prophylaxis)**

- Even if the throat swab is negative at presentation, the streptococcal trigger must be eradicated to prevent ongoing antigenic stimulation.
- **Drug of choice:** Penicillin. Oral penicillin, amoxicillin, or erythromycin is given for 10 to 14 days.

**2. Anti-inflammatory Therapy (For Arthritis and Carditis)**

- **Aspirin (Acetylsalicylic acid):** Administered at a dose of 65 to 130 mg/kg/day orally in divided doses for rheumatic fever. It provides dramatic and rapid relief for migratory polyarthritis.
- **Corticosteroids:** _(Outside information)_ Indicated specifically for severe carditis (e.g., carditis with cardiomegaly or congestive heart failure). Prednisolone is typically used for a few weeks, followed by a gradual taper. Aspirin is usually overlapped during the taper to prevent clinical rebound.

**3. General Measures & Supportive Care** _(Outside information)_

- Strict bed rest during the acute phase, especially in the presence of active carditis, to reduce myocardial workload.
- If Congestive Cardiac Failure (CCF) develops secondary to severe mitral regurgitation, it is managed with fluid/salt restriction, diuretics, and appropriate afterload reducers.

**4. Secondary Prophylaxis (Prevention of Recurrences)**

- To prevent subsequent streptococcal infections and progressive valvular heart damage, long-term antibiotic prophylaxis is mandatory.
- **Regimen:** Long-acting penicillin (e.g., Benzathine Penicillin G) is administered intramuscularly every 3 to 4 weeks.
- _Duration (Outside information):_ For a patient with carditis and residual heart disease (like persistent mitral regurgitation), secondary prophylaxis is typically continued for 10 years after the last episode or until the age of 40 years (whichever is longer), and sometimes lifelong.
