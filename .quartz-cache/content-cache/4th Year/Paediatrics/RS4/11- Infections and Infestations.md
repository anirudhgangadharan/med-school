1. A 10-month-old unimmunized child presents with fever, cold and cough of 5 days duration. He developed a maculopapular rash starting on day 4 of fever which started on face and progressed to involve the entire body.
	a. List the viruses causing maculopapular rash.
	 b. Describe the clinical features of measles. 
	 c. Complications and management of measles.


**A. VIRUSES CAUSING MACULOPAPULAR RASH**

- **Measles (Rubeola) Virus:** A single-stranded RNA virus belonging to the paramyxovirus group.
- **Rubella Virus (German Measles):** A myxovirus.
- **Human Herpes Virus II:** Causes Roseola Infantum.
- **Paravirus (Parvovirus B19):** Causes Erythema Infectiosum or Fifth disease,.
- **Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV):** Causes Infectious Mononucleosis; a maculopapular rash often becomes manifest, especially following the administration of ampicillin,,.
- **Dengue Virus:** A flavivirus,.
- **Enteroviruses:** Such as ECHO and Coxsackie viruses.
- **Adenoviruses**.

**B. CLINICAL FEATURES OF MEASLES**

The average incubation period of measles is 11 days, with a range of 10 to 12 days. The clinical course is divided into three distinct stages:

**1. Prodromal (Catarrhal) Phase (3-5 days)**

- Characterized by upper respiratory catarrh, fever, malaise, conjunctivitis, and photophobia.
- Posterior cervical lymphadenopathy may be present.
- **Koplik’s Spots:** This is the **pathognomonic sign** of measles. They are fine, tiny grain-like papules on a faint erythematous base. They first appear on the buccal mucosa opposite the first lower molar and spread to other oral sites, typically disappearing by the 5th day (usually a day after the rash appears).

**2. Eruptive Phase**

- The rash appears 3 to 5 days after the onset of the illness.
- It is a pink, blotchy irregular macular erythema that fades on pressure and quickly darkens, blending into large red patches of varying sizes and shapes.
- **Progression:** It appears first on the face and retroauricular areas, spreading downwards to the neck, trunk, and limbs over the next 3 to 4 days,.
- **Distribution:** It has a tendency to become confluent over the upper part of the body, while remaining discrete on the lower part.
- With the appearance of the rash, the fever begins to regress but takes about 3 days to completely disappear.

**3. Convalescent Phase**

- The rash lasts for 4 to 7 days and starts fading from the 3rd to 4th day in the exact order of its appearance,.
- It leaves behind a fine shedding (desquamation) of the superficial skin, which characteristically spares the hands and feet,.
- A light-brown pigmentation (staining) is left behind after the rash fades,.
- Fever and other constitutional symptoms disappear during this phase.

**C. COMPLICATIONS AND MANAGEMENT OF MEASLES**

**Complications** The primary danger of measles lies in its complications, which significantly increase morbidity and mortality, especially in malnourished children:

- **Respiratory:** Otitis media is the most frequent complication. Others include tracheobronchitis, laryngotracheobronchitis, bronchiolitis, bronchopneumonia, and subcutaneous emphysema. A rare but fatal complication is **Giant-cell pneumonia**.
- **Gastrointestinal:** Stomatitis, enteritis, appendicitis, and cancrum oris (noma), the latter being particularly seen in malnourished children.
- **Neurological (Immediate):** Encephalitis occurs in about 0.1% of cases, often leaving sequelae like mental retardation,. Other rare manifestations include Guillain-Barré syndrome, cerebral thrombophlebitis, hemiplegia, and retrobulbar neuritis.
- **Neurological (Late):** **Subacute Sclerosing Panencephalitis (SSPE)**, a universally fatal degenerative CNS disease that occurs on average 6 years after the measles attack,.
- **Ophthalmic:** Keratitis and corneal ulceration, which occur secondary to measles-induced Vitamin A deficiency.
- **Others:**
    - Activation of pre-existing, dormant tuberculosis due to a transient loss of hypersensitivity (anergy),.
    - Hemorrhagic ("black") measles, marked by a stormy onset, high fever, coma, and severe bleeding diathesis.
    - Severe malnutrition.
    - Acute glomerulonephritis, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, myocarditis, and transient ECG changes.

**Management** There is no specific antiviral therapy available for measles; treatment is entirely symptomatic and supportive.

- **General Measures:** Immediate isolation of the affected child, bed rest, and maintenance of proper fluid and dietary intake.
- **Symptomatic Relief:** Administration of antipyretics for fever, cough sedatives, vasoconstrictor nasal drops for severe catarrh, and antihistamines to relieve itching caused by the rash.
- **Local Care:** Strict attention must be paid to the hygiene of the eyes and the oral mucous membranes.
- **Vitamin A Supplementation:** Highly recommended to reduce the overall morbidity of the disease and to prevent sight-threatening complications like corneal ulceration.
- **Antibiotics:** Routine antibiotics are not indicated. They should only be administered if a secondary bacterial infection (e.g., bronchopneumonia, otitis media) is superimposed.
