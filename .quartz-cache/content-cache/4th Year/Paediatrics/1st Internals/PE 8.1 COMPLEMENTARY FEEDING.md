### **1. DEFINITION**

Complementary feeding (previously referred to as "weaning") is defined as the introduction of nutritious foods over and above breastfeeding. It marks the transition from exclusive breastfeeding to family foods, ensuring the child receives energy-dense, cost-effective, and well-tolerated nutrition to meet growing demands.

### **2. RECOMMENDED AGE FOR INITIATION**

- **Initiation:** Complementary feeding must be started exactly after 6 months of age, while continuing breastfeeding. Up to 6 months, breast milk alone is sufficient to meet the infant's nutritional requirements.
- **Continuation of Breastfeeding:** Breastfeeding should be encouraged on demand, day and night, up to two years of age or beyond.
- **Transition:** The process of transition to adult/family diet should gradually be completed by 9 months to 1 year of age. By 12 months, most children can eat the same types of foods as consumed by the rest of the family.

### **3. PREREQUISITES AND ATTRIBUTES OF AN IDEAL COMPLEMENTARY FOOD**

To be optimal for an infant's growth, complementary foods must possess the following characteristics:

- **Home-made/Available:** Should be fresh, easily available, and provide exposure to a variety of tastes.
- **Fresh and Hygienic:** Must be freshly prepared with proper hand washing and utensil hygiene, avoiding prolonged storage to prevent infections.
- **Palatable:** Should be soft, tasty, and easy to chew and swallow.
- **Cost-effective/Affordable:** Must be well within the socioeconomic reach of the family.
- **Culturally Acceptable:** Locally available family foods appropriately modified for the infant are preferred.
- **Energy Dense:** Since children have a small stomach capacity, foods should be energy-dense. This is achieved by adding oils, fats, and using malted/sprouted grains.

### **4. CLASSIFICATION OF COMPLEMENTARY FOODS (THE 3 'A's)**

Complementary foods are categorized based on their nutritional advantages and availability:

- **Appropriate (First-line) Foods:** Fresh home-made, culturally acceptable, and energy-dense foods. Examples: Combinations of cereals and pulses (khichari, dal-rice), porridge, suji, dalia, kheer, mashed banana, sweet potato, and sprouted legumes.
- **Acceptable (Second-line) Foods:** Home-available ready-made foods. Examples: Breads, biscuits, cheese, and calorie-dense fruits.
- **Avoidable (Third-line) Foods:** These must be strictly restricted. Examples: Commercially available artificial/tinned foods, junk foods, low-caloric fruit juices, and repeatedly fried foods containing harmful trans-fatty acids.

### **5. PRINCIPLES AND REGIMEN OF COMPLEMENTARY FEEDING**

- **Amount and Frequency:**
    - _At 6 months:_ Start with small amounts (1–2 teaspoons) and gradually increase to tablespoons, and then to a 'katori' (bowl). Give 2 to 3 meals each day, along with 1 or 2 snacks.
    - _6–12 months:_ Give at least 1 katori serving at a time. Frequency should be 3 to 4 times/day if breastfed, and 5 times/day if not breastfed.
    - _12 months–2 years:_ Give at least $1\frac{1}{2}$ katori servings at a time. Frequency should be 3 to 5 times/day, plus nutritious snacks.
- **Consistency:** Start with pureed, mashed, and semisolid foods at 6 months. Gradually shift to solid family foods by 12 months.
- **Introduction of New Foods:** Introduce one new food at a time, every 1 or 2 weeks. If the child dislikes a food, omit it temporarily.
- **Allergenic Foods:** Fish, egg, and meat should be introduced later to prevent the risk of protein allergies. When introducing eggs, begin with the yolk.

### **6. METHODS TO INCREASE ENERGY DENSITY**

Infants cannot consume large bulky quantities due to their small stomach capacity. The energy density of the food must be increased without increasing its bulk:

- **Addition of Fat/Sugar:** Adding oil, ghee, sugar, or jaggery provides concentrated energy.
- **Amylase-Rich Foods (ARF):** Thin gruels have high water content but low energy. Thick gruels are energy-dense but hard to swallow. Adding ARF (made by germinating, drying, and grinding whole grain cereals or pulses) reduces the viscosity of thick foods, making them easier to eat in larger quantities while retaining high caloric value.

### **7. HAZARDS OF IMPROPER TIMING (TOO EARLY VS. TOO LATE)**

**Hazards of Too Early Initiation (< 6 months):**

- _Short-term:_ Decreased suckling frequency leading to reduced breast milk production, iron deficiency (if foods lack iron), hypernatremia, and weaning diarrhea due to infections.
- _Long-term:_ Malnutrition (in poor socioeconomic groups) or obesity (in affluent groups), hypertension, and arteriosclerosis.

**Hazards of Too Late Initiation (> 6 months):**

- After 6 months, breast milk alone cannot meet the energy and protein requirements of the growing child.
- Delayed introduction predisposes the child to undernutrition, growth retardation (stunting and wasting), and specific micronutrient deficiencies.

### **8. KEY COUNSELING GUIDELINES FOR MOTHERS**

- Make feeding a pleasant, interactive experience ("Pyar se, aaram se, bahla ke").
- The child should be comfortably seated, and the meal environment must be free from distractions like TV or mobile screens.
- Feed the young child with patience; do not force-feed. Encourage the child to finish the serving.
- Practice strict hygiene: Wash hands of both the mother and the child with soap and water before every feed. Use clean utensils and avoid the use of feeding bottles.
