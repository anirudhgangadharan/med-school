### **1. DEFINITION OF DEVELOPMENT**

Development specifies the maturation of functions and is related to the maturation and myelination of the nervous system. It denotes the functional or physiological maturation and indicates the acquisition of various skills for the individual’s optimal functioning. It encompasses the accomplishment of mental (cognitive skills), emotional (attitudes), and social (adaptation to family and society) abilities.

### **2. PRINCIPLES AND COMMON PATTERNS OF DEVELOPMENT**

The complex process of human development follows certain basic facts and principles:

- **Continuous Process:** Development is a continuous process that starts in utero and progresses through many developmental stages until achieving maturity.
- **Neurological Maturation:** Development depends on the functional maturation of the nervous system. A child must possess central nervous system maturity to learn a specific milestone or skill. Without practice, however, the child may not learn skills despite having neurological maturation.
- **Uniform Sequence:** The sequence of attainment of milestones is the same in all children, such as babbling before speaking words and sitting before standing. However, variations may exist in the exact time and manner of their attainment.
- **Cephalocaudal Direction:** Development progresses in a cephalocaudal direction. Head control precedes trunk control, which in turn precedes the ability to use the lower limbs.
- **Proximal to Distal:** The control of limbs happens from proximal to distal, meaning a child learns to use his hands before his fingers.
- **Disappearance of Primitive Reflexes:** The attainment of certain milestones requires that relevant primitive reflexes disappear. For example, the palmar grasp reflex must disappear before a child can voluntarily hold an object, and the asymmetric tonic neck reflex must vanish before a child can roll over.
- **Replacement of Mass Activity:** Specific actions replace initial disorganized mass activity. For instance, a 3-4-month-old infant squeals and excitedly moves all limbs on seeing a toy, whereas a 3-4-year-old child may simply smile and ask for it.

### **3. NORMAL DEVELOPMENTAL MILESTONES**

The sequential attainment of developmental milestones reflects the maturation and myelination of the nervous system.

**a. Gross Motor Milestones**

- **3 months:** Head/neck holding.
- **5 months:** Sitting with support.
- **7–8 months:** Sitting without support.
- **9 months:** Standing with support.
- **10 months:** Walking with support.
- **11 months:** Crawling/creeping.
- **12 months:** Standing without support.
- **13 months:** Walking without support.
- **18 months:** Running.
- **24 months:** Climbing upstairs.
- **36 months:** Riding a tricycle.

**b. Fine Motor Milestones**

- **4 months:** Grasping a rattle (when placed in hand). Fixes attention on an object and attempts a bidextrous grasp approach.
- **5 months:** Reaching out for a bright object intentionally and grasping it with both hands (bidextrous grasp).
- **6 months:** Immature palmar grasp.
- **7 months:** Holding an object with a crude grasp from the palm (palmar grasp). The child can transfer a cube from one hand to another.
- **8–10 months:** Crude radial grasp, using the radial aspect of the hand.
- **9 months:** Holding a small object between the index finger and thumb (pincer grasp). Also looks for a dropped cube (object permanence).
- **1 year:** Mature radial grasp utilizing the thumb and index finger.

**c. Personal/Social Milestones**

- **4–8 weeks:** Social smile.
- **3 months:** Recognition of mother.
- **6 months:** Smiling at a mirror image.
- **9 months:** Waving "bye-bye".
- **1 year:** Playing a simple ball game.
- **3 years:** Knowing gender.

**d. Language Milestones**

- **1 month:** Turning head to sound (like a rattle or bell).
- **3 months:** Cooing.
- **6 months:** Monosyllables (e.g., "ma," "pa," "ba").
- **9 months:** Bisyllables (e.g., "mama," "papa," "baba").
- **1 year:** Two words with meaning.
- **2 years:** Simple sentences.
- **3 years:** Telling a story.
- **4 years:** Giving an account of recent events.

### **4. METHODS OF ASSESSMENT OF DEVELOPMENT**

Developmental delay should be identified early, as prompt intervention maximizes developmental potential.

**a. Prerequisites and Steps**

- **Prerequisites:** The environment should be comfortable and free from distractions, and the child should not be hungry, ill, tired, or irritated. A development assessment kit (containing items like red ring, nine red cubes, bell, picture book, spoon, cup) should be available.
- **History Taking:** Obtain information on risk factors, the rate of milestone acquisition, and any history of milestone regression.
- **Physical Examination:** Examine physical growth, head circumference, dysmorphic features, stigmata of intrauterine infections, signs of hypothyroidism, vision, hearing, and primitive reflexes.
- **Developmental Quotient (DQ):** One can assign a Developmental Quotient for any developmental sphere. It is calculated as: DQ = (Developmental Age / Chronological Age) x 100. A DQ below 70% warrants a detailed evaluation by a trained child psychologist.

**b. Developmental Surveillance and Screening Tests** Developmental screening is a brief assessment to identify children requiring more intensive diagnosis. Routine screening tests include:

- **Denver Developmental Screening Test (DDST) / Denver II:** Originally developed in 1967 and later revised as Denver II, it assesses children up to 3 years across four domains: gross motor, fine motor adaptive, language, and personal-social behavior. It does not measure a developmental quotient, but identifies deviations from expected age norms.
- **Phatak's Baroda Screening Test:** This is India's best-known development testing system, adapted from the Bayley scale, for use in children up to 30 months of age. It evaluates gross motor, fine motor, and cognitive domains.
- **Trivandrum Development Screening Chart (TDSC):** Developed in India and based on Baroda norms, it consists of 51 test items for children between 0 and 6 years. It is highly practical for community settings.
- **Ages and Stages Questionnaire (ASQ-3):** A parent-completed, age-based questionnaire for children from 1 month to 5.5 years, assessing communication, gross motor, fine motor, problem-solving, and personal-social domains.
- **Goodenough-Harris Drawing Test:** A paper-pencil test for children aged 3 to 15 years where the child draws a man. The child's mental age is determined by comparing the maturity of the drawing with a normative sample.
- **Clinical Adaptive Test and Clinical Linguistic and Auditory Milestone Scale (CAT/CLAMS):** Assesses cognitive and language skills using direct testing and parental reports from birth to 36 months.

**c. Definitive Developmental Tests** Once a screening test is abnormal, definitive tests are employed to accurately define the impairment.

- **Development Assessment Scale for Indian Infants (DASII):** Evaluates motor (67 items) and mental domains (163 items) from birth to 30 months.
- **Bayley Scale for Infant Development IV:** Evaluates language, cognitive, motor, and social-emotional domains for children from 16 days to 42 months.
- **Stanford-Binet Intelligence Scales (5th Ed):** Provides full-scale IQ, verbal IQ, and nonverbal IQ from 2 to 85 years of age.
- **Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children IV:** Assesses verbal and performance skills for children 6 to 17 years old.
- **Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scale III:** Measures personal and social skills as reported by a caregiver across domains like communication, daily living, and socialization.
