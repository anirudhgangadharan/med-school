### **1. ENURESIS (BEDWETTING)**

**a. Definition** Enuresis is defined as normal, nearly complete evacuation of the urinary bladder at a wrong place and time at least twice a month after 5 years of age. It should be differentiated from continuous or intermittent incontinence or dribbling (where the bed is soaking wet compared to loss of urine without normal bladder emptying in the latter).

**b. Classification**

- **Based on Onset:**
    - _Primary (Persistent) Enuresis:_ The child has never been dry at night since infancy.
    - _Secondary (Regressive) Enuresis:_ Bedwetting starts after a minimum period of 6 months of dryness. It is often triggered by stressful environmental events.
- **Based on Daytime Symptoms:**
    - _Type I:_ Monosymptomatic nocturnal enuresis (no lower urinary tract symptoms).
    - _Type II:_ Diurnal enuresis without daytime frequency.
    - _Type III:_ Nocturnal enuresis with daytime frequency.
    - _Type IV:_ Nocturnal diuresis with daytime frequency and voiding dysfunction.

**c. Etiopathogenesis**

- **Maturational Delay:** The most likely cause of nocturnal enuresis, characterized by high spontaneous cure rates (15% per year).
- **Psychological Factors:** Maladjustment and family conflicts (e.g., too strict parents, rejection, sibling rivalry, marital conflict, or death in the family).
- **Improper Toilet Training:** Too late, too early, or erratic training by overanxious parents (e.g., forcing the child to sit on the pottie for too long).
- **Organic/Physical Factors (<5% of nocturnal enuresis):**
    - Lack of circadian rhythm of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) secretion.
    - Inadequate sleep arousal.
    - Genitourinary infections (UTI).
    - Intestinal parasitosis (especially threadworm/pinworm infestation).
    - Bladder bowel dysfunction (BBD) and chronic constipation.
    - Metabolic disorders (Diabetes mellitus or Diabetes insipidus).
    - Anatomical/Neurological defects (neurogenic bladder, sacral agenesis, ectopic ureter, posterior urethral valves).

**d. Evaluation and Diagnosis**

- **History Taking:** Determine if primary or secondary, and elicit associated daytime symptoms, voiding difficulties, or stressful family events.
- **Voiding Diary:** Essential tool involving frequency and volume charting of urine output and fluid intake for at least 2 days, with a record of daytime accidents, bladder symptoms, and bowel habits for 7 days.
- **Physical Examination:** Rule out acute stressful conditions and spinal/neurological anomalies.
- **Investigations:** Urinalysis and urine culture to rule out UTI, proteinuria, and glucosuria. Ultrasonography and Micturating Cystourethrogram (MCU) are reserved for suspected neurological or urological dysfunction.

**e. Management** Active treatment is generally not recommended before the age of 6 years.

- **Motivational/Behavioral Therapy (First-line):**
    - Reassurance and emotional support to the child and parents. Avoid punishments, anger, or ridicule.
    - Bladder training: Encourage frequent daytime voiding to avoid urgency, establish good bowel habits, and restrict large quantities of fluids (especially tea, coffee, sodas) in the evening.
    - Positive reinforcement: Charting dry nights on a calendar and rewarding the child.
- **Alarm Therapy:** Uses a device to elicit a conditioned response of awakening to the sensation of a full bladder. It is successful in up to 60-70% of children but requires high motivation.
- **Pharmacotherapy (If other methods fail):**
    - _Desmopressin (DDAVP):_ Synthetic antidiuretic agent (0.2–0.4 mg orally or 120–240 µg melt) reduces urine volume. Best for special occasions (e.g., sleepovers) due to relapse risk upon stopping.
    - _Anticholinergic Drugs:_ Oxybutynin (5-10 mg/day) or Tolterodine (2 mg at bedtime) are useful for daytime urge incontinence and uninhibited bladder contractions.
    - _Tricyclic Antidepressants:_ Imipramine may be used, but carries a risk of severe adverse cardiac arrhythmias, hence not routinely recommended.

### **2. ENCOPRESIS (FECAL SOILING)**

**a. Definition** Encopresis is defined as the involuntary passage of stools in clothes or inappropriate places beyond an age when bowel control should have been achieved (usually 4 to 5 years). It indicates a more serious emotional disturbance than enuresis.

**b. Classification**

- **Primary Encopresis:** Chronic soiling that persists from infancy onward (the child has never achieved bowel control).
- **Secondary (Regressive) Encopresis:** Soiling occurs after the child had successfully attained bowel control for a period of time.
- **Pathophysiological Types:** Retentive (associated with severe constipation) vs. Non-retentive (never achieved bowel control or loss of control).

**c. Etiopathogenesis**

- **Chronic Constipation (Overflow Encopresis):** Accounts for the vast majority of cases (especially in boys). Severe chronic constipation leads to fecal impaction. Liquid fecal matter simply spills or leaks around the edges of a solid, impacted fecal mass situated low in the colon or rectum.
- **Emotional/Psychological Disturbances:** Often seen in secondary encopresis. Subconscious anger, defiance, separation from parents, starting school, or the arrival of a new sibling can precipitate soiling.
- **Mental Retardation:** Failure to understand normal anatomical relationships or lacking the consciousness of the urge to defecate.
- **Neurological/Anatomical Defects:** Degenerative diseases, meningomyelocele, spinal cord tumors/trauma, or absence of external and internal sphincter mechanisms.

**d. Clinical Features and Diagnosis**

- **Clinical Presentation:** Chronic soiling of undergarments, often confused by parents as "diarrhea" (which is actually overflow of liquid feces). It is associated with chronic constipation, fecal impaction, and overflow incontinence.
- **Psychosocial Impact:** The offensive odor leads to the child becoming a target of scorn and rebuke, causing poor school attendance, poor academic performance, and impaired social relationships.
- **Diagnosis:** Rectal examination is imperative and will reveal a rectum full of large, hard fecal lumps in overflow encopresis. A thorough neurological and developmental evaluation is required to rule out organic causes like Hirschsprung disease.

**e. Management**

- **Behavioral Therapy:** Essential for non-retentive encopresis and as an adjunct for retentive cases. Positive reinforcement, proper bowel habit training (sitting on the toilet after meals), and psychiatric counseling if emotional disturbances are severe.
- **Medical Treatment (for Retentive type):**
    - Primary encopresis is harder to treat than secondary.
    - Treatment strictly mirrors the management of severe constipation: Initial disimpaction of the hard fecal mass followed by maintenance laxative therapy and dietary modifications (high residue diet and adequate fluids) to ensure regular, soft stools.
