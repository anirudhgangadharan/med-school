### **1. INTRODUCTION**

Breast milk is the ideal nutrition containing all the nutrients required for the optimum growth and development of a neonate from birth to six months. It is species-specific, tailor-made, and is recommended to be initiated within half an hour to one hour of birth. Exclusive breastfeeding is the most effective public health intervention to reduce neonatal morbidity (especially diarrhea and pneumonia) and should be continued for 6 months.

### **2. PHYSIOLOGY OF LACTATION**

The production and ejection of milk are governed by a complex interplay of maternal hormones and infant reflexes.

**a. Maternal Reflexes**

- **Prolactin Reflex (Milk Secretion Reflex):** Suckling by the baby at the breast stimulates sensory nerve endings in the nipple, sending impulses to the anterior pituitary to secrete prolactin. Prolactin acts on the alveolar epithelial cells to produce milk. More prolactin is secreted at night, making night feeding crucial for maintaining milk supply.
- **Oxytocin Reflex (Milk Ejection or Let-down Reflex):** Suckling, or even the thought, sight, or sound of the baby, stimulates the posterior pituitary to secrete oxytocin. Oxytocin causes contraction of the myoepithelial cells surrounding the alveoli, ejecting milk into the lactiferous sinuses. This reflex can be hindered by maternal stress, worry, pain, or lack of confidence.

**b. Infant Reflexes**

- **Rooting Reflex:** When the cheek or side of the mouth is touched, the neonate turns their head, opens their mouth, and searches for the nipple.
- **Suckling Reflex:** When the nipple touches the palate, the neonate draws the nipple and areola into the mouth, elongating it into a teat, and compresses it between the tongue and palate to draw milk.
- **Swallowing Reflex:** When the mouth fills with milk, the swallowing reflex is triggered. The suckle-swallow-breathe cycle takes about one second.

### **3. COMPOSITION OF BREAST MILK**

The composition of breast milk varies with time and gestation to meet the baby's exact requirements.

- **Colostrum:** Secreted during the initial 3–4 days post-delivery. It is small in quantity, thick, and yellow. It is highly rich in antibodies (secretory IgA), immune-competent cells, and vitamins A, D, E, and K.
- **Transitional Milk:** Secreted from 3–4 days until two weeks. Immunoglobulin and protein content decreases while fat and sugar content increases.
- **Mature Milk:** Thinner and watery, following the transitional phase, containing all essential nutrients.
- **Nutritional Superiority:**
    - _Carbohydrates:_ High lactose content helps absorb calcium and enhances the growth of protective lactobacilli.
    - _Proteins:_ Easily digestible whey proteins (lactalbumin and lactoglobulin). Rich in taurine and cysteine, essential for brain growth.
    - _Fats:_ Rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), omega-3, and omega-6 fatty acids needed for neural myelination.
    - _Prebiotics:_ Human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs) support a healthy microbiome.
    - _Water:_ Contains 88% water; thus, breastfed neonates do not require additional water even in summer.

### **4. ADVANTAGES OF BREASTFEEDING**

**a. Benefits to the Infant**

- **Immunological Protection:** Contains macrophages, lymphocytes, lactoferrin, lysozyme, and secretory IgA. A breastfed neonate is 14 times less likely to die of diarrhea and almost 4 times less likely to die of pneumonia.
- **Optimal Growth and CNS Development:** Higher IQ and better mental growth.
- **Protection against Allergy and Chronic Diseases:** Reduces the risk of eczema, asthma, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, ulcerative colitis, and obesity in later life.
- **Safety:** Free from contamination, sterile, and always at the right temperature.

**b. Benefits to the Mother**

- **Uterine Involution:** Oxytocin release helps in uterine contraction, reducing postpartum hemorrhage.
- **Contraception:** Lactational amenorrhea provides effective contraception for the initial 6 months.
- **Reduced Cancer Risk:** Decreases the risk of breast and ovarian cancer.
- **Weight Loss:** Helps the mother shed extra weight gained during pregnancy.

**c. Benefits to the Community**

- It is highly economical, saving costs on formula and healthcare. It promotes family planning and significantly reduces infant morbidity and mortality.

### **5. TECHNIQUE OF BREASTFEEDING**

Correct technique is vital for establishing successful lactation and preventing nipple injury. **a. Positioning**

- The mother should be in a comfortable and relaxed posture (sitting or lying down).
- The infant's whole body should be supported, with the head and neck in a straight line.
- The infant should face the breast with their abdomen touching the mother's abdomen, and the baby's cheek should touch the breast.

**b. Attachment (Latching)** Good attachment is indicated by:

- The infant's mouth is wide open.
- The lower lip is turned outward (everted).
- The infant's chin touches the mother's breast.
- More of the areola is visible above the infant's mouth than below it (most of the areola is inside the mouth).

**c. Burping**

- After feeding, the infant should be held upright over the mother's shoulder, supporting the head/neck, and gently patted or rubbed on the back to expel swallowed air. This prevents regurgitation and vomiting.

### **6. SIGNS OF ADEQUATE BREASTFEEDING**

To evaluate if the neonate is receiving enough milk, observe for:

- Passage of urine 6 to 8 times in 24 hours.
- The neonate is satisfied and sleeps well for 2–3 hours after feeds.
- Adequate and consistent weight gain.

### **7. COMMON BREASTFEEDING PROBLEMS AND MANAGEMENT**

- **Inverted or Flat Nipples:** Does not usually pose a problem if attachment is correct, but can make latching difficult. Managed by manually stretching and rolling the nipples or using a plastic syringe (cut from the nozzle end to create negative pressure) to pull the nipple out.
- **Sore or Cracked Nipples:** Caused by incorrect attachment or pulling the neonate off the breast forcibly. Managed by correcting the attachment, applying hindmilk to the nipple after feeds, keeping it dry between feeds, and avoiding washing with soap.
- **Breast Engorgement:** Breasts become swollen, hard, warm, and tender with shiny skin due to delayed, infrequent, or ineffective feeding. Managed by frequent breastfeeding, ensuring correct attachment, hot fomentation, and gentle massage/expression of milk.
- **Breast Abscess:** A complication of untreated engorgement or cracked nipples presenting with severe pain, high-grade fever, and localized swelling. Managed with analgesics, antibiotics, and incision & drainage. The mother can usually continue feeding from the unaffected breast.
- **"Not Enough Milk":** Often a perceived issue rather than a reality. Managed by reassuring the mother (if weight gain and urine output are normal), counseling, reducing maternal stress, ensuring adequate maternal rest/nutrition, and encouraging frequent night feeds.

### **8. CONTRAINDICATIONS TO BREASTFEEDING**

Absolute contraindications are rare, but breastfeeding may be avoided in: **Maternal Conditions:**

- Active untreated tuberculosis (though some advise continuing with chemotherapeutic coverage), leprosy, or malignancy.
- Mothers strictly addicted to alcohol, heavy narcotics, or those on antimetabolites, radioactive isotopes, high-dose steroids, or heavy metals.
- Severe psychosis.
- HIV/AIDS (According to local/national guidelines, though replacement feeding must be acceptable, feasible, affordable, sustainable, and safe).

**Infant Conditions:**

- Inborn errors of metabolism such as galactosemia, phenylketonuria, or lactose intolerance.
- Gross prematurity or conditions where the infant physically cannot suckle (requires expressed breast milk via alternative methods like gavage or paladai).

### **9. WHO/UNICEF "TEN STEPS TO SUCCESSFUL BREASTFEEDING"**

A "Baby-Friendly Hospital" implements the following ten steps:

1. Have a written breastfeeding policy that is routinely communicated to all health care staff.
2. Train all health care staff in skills necessary to implement this policy.
3. Inform all pregnant women about the benefits and management of breastfeeding.
4. Help mothers initiate breastfeeding within a half-hour of birth.
5. Show mothers how to breastfeed and how to maintain lactation even if separated from their infants.
6. Give newborn infants no food or drink other than breast milk, unless medically indicated.
7. Practice rooming-in (allow mothers and infants to remain together 24 hours a day).
8. Encourage breastfeeding on demand.
9. Give no artificial teats or pacifiers (dummies or soothers) to breastfeeding infants.
10. Foster the establishment of breastfeeding support groups and refer mothers to them on discharge.
