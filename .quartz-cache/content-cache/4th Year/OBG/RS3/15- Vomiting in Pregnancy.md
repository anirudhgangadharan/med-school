
1. Hyperemesis gravidarum—investigations, treatment, complications
**HYPEREMESIS GRAVIDARUM (HG)**

**DEFINITION** It is a severe type of vomiting of pregnancy which has a deleterious effect on the health of the mother and/or incapacitates her in day-to-day activities. **Triad of adverse effects:** Protracted nausea and vomiting in pregnancy (NVP) leads to (a) >5% loss of pre-pregnancy weight, (b) dehydration, and (c) electrolyte imbalance.

**INVESTIGATIONS** The primary goal of investigations is to assess the severity of dehydration, electrolyte/metabolic derangements, and to exclude other obstetric, medical, or surgical causes.

**1. Urinalysis**

- **Quantity & Color:** Small quantity and dark color.
- **Specific Gravity & Reaction:** High specific gravity with an acid reaction.
- **Abnormal Constituents:** Presence of acetone (ketonuria), occasional presence of protein, and rarely bile pigments.
- **Electrolytes:** Diminished or even absence of chloride.

**2. Biochemical and Circulatory Changes**

- **Liver Function Tests (LFTs):** Abnormal in about 40% of patients, with a rise in serum transaminases and bilirubin; jaundice may be present.
- **Serum Electrolytes:** Routine and periodic estimation of sodium, potassium, and chloride is crucial. HG is typically associated with hyponatremia and hypokalemia.
- **Other Blood Parameters:** Low serum urea level, raised hematocrit (due to hemoconcentration), and metabolic alkalosis (which turns into metabolic acidosis in severe cases).

**3. Hormonal Assays**

- **Serum TSH, T3, and free T4:** About 60% of women may suffer from a transient phase of thyroid dysfunction (clinical or subclinical thyrotoxicosis).

**4. Special Investigations**

- **Ultrasonography (USG):** Highly useful to confirm the viability of the pregnancy and to exclude other obstetric causes of severe vomiting such as hydatidiform mole or multiple pregnancy, as well as gynecological/surgical causes.
- **Electrocardiogram (ECG):** Required when there is an abnormal serum potassium level.
- **Ophthalmoscopic Examination:** Required in seriously ill patients. Retinal hemorrhage and detachment of the retina are the most unfavorable signs.

---

**COMPLICATIONS** The majority of clinical manifestations and complications arise from dehydration, starvation, and resultant ketoacidosis.

**1. Maternal Complications (Rare nowadays)**

- **Neurologic Complications:** Wernicke's encephalopathy and beriberi (due to vitamin B1/thiamine deficiency), pontine myelinolysis, peripheral neuritis, Korsakoff's psychosis, and depression.
- **Gastrointestinal:** Stress ulcer in the stomach, esophageal tear and bleeding (Mallory-Weiss syndrome).
- **Hepatic:** Jaundice and hepatic failure.
- **Renal:** Renal failure.
- **Hematologic:** Hypoprothrombinemia (due to vitamin K deficiency).
- **Systemic:** Convulsions and coma.

**2. Fetal Complications**

- The fetus generally remains unaffected once the condition resolves.
- Fetal risks primarily include Small for Gestational Age (SGA) / low birth weight and preterm birth (18%).

---

**TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENT**

**Principles of Management** The core principles include maintaining hydration, controlling vomiting, correcting fluid and electrolyte imbalances, correcting metabolic disturbances (acidosis or alkalosis), preventing serious complications, and ensuring the care of the pregnancy.

**1. Hospitalization**

- **Indications:** Protracted NVP despite oral antiemetics, continued NVP with ketonuria and/or >5% weight loss, or the presence of any medical/surgical comorbidity.

**2. Fluid and Nutritional Therapy**

- Oral feeding is strictly withheld for at least 24 hours after the cessation of vomiting.
- **Intravenous (IV) Fluids:** Fast hydration IV therapy (1-1.5 L) with normal saline and potassium (if not contraindicated) is administered to rectify dehydration, ketoacidosis, and electrolyte imbalance.
- Enteral nutrition through a nasogastric tube may be used if needed.
- **Diet Resumption:** Before IV fluids are omitted, dry carbohydrate foods (like biscuits, bread, and toast) are introduced. Small, frequent feeds are recommended, gradually restoring a full diet.

**3. Pharmacotherapy (Antiemetics)**

- **First-line:** Cyclizine (50 mg PO/IM/IV 8 hourly), Prochlorperazine (5-10 mg PO or 12.5 mg IM/IV), Promethazine, or Chlorpromazine. Doxylamine combined with Vitamin B6 is also a safe and effective first-line option.
- **Second-line:** Metoclopramide (5-10 mg 8 hourly PO/IV/IM), Domperidone, or Ondansetron (4-8 mg PO/IV).
- **Third-line (Corticosteroids):** Used in intractable vomiting or cases with hypotension. Hydrocortisone 100 mg twice daily IV is given; once improvement occurs, it is converted to oral prednisolone (40-50 mg daily) and gradually tapered.

**4. Supplements and Prophylactic Medications**

- **Vitamin Supplementation:** IV administration of Vitamin B1 (thiamine) 100 mg daily is critical to prevent Wernicke's encephalopathy. Vitamins B6, C, and B12 are also supplemented.
- **Gastric Protection:** Proton pump inhibitors (IV) are used to prevent stress ulcers.
- **Thromboprophylaxis:** Women admitted with severe HG are at risk of hemoconcentration and require thromboprophylaxis with Low Molecular Weight Heparin (LMWH).

**5. General and Nursing Care**

- Sympathetic but firm handling with social and psychological support is essential.
- A hyperemesis progress chart must be maintained to record pulse, temperature, blood pressure, intake-output, urine (for acetone, protein, bile), blood biochemistry, and ECG.
- **Signs of Improvement:** Subsidence of vomiting, feeling of hunger, disappearance of acetone from breath/urine, and normalization of vital signs and electrolytes.
- **Termination of pregnancy:** Rarely indicated nowadays due to effective medical therapies.
4. **MANAGEMENT OF HYPEREMESIS GRAVIDARUM**

**Definition and Clinical Features** Hyperemesis gravidarum is a severe type of vomiting of pregnancy that has a deleterious effect on the mother's health and incapacitates her day-to-day activities. The condition is characterized by a triad of adverse effects: >5% loss of pre-pregnancy weight, dehydration, and electrolyte imbalance. Left untreated, it can lead to metabolic acidosis, starvation, and ketoacidosis.

**Principles of Management** The core principles of managing hyperemesis gravidarum include:

- Maintenance of hydration.
- Control of vomiting.
- Correction of fluid and electrolyte imbalance.
- Correction of metabolic disturbances (acidosis or alkalosis).
- Prevention of serious complications (like Wernicke's encephalopathy).
- Care of the pregnancy.

**Indications for Hospitalization** Patients must be admitted to the hospital if they present with:

- Protracted nausea and vomiting despite the use of oral antiemetics.
- Continued vomiting accompanied by ketonuria and/or >5% weight loss.
- Presence of any medical or surgical comorbidity.

**1. Fluid and Electrolyte Therapy**

- Oral feeding is strictly withheld for at least 24 hours after the cessation of vomiting.
- During this fasting period, fast hydration IV therapy (1-1.5 L) using normal saline and potassium (if not contraindicated) is administered.
- This regimen rapidly rectifies dehydration, ketoacidosis, and water and electrolyte imbalances.
- Serum electrolytes must be estimated and corrected if any abnormality is detected.
- Enteral nutrition through a nasogastric tube may be considered in severe cases.

**2. Pharmacotherapy (Antiemetics)**

- **First-line drugs:** Promethazine, cyclizine, and prochlorperazine are effective. A combination of Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine 25 mg) and doxylamine (25 mg) is also highly safe and effective.
- **Second-line drugs:** Metoclopramide is useful as it stimulates gastric and intestinal motility without stimulating secretions. Ondansetron is another safe and effective second-line option.
- **Third-line drugs (Corticosteroids):** Indicated for intractable vomiting or when associated with hypotension. Hydrocortisone 100 mg twice daily IV is given, and once clinical improvement occurs, it is converted to oral prednisolone (40-50 mg daily), tapering gradually to the lowest maintenance dose.

**3. Nutritional Supplementation**

- Parenteral thiamine (Vitamin B1) 100 mg IV daily is mandatory to prevent Wernicke's encephalopathy, a severe neurological complication.
- Additional nutritional supplementation with Vitamin B6, Vitamin C, and Vitamin B12 is also provided.

**4. Prevention of Complications**

- **Thromboprophylaxis:** Hemoconcentration increases the risk of venous thromboembolism. Women admitted with severe hyperemesis should routinely be given thromboprophylaxis using Low Molecular Weight Heparin (LMWH).
- **Stress Ulcer Prophylaxis:** Intravenous proton pump inhibitors are used to prevent stress ulcers.

**5. Nursing Care and Monitoring**

- **Multidisciplinary Approach:** Management requires a team involving an obstetrician, physician, psychiatrist, nutritionist, and midwife.
- **General Care:** Sympathetic but firm handling, along with social and psychological support, is essential.
- **Progress Chart:** A daily hospital chart must record the pulse, temperature, blood pressure (at least twice daily), intake-output, and urine tests for acetone, protein, and bile. Blood biochemistry and an ECG (if serum potassium is abnormal) are monitored.

**6. Dietary Management**

- Clinical improvement is marked by the subsidence of vomiting, return of hunger, disappearance of acetone from breath/urine, and normal electrolytes.
- Before discontinuing IV fluids, oral feeding is resumed starting with dry carbohydrate foods like biscuits, bread, and toast.
- Patients are advised to take small, frequent feeds and to drink fluids in between meals rather than immediately after meals. High-fat and spicy foods should be avoided. Gradually, a full diet is restored.

**Termination of Pregnancy** Intractable hyperemesis gravidarum that fails to respond to aggressive inpatient therapy is exceedingly rare today; therefore, termination of pregnancy is rarely indicated.
