1.  Braxton Hicks contraction.


**BRAXTON-HICKS CONTRACTIONS**

**Definition** Named after John Braxton Hicks who first described them, these are spontaneous, involuntary, irregular, infrequent, spasmodic, and painless uterine contractions that occur throughout normal pregnancy.

**Key Characteristics**

- **Onset:** They occur from the very early weeks of pregnancy. They can be appreciated during early bimanual pelvic examination, and later via abdominal palpation.
- **Nature:** They are painless and irregular, and they do not progressively increase in frequency or regularity.
- **Palpatory Findings:** During palpation, the uterus feels firmer at one moment and softer at another. These contractions can be actively excited or stimulated by rubbing the uterus.
- **Effect on Cervix:** Unlike true labor pains, Braxton-Hicks contractions have no effect on the dilatation or effacement of the cervix.
- **Intrauterine Pressure:** The pressure generated during a contraction remains low, typically below 8 mm Hg.
- **Maternal Perception:** For the majority of the pregnancy, the patient is completely unconscious of these contractions.
- **Progression Near Term:** As term approaches, the contractions become more frequent and increase in intensity, which may produce some discomfort to the mother. Ultimately, they merge into the regular, painful uterine contractions of true labor.

**Physiological Effects**

- During a contraction, there is complete closure of the uterine veins along with partial occlusion of the arteries supplying the intervillous space.
- This results in the stagnation of blood in the intervillous space and transiently diminishes placental perfusion.
- This can cause transient fetal hypoxia, leading to a temporary fetal bradycardia that coincides with the contraction.

**Clinical Significance**

- **Sign of Pregnancy:** The presence of Braxton-Hicks contractions is considered a "probable sign" of pregnancy. They become more clinically evident in the second trimester (13-20 weeks) and third trimester (>20 to 40 weeks).
- **Obstetric Examination:** During abdominal palpation (Leopold maneuvers) or during labor, palpation should be temporarily suspended while a Braxton-Hicks or uterine contraction is occurring to avoid undue uterine irritability and to ensure accurate clinical findings.

**Diagnostic Importance (Absence of Braxton-Hicks Contractions)** The absence of these contractions is a crucial diagnostic clue in differentiating normal intrauterine pregnancy from other pathologies:

- **Abdominal Pregnancy:** Braxton-Hicks contractions are notably absent. Consequently, the uterine contour cannot be well-defined even when massaging the abdominal wall.
- **Intrauterine Fetal Death (IUFD):** In cases of fetal demise, the uterine tone is diminished, the uterus feels flaccid, and Braxton-Hicks contractions are no longer easily felt.
- **Cystic Ovarian Tumor:** The complete absence of Braxton-Hicks contractions (along with the absence of positive signs of pregnancy and amenorrhea) helps differentiate a slow-growing cystic ovarian tumor from a pregnant uterus.


2. Maternal physiologic cardiologic and hematologic changes during pregnancy.
**MATERNAL PHYSIOLOGICAL CARDIOVASCULAR CHANGES**

**I. Anatomical Changes**

- **Position of the Heart:** Due to the elevation of the diaphragm by the enlarged gravid uterus, the heart is pushed upward and outward, with a slight rotation to the left.
- **Apex Beat:** Shifts to the 4th intercostal space, about 2.5 cm outside the midclavicular line.

**II. Clinical and Electrocardiographic (ECG) Findings**

- **Auscultation (Heart Sounds):** Splitting of the first heart sound (S1) is normal, the second sound (S2) remains unchanged, and the third heart sound (S3) becomes loud due to rapid diastolic filling.
- **Murmurs:**
    - A systolic ejection murmur is often audible at the left sternal border (apical or pulmonary areas) due to decreased blood viscosity, hyperdynamic circulation, and torsion of the great vessels.
    - A continuous hissing murmur called the "mammary souffle" may be heard over the tricuspid area (2nd to 4th intercostal space), resulting from increased blood flow through the internal mammary vessels.
- **ECG Changes:** Left axis deviation is seen, along with Q waves in leads II, III, and aVF, and flat or inverted T-waves in leads III and V1-V3.
- **Echocardiography:** Reveals an increase in left ventricular end-diastolic diameter, increased left and right atrial diameters, and occasionally a benign pericardial effusion.

**III. Hemodynamic Changes**

- **Cardiac Output (CO):**
    - Increases from the 5th week of pregnancy, reaching a peak of 40–50% (from 4.5 L/min to 6.26 L/min) above nonpregnant levels at 30–34 weeks, and remains static until term.
    - The increase in CO is the product of an increased Stroke Volume (+27%, rising from 65 mL to 75 mL) and an increased Heart Rate (+17%, rising from 70 to 85 bpm).
    - _Positional variation:_ CO is highest in the lateral or knee-chest position and lowest in the sitting or supine position.
    - _Labor and Postpartum:_ CO increases further during labor (+50% over prelabor values) and peaks immediately following delivery (+70%) due to autotransfusion of blood squeezed from the contracting uterus into the systemic circulation.
- **Systemic and Pulmonary Vascular Resistance:** Systemic Vascular Resistance (SVR) decreases by 21%, and Pulmonary Vascular Resistance (PVR) decreases by 34%. This marked vasodilatation is due to the smooth muscle relaxing effects of progesterone, nitric oxide, prostaglandins, and Atrial Natriuretic Peptide (ANP).
- **Central Hemodynamics:** Central Venous Pressure (CVP), Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP), and Pulmonary Capillary Wedge Pressure (PCWP) show no significant change. Colloid oncotic pressure decreases by 14% (from 20 mm Hg to 18 mm Hg).

**IV. Blood Pressure and Venous Pressure Changes**

- **Blood Pressure (BP):** Despite the 40–50% increase in CO, maternal BP decreases due to the significant fall in SVR. There is a mid-pregnancy drop in diastolic BP and MAP by 5–10 mm Hg, which gradually rises back to nonpregnant levels by term.
- **Venous Pressure:** Antecubital venous pressure is unaffected, but femoral venous pressure is markedly raised (increasing by 100%, from 8–10 cm water in the nonpregnant state to 20–25 cm water during pregnancy in the supine position, and up to 80–100 cm water while standing). This is due to mechanical compression of the common iliac veins and inferior vena cava by the gravid uterus (dextrorotation), leading to ankle edema, varicose veins, hemorrhoids, and a predisposition to deep vein thrombosis (DVT).
- **Supine Hypotension Syndrome (Postural Hypotension):** In about 10% of women during late pregnancy, the supine position causes severe compression of the inferior vena cava without adequate opening of collateral veins, causing curtailed venous return, hypotension, tachycardia, and syncope. Left lateral positioning quickly restores normal BP.

**V. Regional Distribution of Blood Flow**

- **Uterus:** Uterine blood flow increases from 50 mL/min to ~750 mL/min at term, utilizing about 15% (10% to 15%) of the total maternal cardiac output.
- **Lungs:** Pulmonary blood flow increases by 2500 mL/min.
- **Kidneys:** Renal blood flow increases by 400 mL/min by the 16th week and is maintained until term.
- **Skin and Mucous Membranes:** Blood flow reaches a maximum of 500 mL/min by the 36th week, explaining complaints of feeling hot, sweating, and nasal stuffiness.

**MATERNAL PHYSIOLOGICAL HEMATOLOGIC CHANGES**

**I. Blood Volume, Plasma, and Red Blood Cells**

- **Total Blood Volume:** Expands rapidly starting from the 6th week, reaching a maximum of 40–50% (an increase of ~1500 mL) above the nonpregnant level at 30–34 weeks, and remains static till delivery. Total blood volume reaches about 5500 mL at term.
- **Plasma Volume:** Increases by 40–50%, reaching a total increment of about 1.25 liters. The increase is greater in multigravidae and multiple pregnancies.
- **Red Blood Cell (RBC) Mass:** Increases by 20–30% (about 350 mL). This increase is stimulated by increased erythropoietin levels to meet the oxygen transport demands.
- **Hemodilution (Physiological Anemia):** The disproportionate increase in plasma volume relative to RBC volume produces a state of hemodilution. The hematocrit value falls to approximately 32%. Total hemoglobin mass increases by 18–20% (85 g), but the hemoglobin concentration falls by about 2 g/dL at term.
    - _Advantages of Hemodilution:_ Diminished blood viscosity ensures optimum fetomaternal gaseous exchange and protects the mother against the adverse effects of supine/erect posture and blood loss during delivery.

**II. Iron Metabolism**

- The total extra iron requirement during pregnancy is approximately 1,000 mg.
- Distribution: 300 mg goes to the fetus and placenta, 500 mg is utilized for the expanded red cell mass, and 200 mg is lost through normal obligatory routes. Pregnancy is considered an inevitable iron-deficiency state despite increased intestinal absorption.

**III. Leukocytes and Immune System**

- **WBC Count:** Neutrophilic leukocytosis occurs, rising to 8,000/mm³ during pregnancy and up to 20,000/mm³ during labor, likely due to increased levels of estrogen and cortisol.
- **Immune Modulation:** The major immune change is a modulation away from a cell-mediated (Th-1) cytotoxic response toward increased humoral and innate (Th-2) immune responses, which is beneficial for pregnancy maintenance.

**IV. Plasma Proteins**

- **Total Protein:** Increases from 180 g (nonpregnant) to 230 g at term.
- **Protein Concentration:** Due to hemodilution, plasma protein concentration falls from 7 g/dL to 6 g/dL.
- **Albumin and Globulin:** Albumin levels fall by about 30% (from 4.3 to 3.0 g/dL), while globulin (mainly α-globulin) slightly rises (2.7 to 3.0 g/dL).
- **A:G Ratio:** The normal albumin to globulin ratio of 1.7:1 is diminished to 1:1.

**V. Blood Coagulation Factors**

- **Hypercoagulable State:** Pregnancy is a hypercoagulable state. This is a physiological protective mechanism against the risk of postpartum hemorrhage.
- **Fibrinogen (Factor I):** Increases significantly by 50%, rising from 200–400 mg/dL to 300–600 mg/dL.
- **Other Clotting Factors:** There is an increase in plasma levels and activities of factors VII, VIII, IX, X, and I. Factors II, V, and XII are unchanged or mildly increased, while factors XI and XIII are slightly decreased.
- **Platelets:** Platelet count is generally static or shows a slight reduction (15% drop) to 1,60,000–2,00,000/mm³.
- **Fibrinolytic Activity:** Depressed during pregnancy and labor, returning to normal within 1 hour after placental delivery due to the liberation of plasminogen inhibitors from the placenta.
- **Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR):** Marked 4-fold increase from 10 mm/h to 40 mm/h, giving it little diagnostic value during pregnancy.

3. Breast changes in pregnancy
**BREAST CHANGES DURING PREGNANCY**

Breast changes are most evident in primigravidae; in multiparae who have previously lactated, the changes may not be as clearly defined.

**1. Symptoms**

- **Breast Discomfort:** A feeling of fullness and a 'pricking sensation' is usually evident as early as the 6th to 8th week of pregnancy, particularly in primigravidae.

**2. Size and Stroma**

- **Enlargement:** Increased breast size becomes evident in the early weeks.
- **Tissue Proliferation:** The enlargement is due to marked hypertrophy and proliferation of the ductal system and the alveoli, which is especially prominent in the peripheral lobules. There is also hypertrophy of the connective tissue stroma, and the myoepithelial cells become prominent.
- **Axillary Tail:** The axillary tail of Spence (a prolongation of breast tissue undercover of the pectoralis major muscle) may become enlarged and painful.

**3. Vascular and Cutaneous Changes**

- **Vascularity:** Increased vascular engorgement results in the appearance of delicate, bluish veins running under the skin, which becomes visible between 6 and 8 weeks.
- **Striae Gravidarum:** Mechanical stretching of the cutis (skin) often leads to the formation of striae (stretch marks) of varying degrees over the breasts.

**4. Nipple and Areolar Changes**

- **Nipples:** The nipples become larger, erectile, and deeply pigmented.
- **Primary Areola:** The areola becomes deeply pigmented, which is especially noticeable in dark-complexioned women.
- **Montgomery's Tubercles:** A variable number (5-15) of sebaceous glands situated in the areola, which are invisible in the nonpregnant state, become hypertrophied and prominent. They eventually extend into the secondary areola. Their secretion keeps the nipple and areola moist and healthy.
- **Secondary Areola:** An outer zone of less marked and irregularly pigmented area appears around the primary areola during the second trimester (typically around the 20th week).

**5. Secretory Changes (Colostrum)**

- **12th Week:** Secretion can be squeezed out of the breasts at about the 12th week, which initially appears sticky.
- **16th Week:** By the 16th week, the secretion becomes thick and yellowish, known as colostrum.
- **Clinical Significance:** The demonstration of secretion from the breasts of a woman who has never lactated is an important diagnostic sign of pregnancy.

**6. Hormonal Control**

- **Estrogen:** Responsible for the hypertrophy and proliferation of the breast ducts.
- **Estrogen and Progesterone (Combined):** Stimulate the growth, development, and proliferation of the lobuloalveolar system.
