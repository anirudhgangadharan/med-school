**DEFINITION OF LABOR AND NORMAL LABOR**

**Labor:** Labor is defined as the series of events that take place in the genital organs in an effort to expel the viable products of conception (fetus, placenta, and the membranes) out of the womb through the vagina into the outer world.

**Normal Labor (Eutocia):** Labor is classified as normal if it strictly fulfills the following criteria:

1. Spontaneous in onset and at term.
2. With vertex presentation.
3. Without undue prolongation.
4. Natural termination with minimal aids.
5. Without having any complications affecting the health of the mother and/or the baby.

---

**MECHANISM OF NORMAL LABOR**

**Definition:** The series of movements that occur on the fetal head in the process of adaptation during its journey through the pelvis is called the mechanism of labor.

While the principal movements occur on the head, the rest of the fetal trunk is also involved. In normal labor, the head enters the pelvic brim most commonly through the available transverse diameter (70%) or an oblique diameter (left occipitoanterior being more common). The engaging transverse diameter is the biparietal diameter (9.5 cm).

The principal movements in an occipitolateral position are sequentially:

**1. Engagement:** Engagement occurs when the greatest transverse diameter of the fetal head passes through the pelvic inlet. The head enters the brim in synclitism, or sometimes with asynclitism (deflection of the head in relation to the pelvis).

**2. Descent:** Descent is a continuous process throughout labor, heavily pronounced in the second stage. It is facilitated by:

- Uterine contraction and retraction.
- Maternal bearing-down efforts.
- Straightening of the fetal ovoid after the rupture of membranes.

**3. Flexion:** While some degree of flexion is present initially, complete flexion is achieved as the head descends and meets resistance from the birth canal. Increasing flexion ensures that the smaller suboccipitobregmatic diameter (9.5 cm) presents to the pelvic cavity instead of the larger occipitofrontal diameter (11.5 cm).

**4. Internal Rotation:** This is a crucial movement where the occiput rotates anteriorly to lie behind the symphysis pubis. The rotation typically occurs through 2/8ths of a circle. It is governed by the "law of the pelvic floor" (Hart's rule), guided by the tone of the levator ani muscles, well-flexed head, and efficient uterine contractions.

**5. Crowning:** After internal rotation, further descent stretches the vulval outlet. Crowning occurs when the maximum diameter of the head (biparietal diameter) stretches the vulval ring without any recession of the head even after the uterine contraction passes off.

**6. Delivery of the Head by Extension:** The head is born by extension due to the "couple of force" mechanism. The downward driving force of the uterus and the upward/forward resistance of the pelvic floor neutralize to create a forward thrust. The vertex, brow, and face are born successively over the stretched perineum.

**7. Restitution:** This is the visible passive movement of the head due to the untwisting of the neck. The head rotates 1/8th of a circle in the opposite direction of the internal rotation.

**8. External Rotation:** This movement is the external manifestation of the internal rotation of the shoulders. The head rotates further by 1/8th of a circle in the same direction as restitution, bringing the occiput to point toward the maternal thigh corresponding to the side it engaged.

**9. Birth of Shoulders and Trunk:** The anterior shoulder escapes below the symphysis pubis first. Then, by a movement of lateral flexion of the fetal spine, the posterior shoulder sweeps over the perineum, followed by the rest of the trunk.

**MANAGEMENT OF THE THIRD STAGE OF LABOR**

The third stage of labor begins after the expulsion of the fetus and ends with the expulsion of the placenta and membranes. Its average duration is 15 minutes, but it is the most crucial stage of labor because severe complications like Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH) can arise rapidly.

**Principles of Management:**

1. Ensure strict vigilance.
2. Prevent complications, primarily PPH.
3. Ensure complete expulsion of the placenta and membranes.

There are two methods of management: Active Management (the standard of care) and Expectant Management.

**A. Active Management of the Third Stage of Labor (AMTSL):** AMTSL is routinely recommended by the WHO for all births as it reduces the incidence of PPH by 60%, minimizes blood loss to one-fifth, shortens the duration of the third stage, and reduces the need for blood transfusions.

The key components include:

- **Administration of a Uterotonic:** Injection Oxytocin (10 IU, IM/IV) or Injection Methergine 0.2 mg IM is administered to the mother within one minute of the birth of the baby. Oxytocin is the preferred drug.
- **Delayed Cord Clamping:** Wait to clamp the cord to allow blood transfer to the infant, unless contraindicated.
- **Controlled Cord Traction (Brandt-Andrews Technique):** Once the uterus is contracted, the placenta is delivered by applying steady, downward traction on the umbilical cord while simultaneously applying counter-traction (upward and backward pressure) on the uterine fundus with the other hand.
- **Uterine Massage:** Following the delivery of the placenta, the fundus is massaged to ensure the uterus remains hard and well-retracted.

**B. Expectant (Physiological) Management:** In cases where active management is not utilized, a watchful expectancy is adopted.

- Wait for the classic signs of placental separation: (1) Uterus becomes globular, firm, and ballottable, (2) Permanent lengthening of the umbilical cord, (3) A slight gush of vaginal bleeding, and (4) Slight bulging in the suprapubic region.
- Once separation is confirmed, the mother is encouraged to bear down during a uterine contraction to expel the placenta.
- As the placenta passes through the introitus, it is grasped and twisted round and round with gentle traction to ensure the membranes are stripped intact without tearing.

**Immediate Post-Delivery Care (Applicable to Both Methods):**

- **Examination of the Placenta and Membranes:** The placenta is placed on a tray, washed, and the maternal surface is inspected for completeness (missing cotyledons or membranes). Any missing fragments demand urgent exploration of the uterus.
- **Inspection of the Birth Canal:** The vulva, vagina, and perineum are meticulously inspected for any lacerations or injuries. If present, or if an episiotomy was performed, they are promptly repaired.
- **Observation:** The mother's pulse, blood pressure, uterine tone, and vaginal bleeding are monitored closely for at least 1 hour after delivery (the fourth stage of labor).

**MECHANISM OF NORMAL LABOR IN LEFT OCCIPITO-ANTERIOR (LOA) POSITION**

**Definition and Basic Parameters**

- **Mechanism of Labor:** The series of movements that occur on the fetal head in the process of adaptation during its journey through the pelvis is called the mechanism of labor.
- **LOA Position:** The vertex occupying the left anterior quadrant of the pelvis is the most common position, known as the first vertex position or Left Occipitoanterior (LOA).
- **Lie:** Longitudinal.
- **Presentation:** Cephalic (Vertex).
- **Attitude:** Universal flexion.
- **Engaging Diameters:** The engaging anteroposterior diameter is the suboccipitobregmatic (9.5 cm) in complete flexion, or suboccipitofrontal (10 cm) in slight deflexion. The engaging transverse diameter is the biparietal diameter (9.5 cm).

**Principal Movements in LOA** The principal movements of the mechanism of labor in an LOA position occur in the following sequence:

1. **Engagement:** The head enters the pelvic brim. In primigravidae, it may occur before the onset of labor, while in multiparae, it usually occurs in the late first stage with the rupture of membranes.
2. **Descent:** This is a continuous process throughout labor, facilitated by uterine contraction and retraction, maternal bearing-down efforts, and straightening of the fetal ovoid after membrane rupture.
3. **Flexion:** As the head meets the resistance of the birth canal (unfolding cervix, pelvic walls, or pelvic floor), full flexion is achieved. This is explained by the "two-arm lever theory," which reduces the engaging diameter to the smaller suboccipitobregmatic plane.
4. **Internal Rotation:** The occiput rotates anteriorly by one-eighth of a circle to lie directly behind the symphysis pubis. This is guided by the slope of the pelvic floor muscles (Hart's rule) and the shape of the pelvis.
5. **Crowning:** Following internal rotation and further descent, the maximum transverse diameter of the head (biparietal diameter) stretches the vulval outlet without any recession of the head even after the contraction passes off.
6. **Delivery of the Head by Extension:** The head is born by extension via the 'couple of force' theory. The driving force pushes the head downward, while the pelvic floor offers resistance upward and forward. The subocciput hinges under the pubic arch, and the vertex, brow, and face are born successively over the stretched perineum.
7. **Restitution:** This is a visible passive movement of the head due to the untwisting of the neck. The head rotates by one-eighth of a circle in the direction opposite to internal rotation, placing the occiput pointing toward the maternal left thigh.
8. **External Rotation:** As the anterior shoulder rotates internally to fit the anteroposterior diameter of the outlet, the head is carried in a movement of external rotation through another one-eighth of a circle in the same direction as restitution. The occiput now points directly toward the maternal left thigh.
9. **Birth of the Shoulders and Trunk:** The anterior shoulder escapes below the symphysis pubis first, followed by the posterior shoulder sweeping over the perineum via lateral flexion of the spine. The rest of the trunk is then expelled by lateral flexion.

**MANAGEMENT OF NORMAL LABOR (LOA POSITION)**

The management of normal labor is divided by the stages of labor, prioritizing noninterference with watchful expectancy and maternal-fetal monitoring.

**I. Management of the First Stage**

- **General Care:** The woman may remain ambulatory if membranes are intact. Supine position should be avoided to prevent aortocaval compression; the left lateral tilt is preferred. Oral fluids (clear water, ice chips) are allowed, but solid food is withheld.
- **Bladder and Bowel:** Encourage spontaneous voiding; catheterize if necessary, as a full bladder can inhibit uterine contractions.
- **Pain Relief:** Provide adequate analgesia; continuous lumbar epidural is highly effective for complete pain relief. Emotional support and the presence of a labor companion significantly reduce labor duration and the need for analgesia.
- **Maternal and Fetal Monitoring (Using Partograph):**
    - Labor events are recorded on a partograph to quickly detect abnormal progress.
    - Check maternal vitals: pulse every 30 minutes, blood pressure every hour, and temperature every 2 hours.
    - Monitor uterine contractions (frequency and duration) every 30 minutes.
    - Fetal heart rate (FHR) should be monitored intermittently or by continuous electronic fetal monitoring.
- **Progress of Labor:** Vaginal examination is done at intervals (e.g., 4 hours) with strict asepsis to assess cervical dilatation, station of the head, and status of the membranes/liquor.

**II. Management of the Second Stage**

- **Preparation:** The patient is placed in the dorsal position with a 15° left lateral tilt or a semi-sitting/upright position. Maintain strict asepsis (Three Cs: Clean hands, clean surface, clean cutting of the cord).
- **Bearing Down:** The woman is encouraged to push voluntarily only during uterine contractions. Sustained pushing beyond the contraction is discouraged.
- **Delivery of the Head:** Maintain flexion of the head by pushing the occiput downward and backward while pressing the perineum with a sterile pad (guarding the perineum/perineal massage) to prevent severe tears. Deliver the head slowly between contractions by extension, using Ritgen's maneuver (pushing the chin through the anococcygeal region while applying pressure on the occiput).
- **Immediate Care:** Clear the baby's airway (oropharynx) using a bulb syringe or suction catheter. Check for the umbilical cord around the neck.
- **Delivery of Shoulders:** Wait for restitution and external rotation. Apply gentle downward traction to release the anterior shoulder from under the symphysis pubis, followed by upward traction to deliver the posterior shoulder. Avoid hooking fingers in the axilla to prevent brachial plexus injury.
- **Clamping the Cord:** The cord is clamped at two places and cut in between. Delayed cord clamping is recommended for term babies. Record the 1-minute and 5-minute Apgar scores.

**III. Management of the Third Stage** Active Management of the Third Stage of Labor (AMTSL) is routinely recommended as it reduces the risk of Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH) by 60%.

- **Uterotonic Administration:** Administer Oxytocin 10 IU intramuscularly (preferred) or Methergine 0.2 mg intravenously within 1 minute of the baby's birth.
- **Delivery of Placenta:** Once the uterus is contracted, the placenta is delivered by Controlled Cord Traction (CCT/Brandt-Andrews technique) during a uterine contraction.
- **Uterine Massage:** Immediately massage the uterus to ensure it remains hard and contracted.
- **Inspection:** Carefully inspect the expelled placenta and membranes for completeness. Thoroughly inspect the lower genital tract (vulva, vagina, perineum) for any traumatic lacerations and repair them, including the episiotomy.

**IV. Management of the Fourth Stage (Observation)**

- The mother is strictly observed in the labor room for at least 1 hour after delivery.
- Constantly monitor the pulse, blood pressure, the tone of the uterus (ensuring it is well retracted and hard), and check for any abnormal vaginal bleeding.


3. Mechanism of labor—phase and reason for each phase
**MECHANISM OF NORMAL LABOR**

**Definition** The series of movements that occur on the fetal head in the process of adaptation during its journey through the pelvis is called the mechanism of labor. While the principal movements take place in the head, the rest of the fetal trunk is also involved. The normal mechanism is typically described for an occipitolateral or oblique occipitoanterior position, which is the most common.

The principal phases of the mechanism of labor and the reasons/factors responsible for each are detailed below:

**1. Engagement**

- **Phase:** The head enters the pelvic brim, most commonly through the available transverse diameter or an oblique diameter. The sagittal suture may be deflected towards the symphysis pubis or sacral promontory, a phenomenon known as asynclitism.
- **Reason:** This allows the head to accommodate itself to the shape of the pelvic inlet. Lateral flexion of the head helps glide the parietal bones past the symphysis pubis or sacral promontory, enabling the head to enter the brim and eventually achieve synclitism.

**2. Descent**

- **Phase:** Descent is a continuous downward movement of the fetus throughout labor, becoming particularly pronounced in the second stage.
- **Reason:** Descent is facilitated by three main factors:
    1. Uterine contraction and retraction.
    2. Voluntary bearing-down efforts of the mother (contraction of abdominal muscles).
    3. Straightening of the fetal ovoid, especially after the rupture of the membranes.

**3. Flexion**

- **Phase:** The fetal head becomes fully flexed, bringing the chin close to the fetal chest.
- **Reason:** Flexion is achieved due to the resistance the descending head meets from the unfolding cervix, the walls of the pelvis, or the pelvic floor. It is biomechanically explained by the "two-arm lever theory". Complete flexion is essential as it reduces the shape and size of the advancing plane, bringing the smaller suboccipitobregmatic diameter (9.5 cm) to engage instead of larger diameters.

**4. Internal Rotation**

- **Phase:** The occiput rotates anteriorly (usually through 2/8ths or 3/8ths of a circle) to lie behind the symphysis pubis.
- **Reason:** This movement is of great importance, without which further descent cannot occur. The reasons for anterior rotation are explained by two main theories:
    - _Slope of the pelvic floor (Hart's rule):_ The two halves of the levator ani muscles form a gutter. During a contraction, the well-flexed occiput stretches the levator ani; as the contraction passes, the elastic recoil of the muscle brings the occiput forward toward the midline.
    - _Pelvic shape:_ The forward inclination of the side walls of the cavity, narrow bispinous diameter, and long anteroposterior diameter of the outlet guide the head into the anteroposterior plane.

**5. Crowning**

- **Phase:** Further descent occurs until the subocciput lies underneath the pubic arch. The maximum diameter of the head (biparietal diameter) stretches the vulval outlet without any recession of the head even after the uterine contraction passes off.
- **Reason:** The head is held up in advance by the permanent retraction of the uterine muscles, completely overcoming the elastic recoil of the pelvic floor.

**6. Extension**

- **Phase:** The delivery of the head takes place through the stretched vulval outlet. The successive parts to be born are the vertex, brow, and face.
- **Reason:** Delivery by extension is explained by the "couple of force" theory. The driving force of uterine contractions pushes the head in a downward direction, while the pelvic floor offers resistance in an upward and forward direction. These forces neutralize each other, and the remaining forward thrust results in the extension of the head.

**7. Restitution**

- **Phase:** Immediately after the head is born and drops down, there is a visible passive movement of the head where it turns to one side (1/8th of a circle).
- **Reason:** This occurs due to the untwisting of the fetal neck. During internal rotation of the head inside the pelvis, the shoulders remained in the oblique diameter, causing the neck to twist. Restitution untwists the neck to restore normal anatomical alignment with the shoulders.

**8. External Rotation**

- **Phase:** The delivered head rotates externally through another 1/8th of a circle in the same direction as restitution. The occiput now points directly toward the maternal thigh corresponding to the side it originally directed at the time of engagement.
- **Reason:** This movement occurs simultaneously with the internal rotation of the shoulders inside the pelvis. The shoulders rotate from the oblique diameter to the anteroposterior diameter of the pelvic outlet to facilitate their escape, causing the head to externally rotate in unison.

**9. Birth of the Shoulders and Trunk**

- **Phase:** The anterior shoulder escapes below the symphysis pubis first, followed by the posterior shoulder sweeping over the perineum. The rest of the trunk is then quickly expelled.
- **Reason:** The shoulders are born via a movement of lateral flexion of the fetal spine as they negotiate the curve of the birth canal. Once the shoulders are released, the smaller trunk is easily expelled by continuing lateral flexion.

4. Management of second stage of labor.

**MANAGEMENT OF SECOND STAGE OF LABOR**

**DEFINITION AND ONSET** The second stage of labor begins with the complete dilatation of the cervix and ends with the expulsion of the fetus. The transition from the first stage to the second stage is characterized by:

- Increasing intensity of uterine contractions.
- Appearance of voluntary bearing-down efforts.
- An urge to push or defecate accompanied by the descent of the presenting part.
- Complete dilatation of the cervix confirmed via vaginal examination.

**PRINCIPLES OF MANAGEMENT**

1. To assist in the natural expulsion of the fetus slowly and steadily.
2. To prevent perineal injuries.

**GENERAL MEASURES AND PREPARATION**

- **Constant Supervision:** The patient should be in bed under constant supervision, and the fetal heart rate (FHR) must be recorded every 5 minutes.
- **Maternal Position:** A dorsal position with a 15° left lateral tilt is commonly favored as it avoids aortocaval compression and facilitates pushing. Upright positions (sitting, kneeling, squatting) may also be chosen based on the woman's preference; they are associated with reduced duration, less pain, and fewer FHR abnormalities, though they may increase blood loss.
- **Analgesia:** Inhalation analgesics (such as N2O and O2) may be administered to relieve pain during contractions.
- **Vaginal Examination:** Performed at the beginning of the second stage to confirm the onset, exclude accidental cord prolapse, and evaluate the position, station, and progressive descent of the head.
- **Asepsis (The Three Cs):** Maintenance of strict surgical asepsis is paramount. It involves: (a) Clean hands, (b) Clean surface, and (c) Clean cutting and ligaturing of the cord. The accoucheur must scrub, wear sterile attire, and toilet the external genitalia and inner thighs with antiseptics (Savlon or Dettol).
- **Bladder Care:** The bladder should be catheterized if it is full.

**CONDUCTION OF DELIVERY** The assistance required during a spontaneous vaginal delivery is divided into three sequential phases:

**1. Delivery of the Head**

- **Objective:** To maintain flexion of the head, prevent early extension, and regulate its slow escape out of the vulval outlet.
- **Pushing:** The woman is encouraged and supported to follow her own urge to push (bear down) only during uterine contractions to facilitate descent.
- **Maintaining Flexion:** When the scalp is visible (about 5 cm in diameter), flexion is maintained during contractions by pushing the occiput downward and backward using the thumb and index fingers, while simultaneously pressing the perineum with the right palm and a sterile pad.
- **Crowning:** The head continues to descend until the subocciput lies underneath the pubic arch. At this point, the maximum diameter of the head (biparietal diameter) stretches the vulval outlet without receding even after the contraction passes off. This is termed 'crowning of the head'.
- **Episiotomy:** Routine or liberal use of episiotomy is not recommended by the WHO. However, a selective mediolateral episiotomy (at a 60° angle) is performed under 1% lignocaine infiltration when the perineum is fully stretched, thinned out, and threatens to tear, especially in primigravidae.
- **Ritgen’s Maneuver:** The head is delivered slowly by extension _in between_ uterine contractions. The chin is pushed forward using sterile-towel-covered fingers placed over the anococcygeal region, while the left hand exerts downward pressure on the occiput. This successively releases the forehead, nose, mouth, and chin over the stretched perineum.

**2. Care Following Delivery of the Head**

- **Airway Clearance:** Immediately after the head is born, the infant's air passage (oropharynx) must be cleared of mucus and liquor using a bulb syringe or suction catheter.

**3. Delivery of the Shoulders**

- **Wait for Rotation:** Do not be hasty. Wait for the next uterine contraction and for the natural movements of restitution and external rotation of the head to occur.
- **Extraction:** During the contraction, the anterior shoulder is born spontaneously behind the symphysis pubis. If delayed, the head is gently grasped by both hands and drawn posteriorly to release the anterior shoulder, followed by an upward draw to deliver the posterior shoulder over the perineum.
- **Caution:** Forcible traction from below must be strictly avoided to prevent early extension, entrapment of the aftercoming head, or birth trauma. Manual fundal pressure to facilitate childbirth during the second stage is highly contraindicated and not recommended.

**4. Delivery of the Trunk**

- Following the delivery of the shoulders, the rest of the trunk is effortlessly expelled by lateral flexion of the spine.

**IMMEDIATE CARE OF THE NEWBORN**

- **Apgar Scoring:** Rating is recorded at 1 minute and 5 minutes post-delivery.
- **Cord Clamping:** Delayed cord clamping is advantageous. The umbilical cord is clamped by two Kocher's forceps (the near one placed 6-10 cm away from the umbilicus) and cut in between. Two separate ligatures are firmly applied with sterile cotton threads about 1 cm apart.
- **Warmth and Identification:** The baby is wiped clean, wrapped in a dry warm towel, and identification tapes are tied to both the mother and the baby.

5. Management of third stage of labor
**MANAGEMENT OF THE THIRD STAGE OF LABOR**

The third stage of labor is the most crucial stage. A previously uneventful labor can quickly become abnormal with disastrous consequences, such as postpartum hemorrhage (PPH). It comprises the separation, descent, and expulsion of the placenta and its membranes.

**PRINCIPLES OF MANAGEMENT**

- To ensure strict vigilance.
- To strictly follow management guidelines to prevent complications, particularly postpartum hemorrhage (PPH).

Currently, there are two accepted methods of management: Active Management (preferred) and Expectant Management.

---

**I. ACTIVE MANAGEMENT OF THIRD STAGE OF LABOR (AMTSL)**

AMTSL is the preferred, WHO-recommended method for all births.

- **Underlying Principle:** To excite powerful uterine contractions within 1 minute of the delivery of the baby by administering a parenteral oxytocic. This facilitates early separation and effective uterine contractions.

**Steps of AMTSL:**

1. **Administration of Uterotonics:** Injection of oxytocin 10 units IM (the preferred drug), tablet misoprostol 600 µg (orally or rectally), or injection methergine 0.2 mg IM must be given to the mother within 1 minute of the birth of the baby.
2. **Controlled Cord Traction (CCT):** If the placenta is not delivered soon after the baby, it is expelled using the controlled cord traction (Brandt-Andrews) technique. This is performed by applying traction to the clamped cord while applying simultaneous counter-pressure on the uterus, strictly while the uterus remains contracted.
3. **Uterine Massage:** The uterus is massaged immediately after the delivery of the placenta to incite and maintain firm uterine contractions.

**Advantages of AMTSL:**

- Minimizes blood loss in the third stage to approximately one-fifth.
- Reduces the overall incidence of PPH by 60%.
- Shortens the duration of the third stage to half (typically around 5 minutes).
- Reduces maternal anemia and decreases the need for blood transfusions.

**Disadvantages & Precautions:**

- **Disadvantage:** There is a slight increase in the incidence of retained placenta (1–2%), which may require manual removal.
- **Caution in Twins:** In undiagnosed or diagnosed twins, oxytocics must strictly be withheld until _after_ the delivery of the second baby. Accidental administration after the first baby can cause tetanic uterine contractions, leading to asphyxia and danger to the unborn second baby.

---

**II. EXPECTANT (PHYSIOLOGICAL) MANAGEMENT**

- **Principle:** Placental separation and its descent into the vagina are allowed to occur spontaneously, with minimal assistance given for expulsion.
- **Watchful Expectancy:** A hand is placed over the fundus to recognize signs of separation and monitor uterine tone. The classic signs of placental separation include:
    - The uterus becomes discoid in shape, firm, and non-ballottable.
    - A slight gush of vaginal bleeding.
    - Permanent lengthening of the umbilical cord outside the vulva.
- **Expulsion:** Once separation and descent into the lower segment are confirmed, the patient is asked to bear down simultaneously with the hardening of the uterus.
- As the placenta passes through the introitus, it is grasped and twisted round and round with gentle traction to ensure the membranes are stripped intact without tearing.

---

**III. CARE FOLLOWING PLACENTAL EXPULSION (APPLICABLE TO BOTH METHODS)**

1. **Examination of Placenta and Membranes:** A careful routine examination of the expelled placenta and membranes must be done for completeness. Evidence of a missing cotyledon or missing membranes demands urgent exploration of the uterus.
2. **Inspection of the Genital Tract:** The vulva, vagina, and perineum are meticulously inspected for any traumatic injuries or tears. Any identified lacerations, as well as the episiotomy wound, must be repaired immediately.
3. **Postpartum Vigilance (Fourth Stage of Labor):** The mother must be closely observed for at least 1 hour after the delivery of the afterbirths.
    - Palpate the uterus every 15 minutes to ensure it remains well-retracted and firm.
    - Monitor vital signs (pulse and blood pressure).
    - Check for any abnormal vaginal bleeding. The patient is transferred to the ward only when she is hemodynamically stable and the uterus is firmly contracted.
4. Stages of labor
**STAGES OF LABOR**

Conventionally, the events of normal labor are divided into four distinct stages,.

**1. FIRST STAGE (Cervical Stage)**

- **Definition:** It starts from the onset of true labor pain and ends with the full dilatation of the cervix.
- **Events:** This stage is chiefly concerned with the preparation of the birth canal. The main events include:
    - Progressive dilatation and effacement (taking up) of the cervix,.
    - Full formation of the lower uterine segment.
- **Duration:** The average duration is about 12 hours in primigravidae and 6 hours (WHO defines up to 10 hours) in multiparae.
- **Phases:** It is divided into two phases:
    - **Latent Phase:** The preparatory phase characterized by painful contractions and variable cervical changes. It ends when the cervix is 5 cm dilated (WHO) or 6 cm dilated (ACOG),,,.
    - **Active Phase:** A period of rapid cervical dilatation starting from 5 cm (or 6 cm) until full dilatation (10 cm),.

**2. SECOND STAGE (Stage of Expulsion)**

- **Definition:** It starts from the full dilatation of the cervix (not from the rupture of the membranes) and ends with the expulsion of the fetus from the birth canal.
- **Events:** This stage is primarily concerned with the descent and delivery of the fetus.
- **Duration:** The average duration is 2 hours (WHO allows up to 3 hours) in primigravidae and 30 minutes (WHO allows up to 2 hours) in multiparae,.
- **Phases:** It consists of two phases:
    - **Propulsive (Passive) Phase:** Starts from full cervical dilatation until the descending presenting part touches the pelvic floor,.
    - **Expulsive (Active) Phase:** Characterized by maternal bearing-down efforts (irresistible urge to push) and ends with the delivery of the baby,.

**3. THIRD STAGE (Placental Stage)**

- **Definition:** It begins immediately after the expulsion of the fetus and ends with the expulsion of the placenta and the fetal membranes (afterbirths),.
- **Events:** It comprises three main phases:
    - Placental separation (due to marked reduction in the surface area of the placental site),.
    - Descent of the placenta into the lower uterine segment,.
    - Expulsion of the placenta and membranes to the outside.
- **Duration:** The average duration is about 15 minutes in both primigravidae and multiparae,. However, the duration is reduced to 5 minutes if active management of the third stage of labor (AMTSL) is practiced.

**4. FOURTH STAGE (Stage of Observation)**

- **Definition:** It is the stage of critical observation for at least 1 hour following the expulsion of the afterbirths.
- **Significance & Management:**
    - During this period, maternal vital signs (pulse and blood pressure), uterine retraction (ensuring the uterus is firm and well-retracted), and any abnormal vaginal bleeding are strictly monitored,.
    - The newborn baby is also examined.
    - The primary goal is to ensure both the mother and the baby are stable and to prevent/detect immediate postpartum hemorrhage,.

5. Fetal axis pressure.
**FETAL AXIS PRESSURE**

**Definition and Mechanism**

- During labor, in a fetus with a longitudinal lie and a well-fitted (flexed) head resting on the cervix, the contractions of the circular muscle fibers of the uterine body cause the fetal vertebral column to straighten.
- This straightening effect allows the strong contraction force generated at the uterine fundus to be effectively transmitted through the fetal podalic (breech) pole, down the fetal vertebral column, and directly to the well-fitted presenting head.
- With each uterine contraction, the contractions of the circular muscle fibers keep the fetal axis straight, which results in the elongation of the uterine ovoid and a simultaneous decrease in its transverse diameter.

**Role in Normal Labor**

- **Mechanical Stretching and Dilatation:** The downward thrust of the fetal head, driven by the fundal force transmitted through the fetal axis, causes mechanical stretching of the lower uterine segment and facilitates the opening up (dilatation) of the cervical canal.
- **Segmental Changes:** Under the influence of this pressure, along with progressive uterine contraction and retraction, the upper uterine segment becomes shorter and thicker, while the lower uterine segment becomes wider and thinner as the cervix dilates.

**Clinical Significance in Abnormal Labor**

- **Transverse Lie:** Fetal axis pressure is completely absent in a transverse lie, which is a key reason why there is no mechanism of labor and the cervix fails to dilate properly.
- **Occiput Posterior Position:** In an occiput posterior position where the fetal head is persistently deflexed, the driving force transmitted through the fetal axis is not in proper alignment with the axis of the pelvic inlet. This misalignment is a primary reason for delayed engagement in such malpositions.

6. Lower uterine segment.

**LOWER UTERINE SEGMENT (LUS)**

**Definition and Origin** The lower uterine segment (LUS) is the expanded and thinned-out portion of the uterus that is developed from the isthmus of the nonpregnant uterus. It acts as the completely passive segment of the uterus during labor.

**Anatomical Boundaries**

- **Before Labor:** It is bounded above by the anatomical internal os and below by the histological internal os.
- **During Labor:** It is bounded above by the physiological retraction ring and below by the fibromuscular junction of the cervix and uterus.

**Formation and Chronological Changes**

- **First Trimester:** The isthmus hypertrophies, becomes softer, and elongates to about 3 times its original length. The circular muscle fibers here act as a sphincter to retain the fetus.
- **Beyond 12 Weeks:** It progressively unfolds from above downwards until it is fully incorporated into the uterine cavity.
- **During Labor:** The LUS is maximally formed during labor. By the second stage of labor, it becomes cylindrical in shape and measures approximately 7.5–10 cm in length.
- **Mechanism of Thinning:** The wall of the LUS becomes progressively thin during labor due to three factors:
    1. Relaxation of the muscle fibers allowing for elongation.
    2. The muscle fibers are actively drawn up by the contracting and retracting fibers of the active upper uterine segment.
    3. Further stretching and thinning caused by the downward descent of the fetal presenting part.
- **Puerperium (Involution):** Immediately following delivery, the lower segment becomes thin and flabby. It takes a few weeks to completely revert to the normal shape and size of the nonpregnant isthmus.

**Anatomical Characteristics**

- It consists of poor retractile properties when compared to the active upper segment.
- The peritoneum over the anterior surface of the LUS is loosely attached.
- It exhibits a poor decidual reaction.

**Clinical and Obstetrical Significance (High-Yield)**

- **Facilitation of Delivery:** The physiological phenomenon of "receptive relaxation" in the LUS enables the expulsion of the fetus by forming a continuous, complete birth canal along with the fully dilated cervix.
- **Placenta Previa:** Implantation of the placenta in the lower segment is termed placenta previa. The LUS expands significantly from 0.5 cm at 20 weeks to >5 cm at term. Because the placenta is inelastic, this disproportionate growth shears the placenta off the wall of the LUS, causing inevitable bleeding.
- **Morbidly Adherent Placenta:** Due to the inherently poor decidual reaction in this segment, any placenta implanted here has a high risk of developing into a morbidly adherent placenta (placenta accreta, increta, or percreta).
- **Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH):** Because the LUS has poor retractile properties (imperfect retraction), there is a significant risk of severe postpartum hemorrhage if the placenta is implanted in this area.
- **Site for Cesarean Section:** It is the preferred site through which a Lower Segment Cesarean Section (LSCS) is performed. A transverse incision here results in a sound scar with perfect muscle apposition, minimal bleeding, and a remarkably low risk of scar rupture in future pregnancies (0.5–1.5%) compared to classical scars.
- **Rupture of Uterus in Obstructed Labor:** In cases of neglected or obstructed labor, the upper segment retracts vigorously while the lower segment progressively dilates, elongates, and becomes excessively thinned out. A pathological retraction ring (Bandl's ring) forms, and the excessively stretched LUS eventually gives way and ruptures, a catastrophe seen especially in multiparous women. Spontaneous obstructive rupture classically involves the anterior lower segment transversely.

7. Asynclitism.

**ASYNCLITISM**

**Definition**

- Asynclitism is defined as the deflection of the fetal head in relation to the pelvis during engagement.
- Instead of the sagittal suture lying exactly midway between the symphysis pubis and the sacral promontory (which is called synclitism), it is deflected anteriorly or posteriorly.
- Consequently, the two parietal eminences cross the pelvic brim one at a time rather than simultaneously.

**Types of Asynclitism** Based on the leading presenting bone, asynclitism is classified into two types:

**1. Posterior Asynclitism (Posterior Parietal Presentation / Litzmann's Obliquity)**

- **Feature:** The sagittal suture lies anteriorly (towards the symphysis pubis), making the posterior parietal bone the leading presenting part.
- **Occurrence:** It is more frequently found in primigravidae.
- **Reason:** This occurs due to the good uterine tone and a tight abdominal wall commonly seen in first-time mothers.
- **Correction:** Posterior lateral flexion of the head occurs to glide the anterior parietal bone past the symphysis pubis.

**2. Anterior Asynclitism (Anterior Parietal Presentation / Naegele's Obliquity)**

- **Feature:** The sagittal suture lies more posteriorly (towards the sacral promontory), making the anterior parietal bone the leading presenting part.
- **Occurrence:** It is more commonly found in multiparae.
- **Correction:** Lateral flexion in the reverse direction occurs to glide the posterior parietal bone past the sacral promontory.

**Advantages and Mechanism**

- Mild asynclitism is highly beneficial in the mechanism of head engagement.
- When the head engages with asynclitism, it allows the lesser **super-subparietal diameter (8.5 cm)** to cross the pelvic brim instead of the larger biparietal diameter (9.5 cm) that engages during synclitism.
- Following the compensatory lateral flexion early in labor, the head successfully enters the brim and synclitism is eventually achieved.

**Clinical Significance and Prognosis**

- **Normal Finding:** Mild degrees of asynclitism are a common and physiological mechanism of labor.
- **Pathological Indicator:** Marked and persistent asynclitism is abnormal.
- Severe degrees of asynclitism, or marked asynclitism of the presenting head during the course of labor, strongly indicate **cephalopelvic disproportion (CPD)** and suggest contraction of the pelvic inlet.


8. **Restitution (stages of labor).**

**RESTITUTION IN THE MECHANISM OF LABOR**

**Definition** Restitution is the visible, passive movement of the fetal head that occurs immediately following its delivery through the vulval outlet.

**Mechanism and Reason**

- During the mechanism of labor, as the head undergoes internal rotation to navigate the birth canal, the fetal shoulders remain in the oblique diameter, which causes the fetal neck to sustain a twist.
- Once the head is born (freed from the constraints of the maternal pelvis), restitution occurs as a natural untwisting of the neck to restore its normal anatomical alignment with the shoulders.

**Degree and Direction of Movement**

- **Degree:** The head rotates through exactly **one-eighth (1/8th) of a circle**.
- **Direction:** The rotation occurs in the **opposite direction** to that of the internal rotation.

**Resultant Position**

- Following restitution, the fetal occiput points directly toward the maternal thigh corresponding to the side to which it originally lay at the time of engagement.

**Chronological Sequence in Normal Labor** It is a cardinal movement of the second stage of labor, occurring sequentially after the delivery of the head by extension and immediately preceding external rotation.

**Restitution in Specific Positions and Presentations**

- **Occipitoposterior (OP) Position:** Restitution involves a movement to the extent of 1/8th of a circle in the opposite direction of the internal rotation of the head.
- **Face Presentation:** The delivered head moves 1/8th of a circle in the opposite direction of internal rotation, which turns the fetal face to look toward the mother's left thigh in ROP (Right Occipitoposterior) and toward the right thigh in LOP (Left Occipitoposterior).
- **Breech Presentation:** In breech delivery, restitution refers to the untwisting of the fetal _trunk_. This movement puts the anterior shoulder toward the right thigh in Left Sacroanterior (LSA) position and toward the left thigh in Right Sacroanterior (RSA) position.

9. Signs of placental separation.
**SIGNS OF PLACENTAL SEPARATION**

During the third stage of labor, the placenta typically separates within 5 minutes in conventional management. The clinical signs that confirm the complete separation of the placenta are classically divided into abdominal and vaginal findings.

**1. Per Abdomen Signs**

- **Change in Uterine Shape and Feel:** Before separation, the uterus is discoid, firm, and non-ballottable. Following separation, the uterus characteristically becomes **globular, firm, and ballottable**.
- **Rise in Fundal Height:** The fundal height becomes slightly raised. This occurs because the completely separated placenta drops down into the lower uterine segment and the vagina, causing the contracted, globular uterus to rest on top of it.
- **Suprapubic Bulge:** A slight bulging appears in the suprapubic region. This is due to the distension of the lower uterine segment by the bulky, separated placenta.

**2. Per Vaginam Signs**

- **Gush of Bleeding:** There is typically a slight gush of vaginal bleeding as the blood collected behind the placenta (retroplacental hematoma) escapes.
- **Permanent Lengthening of the Cord:** This is a highly reliable sign. It can be elicited clinically by two methods:
    - _Fundal Pressure:_ When the uterine fundus is pushed down, a further length of the umbilical cord comes outside the vulva and remains permanently outside even after the pressure is released.
    - _Suprapubic Pressure:_ On applying upward suprapubic pressure with the fingers, there is no indrawing (recession) of the cord, and it remains unchanged outside the vulva. Before separation, upward pressure would cause the cord to be drawn back into the vagina.
8. Active management of 3rd stage of labor (AMTSL).
**ACTIVE MANAGEMENT OF THIRD STAGE OF LABOR (AMTSL)**

**PRINCIPLE** The underlying principle in the active management of the third stage of labor is to excite powerful uterine contractions within 1 minute of the delivery of the baby by administering a parenteral oxytocic. This intervention facilitates early separation of the placenta and produces effective uterine contractions following its separation, thereby securing hemostasis.

**INDICATIONS**

- **Routine Use:** AMTSL is routinely recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) for all births as a prophylactic measure for the prevention of postpartum hemorrhage (PPH).
- **High-Risk Cases:** It is especially valuable for cases likely to develop PPH, such as women with anemia, hydramnios, twin pregnancies, multiparity, and a previous history of PPH.

**STEPS OF MANAGEMENT** The active management protocol typically comprises the following sequential steps:

**1. Administration of a Uterotonic Agent**

- An oxytocic drug must be administered to the mother within 1 minute of the birth of the baby.
- **Preferred Drug:** Injection Oxytocin (10 IU) given intramuscularly (IM) or intravenously (IV) is the recommended uterotonic for the prevention of PPH.
- **Alternatives:** If oxytocin is unavailable, Tablet Misoprostol 600 $\mu$g (orally or rectally), or Injection Methergine 0.2 mg IM can be given. Misoprostol can safely be administered by a community health worker in home delivery settings.

**2. Delayed Umbilical Cord Clamping**

- Delayed umbilical cord clamping (not earlier than 1 minute after birth) is recommended for improved maternal and infant health and nutrition outcomes.

**3. Controlled Cord Traction (CCT) / Brandt-Andrews Technique**

- The placenta is delivered by controlled cord traction after clamping the cord, strictly while the uterus remains contracted.
- _Technique:_ The palmar surface of the fingers of the left hand is placed above the symphysis pubis to apply counter-traction (pushing the fundus downward and backward), while the right hand applies steady traction on the umbilical cord.

**4. Post-Delivery Assessment**

- **Uterine Massage:** While traditional guidelines advocated for routine massage, the WHO currently states that _sustained_ uterine massage is not recommended as a prophylactic intervention to prevent postpartum hemorrhage. However, the uterus must be checked to ensure it remains well-retracted and firm.
- The expelled placenta and membranes must be meticulously examined for completeness.

**ADVANTAGES OF AMTSL**

- **Reduction in PPH:** The use of AMTSL reduces the overall incidence of postpartum hemorrhage by 60%.
- **Decreased Blood Loss:** It minimizes blood loss during the third stage of labor to approximately one-fifth of what is typically lost.
- **Shortened Duration:** It shortens the average duration of the third stage of labor by half, reducing it to approximately 5 minutes.
- **Reduced Morbidity:** It significantly reduces maternal anemia and decreases the need for subsequent blood transfusions and PPH management.
- **Reduced Medication Need:** It reduces the need for additional therapeutic oxytocics by 50%.

**DISADVANTAGES AND PRECAUTIONS**

- **Retained Placenta:** The only notable disadvantage is a slightly increased incidence of retained placenta (1–2%), which leads to a consequent increased incidence of manual removal of the placenta.
- **Strict Precaution in Twin Pregnancies:** In cases of twins (diagnosed or undiagnosed), the oxytocic agent must be strictly withheld until _after_ the delivery of the second baby. Accidental administration following the birth of the first baby causes tetanic contraction of the uterus, posing a severe danger of asphyxia to the unborn second baby.
- **Contraindications for Specific Uterotonics:** Methergine (methylergometrine) must not be used in women with cardiac diseases or severe pre-eclampsia/eclampsia. In cardiac cases, it risks precipitating cardiac overload and failure, and in hypertensive cases, it can severely aggravate blood pressure and precipitate eclamptic convulsions.
