### **1. DEFINITION OF ANEMIA IN PREGNANCY**

- **WHO Definition:** Anemia in pregnancy is present when the hemoglobin (Hb) concentration in the peripheral blood is 11 g/dL or less, or the hematocrit is less than 33%.
- **CDC Definition:** Hemoglobin concentration of <11 g/dL (hematocrit <33%) in the first or third trimester, or a hemoglobin concentration of <10.5 g/dL (hematocrit <32%) in the second trimester.
- **Clinical Benchmark:** Due to physiological hemodilution, a hemoglobin level dropping below 10 g/dL at any time during pregnancy is considered anemia and a level at or below 9 g/dL requires detailed investigations and appropriate treatment.

### **2. CLASSIFICATION OF ANEMIA**

**General Classification**

1. **Physiological Anemia of Pregnancy**.
2. **Pathological Anemia:**
    - **Deficiency Anemia (isolated or combined):** Iron deficiency (accounts for 95% of cases), Folic acid deficiency, Vitamin B12 deficiency, and Protein deficiency.
    - **Hemorrhagic Anemia:** Acute (following bleeding in early months or antepartum hemorrhage) and Chronic (hookworm infestation, bleeding piles).
    - **Hereditary/Hemoglobinopathies:** Thalassemias, Sickle cell hemoglobinopathies, and Hereditary hemolytic anemias (e.g., spherocytosis).
    - **Bone Marrow Insufficiency:** Hypoplasia or aplasia due to radiation or drugs.
    - **Anemia of Infection:** Malaria, tuberculosis, kala-azar.
    - **Chronic Disease or Neoplasm:** Chronic renal disease or hematologic malignancy (leukemias, lymphomas).
    - **Hemolytic Anemia:** Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE), HELLP syndrome, autoimmune hemolysis, drug-induced G6PD deficiency.

**WHO Grading of Anemia (Based on Hb Levels)**

- **Normal in pregnancy:** $\ge$ 11 g/dL.
- **Mild Anemia:** 10.0 – 10.9 g/dL.
- **Moderate Anemia:** 7.0 – 9.9 g/dL.
- **Severe Anemia:** < 7 g/dL.
- **Very Severe Anemia:** < 4 g/dL.

### **3. ETIOLOGY**

**Causes of Iron Deficiency Anemia (IDA)** About 75-95% of anemia during pregnancy is secondary to iron deficiency. The causes are divided into pre-pregnancy and during-pregnancy factors:

- **Inadequate Dietary Intake:** Vegetarian or iron-poor diet, anorexia, and vomiting in pregnancy.
- **Faulty Absorption:** High phosphate/phytic acid in carbohydrate-rich diets, intestinal hurry, hypochlorhydria, and the use of antacids/H2 blockers which inhibit iron absorption.
- **Increased Demand:** The demand for iron is markedly increased in pregnancy, and even more so in multiple pregnancies and rapidly recurring pregnancies (interval < 2 years).
- **Excessive Blood Loss:** Heavy Menstrual Bleeding (HMB) before pregnancy, bleeding piles, dysentery, hookworm infestation, and malaria.

**Causes of Megaloblastic Anemia** Megaloblastic anemia in pregnancy is almost always due to folic acid deficiency, while Vitamin B12 deficiency is rare.

- **Folic Acid Deficiency:** Due to inadequate intake (excessive cooking destroys folate), increased demand (growing fetus, multiple pregnancy), diminished absorption (malabsorption syndrome), and abnormal demand (hemorrhagic or hemolytic states).
- **Vitamin B12 Deficiency:** Rare, but can occur due to a strict vegetarian diet, gastrectomy, bariatric surgery, Crohn's disease, or Addisonian pernicious anemia.

### **4. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY**

**Physiological Anemia of Pregnancy**

- Maternal plasma volume expands rapidly from the 6th week, reaching a maximum of 40-50% (about 1.25 liters) above the nonpregnant level by 30-34 weeks.
- Simultaneously, the Red Blood Cell (RBC) mass increases by only 20-30% (about 350 mL).
- This disproportionate increase produces a state of relative hemodilution, causing a physiological drop in the hemoglobin concentration and hematocrit, despite a total increase in hemoglobin mass.

**Pathophysiology of Iron Deficiency**

- The total iron requirement during pregnancy is approximately 1,000 mg (300 mg for the fetus and placenta, 500 mg for the expanded red cell mass, and 200 mg for obligatory loss).
- This extra demand is predominantly concentrated in the second half of the pregnancy, translating to a daily requirement of 6-7 mg, which cannot be met by diet alone.
- When iron stores are depleted, erythropoiesis is restricted, leading to a reduced amount of hemoglobin and erythrocytes (microcytic hypochromic anemia).

**Pathophysiology of Megaloblastic Anemia**

- Deficiency of either Vitamin B12 or folate impairs DNA synthesis.
- This leads to derangement in red cell maturation and the production of abnormal, large precursors known as megaloblasts in the bone marrow.

### **5. DIAGNOSIS (CLINICAL FEATURES)**

**Symptoms**

- Often asymptomatic in mild cases and detected on routine blood testing.
- Easy fatigue, weakness, and exhaustion.
- Anorexia and indigestion.
- Palpitations, dyspnea (breathlessness), and giddiness.
- In megaloblastic anemia, patients may also present with occasional diarrhea and unexplained fever.

**Signs**

- Pallor of varying degrees (examined at the conjunctiva, tongue, nails, and palmar creases).
- Glossitis and stomatitis.
- Nail changes: Brittle, fragile nails with ridges, or Koilonychia (spoon-shaped nails).
- Edema of the legs (due to associated hypoproteinemia).
- In severe cases, a systolic flow murmur may be auscultated over the cardiac apex.

### **6. INVESTIGATIONS**

The diagnostic workup aims to determine the severity, type, and exact cause of the anemia.

**To Note the Degree of Anemia**

- **Hemoglobin (Hb) estimation:** Confirms the diagnosis if <11 g/dL.
- **Total Red Cell Count and Packed Cell Volume (PCV/Hematocrit):** PCV < 33% is indicative of anemia.

**To Ascertain the Type of Anemia**

- **Peripheral Blood Smear:** Stained with Leishman stain. Abundant small, pale-staining cells with variation in size (anisocytosis) and shape (poikilocytosis) confirm microcytic hypochromic anemia (typical of iron deficiency). The presence of hypersegmented neutrophils and macrocytes indicates megaloblastic anemia.
- **Hematological Indices:**
    - _Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin Concentration (MCHC):_ The most sensitive index of iron deficiency anemia (typically < 30%).
    - _Mean Corpuscular Volume (MCV):_ < 75 fL in iron deficiency; > 94 fL in megaloblastic anemia.
    - _Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin (MCH):_ < 25 pg in iron deficiency.

**Iron Profile & Specialized Blood Tests**

- **Serum Ferritin:** The single best test to diagnose iron deficiency anemia; it reflects total body iron stores (low in IDA).
- **Serum Iron and Total Iron Binding Capacity (TIBC):** In IDA, serum iron is low and TIBC is elevated.
- **Folate and B12 Levels:** Red cell folate < 3 ng/mL and Serum Vitamin B12 < 90 pg/mL confirm megaloblastic anemia.

**To Find Out the Cause**

- **Stool Examination:** To detect helminthic ova (especially hookworm) or occult blood.
- **Urine Analysis (Routine and Culture):** To rule out asymptomatic bacteriuria or overt urinary tract infection which can depress bone marrow.
- **Hemoglobin Electrophoresis (HPLC):** Indicated for women with low MCV but normal iron profile to screen for hemoglobinopathies like Thalassemia (checking HbA2 levels) or Sickle Cell Trait.
