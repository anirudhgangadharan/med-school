### **1. INTRODUCTION TO AMNIOTIC FLUID PARAMETERS**

- **Normal Volume:** Amniotic fluid volume correlates with gestational age. It reaches a peak of about 1 Liter at 36–38 weeks and decreases to about 600–800 mL at term.
- **Circulation:** It is a highly dynamic fluid, completely replaced every 3 hours.
- **Disorders:** Abnormalities in amniotic fluid volume—either an excess (polyhydramnios) or a deficit (oligohydramnios)—are significant indicators of underlying maternal or fetal pathology and carry high risks of perinatal morbidity and mortality.

### **2. POLYHYDRAMNIOS (HYDRAMNIOS)**

**Definition and Incidence**

- **Anatomical Definition:** A state where the liquor amnii exceeds 2,000 mL.
- **Clinical Definition:** Excessive accumulation of liquor amnii causing discomfort to the patient and/or requiring imaging to substantiate the clinical diagnosis of the fetal lie and presentation.
- **Sonographic Definition:** Amniotic Fluid Index (AFI) > 25 cm (>95th centile) and a Maximum Vertical Pocket (MVP) > 8 cm.
- **Incidence:** 1–2% of pregnancies, more common in multiparae than primigravidae.

**Etiology** The exact cause is often a mix of excessive production and deficient absorption of liquor.

- **Fetal Anomalies (20%):**
    - _CNS:_ Anencephaly (causes transudation from exposed meninges, absent swallowing reflex, lack of ADH), open spina bifida.
    - _GIT:_ Esophageal or duodenal atresia (prevents swallowing of liquor).
    - _Others:_ Facial clefts, neck masses, hydrops fetalis, aneuploidy, muscular dystrophy, and fetal infections (TORCH, parvovirus B19).
- **Placental Factors:** Chorioangioma (tumor of a single villus causing increased transudation).
- **Multiple Pregnancy:** 10 times more common; typically affects the second sac in monozygotic twins. In Twin-to-Twin Transfusion Syndrome (TTTS), the recipient twin develops polyhydramnios.
- **Maternal Factors:** Diabetes Mellitus (present in ~30% of cases; maternal hyperglycemia causes fetal hyperglycemia and diuresis), cardiac or renal disease (causes placental edema).
- **Idiopathic:** 50–60% of cases have no identifiable cause.

**Clinical Types and Features**

- **Types:**
    - _Chronic (Most Common):_ Insidious onset over a few weeks. Divided into Mild (MVP 8-11 cm), Moderate (MVP 12-15 cm), and Severe (MVP >16 cm).
    - _Acute (Rare):_ Sudden accumulation within a few days.
- **Symptoms:** Mechanical distress including dyspnea (patient may need to sit up to breathe), palpitation, edema of legs/vulva, varicosities, and hemorrhoids.
- **Signs:** Markedly enlarged globular abdomen with tense, shiny skin and large striae. Fundal height > period of amenorrhea, increased abdominal girth, presence of a fluid thrill. Fetal parts and FHS are extremely difficult to palpate or auscultate; external ballottement is easily elicited.

**Diagnosis and Investigations**

- **Ultrasonography:** The diagnostic gold standard. Identifies MVP > 8 cm or AFI > 25 cm, rules out multiple fetuses, checks fetal lie/presentation, and screens for structural/genetic anomalies.
- **Blood Tests:** ABO/Rh grouping (to rule out isoimmunization/hydrops), Postprandial blood sugar or GTT (for diabetes).
- **Amniotic Fluid:** Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) estimation (markedly elevated in open neural tube defects).
- **Differential Diagnosis:** Twins, large ovarian cyst, maternal ascites.

**Complications**

- **Maternal (Pregnancy & Labor):** Pre-eclampsia (25%), malpresentation, premature rupture of membranes (PROM), preterm labor, accidental hemorrhage (abruptio placentae due to sudden decompression), cord prolapse, uterine inertia, and postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) due to uterine atony.
- **Fetal:** Increased perinatal mortality (~50%) mainly due to prematurity and congenital anomalies. Also risk of cord prolapse and hypoxia.

**Management**

- **Mild Polyhydramnios:** Usually requires no active treatment except extra bed rest. Often transient.
- **Severe Polyhydramnios:** Requires hospitalization in a high-risk unit.
    - _Supportive Therapy:_ Bed rest, treatment of pre-eclampsia/diabetes.
    - _Medical Therapy:_ Indomethacin (COX-2 inhibitor) can reduce fetal urine output, but prolonged use risks premature closure of the ductus arteriosus.
    - _Pregnancy < 37 weeks (with maternal distress):_ Amnioreduction (slow decompression removing ~500 mL/hour) to relieve distress and prolong pregnancy until AFI < 25 cm.
    - _Pregnancy > 37 weeks:_ Induction of labor via controlled Artificial Rupture of Membranes (ARM) combined with oxytocin infusion. Care must be taken to prevent sudden gush of liquor to avoid placental abruption or cord prolapse.
    - _Fetal Anomaly Present:_ Referral to a fetal medicine unit and termination of pregnancy irrespective of gestational age.
    - _Intrapartum:_ Strict monitoring for cord prolapse and active management of the 3rd stage with oxytocics (e.g., IV methergine) to prevent PPH.

### **3. ACUTE POLYHYDRAMNIOS**

- Extremely rare, occurs before 20 weeks of pregnancy. Rapid accumulation of fluid over a few days.
- Usually associated with monozygotic twins (TTTS) or placental chorioangioma.
- Presents with acute abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting.
- Often ends in spontaneous abortion. In TTTS, repetitive amnioreduction or laser ablation improves outcomes.

### **4. OLIGOHYDRAMNIOS**

**Definition**

- An extremely rare condition where the liquor amnii is deficient, measuring less than 200 mL at term.
- **Sonographic Definition:** Maximum Vertical Pocket (MVP) < 2 cm or an Amniotic Fluid Index (AFI) < 5 cm (< 5th percentile). Absence of any measurable pocket is termed _anhydramnios_.

**Etiology**

- **Fetal Factors:** Chromosomal or structural anomalies (especially renal agenesis or obstructed uropathy), PROM, intrauterine infection, Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR), postmaturity.
- **Maternal Factors:** Hypertensive disorders, uteroplacental insufficiency, dehydration, Antiphospholipid syndrome.
- **Placental Factors:** Amnion nodosum (failure of secretion by amnion cells).
- **Drugs:** Prostaglandin (PG) inhibitors (e.g., Indomethacin), ACE inhibitors.

**Diagnosis**

- **Clinical:** Uterine size is much smaller than the period of amenorrhea, decreased fetal movements, uterus feels "full of fetus," and malpresentation (e.g., breech) is common.
- **Sonography:** Confirms AFI < 5 cm or MVP < 2 cm. It helps evaluate fetal urinary bladder filling/emptying to rule out urinary tract abnormalities.

**Complications**

- **Fetal:** Abortion, skeletal deformities due to compression and intra-amniotic adhesions (e.g., club foot, wry neck, limb amputation), fetal pulmonary hypoplasia (can be cause or effect), umbilical cord compression, and high fetal mortality.
- **Maternal:** Prolonged labor due to uterine inertia and increased rates of operative interference (cesarean delivery) due to malpresentation and fetal distress.

**Management**

- **Antenatal:** Directed at finding the cause. Severe anomalies warrant referral to a fetal medicine unit. Isolated oligohydramnios in the 3rd trimester with a normal fetus is managed conservatively. Maternal hydration (oral water) can increase amniotic fluid volume.
- **Intrapartum:** Constant fetal monitoring for signs of cord compression (variable decelerations). **Amnioinfusion** (infusion of normal saline into the amniotic cavity) is utilized during labor to relieve cord compression or to dilute meconium-stained liquor, which significantly improves the neonatal outcome. Delivery is recommended if there are signs of fetal compromise.
