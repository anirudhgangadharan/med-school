### **1. Investigations of Anemia in Pregnancy**

The investigations are designed to determine the severity, type, and underlying cause of the anemia.

**A. To Note the Degree of Anemia:** This requires a basic hematological examination, which includes the estimation of:

- Hemoglobin (Hb) concentration.
- Total Red Blood Cell (RBC) count.
- Packed Cell Volume (PCV) or Hematocrit.

**B. To Ascertain the Type of Anemia:**

- **Peripheral Blood Smear (PBF):** Essential to study the morphology of red blood cells. In iron deficiency anemia, the smear shows abundant small, pale-staining cells (microcytic hypochromic) with variation in size (anisocytosis) and shape (poikilocytosis). In megaloblastic anemia, it shows hypersegmentation of neutrophils (5 or more lobes), macrocytosis, giant polymorphs, megaloblasts, and Howell-Jolly bodies.
- **Hematological Indices:**
    - Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin Concentration (MCHC): <30% (This is the most sensitive index of iron deficiency anemia).
    - Mean Corpuscular Volume (MCV): <75 µ³ (or <79 fL).
    - Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin (MCH): <25 pg.
- **Serum Iron Studies (Diagnostic of Iron Deficiency):**
    - Serum Ferritin: <30 µg/L (This is the most reliable indicator of iron deficiency, as it is the main storage protein for iron).
    - Serum Iron: <30 µg/100 mL.
    - Total Iron Binding Capacity (TIBC): Raised (>400 µg/dL).
    - Transferrin Saturation: $\le$10% to <16%.
- **Specific Tests for Megaloblastic Anemia:**
    - Red cell folate: <3 ng/mL (Normal nonpregnant level is 2.8–8 ng/mL).
    - Serum Vitamin B12: <90 pg/mL (Normal level is 300 pg/mL).

**C. To Find Out the Cause of Anemia:**

- **Stool Examination:** Done as a routine in the tropics to detect helminthic (particularly hookworm) infestation.
- **Urine Examination:** Checked for protein, sugar, and pus cells. A 'clean catch' midstream specimen is subjected to culture and colony count to detect asymptomatic bacteriuria or urinary tract infection (counts >$10^5$/mL).
- **Hemoglobin Electrophoresis (HPLC):** Indicated to screen for hemoglobinopathies like Thalassemia or Sickle cell trait, especially when MCV is low but iron studies are normal.
- **Chest X-ray:** Indicated if pulmonary tuberculosis is suspected.
- **Bone Marrow Study:** Not a routine test. It is indicated only when the anemia is refractory (not responding to appropriate therapy), to diagnose hypoplastic/aplastic anemia, or to detect _Leishmania donovani_ (LD) bodies for kala-azar.

### **2. Adverse Effects of Anemia in Pregnancy**

Anemia in pregnancy directly or indirectly contributes to about 20% of maternal deaths in low and middle-income countries.

**A. Maternal Complications:**

- **During Pregnancy:**
    - Increased incidence of pre-eclampsia (which may be related to hypoproteinemia).
    - Intercurrent infections (pre-existing diseases may flare up, and infection further depresses bone marrow erythropoiesis).
    - Heart failure (highest risk around 30-32 weeks of gestation) and angina pectoris.
    - Preterm labor and prematurity.
    - Atrophic glossitis and sideropenic dysphagia (Plummer-Vinson syndrome).
- **During Labor:**
    - Postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) is a real threat, as the anemic patient cannot tolerate even a minimal amount of normal blood loss.
    - Congestive Cardiac Failure (CCF), which mostly occurs during labor or immediately following delivery due to the sudden volume load squeezed into the general circulation from the contracting uterus.
    - Profound shock and lethal hypoxia following even slight hemorrhage.
- **During Puerperium:**
    - Increased risk of puerperal sepsis, delayed wound healing, and subinvolution.
    - Puerperal venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism.
    - Poor lactation and postpartum depression.

_High-Yield Note: The high-risk periods for sudden maternal death in anemia are: (1) 30–32 weeks of pregnancy, (2) During labor, (3) Immediately following delivery, and (4) 7–10 days postpartum (due to cardiac failure or pulmonary embolism)._

**B. Fetal and Neonatal Complications:** The amount of iron transferred to the fetus is generally unaffected unless the mother suffers from severe iron deficiency anemia. However, the sum effect leads to significantly increased perinatal loss due to:

- Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR).
- Prematurity and preterm delivery.
- In severe anemia: low fetal oxygenation, reduced amniotic fluid volume, and Intrauterine Death (IUD).
- Anemia in infancy (neonates may appear normal at birth but have depleted iron stores).
- _Megaloblastic Anemia Specifics:_ Folic acid deficiency specifically increases the risk of fetal malformations such as neural tube defects, cleft lip, and cleft palate, as well as abruptio placentae and dysmaturity.
