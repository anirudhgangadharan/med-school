### **1. DEFINITION AND NORMAL PARAMETERS**

- **Definition:** Menstruation is the visible manifestation of cyclic physiologic uterine bleeding due to the shedding of the endometrium following the invisible interplay of hormones, mainly through the hypothalamo-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis.
- **Cycle Interval:** Normal menstrual cycle interval is 28 days, ranging from 21 to 35 days.
- **Duration of Flow:** The menstrual flow typically lasts for 4 to 5 days.
- **Menstrual Blood Loss:** Normal blood loss is 20–80 mL, with an average of 35 mL. Nearly 70% of the total menstrual blood loss occurs in the first 2 days.
- **Composition:** The menstrual discharge consists mainly of dark altered blood, mucus, vaginal epithelial cells, fragments of endometrium, prostaglandins, enzymes, and bacteria.

### **2. PREREQUISITES FOR NORMAL MENSTRUATION**

For regular physiological menstruation to occur, several basic factors must be present:

- Normal female chromosomal pattern (46,XX).
- An actively coordinated hypothalamo-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis.
- An endometrium that is responsive to ovarian hormones (estrogen and progesterone).
- Active support from the thyroid and adrenal glands.
- Anatomical presence and patency of the outflow tract (uterovaginal canal).

### **3. OVARIAN CYCLE**

The ovarian cycle refers to the development and maturation of a follicle, ovulation, and the formation and degeneration of the corpus luteum.

- **Recruitment of Follicles:** The initial growth of primordial follicles (up to 2-5 mm) is independent of hormones, but further growth and differentiation are controlled by the follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH). About 20 antral follicles are recruited in each cycle.
- **Selection of a Dominant Follicle:** By day 5–7, one follicle becomes dominant. The dominant follicle is characterized by the highest antral concentration of estrogen, the lowest androgen-to-estrogen ratio, and granulosa cells containing the maximum number of FSH receptors. The remaining recruited follicles undergo atresia.
- **Ovulation:** High sustained estrogen levels trigger the luteinizing hormone (LH) surge. Ovulation occurs approximately 12–16 hours after the LH peak or 32–36 hours after the onset of the LH surge. The LH surge stimulates the completion of the first meiotic division of the oocyte (extruding the first polar body) and helps in the physical act of ovulation.
- **Corpus Luteum Formation and Demise:** After ovulation, the ruptured Graafian follicle develops into the corpus luteum under the influence of LH. The corpus luteum secretes progesterone and estrogen. In an infertile cycle, its lifespan is about 12–14 days, after which it regresses (luteolysis).

### **4. ENDOMETRIAL CYCLE**

The functional zone of the endometrium undergoes cyclic changes under the influence of fluctuating ovarian hormones, divided into four phases.

- **Regenerative Phase:** Regeneration of the endometrium starts before menstruation ceases and is completed 2–3 days after the period ends. New blood vessels, glands, and stromal cells regenerate from the remnants in the basal zone.
- **Proliferative Phase:** Extending from the 5th or 6th day to the 14th day, this phase is driven by a rise in ovarian estrogens. There is rapid proliferation of the glands (which become tubular) and stroma, with the endometrial thickness reaching 10–12 mm by ovulation.
- **Secretory Phase:** Beginning on day 15, this phase is characterized by the combined effects of estrogen and progesterone from the corpus luteum. Progesterone acts on the estrogen-primed endometrium. The glands become tortuous and corkscrew-shaped, and the subnuclear vacuolation of the glandular epithelium appears as the earliest histological evidence of ovulation (36–48 hours post-ovulation). The stroma becomes edematous, and blood vessels undergo marked spiraling.
- **Menstrual Phase:** This phase represents the degeneration and casting off of the prepared endometrium due to the withdrawal of hormonal support following corpus luteum regression.

### **5. HORMONAL CONTROL (HPO AXIS)**

- **Follicular Phase:** At the beginning of the cycle, low estrogen and inhibin levels from the regressing corpus luteum remove negative feedback, allowing FSH levels to rise. FSH recruits follicles and stimulates estrogen production. Rising estrogen levels eventually lower FSH via negative feedback but stimulate an increase in LH receptors on granulosa cells, maturing the follicle.
- **Ovulatory Phase:** When estradiol levels exceed 200 pg/mL and are sustained for 24–48 hours, estrogen exerts a positive feedback effect on the anterior pituitary, triggering the LH surge necessary for ovulation.
- **Luteal Phase:** Progesterone and estrogen secreted by the corpus luteum exert a strong negative feedback effect on LH and FSH, causing their levels to drop to a minimum.
- **Luteal-Follicular Shift:** With the demise of the corpus luteum, the subsequent fall in serum estradiol, progesterone, and inhibin-A removes the suppressive effect on the pituitary. This allows FSH to rise again about 2 days before the onset of menses to recruit follicles for the next cycle.

### **6. MECHANISM OF MENSTRUAL BLEEDING**

- **Vascular Changes:** Endometrial growth ceases 5–6 days prior to menstruation due to dehydration of the glands. The withdrawal of estrogen and progesterone causes intense spasm of the spiral arterioles at the basal part, leading to stasis and tissue anoxemia.
- **Enzymatic Breakdown:** There is an infiltration of leukocytes and monocytes in the stroma. There is intense breakdown of the endometrial tissues by proteolytic enzymes belonging to the matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) family, which are stimulated by the inflammatory process. This causes enzymatic autodigestion of the functional zone.
- **Shedding and Liquefaction:** Bleeding occurs from the broken arteries, veins, capillaries, and stromal hematomas. The blood, along with the superficial functional layer, is shed into the uterine cavity. The shed blood initially coagulates but soon liquefies through the action of plasmin.
- **Hemostasis:** Menstrual flow eventually stops due to a combined effect of prolonged vasoconstriction, myometrial contraction, and local aggregation of platelets with fibrin deposition. Endothelin and platelet-activating factor within the endometrium act as potent vasoconstrictors to aid this process. Resumption of estrogen secretion promotes clot formation over the decapitated vascular stumps and initiates endometrial repair.

### **7. ROLE OF PROSTAGLANDINS**

Prostaglandins are synthesized in the endometrium from arachidonic acid via the cyclooxygenase enzyme and play a vital role in menstruation.

- **PGF2α:** Causes myometrial contraction and intense vasoconstriction. It plays a dominant role in normal cycles, reducing bleeding.
- **PGE2:** Produces myometrial contraction but causes vasodilatation.
- **PGI2 (Prostacyclin):** Causes myometrial relaxation and vasodilatation, and also inhibits platelet activity.
- _Clinical Correlation:_ Menstrual pain and blood flow volume are related to the relative proportions of these different prostaglandins in the endometrium.
