### **1. Definition and Incidence**

- **Definition:** Recurrent miscarriage is defined by the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM-2013) as the sequence of two or more spontaneous abortions (documented by sonography or histopathology) before 20 weeks of gestation. The earlier definition by RCOG and ESHRE states it as prior three or more consecutive miscarriages before 24 weeks of gestation.
- **Incidence:** It affects approximately 1% of all women of reproductive age. The risk of a subsequent miscarriage increases with each successive abortion, reaching over 30% after three consecutive losses.

### **2. Etiology**

The causes of recurrent miscarriage are complex, often obscure, and vary depending on the trimester of the loss.

**First Trimester Causes:**

- **Genetic Factors (3-8%):** Parental chromosomal abnormalities are a proven cause of recurrent early pregnancy loss. The most common abnormality is a balanced translocation (risk of miscarriage >25%), followed by chromosome insertions, deletions, and inversions. The most common aneuploidies found in abortuses are trisomies, though trisomic miscarriage does not increase the risk for the next pregnancy.
- **Endocrine and Metabolic Factors:**
    - Poorly controlled diabetes mellitus increases early pregnancy failure.
    - Thyroid abnormalities: Untreated hypothyroidism and the presence of thyroid autoantibodies are associated with increased risk; women should be euthyroid before pregnancy.
    - Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS): Factors responsible include elevated LH levels, insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, hyperandrogenemia, and hyperhomocysteinemia.
    - Luteal Phase Defect (LPD): Deficient progesterone production fails to support early pregnancy.
    - Hyperprolactinemia: Competes with the HPO axis resulting in insufficient folliculogenesis, oocyte maturation, and LPD.
- **Immunological Factors (10-15%):**
    - _Autoimmunity:_ The presence of autoantibodies (antinuclear, anti-DNA, and antiphospholipid antibodies) can cause rejection of early pregnancy. Antiphospholipid Antibody Syndrome (APAS) causes miscarriage by inhibiting trophoblast proliferation, releasing inflammatory cytokines, activating the complement pathway, and causing thrombosis of the spiral arteries and intervillous spaces.
    - _Cytokine Response:_ Successful pregnancy requires an anti-inflammatory T-helper 2 (Th2) response. Women with recurrent miscarriage often demonstrate a pro-inflammatory T-helper 1 (Th1) dominant response.
- **Thrombophilias:** Inherited thrombophilias cause intravascular and placental intervillous thrombosis. The most common cause is Protein C resistance (factor V Leiden mutation). Others include deficiencies of proteins C, S, and antithrombin III, prothrombin gene mutation, and hyperhomocysteinemia.
- **Infections:** Bacterial vaginosis is a known risk factor, though no specific infection has been definitively linked to _recurrent_ miscarriage.
- **Unexplained:** In 40-60% of cases, the exact cause remains unknown despite thorough investigation.

**Second Trimester Causes:**

- **Anatomic Abnormalities (10-15%):** These can be congenital or acquired.
    - _Congenital:_ Defects in Müllerian duct fusion such as a bicornuate, septate, double, or unicornuate uterus. Implantation on a poorly vascularized septum leads to defective placentation and subsequent loss.
    - _Acquired:_ Intrauterine adhesions (synechiae/Asherman's syndrome), uterine fibroids (especially submucous), and cervical incompetence.
- **Chronic Maternal Illness:** Uncontrolled diabetes, hemoglobinopathies, chronic renal disease, and Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE).

### **3. Diagnostic Investigations**

A meticulous history and evaluation are mandatory to pinpoint the etiology.

- **History & Physical Examination:** Detailed recording of previous abortion processes, karyotyping/histology of previous abortuses, and screening for chronic maternal illness. For suspected cervical incompetence, a history of recurrent painless mid-trimester cervical dilatation and expulsion of the conceptus is characteristic.
- **Laboratory Tests:**
    - Blood glucose (fasting and postprandial), VDRL, thyroid function tests, ABO and Rh grouping (for both partners), and Toxoplasma antibodies (IgG/IgM).
    - Serum LH on day 2 or 3 of the menstrual cycle.
    - Autoimmune & Thrombophilia screening: Lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin antibodies, and genetic tests for inherited thrombophilias.
- **Genetic Testing:** Parental karyotyping is indicated for both the husband and wife.
- **Imaging and Endoscopy:**
    - Ultrasonography to detect congenital uterine malformations, polycystic ovaries, and fibroids.
    - Hysterosalpingography (HSG) in the secretory phase to evaluate the uterine cavity for synechiae, malformations, and cervical incompetence.
    - Hysteroscopy, laparoscopy, or MRI for definitive diagnosis of anatomical defects.
- **Cervical Evaluation:** In the interconceptional period, the easy passage of a number 6-8 Hegar dilator beyond the internal os without resistance indicates cervical incompetence. Endocervical swabs should be taken for Chlamydia, Mycoplasma, and bacterial vaginosis.

### **4. Principles of Management**

**A. Interconceptional Period**

- **Counseling & Reassurance:** Alleviating anxiety is crucial. The couple should be assured that even after three consecutive miscarriages, the chance of a successful pregnancy remains as high as 70%.
- **Surgical Correction:** Hysteroscopic resection of uterine septa, synechiae, and submucous myomas significantly improves outcomes. A metroplasty (uterine unification operation) is indicated for a bicornuate uterus.
- **Genetic Counseling:** If balanced translocations or inversions are found, couples are counseled for Preimplantation Genetic Diagnosis (PGD) or the use of donor gametes.
- **Medical Treatment:** Genital tract infections should be treated appropriately; empirical treatment with doxycycline or erythromycin is cost-effective. Medical complications like SLE or cyanotic heart disease should be optimally treated before allowing conception.

**B. Management During Pregnancy**

- **Tender Loving Care (TLC):** Psychological support, reassurance, and rest reduce stress and improve uterine blood flow. In unexplained recurrent miscarriage, TLC alone can improve pregnancy outcomes by up to 70%.
- **Rest and Activity:** The patient should take adequate rest and avoid strenuous activities, traveling, and intercourse.
- **Progesterone Therapy:** To counteract Luteal Phase Defect (LPD) and leverage its immunomodulatory role (shifting the pro-inflammatory Th1 response to a protective Th2 response), natural micronized progesterone (100 mg daily as a vaginal suppository) is administered starting 2 days after ovulation and continued until 10-12 weeks of gestation.
- **Anticoagulation Therapy:** For women with Antiphospholipid Syndrome (APAS) or thrombophilias, a combination of low-dose aspirin (75-150 mg/day) and Low Molecular Weight Heparin (enoxaparin) or unfractionated heparin (5,000 IU SC twice daily) is highly effective and continued up to 34 weeks.
- **Folic Acid:** Prepregnancy folic acid therapy (5 mg daily) should be initiated 1 month prior to conception and continued through the first trimester, especially for women with hyperhomocysteinemia or a history of neural tube defects.

**C. Management of Cervical Incompetence**

- **Cervical Cerclage:** The incompetent cervix is reinforced surgically using a nonabsorbable tape (Shirodkar or McDonald operation).
- **Timing:**
    - _History-indicated cerclage:_ Placed electively between 12-14 weeks for women with three or more mid-trimester losses.
    - _Ultrasound-indicated cerclage:_ Placed between 16-24 weeks if serial sonography reveals a short cervix (<25 mm).
- The stitch is typically removed after 36 completed weeks of gestation, or earlier if labor begins.

### **5. Prognosis**

The overall risk of recurrent miscarriage stands at approximately 25-30% irrespective of the number of previous losses. However, the prognosis is generally favorable; even without specific therapy, spontaneous success is possible, and with effective, targeted therapy, the chance of a successful pregnancy is between 70% and 80%.
