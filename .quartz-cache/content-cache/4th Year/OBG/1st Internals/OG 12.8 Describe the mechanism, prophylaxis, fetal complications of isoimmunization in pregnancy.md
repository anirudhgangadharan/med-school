### **1. Mechanism of Isoimmunization**

**Pathogenesis and Fetomaternal Hemorrhage**

- Isoimmunization (alloimmunization) occurs when a Rh-negative mother carries a Rh-positive fetus,.
- Under normal circumstances, fetal red blood cells (RBCs) rarely enter the maternal circulation. Sensitization happens when fetomaternal hemorrhage occurs, which can be during early pregnancy events (miscarriage, MTP, ectopic pregnancy, hydatidiform mole), invasive procedures (amniocentesis, CVS, cordocentesis), late pregnancy complications (placenta previa, placental abruption, external cephalic version), or most commonly, during the third stage of labor and delivery.
- A critical sensitizing volume of at least 0.1 mL of fetal blood entering the maternal circulation is required to initiate the immune response,.

**Mechanism of Antibody Formation**

- Once Rh-positive fetal RBCs enter the maternal blood, they are processed and broken down by the maternal reticuloendothelial system, liberating the Rh antigen,.
- Antibody production is a slow process, meaning that primary immunization usually takes 10 to 16 weeks to yield detectable antibodies, rendering the first pregnancy largely unaffected unless prior sensitization occurred,.
- **Types of Antibodies Formed:**
    - **IgM:** Appears first. Due to its large molecular size, it cannot cross the placental barrier and is harmless to the fetus.
    - **IgG (Blocking Antibody):** Appears later. Its small molecular size allows it to readily cross the placental barrier and cause damage to fetal RBCs.

### **2. Prophylaxis of Rh Alloimmunization**

**Principles of Prevention** The prevention of isoimmunization rests on three pillars: preventing active immunization, minimizing fetomaternal bleed, and avoiding mismatched blood transfusions.

**Anti-D Immunoglobulin Administration Protocol** Active immunization is prevented by administering anti-D immunoglobulin (IgG) intramuscularly to an unimmunized Rh-negative mother. This antibody-mediated immune suppression (AMIS) works by blocking the Rh-antigen, facilitating the rapid clearance of coated fetal RBCs by the spleen or lymph nodes, and causing central inhibition of B cell antibody production,.

- **Postpartum Prophylaxis:** A standard dose of 300 µg (1500 IU) IM is administered within 72 hours of delivering a Rh-positive, direct Coombs' test negative baby,. This dose neutralizes up to 30 mL of fetal whole blood (15 mL of fetal RBCs).
- **Early Pregnancy Prophylaxis:** 50 µg IM is administered within 72 hours following spontaneous or induced abortion, ectopic pregnancy, or chorionic villus sampling in the first trimester. For gestations beyond 12 weeks, the full 300 µg dose is given.
- **Routine Antenatal Anti-D Prophylaxis (RAADP):** Administering a 300 µg dose at 28-30 weeks of gestation reduces the third-trimester alloimmunization rate from 2% down to 0.1%,.
- **Kleihauer-Betke Test:** This acid elution test is used to calculate the exact volume of fetomaternal bleed. If the hemorrhage exceeds 30 mL, an additional dose of anti-D (10 µg for every 1 mL of fetal whole blood) is required,,.

**Prevention of Fetomaternal Bleed**

- During cesarean delivery, prevent blood spillage into the peritoneal cavity and avoid routine manual removal of the placenta.
- Avoid using prophylactic ergometrine during the delivery of the anterior shoulder, as it may force more fetoplacental bleed.
- Amniocentesis must always be performed under direct ultrasound guidance.
- Avoid forceful attempts at external cephalic version under anesthesia.

**Avoidance of Mismatched Transfusion**

- An Rh-positive blood transfusion should never be given to an Rh-negative female from birth to menopause, as it will cause permanent sensitization.

### **3. Fetal Complications (Hemolytic Disease of the Fetus and Newborn)**

Once maternal IgG antibodies cross the placenta, they attach to the Rh antigens on fetal erythrocytes, leading to their rapid destruction by the fetal reticuloendothelial system,. The degree of fetal affection varies based on the severity of the hemolysis.

**1. Congenital Anemia of the Newborn**

- This is the mildest form of Hemolytic Disease of the Fetus and Newborn (HDFN), characterized by slow ongoing hemolysis.
- Anemia develops slowly within the first few weeks of life, and jaundice is usually not evident.
- The liver and spleen become enlarged as they act as sites for compensatory extramedullary erythropoiesis.

**2. Icterus Gravis Neonatorum**

- While in utero, the unconjugated bilirubin liberated from red cell destruction is cleared across the placenta into the maternal circulation. Therefore, the baby is typically born without jaundice.
- Post-delivery, the neonatal liver is too immature to adequately conjugate the massive bilirubin load,.
- Severe jaundice develops rapidly, typically within 24 hours of birth,.
- If untreated, the high levels of unconjugated bilirubin cross the blood-brain barrier, resulting in a fatal or severely debilitating neurological complication known as **kernicterus**.

**3. Hydrops Fetalis**

- This is the most severe and life-threatening form of HDFN.
- Excessive destruction of fetal RBCs leads to profound anemia, severe tissue anoxemia, and metabolic acidosis, causing adverse effects on the fetal heart and brain.
- The severe anoxia causes significant hepatic damage, which impairs protein synthesis,.
- The resulting hypoproteinemia decreases oncotic pressure, leading to massive generalized subcutaneous edema, ascites, and hydrothorax.
- The placenta undergoes marked hyperplasia in a compensatory attempt to increase oxygen transfer.
- Fetal death inevitably occurs sooner or later due to profound cardiac failure.
