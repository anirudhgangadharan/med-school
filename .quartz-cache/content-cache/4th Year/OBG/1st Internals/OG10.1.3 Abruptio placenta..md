### **1. Definition and Incidence**

- **Definition:** Abruptio placentae (accidental hemorrhage or premature separation of placenta) is one form of antepartum hemorrhage where bleeding occurs due to premature separation of a normally situated placenta. It is defined as bleeding from or into the genital tract after the 20th week of pregnancy but before the birth of the baby.
- **Incidence:** The overall incidence is about 1 in 100 deliveries. It contributes to nearly 30% of all antepartum hemorrhage cases, with the majority (60%) occurring in the third trimester of pregnancy.

### **2. Varieties (Types)**

- **Revealed:** Following separation, blood insinuates downwards between the membranes and decidua, escaping out of the cervical canal to be visible externally. This is the most common type.
- **Concealed:** The blood collects behind the separated placenta or between the membranes and decidua. The presenting part presses on the lower segment, preventing the blood from coming out. This type is rare (10-20%).
- **Mixed:** Some part of the blood collects inside (concealed) and a part is expelled out (revealed). This is quite common.

### **3. Etiology and Risk Factors**

The exact etiology remains obscure in the majority of cases. Known risk factors include:

- **Hypertensive disorders in pregnancy:** Pre-eclampsia, gestational hypertension, and essential hypertension are associated in 10-50% of cases. Spasm of the decidual spiral artery leads to anoxic endothelial damage, rupture, and extravasation of blood (retroplacental hematoma).
- **Prior abruption:** Risk of recurrence varies from 5-17%.
- **General factors:** High parity ($\geq$ 5 pregnancies), advancing maternal age, poor socioeconomic condition, poor nutrition, and smoking.
- **Trauma:** External cephalic version (especially under anesthesia), road traffic accidents, blunt abdominal trauma, and needle puncture at amniocentesis.
- **Sudden uterine decompression:** Following delivery of the first twin, sudden escape of liquor amnii in polyhydramnios, and premature rupture of membranes (PROM).
- **Uterine and Placental factors:** Short umbilical cord, circumvallate placenta, sick (ischemic) placenta, placenta implanted over a septum or submucous fibroid, and uterine torsion.
- **Other maternal conditions:** Supine hypotension syndrome, folic acid deficiency, cocaine abuse, inherited or acquired thrombophilias, and hyperhomocysteinemia.

### **4. Pathogenesis and Couvelaire Uterus**

- **Pathogenesis:** Premature placental separation is initiated by hemorrhage into the decidua basalis. The decidual hematoma leads to degeneration, infarction, and necrosis of the decidua basalis and adjacent placenta. When a major spiral artery ruptures, a large hematoma forms, triggering the coagulation cascade and inflammatory cytokines.
- **Couvelaire Uterus (Uteroplacental Apoplexy):** A pathological entity where there is massive intravasation of blood into the uterine musculature up to the serous coat. The uterus appears dark port wine in color. It is diagnosed only on laparotomy.

### **5. Clinical Classification (Grading)**

Depending upon the degree of separation and clinical effects, abruptio placentae is graded as:

- **Grade 0:** Clinical features absent; diagnosis made retrospectively after inspecting the placenta post-delivery.
- **Grade 1 (40%):** Slight vaginal bleeding, irritable uterus with minimal or absent tenderness, maintained maternal BP and fibrinogen, and good fetal heart sound (FHS).
- **Grade 2 (45%):** Mild to moderate vaginal bleeding, uterine tenderness always present, maternal tachycardia with maintained BP, decreased fibrinogen, and fetal distress or death.
- **Grade 3 (15%):** Moderate to severe or concealed bleeding, marked uterine tenderness, pronounced maternal shock, frequent coagulation defects or anuria, and fetal death is the rule.

### **6. Clinical Features**

Clinical presentation depends heavily on whether the abruption is revealed or concealed.

- **Symptoms:** Revealed abruption presents with abdominal discomfort followed by slight vaginal bleeding. Mixed/concealed abruption presents with acute, intense abdominal pain followed by slight bleeding; pain becomes continuous.
- **Signs:**
    - _General Condition:_ In revealed types, anemia/shock is proportionate to visible blood loss. In concealed types, shock is out of proportion to visible blood loss. Features of pre-eclampsia may be present in one-third of cases.
    - _Uterine Palpation:_ In revealed types, the uterus feels normal with localized tenderness. In concealed types, the uterus is tense, tender, rigid, and woody hard, and may be disproportionately enlarged.
    - _Fetal Status:_ Fetal parts are easily felt and FHS is usually present in revealed types. In concealed types, fetal parts are difficult to make out, and FHS is irregular (hypoxia) or absent (fetal death).

### **7. Investigations and Diagnosis**

- **Blood Tests:** Hemoglobin and hematocrit (markedly low in concealed type), ABO and Rh grouping.
- **Coagulation Profile:** Fibrinogen level, prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, FDP, D-dimer, and platelet count. Overt hypofibrinogenemia (<150 mg/dL) is noted in consumptive coagulopathy.
- **Urine Output and Analysis:** Monitored for oliguria and tested for protein.
- **Ultrasonography (USG):** Differentiates from placenta previa. Can localize hemorrhage as retroplacental (50% fetal mortality), subchorionic (<10% fetal mortality), or pre-placental.

### **8. Complications**

- **Maternal Complications:**
    - Hemorrhage and pronounced shock.
    - Blood coagulation disorders (consumptive coagulopathy/DIC).
    - Acute kidney injury (oliguria, acute tubular necrosis, cortical necrosis) due to hypovolemia and renal ischemia.
    - Postpartum hemorrhage (due to atony).
    - Sheehan's syndrome (ischemic pituitary necrosis causing failure of lactation later).
    - Maternal mortality varies from 2-8%.
- **Fetal Complications:**
    - Prematurity and anoxia.
    - Fetal hypoxia leading to periventricular leukomalacia.
    - Intrauterine fetal death is high (50-100% in concealed types).

### **9. Management**

**A. Preventive Measures**

- Early detection and effective therapy of pre-eclampsia and hypertensive disorders.
- Avoidance of trauma (e.g., forceful external cephalic version) and performing amniocentesis strictly under USG guidance.
- Avoidance of sudden uterine decompression (e.g., amniocentesis is preferred over artificial rupture of membranes in acute hydramnios).
- Advising the left lateral position in late pregnancy to avoid supine hypotension.

**B. Emergency Resuscitation (Hospital Treatment)**

- Initial assessment includes evaluating blood loss, fetal maturity, labor status, and the grade of abruption.
- Ringer's solution drip is started via a wide-bore cannula.
- Blood transfusion is arranged; at least 1 L of blood should be transfused for concealed hemorrhage.
- Central Venous Pressure (CVP) monitoring is the best guide, maintained at 10 cm of water.
- Anti-D immunoglobulin (300 $\mu$g) is given to all Rh-negative women to combat fetomaternal hemorrhage.

**C. Definitive Obstetric Management**

- **Expectant Management:** This is an exception and not the rule. It is considered only for chronic/small abruptions (Grade 1) where bleeding has stopped, the fetus is reactive (<34 weeks), and close electronic monitoring is available. Betamethasone is given to accelerate fetal lung maturity, and tocolysis (MgSO4) may reduce further separation.
- **Patient in Labor:** Amniotomy (Artificial Rupture of Membranes - ARM) with or without oxytocin is done to expedite delivery, initiate contractions, and reduce the entry of thromboplastin into maternal circulation.
- **Patient not in Labor (Active Interference):**
    - _Vaginal Delivery (Induction):_ Initiated by low rupture of membranes and oxytocin augmentation if the abruption is limited, FHR is reassuring, or if the fetus is dead.
    - _Cesarean Section:_ Indicated for severe abruption with a live and viable fetus, unfavorable cervix preventing amniotomy, appearance of adverse features (falling fibrinogen, oliguria), or failure of amniotomy to control bleeding and progress labor. Regional anesthesia is generally avoided to prevent profound hypotension.
- **Postpartum Care:** Injection oxytocin 10 IU IV or methergine 0.2 mg is given at the delivery of the baby to prevent postpartum hemorrhage.
