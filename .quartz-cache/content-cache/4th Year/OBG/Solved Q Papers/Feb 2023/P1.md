1. A 28 yrs old G2P1L1 with 34 weeks of gestation came to the casualty with the complaint of bleeding per Vaginum. a) What are the probable causes of bleeding? b) How will you differentiate between abruptio placenta and placenta praevia? c) How will you manage this patient if she is a case of abruptio placenta?
**a) Probable Causes of Bleeding (Antepartum Hemorrhage)**

Bleeding from the genital tract after 20-28 weeks of gestation but before the birth of the baby is termed Antepartum Hemorrhage (APH). The probable causes of APH are broadly classified into:

- **Placental Bleeding (70%):**
    - **Placenta Previa (35%):** Implantation of the placenta partially or completely over the lower uterine segment,.
    - **Abruptio Placentae (35%):** Premature separation of a normally situated placenta.
- **Unexplained or Indeterminate Bleeding (25%):**
    - Vasa previa (umbilical vessels run over the cervical os),.
    - Bleeding from a succenturiate lobe.
    - Marginal sinus hemorrhage.
    - Circumvallate placenta.
- **Extra-placental Causes / Local Lesions (5%):**
    - Cervical polyp.
    - Carcinoma cervix.
    - Varicose veins.
    - Local trauma.
    - Cervical ectopy.

---

**b) Differentiation between Abruptio Placenta and Placenta Previa**

|**Parameters**|**Placenta Previa**|**Abruptio Placentae**|
|:--|:--|:--|
|**Nature of bleeding**|Painless, apparently causeless, and recurrent.|Painful, often attributed to pre-eclampsia or trauma, and continuous.|
|**Visibility of bleeding**|Bleeding is always revealed.|Revealed, concealed, or usually mixed.|
|**Character of blood**|Bright red.|Dark colored.|
|**General condition & Anemia**|Proportionate to the visible blood loss.|Out of proportion to the visible blood loss (especially in concealed or mixed variety).|
|**Pre-eclampsia features**|Not relevant.|Present in about one-third of cases.|
|**Height of uterus**|Proportionate height to gestational age.|May be disproportionately enlarged in the concealed type.|
|**Feel of uterus**|Soft and relaxed.|Tense, tender, and rigid (woody hard).|
|**Malpresentation**|Malpresentation is common. The fetal head is usually high floating.|Unrelated; the fetal head may be engaged.|
|**Fetal Heart Sound (FHS)**|Usually present.|Usually absent, especially in the concealed type (indicating fetal hypoxia/death).|
|**Placentography (USG / MRI)**|Placenta is located in the lower uterine segment.|Placenta is located in the upper uterine segment.|
|**Vaginal examination**|Placenta is felt on the lower segment (examination contraindicated unless in OT).|Placenta is not felt on the lower segment; blood clots may be present.|

---

**c) Management of Abruptio Placenta at 34 Weeks**

The primary principle in the management of abruptio placentae at 34 weeks of gestation involves prompt maternal resuscitation, fetal monitoring, and expeditious delivery. Expectant management is an exception and not the rule for this condition,.

**1. Initial Assessment and Emergency Resuscitation**

- **Hospitalization:** Admit immediately to an equipped maternity unit with blood bank and NICU facilities.
- **Assessment:** Rapidly evaluate the amount of blood loss, maternal vitals, grade of abruption (Grade 0 to 3), and fetal status via clinical examination and USG,,,.
- **Emergency Investigations:** Send blood for Hb%, hematocrit, ABO and Rh grouping, coagulation profile (fibrinogen level, FDP, prothrombin time, APTT, platelets), and urine for protein,.
- **IV Access & Fluids:** Start a wide-bore IV cannula and rapidly infuse Ringer’s solution.
- **Blood Transfusion:** Arrange cross-matched whole blood and keep it ready for transfusion to combat hypovolemic shock,.

**2. Maternal and Fetal Monitoring**

- **Maternal:** Monitor pulse, BP, and strictly record hourly urine output via a Foley catheter (maintain output $\ge$ 30 mL/h to prevent acute renal failure). Central Venous Pressure (CVP) monitoring may be established to guide fluid replacement,.
- **Fetal:** Initiate continuous electronic fetal monitoring (CTG) to assess for fetal hypoxia or nonreassuring status.

**3. Definitive Treatment (Delivery)** Given the gestational age of 34 weeks, delivery is typically the definitive treatment regardless of whether the patient is in labor or not,.

- **If the patient is in labor:**
    - Accelerate labor by performing an amniotomy (low rupture of the membranes). Amniotomy initiates contraction, speeds delivery, compresses spiral arteries, and reduces thromboplastin entry into maternal circulation,.
    - Augment with an oxytocin infusion if uterine contractions are inadequate.
    - Vaginal delivery is preferred if the abruption is limited, fetal heart tracing is reassuring, or if the fetus is dead.
- **If the patient is not in labor:**
    - _Induction of Labor:_ Accomplished by amniotomy followed by oxytocin infusion. Labor generally starts and completes quickly (4-6 hours).
    - _Cesarean Section:_ Strongly indicated for severe abruption with a live, viable fetus; appearance of adverse maternal/fetal features (fetal distress, severe oliguria, falling fibrinogen levels); unfavorable cervix precluding amniotomy; or failure of amniotomy and oxytocin to arrest bleeding and progress labor,. (Note: Regional anesthesia is avoided in cases of significant hemorrhage to prevent profound hypotension).

**4. Management of Third Stage and Complications**

- **PPH Prophylaxis:** Inject oxytocin 10 IU IV (slowly) or IM, or methergine 0.2 mg IV immediately with the delivery of the baby to improve uterine tone and minimize postpartum hemorrhage.
- **Coagulopathy (DIC):** If consumption coagulopathy occurs, treat by replenishing fibrinogen levels (>150 mg/dL) using fresh blood, Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP), or platelets.
- **Rh-Isoimmunization Prevention:** Administer 300 µg of anti-D immunoglobulin if the mother is Rh-negative to combat fetomaternal hemorrhage.


2. Define eclampsia. What are the differential diagnoses? Discuss the investigations and management of eclampsia in the following headings: general management, anticonvulsant therapy, antihypertensive therapy, obstetric management and management of complications.

**DEFINITION OF ECLAMPSIA** Pre-eclampsia when complicated with grand mal seizures (generalized tonic-clonic convulsions) and/or coma is called eclampsia. It can occur in patients with pre-eclampsia or in patients with pre-eclampsia superimposed on essential hypertension or chronic nephritis.

**DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES** The diseases associated with convulsions and/or coma during pregnancy or puerperium must be differentiated from eclampsia. These include:

- Epilepsy (seizure disorders).
- Hypoglycemia.
- Encephalitis and Meningitis.
- Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome (PRES).
- Cerebral venous thrombosis.
- Hypertensive encephalopathy.
- Cerebral malaria (in tropics).
- Intracranial tumors.
- Hypoxic-Ischemic Encephalopathy (HIE).

**INVESTIGATIONS**

- **Urine Analysis:** For detection and quantification of proteinuria.
- **Blood Tests:** Complete Blood Count (CBC), platelet count, liver enzymes (AST, ALT, LDH), serum creatinine, and uric acid.
- **Coagulation Profile:** Assessed especially if the platelet count is <100,000/mm³ (PT, APTT, fibrinogen, FDPs).
- **Ophthalmoscopy:** To evaluate for retinal edema, hemorrhage, or papilledema.
- **Imaging:** Neuroradiologic imaging (CT/MRI) is strongly recommended in the postpartum period for cases with neurologic symptoms, focal deficits, atypical presentation, or prolonged coma. It helps detect white matter edema (occipital lobe), cerebral hemorrhage, and infarctions.
- **Fetal Monitoring:** Continuous Electronic Fetal Monitoring (EFM) and ultrasonography/Doppler study (UA, MCA) to assess fetal wellbeing.

**MANAGEMENT OF ECLAMPSIA**

**1. General Management (Medical and Nursing Care)**

- **First Aid & Transport:** If outside the hospital, administer prophylactic Magnesium Sulfate (4g IV + 10g IM) and Labetalol 20mg IV, then urgently transport the patient to a tertiary care center with skilled personnel in a lateral recumbent position.
- **Airway and Breathing:** Keep the patient in an intensive care facility with a railed cot. Insert a mouth gag/tongue blade between the teeth to prevent tongue bite during the premonitory stage. Place the patient in the left lateral decubitus position to avoid aspiration. Clear the airway using a mucus sucker and administer oxygen (8-10 L/min) via face mask until cyanosis disappears.
- **Fluid Balance:** Start an IV line with crystalloid (Ringer's solution). Restrict total fluids to the previous 24 hours' urinary output plus 1000 mL (insensible loss), generally not exceeding 2 liters in 24 hours (rate of 1 mL/kg/h) to prevent circulatory overload and pulmonary edema.
- **Monitoring:** Insert a Foley catheter with a urometer to continuously monitor urine output. Record pulse, respiratory rate, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation (SpO2 >95%) every 15-30 minutes.
- **Antibiotics:** Administer broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g., ceftriaxone 1 g IV twice daily) to prevent infection.

**2. Anticonvulsant Therapy** The aim is to control fits and prevent their recurrence.

- **Drug of Choice:** Magnesium sulfate (MgSO4) is the preferred drug as it acts as a membrane stabilizer, neuroprotector, and reduces motor endplate sensitivity without causing maternal or infant CNS depression.
- **Dosage:** Loading dose of 4 g IV (20% solution) given slowly over 3-5 minutes, followed by an infusion of 1-2 g/hr, or 10 g IM (5 g in each buttock).
- **Duration:** Therapy is continued for 24 hours after the last seizure or delivery, whichever is later.
- **Recurrence:** For recurrent fits, a further 2 g IV bolus of MgSO4 is given over 5 minutes. If seizures persist despite MgSO4, Midazolam 1-2 mg IV may be given and repeated in 5-10 minutes.
- **Monitoring Toxicity:** Before repeat doses, ensure knee jerks are present, urine output is >30 mL/hour, and respiratory rate is >12/min. Therapeutic level is 4-7 mEq/L.
- **Antidote:** Calcium gluconate 10% (1 g IV or 10 mL) is the antidote for cardiac/respiratory toxicity due to hypermagnesemia.

**3. Antihypertensive Therapy** Indicated when blood pressure remains >160/110 mm Hg despite anticonvulsants, aiming for a target SBP of 140-160 mm Hg and DBP of 90-100 mm Hg to prevent cerebrovascular accidents.

- **First-line Drugs:**
    - _Labetalol:_ 10-20 mg IV over 2 min, repeated every 10-30 minutes (maximum 300 mg).
    - _Hydralazine:_ 5-10 mg IV every 20-30 minutes.
    - _Nifedipine:_ 10-20 mg PO, repeated in 30 minutes.

**4. Obstetric Management** Delivery is the only definitive treatment for eclampsia. Women with eclampsia should be delivered within 6-8 hours even if the fits are not completely controlled.

- **Fits Controlled & Patient Stable:**
    - _Pregnancy <34 weeks:_ Administer antenatal corticosteroids (betamethasone/dexamethasone) for fetal lung maturity and proceed with delivery.
    - _Term/Favorable Cervix:_ Surgical induction by Artificial Rupture of Membranes (ARM) followed by oxytocin infusion. If the cervix is unfavorable, PGE2 gel is used for ripening before ARM.
- **Fits Not Controlled / Nonreassuring Fetal Status:** Immediate termination of pregnancy by Cesarean Section is indicated.
- **During Labor & Delivery:** Monitor continuously. Epidural anesthesia can be safely used. Shorten the second stage with prophylactic forceps or ventouse.
- **Third Stage Management:** Avoid ergometrine/methergine due to its vasoconstrictive properties (risk of stroke/BP spike). Instead, use Oxytocin 10 IU IM or IV slowly to prevent postpartum hemorrhage.

**5. Management of Complications** Patients with complications require intensive care unit (ICU) multidisciplinary management.

- **Pulmonary Edema / ARDS:** Administer Furosemide (Lasix) 40 mg IV, followed by 20 g mannitol IV to reduce fluid overload. Ensure strict oxygenation (pulse oximeter monitoring) and airway suctioning.
- **Cardiac Failure:** Oxygen inhalation, parenteral Lasix, and digitalis are used.
- **Anuria / Oliguria:** Fluid challenges based on Central Venous Pressure (CVP). If CVP is >8 mm Hg and oliguria persists, Dopamine infusion (1 mg/kg) may be given.
- **Hyperpyrexia:** Managed with cold sponging and antipyretics (acetaminophen).
- **Status Eclampticus:** Managed by an expert anesthetist with Thiopentone sodium 0.5 g IV (dissolved in 20 mL of 5% dextrose) given slowly, endotracheal intubation, muscle relaxants, assisted ventilation, and immediate cesarean delivery.



2. What are the cardiovascular changes during pregnancy?
**Anatomical Changes and Clinical Findings**

- **Heart Displacement:** Due to the elevation of the diaphragm by the enlarged uterus, the heart is pushed upward and outward with a slight rotation to the left.
- **Apex Beat:** The apex beat is shifted to the 4th intercostal space, about 2.5 cm outside the midclavicular line.
- **Pulse Rate:** The pulse rate is slightly raised (+17%), often accompanied by extrasystoles.
- **Heart Murmurs:** A systolic murmur may be audible in the apical or pulmonary area due to decreased blood viscosity and torsion of the great vessels. A continuous hissing murmur (mammary souffle) may be heard over the tricuspid area due to increased blood flow through the internal mammary vessels.
- **Heart Sounds:** There is a splitting of the first heart sound and a loud third heart sound ($S_3$) due to rapid diastolic filling, while the second heart sound remains unchanged. A fourth heart sound ($S_4$) may rarely be auscultated.

**ECG and Echocardiographic Changes**

- **Echocardiography:** Doppler echocardiography demonstrates an increase in left ventricular end-diastolic diameters, as well as increased left and right atrial diameters.
- **ECG:** Reveals a left axis deviation and potentially a benign pericardial effusion. Specific changes include Q waves in leads II, III, and avF, along with flat or inverted T-waves in leads III and V1-V3.

**Cardiac Output (CO)**

- **Increase in CO:** Cardiac output begins to increase from the 5th week of pregnancy, reaching a peak of 40-50% above the nonpregnant level (from 4.5 L/min to 6.26 L/min) around 30-34 weeks, and remains static until term when measured in the lateral recumbent position.
- **Positional Variation:** CO is lowest in the sitting or supine position and highest in the right or left lateral, or knee-chest positions.
- **During Labor and Puerperium:** CO increases further during labor (+50%) and immediately following delivery (+70%) due to autotransfusion (squeezing of blood from the uterus into the maternal circulation). It returns to prelabor values within 1 hour after delivery and to prepregnant levels by 4 weeks.
- **Mechanism:** The increase in CO is the product of an increased stroke volume (+27%, from 65 mL to 75 mL) and an increased heart rate (+17%, an increase of about 15 beats per minute). This helps meet the additional oxygen demand, which increases by 20% in pregnancy and 50% in labor.

**Blood Pressure (BP) and Systemic Vascular Resistance (SVR)**

- **Systemic Vascular Resistance (SVR):** SVR decreases significantly (-21%) due to the smooth muscle-relaxing effects of progesterone, nitric oxide, prostaglandins, and Atrial Natriuretic Peptide (ANP).
- **Blood Pressure:** Despite the large increase in cardiac output, maternal BP decreases because of the marked fall in SVR. There is a characteristic mid-pregnancy drop in diastolic BP and Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP) by 5-10 mm Hg, followed by a gradual rise back toward nonpregnant levels until term.

**Venous Pressure**

- **Antecubital Venous Pressure:** Remains unaffected.
- **Femoral Venous Pressure:** Markedly raised (+100%) in the later months, increasing from 8-10 cm to 25 cm of water in the supine position, and up to 80-100 cm of water in the standing position. This is caused by the gravid uterus compressing the inferior vena cava and common iliac veins, particularly on the right side due to the dextrorotation of the uterus. This stagnation causes physiological edema of the legs, varicose veins, hemorrhoids, and deep vein thrombosis.

**Supine Hypotension Syndrome (Postural Hypotension)**

- In late pregnancy, the gravid uterus exerts a compression effect on the inferior vena cava when the patient is in the supine position.
- In about 10% of women, the collateral circulation (paravertebral and azygos veins) fails to adequately open up, severely curtailing venous return to the heart. This results in hypotension, tachycardia, and syncope.
- Normal BP is quickly restored by turning the patient to the lateral position.

**Central Hemodynamics**

- There is no significant change in Central Venous Pressure (CVP), Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP), and Pulmonary Capillary Wedge Pressure (PCWP) during normal pregnancy. The increase in blood volume and cardiac output is balanced by a significant fall in SVR (-21%), Pulmonary Vascular Resistance (PVR) (-34%), and colloidal osmotic pressure (-14%).

**Regional Distribution of Blood Flow**

- **Uterine Blood Flow:** Increases from 50 mL/min in the nonpregnant state to about 750 mL/min near term, receiving about 15% of the total cardiac output.
- **Pulmonary and Renal Blood Flow:** Pulmonary blood flow increases by 2500 mL/min, and renal blood flow increases by 400 mL/min at the 16th week and remains at that level until term.
- **Skin and Mucous Membranes:** Blood flow reaches a maximum of 500 mL/min by the 36th week, which accounts for the heat sensation, sweating, and nasal stuffiness commonly experienced by pregnant women.

4. How will you assess the gestational age if LMP is known and if LMP is unknown?
**ASSESSMENT OF GESTATIONAL AGE WHEN LMP IS KNOWN**

- **Standard Calculation:** Gestational age is calculated from the first day of the Last normal Menstrual Period (LMP). Accurate LMP is the most reliable clinical parameter for the assessment of gestational age. The total duration of pregnancy is 280 days or 40 weeks.
- **Naegele's Formula:** Provided the menstrual periods are regular (28-day cycle), the Expected Date of Delivery (EDD) is commonly calculated by adding 9 calendar months and 7 days to the first day of the last normal menstrual period.
- **Adjustments for Cycle Length:** If the patient's cycle interval is longer than 28 days, the extra days are to be added to the calculated EDD; if the cycle interval is shorter, the lesser days are to be subtracted to derive the EDD.

---

**ASSESSMENT OF GESTATIONAL AGE WHEN LMP IS UNKNOWN OR INACCURATE**

In 20-30% of cases, patients fail to remember their LMP accurately, or conception occurs during lactation amenorrhea, soon after withdrawal of contraceptive pills, or presents with bleeding in early pregnancy. In such scenarios, the following parameters are used singly or in combination to predict gestational age:

**1. Patient's History and Statements**

- **Date of Fruitful Coitus:** If the exact date of a single fruitful coitus is certain, EDD can be deduced by adding 266 days to this date (accurate to within 7 days in 50% of cases).
- **Date of Quickening:** Quickening (maternal perception of fetal movements) is usually felt at about the 18th week in primigravidae, and about 2 weeks earlier in multiparae. Gestational age can be roughly deduced by adding 22 weeks in primigravidae and 24 weeks in multiparae to the date of quickening.
- **Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART) / IVF:** The date of LMP is calculated as 14 days prior to the date of Embryo Transfer (ET). Therefore, 266 days are added to the exact date of embryo transfer to derive the EDD.

**2. Clinical Examination (Objective Signs)**

- **Uterine Size in First Trimester:** Prior to 12 weeks, the size of the uterus precisely corresponds with the period of amenorrhea. The pregnant uterus enlarges to the size of a hen's egg at 6 weeks, a cricket ball at 8 weeks, and a fetal head by 12 weeks.
- **Fundal Height Landmarks:** The height of the uterus correlates progressively with gestational age:
    - Midway between the symphysis pubis and umbilicus at 16 weeks.
    - At the level of the umbilicus at 24 weeks.
    - At the junction of the lower third and upper two-thirds between the umbilicus and ensiform cartilage at 28 weeks.
    - At the junction of the upper and middle third at 32 weeks.
    - At the level of the ensiform cartilage at 36 weeks.
    - Drops down to the 32-week level at 40 weeks due to the engagement of the presenting part.
- **Symphysis Fundal Height (SFH):** After 24 weeks of pregnancy, the SFH measured in centimeters corresponds to the number of weeks of gestation up to 36 weeks, with an acceptable normal variation of ± 2 cm.
- **Palpation of Fetal Parts:** Fetal parts can be distinctly palpated at the earliest by the 20th week.
- **Auscultation of Fetal Heart Rate (FHR):** Fetal heart sounds can be detected by a stethoscope between 18 and 20 weeks. A hand-held Doppler can pick up the fetal heart rate reliably by the 10th week.

**3. Ultrasonography (USG)**

- **First Trimester (Most Accurate Method):** Transvaginal Ultrasonography (TVS) is the most accurate method to estimate gestational age. Crown-Rump Length (CRL) measurement is the best parameter in the first trimester, carrying a minimal variation of ± 5 days. A gestational sac is seen at 5 weeks, a yolk sac at 5.5 weeks, and an embryo with cardiac motion is visible at 6 weeks.
- **Second Trimester:** Gestational age is determined by measuring the Biparietal Diameter (BPD), Head Circumference (HC), Abdominal Circumference (AC), and Femur Length (FL). This is most accurate when done between 12 and 20 weeks, with a variation of ± 8 to 10 days.
- **Third Trimester:** USG dating in late pregnancy is less reliable, with a variation of ± 16 to 21 days.

**4. Previous Investigation Records**

- **Pregnancy Test:** Gestational age can be roughly estimated by adding 36 weeks from the date of a recorded positive urine or serum pregnancy test.

5. What are components of preconceptional counselling?
**PRECONCEPTIONAL COUNSELING**

**Definition:** Preconceptional counseling involves evaluating and counseling a couple about pregnancy, its course, and outcome well before the time of actual conception.

**Aim:** The primary aim is to ensure that a woman enters pregnancy with an optimal state of health which is safe for both herself and the fetus.

**Key Components and Objectives:**

**1. Optimization of Chronic Medical Illnesses**

- Chronic medical conditions such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, thyroid disease, and psychiatric illnesses have adverse effects on pregnancy outcomes and must be optimally managed before pregnancy.
- A thorough review of all the medications the woman is currently taking should be conducted to avoid teratogenic exposure.

**2. Nutritional Supplementation and Diet**

- **Folic Acid:** The woman should be advised to start prepregnancy folic acid supplementation at a dose of 5 mg a day. This should ideally begin 4 weeks prior to conception and continue up to 12 weeks of pregnancy to significantly reduce the incidence of neural tube defects.
- **Micronutrients:** The dietary adequacy of calcium, iron, and essential vitamins (A, B12, D, and other nutrients) must be maintained.

**3. Lifestyle and Behavioral Modifications**

- **Weight Management:** Women should be encouraged to start their pregnancy with a normal Body Mass Index (BMI).
- **Substance Abuse:** A detailed history regarding the use of alcohol, nicotine products, and opioids must be elicited, and appropriate measures should be taken to improve these habits.
- **Social History:** Assessment for risks of intimate partner violence should be included in the counseling.

**4. Infection Screening and Immunization**

- **Immunization:** Women of reproductive age must have their current immunization status assessed and should be covered appropriately before they conceive.
- **Infectious Diseases:** Screening should be offered for Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs) and any recent exposure to infectious diseases, such as the Zika virus, should be evaluated.

**5. Genetic Counseling**

- A detailed family history is taken to discuss the risks of inheritable diseases (e.g., cystic fibrosis, thalassemia).
- The importance of prenatal diagnosis for various chromosomal and genetic diseases is discussed with the couple.

**6. Educational Classes and Preparation**

- Prenatal educational classes are highly valuable and include discussions regarding childbirth.
- The couple is educated about the possible timing, methods of delivery, and potential interventions that may be required, such as ventouse/forceps or cesarean delivery.


6. What are the phenomena in the third stage of labour? What are the signs and symptoms of placental separation? Steps of active management of third stage of labour (AMTSL)?


**PHENOMENA IN THE THIRD STAGE OF LABOR**

The third stage begins immediately after the expulsion of the fetus and ends with the expulsion of the placenta and membranes (afterbirths). It comprises three main phases: placental separation, its descent into the lower segment, and its ultimate expulsion.

- **Mechanism of Placental Separation:** Following the delivery of the baby, the uterine surface area at the placental site is markedly and abruptly reduced,. Because the placenta is inelastic, this reduction causes it to buckle and shear off from its attachment,,. The plane of separation runs through the deep spongy layer of the decidua basalis,.
- **Types of Separation:**
    - **Central Separation (Schultze method):** Detachment begins at the center, opening uterine sinuses and forming a retroplacental hematoma, which further aids in detachment,.
    - **Marginal Separation (Mathews-Duncan method):** Detachment starts at the unsupported margins. This is the more frequently observed variety.
- **Separation of Membranes:** The membranes are thrown into multiple folds and are peeled off the uterine wall, primarily facilitated by uterine contractions and the downward traction exerted by the weight of the descending placenta,.
- **Mechanism of Hemostasis:** Hemostasis is achieved primarily by the "living ligature" action, where interlacing myometrial fibers powerfully contract and retract to clamp the torn blood vessels,,. This is supplemented by thrombosis of the torn sinuses (favored by the hypercoagulable state of pregnancy) and myotamponade (apposition of the uterine walls),.

---

**SIGNS AND SYMPTOMS OF PLACENTAL SEPARATION**

Before separation, the uterus is discoid, firm, and non-ballottable with the fundus slightly below the umbilicus, and the visible cord length remains static,. Once separation occurs (usually within 5 minutes in active management), the following signs appear:

- **Per Abdomen Signs:**
    - **Change in Uterine Shape:** The uterus becomes globular, firm, and ballottable.
    - **Rising of Fundal Height:** The fundal height slightly rises. This happens because the separated placenta drops down into the lower uterine segment or vagina, pushing the contracted upper uterine segment upwards.
    - **Suprapubic Bulging:** A slight bulge appears in the suprapubic region due to the distension of the lower uterine segment by the separated placenta.
- **Per Vaginam Signs:**
    - **Gush of Blood:** A slight, sudden gush of vaginal bleeding is noticed.
    - **Permanent Lengthening of the Cord:** The length of the umbilical cord visible outside the vulva permanently increases. This is confirmed when applying upward suprapubic pressure on the fundus; if the placenta is separated, the cord does not draw back inside (no indrawing).

---

**STEPS OF ACTIVE MANAGEMENT OF THIRD STAGE OF LABOR (AMTSL)**

The underlying principle of AMTSL is to incite powerful uterine contractions within 1 minute of fetal delivery, which expedites placental separation, shortens the third stage, and reduces the incidence of postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) by 60%,,.

**Step 1: Administration of Uterotonics**

- Administer an oxytocic to the mother within one minute of the birth of the baby,.
- **Drug of choice:** Injection Oxytocin 10 IU intramuscularly (IM) or intravenously (IV) slowly,.
- **Alternatives:** Tablet Misoprostol 600 µg (orally or rectally) or Injection Methergine 0.2 mg IM can also be used depending on availability and contraindications,,.

**Step 2: Delivery of Placenta by Controlled Cord Traction (CCT)**

- The placenta is delivered soon after the baby via the Brandt-Andrews technique (controlled cord traction),.
- While waiting for a strong uterine contraction, the umbilical cord is clamped.
- Once the uterus is firmly contracted, steady downward traction is applied to the cord while the other hand applies counter-pressure upwards and backwards on the uterine fundus (suprapubic region) to prevent uterine inversion,,.

**Step 3: Uterine Massage**

- Immediately following the delivery of the placenta, the uterus is massaged to incite further uterine contractions and ensure it remains hard and well-retracted,.

**Step 4: Examination and Vigilance**

- **Examine Placenta and Membranes:** Carefully inspect the maternal surface of the placenta for missing cotyledons and the membranes for completeness,,.
- **Examine Genital Tract:** Inspect the vulva, vagina, and perineum to exclude traumatic injuries; any lacerations or episiotomy wounds should be promptly repaired,.
- **Postpartum Vigilance (Fourth Stage):** The mother's vitals, uterine retraction, and vaginal bleeding must be strictly monitored at least every 15 minutes for the next 1-2 hours to guard against PPH.


7. How will you grade septic abortion? Discuss the management of septic abortion?
**CLINICAL GRADING OF SEPTIC ABORTION**

Septic abortion is defined as any abortion associated with clinical evidences of infection of the uterus and its contents, typically characterized by a rise in temperature $\ge$ 100.4°F (38°C) for 24 hours or more, offensive or purulent vaginal discharge, and other evidences of pelvic infection.

Depending on the severity and extent of the infection, it is classified into three clinical grades:

- **Grade I:** The infection is localized within the uterus. This is the most common grade and is usually associated with spontaneous abortion.
- **Grade II:** The infection spreads beyond the uterus to the parametrium, tubes, ovaries, or pelvic peritoneum.
- **Grade III:** There is generalized peritonitis and/or endotoxic shock, jaundice, or acute renal failure. This grade is almost always associated with illegally induced (unsafe) abortion.

---

**MANAGEMENT OF SEPTIC ABORTION**

**Principles of Management**

1. To control sepsis.
2. To remove the source of infection.
3. To give supportive therapy to bring back normal homeostatic and cellular metabolism.
4. To assess the response to treatment.

**General Management & Initial Assessment**

- **Hospitalization:** Essential for all cases; the patient should be kept in isolation.
- **Initial Evaluation:** Take a high vaginal or cervical swab for aerobic and anaerobic culture, Gram stain, and drug sensitivity testing prior to internal examination.
- **Clinical Assessment:** Perform a vaginal examination to note the state of the abortion process and the extension of infection. Assess the patient's overall condition and assign the clinical grade.
- **Investigations:** Complete blood count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, ABO and Rh grouping, urine analysis including culture, pelvic/abdominal ultrasonography, and coagulation profile.

**Grade-Specific Management**

**Management of Grade I**

- **Medical Therapy:** Administration of antibiotics, along with analgesics and sedatives as required. Blood transfusion is given to improve anemia and body resistance.
- **Surgical Evacuation:** Since the abortion is often incomplete, evacuation of the uterus should be performed gently at a convenient time within 24 hours following antibiotic therapy. Early emptying minimizes the risk of hemorrhage and removes the nidus of infection. However, excessive bleeding is an urgent indication for immediate evacuation.

**Management of Grade II**

- **Antimicrobial Therapy:** Mixed infections (gram-positive, gram-negative, aerobic, and anaerobic) are common. Empirical broad-spectrum intravenous (IV) therapy is started and later adjusted based on culture reports. A combination of piperacillin-tazobactam or carbapenem plus clindamycin gives the broadest coverage. Metronidazole covers anaerobes, and Vancomycin or teicoplanin can be added for MRSA.
- **Clinical Monitoring:** Strict monitoring of pulse, respiration, temperature, urinary output, progression of lower abdominal pain/mass, and central venous pressure (CVP > 8 mm Hg).
- **Surgical Intervention:**
    - _Evacuation of the uterus:_ Should be done early following an initial dose of antibiotics.
    - _Posterior Colpotomy:_ Indicated when the infection is localized in the pouch of Douglas forming a pelvic abscess (evidenced by spiky temperature, rectal tenesmus, and a boggy mass in the posterior fornix). Drainage of pus relieves symptoms and improves the prognosis.

**Management of Grade III**

- **Intensive Care:** Patients often require Intensive Care Unit (ICU) management due to multi-organ dysfunction. Clinical monitoring and antibiotics are the same as in Grade II.
- **Supportive Therapy:** Directed to treat generalized peritonitis via gastric suction and IV crystalloid infusions. Endotoxic shock and acute renal failure require specific critical care management.
- **Laparotomy (Active Surgery):** Must be performed by an experienced surgeon with a skilled anesthetist.
    - _Indications for Laparotomy:_
        1. Injury to the uterus.
        2. Suspected injury to the bowel.
        3. Presence of a foreign body in the abdomen (detected by USG, X-ray, or bimanual examination).
        4. Unresponsive peritonitis suggestive of pus collection.
        5. Septic shock or oliguria not responding to conservative treatment.
        6. Uterus too big to be safely evacuated vaginally.
    - _Surgical Steps:_ Removal of the uterus (hysterectomy) irrespective of parity. The adnexa is removed or preserved depending on the pathology found. Thorough inspection of the gut and omentum is mandatory. Even if no specific pathology is found upon opening, simple drainage of the pus is highly effective.

8. What are the uses of ultrasound in the first trimester of pregnancy?

**USES OF ULTRASOUND IN THE FIRST TRIMESTER OF PREGNANCY**

Ultrasonography, particularly Transvaginal Sonography (TVS), is the most accurate and commonly used imaging modality for evaluating early pregnancy, as the close proximity of the vaginal probe to the target area provides superior tissue resolution. The high-yield clinical applications of ultrasound in the first trimester are detailed below:

**1. Confirmation of Pregnancy and its Location**

- An intrauterine Gestational Sac (GS) can be visualised by TVS as early as 4.5 to 5 weeks of gestation (29-35 days of menstrual age).
- The appearance of the yolk sac at 5.5 weeks is diagnostic of an intrauterine pregnancy and helps differentiate a true early gestational sac from a pseudogestational sac.
- Confirming the intrauterine location of the sac is a crucial step to rule out a suspected ectopic pregnancy.

**2. Confirmation of Fetal Viability**

- The fetal pole along with embryonic cardiac activity can be detected by 6 weeks of gestation.
- Normal embryonic cardiac activity (>100 beats per minute) can generally be visualised in normal embryos with a Crown-Rump Length (CRL) greater than 5 mm.
- The presence of cardiac motion confirms viability and provides a 98% chance of the pregnancy continuing. Conversely, absent cardiac activity in an embryo is the most important predictor of a poor pregnancy outcome.

**3. Accurate Estimation of Gestational Age (Dating)**

- First trimester TVS is the most accurate method for determining gestational age, making it far superior to relying on the Last Menstrual Period (LMP) alone.
- Measurement of the Crown-Rump Length (CRL) between 7 and 12 weeks is the most reliable parameter for dating, carrying a minimal error margin of $\pm$ 5 days.

**4. Diagnosis of Multiple Pregnancy and Chorionicity**

- Ultrasound can easily confirm multiple gestations by identifying the number of gestational sacs, yolk sacs, and fetal poles with cardiac activity.
- The first trimester (specifically 11-14 weeks) is the optimal time to determine chorionicity and amnionicity by evaluating the thickness of the dividing membranes.
- The presence of the "lambda" or "twin peak" sign indicates a dichorionic placenta, whereas a "T" sign with a thin dividing membrane (<2 mm) suggests a monochorionic diamniotic pregnancy.

**5. Diagnosis of Early Pregnancy Failure (Miscarriage)**

- TVS provides definitive sonographic evidence to diagnose early pregnancy failure, such as a missed miscarriage or an anembryonic pregnancy (blighted ovum).
- Established sonographic criteria for pregnancy failure include a Mean Sac Diameter (MSD) $\ge$ 25 mm with no visible embryo, or a CRL $\ge$ 7 mm without any cardiac motion.
- Other ominous signs include an irregular or collapsing sac margin and the presence of yolk sac calcification.

**6. Diagnosis of Ectopic Pregnancy**

- TVS can successfully detect 90% of tubal ectopic pregnancies.
- Diagnostic findings include an empty uterine cavity in the presence of a positive pregnancy test, echogenic fluid (blood) in the pouch of Douglas, and a complex adnexal mass separate from the ovary.
- Color Doppler sonography can identify the "ring-of-fire" pattern, which represents enhanced vascular blood flow outside the uterine cavity around the ectopic sac.

**7. Screening for Fetal Aneuploidy and Structural Anomalies**

- **Nuchal Translucency (NT):** Measurement of the fluid-filled space at the back of the fetal neck between 11 and 14 weeks is a highly effective screening tool. An NT measurement of $\ge$ 3 mm is abnormal and serves as a strong marker for chromosomal anomalies (e.g., Trisomy 21, 18, 13, Turner's syndrome) as well as major cardiac defects.
- Evaluation of the fetal nasal bone is also performed; its absence at 10-12 weeks is strongly associated with fetal Down's syndrome.
- Major gross structural anomalies, such as anencephaly, can be visualised during this period.

**8. Diagnosis of Gestational Trophoblastic Disease (Molar Pregnancy)**

- Ultrasound is used to evaluate suspected molar pregnancies. A complete hydatidiform mole characteristically appears as an echogenic mass containing multiple anechoic cystic spaces, commonly referred to as a "snowstorm" appearance, with an absence of fetal parts.

**9. Evaluation of Pelvic and Adnexal Masses**

- First-trimester ultrasound is highly valuable for identifying and evaluating co-existing maternal pelvic masses, such as uterine fibroids or ovarian tumors (e.g., dermoid cysts), noting their location, size, and consistency.

**10. Guidance for Invasive Diagnostic Procedures**

- Ultrasound acts as an essential adjunct for visually guiding invasive prenatal diagnostic procedures to ensure safety and accuracy. In the first trimester, it is specifically used to guide Chorionic Villus Sampling (CVS), which is typically performed between 10 and 13 weeks of gestation.


9. What are the maternal and Fetal complications of Twin pregnancy?

**MATERNAL COMPLICATIONS**

**1. During Pregnancy**

- **Nausea and Vomiting:** Occurs with increased frequency and severity.
- **Anemia:** There is an increased requirement for iron and folate by the two fetuses, which can lead to anemia, and folic acid deficiency may specifically cause megaloblastic anemia.
- **Pre-eclampsia / Pregnancy-Induced Hypertension (PIH):** The incidence is increased three times (25%) over a singleton pregnancy, possibly due to the exposure to a superabundance of chorionic villi.
- **Polyhydramnios (Hydramnios):** Occurs in about 10% of cases, is more common in monozygotic twins, and usually involves the second sac.
- **Antepartum Hemorrhage (APH):** There is an increased incidence of placenta previa due to the larger size of the placenta encroaching onto the lower uterine segment, as well as placental abruption resulting from pre-eclampsia, sudden escape of liquor, or folic acid deficiency.
- **Malpresentation:** Malpresentations are quite common; while the first baby presents by vertex in 70% of cases, the second baby is more prone to malpresentation.
- **Preterm Labor:** Frequently occurs (50%), with the mean gestational period for twins being 37 weeks, resulting from uterine overdistension, polyhydramnios, and premature rupture of the membranes.
- **Mechanical Distress:** Pregnant women experience an increase in palpitation, dyspnea, varicosities, and hemorrhoids compared to singleton pregnancies.

**2. During Labor**

- **Early Rupture of Membranes and Cord Prolapse:** Cord prolapse is five times more common than in singleton pregnancies, particularly in relation to the second baby and due to increased malpresentations.
- **Increased Operative Interference:** There is a higher need for cesarean delivery or forceps due to the high prevalence of malpresentation and its associated complications.
- **Intrapartum Bleeding:** Alarming bleeding can occur following the birth of the first baby due to the separation of the placenta caused by the reduction of the placental site.
- **Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH):** This is a real danger in twins due to uterine atony from overdistension, the longer time taken for the large placenta to separate, the bigger placental surface area exposing more uterine sinuses, and the implantation of part of the placenta in the less retractile lower segment.

**3. During Puerperium**

- **Subinvolution:** Increased incidence due to the bigger size of the uterus.
- **Infection:** Higher risk of puerperal sepsis due to increased operative interference, pre-existing anemia, and blood loss during delivery.
- **Lactation Failure:** May occur but can be minimized by providing reassurance and additional support.
- **Thromboembolism:** The risk of thromboembolic events is increased.

---

**FETAL COMPLICATIONS**

- **Miscarriage:** The abortion or miscarriage rate is increased, especially with monozygotic twins.
- **Prematurity and Low Birth Weight:** The premature rate is markedly increased (80%), causing babies to suffer from hazards of prematurity and low birth weight.
- **Discordant Twin Growth:** Found in 20% of cases, true pathological discordance (estimated weight difference of 25% or more) can result from twin-twin transfusion syndrome (TTTS), placental insufficiency, IUGR, or structural anomalies in one fetus.
- **Vanishing Twin:** Occurs in up to 21% of cases where the death and resorption of one fetus happens in the first trimester.
- **Intrauterine Fetal Death (IUFD):** Death of one fetus occurs in 3-7% of cases and is more common in monozygotic twins. If death happens in the second trimester, a fetus papyraceous (compressus) may form, and late fetal death can lead to the death of the co-twin or maternal disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).
- **Appearing Twin:** A phenomenon where the diagnosis of a twin pregnancy is missed on the initial ultrasound but diagnosed on a later scan, common in monozygotic twins.
- **Fetal Anomalies:** Congenital anomalies (e.g., neural tube defects, holoprosencephaly, cardiac anomalies, Down's syndrome) are increased by 2-4% compared to singleton pregnancies, mostly in monozygotic twins.
- **Asphyxia and Stillbirth:** More common due to increased prevalence of pre-eclampsia, malpresentation, placental abruption, and prematurity, with the second baby being more at risk.
- **Mechanical Distress/Accidents:** Increased chances of cord prolapse and locked twins.
- **Specific Complications of Monochorionic Twins:** Monochorionic twins carry the highest risks, which include Twin-Twin Transfusion Syndrome (TTTS), Twin Anemia-Polycythemia Sequence (TAPS), Dead Fetus Syndrome, Twin Reversed Arterial Perfusion (TRAP) sequence, monoamniocity leading to cord entanglement, conjoined twins, and Selective Growth Restriction (sGR).


10. Diagnosis and management of transverse lie in labour.

**DIAGNOSIS OF TRANSVERSE LIE IN LABOR**

Transverse lie occurs when the long axis of the fetus lies perpendicularly to the maternal spine or centralized uterine axis, while in an oblique lie, it is placed at an acute angle. During labor, the shoulder typically presents over the cervical opening, collectively referred to as shoulder presentation.

**1. Abdominal Examination**

- **Inspection:** The uterus appears broader, is often asymmetrical, and loses its normal pyriform shape.
- **Palpation:**
    - **Fundal Height:** It is less than the expected period of amenorrhea.
    - **Fundal Grip:** The fundus feels empty as neither the head nor the breech is palpable.
    - **Lateral Grip:** A soft, broad, and irregular breech is felt on one side of the midline, while a smooth, hard, and globular head is felt on the contralateral iliac fossa at a lower level. The back or irregular small parts are felt anteriorly depending on the position.
    - **Pelvic Grip:** The lower pole of the uterus is typically empty during pregnancy, but during labor, it may be occupied by the shoulder.
- **Auscultation:** Fetal Heart Sounds (FHS) are heard distinctly much below the umbilicus in a dorsoanterior position, but may be higher and indistinct in a dorsoposterior position.

**2. Vaginal Examination**

- **Membranes:** An elongated bag of membranes can be felt if it has not ruptured. Premature rupture is highly common due to the absence of a well-fitting presenting part.
- **Presenting Part:** The shoulder is identified by palpating specific bony landmarks: the acromion process, scapula, clavicle, axilla, and the characteristic "grid-iron" feel of the ribs and intercostal spaces.
- **Prolapse:** Hand or arm prolapse is frequent, sometimes accompanied by a loop of cord.
- **Determining Position:** The side of the prolapsed arm can be identified by "shaking hands" with the fetus; if the examiner's right hand naturally shakes the fetal hand, it is the fetal right arm. The supinated thumb points toward the fetal head, and the angle of the scapula indicates the direction of the fetal back.

**3. Ultrasonography (USG)**

- USG confirms the diagnosis if clinical evaluation is difficult (e.g., due to obesity) and evaluates liquor volume, placental position, and fetal anomalies.

---

**MANAGEMENT OF TRANSVERSE LIE IN LABOR**

There is no mechanism of labor for a transverse lie; an average-sized baby cannot pass through an average-sized pelvis. If left unattended, it leads to a "neglected shoulder" characterized by early membrane rupture, cord prolapse, impacted shoulder, obstructed labor, pathological retraction ring formation, and eventual uterine rupture.

**1. Management in Early Labor**

- **External Cephalic Version (ECV):** Can be attempted in selected cases if there is a good amount of liquor amnii, intact membranes, and no contraindications.
- **Cesarean Delivery:** This is the preferred and safest method of delivery if ECV fails or is contraindicated.
    - **Incision:** A low transverse incision is commonly made, though difficulties may arise due to a poorly developed lower uterine segment, occasionally necessitating a low vertical or "T" shaped incision. Intraoperative cephalic version can often be successfully performed under anesthesia prior to extraction.
- **Internal Podalic Version:** Not recommended in modern obstetrics for a singleton pregnancy due to high maternal and fetal complication rates; its only modern indication is for the delivery of a transverse second twin.

**2. Management in Late Labor (Advanced/Neglected cases)**

- **Baby Alive:** Immediate Cesarean delivery is mandatory. Internal version is strictly contraindicated due to the extreme risk of uterine rupture.
- **Baby Dead:** Cesarean delivery is still the treatment of choice as it is much safer for the mother than destructive vaginal operations like evisceration, which carry a high risk of maternal morbidity and uterine rupture.


11. Define MMR. What are the direct and indirect causes of MMR?
**DEFINITION OF MATERNAL MORTALITY RATIO (MMR)**

- **Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR):** It is expressed as the number of maternal deaths per 100,000 live births.
- **Maternal Death:** It is defined as the death of a woman while pregnant or within 42 days of the termination of pregnancy, irrespective of the duration and the site of pregnancy, from any cause related to or aggravated by the pregnancy or its management, but not from accidental or incidental causes.

---

**CAUSES OF MATERNAL MORTALITY**

The causes of maternal deaths are broadly classified into Direct obstetric causes, Indirect obstetric causes, and Non-obstetric (fortuitous) deaths.

**1. Direct Obstetric Causes (75%)** Direct deaths are those resulting from complications of pregnancy, delivery, or their management. The major direct causes include:

- **Hemorrhage (20–25%):** This is a leading cause of maternal mortality. It is mostly due to Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH) but also includes Antepartum Hemorrhage (abruptio placentae, placenta previa), retained placenta, ectopic pregnancy, and abortion complications.
- **Hypertensive Disorders (12–20%):** Includes severe pre-eclampsia and eclampsia.
- **Infection / Sepsis (11–20%):** Puerperal sepsis is frequently associated with labor and the puerperium. Infections often result from premature rupture of membranes (PROM), and prolonged or obstructed labor.
- **Unsafe Abortion (8–13%):** Termination of pregnancy in unsafe conditions by unskilled personnel.
- **Obstructed Labor (8–9%):** Commonly caused by cephalopelvic disproportion, abnormal lie, or malpresentation.
- _Note:_ Suicidal death in a pregnant or recently delivered woman is classified as a direct maternal death.

**2. Indirect Obstetric Causes (25%)** Indirect deaths include conditions that were present before pregnancy or developed during pregnancy, which are aggravated by the physiological effects of pregnancy and the strain of labor. The major indirect causes include:

- **Anemia (15–20%):** This is the most important single indirect cause of maternal death in developing countries. It is commonly due to dietary deficiencies (iron, folic acid) or infections like hookworm and malaria.
- **Viral Hepatitis (5–10%):** Endemic in India, viral hepatitis carries a high mortality rate. Death mostly occurs in the last trimester due to hepatic coma, coagulation failure, and postpartum hemorrhage.
- **Cardiac Disease:** Pre-existing heart conditions that decompensate under the physiological demands of pregnancy.
- **Other Medical Disorders:** Conditions such as diabetes mellitus, thyroid disease, malaria, tuberculosis, and HIV.


12. What are the National Programmes for Prevention of Anaemia in Pregnancy?

**NATIONAL PROGRAMMES AND STRATEGIES FOR PREVENTION OF ANAEMIA IN PREGNANCY**

**1. Anemia Mukt Bharat**

- Launched as a health programme under the National Iron Plus Initiative (NIPI) in 2018.
- The primary objective of this programme is to reduce the prevalence of anaemia to less than 32%.

**2. Reproductive and Child Health (RCH) Programme**

- Anaemia prevention and management are integrated into the Essential Obstetric Care services provided under the RCH programme.
- It operates alongside other maternal health initiatives under the National Health Mission (NHM), such as the Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram (JSSK) 2011 and Janani Suraksha Yojana (JSY).

**3. Routine Iron and Folic Acid (IFA) Supplementation Policy**

- Because most women have inadequate iron stores (only 20% have the minimum 500 mg required for pregnancy), routine iron supplementation is necessary even with a well-balanced diet.
- **Prophylactic Dosage:** The standard prophylactic procedure is the daily administration of 200 mg of ferrous sulphate (which contains 60 mg of elemental iron) along with 1 mg of folic acid.
- **Initiation:** Supplementary iron therapy is indicated for all pregnant mothers from 16 weeks of gestation onwards.
- **Duration:** Supplementary Iron-Folic Acid (IFA) therapy must be administered daily for a minimum period of at least 100 days after the first trimester.
- **Additional Preconceptional Folic Acid:** Folic acid therapy (4-5 mg/day) should ideally be started in the pre-pregnant state and continued throughout the pregnancy to prevent megaloblastic anaemia and neural tube defects.

**4. Additional Preventive Measures under Antenatal Care**

- **Dietary Guidance:** Prescription of a realistic, well-balanced diet rich in protein, iron, and vitamins is advised.
- **Eradication of Parasitic/Protozoal Infections:** Routine treatment should be instituted to eradicate underlying causes of anaemia such as hookworm infestation, malaria, dysentery, and bleeding piles.
- **Regular Screening:** Early detection of falling haemoglobin levels is mandated; haemoglobin levels must be estimated at the first antenatal visit, at the 30th week, and finally at the 36th week. Minimum spacing of at least 2 years between pregnancies is also advised to replenish lost iron stores.


13. What are components of reactive CTG/NST?
**COMPONENTS OF A REACTIVE CTG / NORMAL NST**

**Definition:** The Non-Stress Test (NST) or Cardiotocography (CTG) involves continuous electronic monitoring of the Fetal Heart Rate (FHR) along with the recording of fetal movements to assess fetal wellbeing.

**Criteria for a Reactive (Reassuring) CTG/NST Trace:** A tracing is considered normal or "reactive" (typically assessed after 32 weeks) when it fulfills the following four core components:

- **1. Baseline Fetal Heart Rate (FHR):**
    
    - The normal baseline FHR must range between **110 and 160 beats per minute (bpm)**.
    - Baseline FHR is the mean level of FHR excluding any temporary accelerations and decelerations.
- **2. Baseline FHR Variability:**
    
    - The normal amplitude of baseline variability must be between **5 and 25 bpm** (termed as moderate variability).
    - Variability represents the oscillation of the baseline FHR (excluding accelerations and decelerations) and is a reflex of normal cardiac behavior in response to intact sympathetic and parasympathetic nerve input.
- **3. Accelerations (The Hallmark of Reactivity):**
    
    - A reactive pattern requires the presence of **at least two or more accelerations** within a **20-minute observation period**.
    - An acceleration is specifically defined as a transient increase in FHR of **15 beats per minute or more** above the baseline, lasting for **at least 15 seconds**.
    - The presence of accelerations (often associated with fetal movement or induced by vibroacoustic stimulation) denotes an intact neurohormonal and cardiovascular activity, confirming a healthy fetus and the absence of fetal acidosis.
- **4. Absence of Concerning Decelerations:**
    
    - In a normal reassuring trace, there should be **no late, variable, or prolonged decelerations**.
    - However, early decelerations of very short duration or variable decelerations with no concerning features lasting for <10 minutes may occasionally be present.

**Clinical Significance:** A reactive NST reliably identifies fetal wellness rather than illness. It is highly reassuring and has a very low false-negative rate (<1%), making it an excellent first-line screening test for both high-risk and low-risk pregnancies.



14. What are the WHO criteria for defining a normal labour?
**NORMAL LABOR (EUTOCIA) CRITERIA**

Labor is considered normal (eutocia) if it strictly fulfills the following five criteria:

1. **Onset and Timing:** It must be spontaneous in onset and occur at term (between 38 and 42 weeks of gestation).
2. **Presentation:** The fetus must be in a vertex presentation.
3. **Duration:** It must progress without undue prolongation.
4. **Termination:** It must result in a natural termination (vaginal delivery) with minimal aids.
5. **Maternal and Fetal Health:** It must conclude without having any complications that affect the health of the mother and/or the baby.

---

**WHO DEFINITIONS & TIME LIMITS FOR NORMAL LABOR STAGES** Under the WHO guidelines for intrapartum care for a positive childbirth experience, the normal progression and duration of labor stages are defined as follows:

- **First Stage (Latent Phase):** Characterized by painful uterine contractions and variable changes of the cervix, including some degree of effacement and slower progression of dilatation up to 5 cm (for first and subsequent labors).
- **First Stage (Active Phase):** Characterized by regular painful uterine contractions, a substantial degree of cervical effacement, and more rapid cervical dilatation from 5 cm until full dilatation. The duration usually does not extend beyond 12 hours in first labors and 10 hours in subsequent labors.
- **Second Stage:** The period between full cervical dilatation and birth of the baby, during which the woman has an involuntary urge to bear down as a result of expulsive uterine contractions. Birth is usually completed within 3 hours in first labors and within 2 hours in subsequent labors.
- **Third Stage:** Involves the separation and expulsion of the placenta and membranes, typically with an average duration of 15 minutes. The WHO recommends Active Management of the Third Stage of Labor (AMTSL) using prophylactic uterotonics (Oxytocin 10 IU, IM/IV) for all births to prevent postpartum hemorrhage.


15. How will you manage Breech presentation at 36 week gestation?

**MANAGEMENT OF BREECH PRESENTATION AT 36 WEEKS GESTATION**

The management of a breech presentation at 36 weeks of gestation involves a systematic approach consisting of clinical evaluation, attempt at correcting the malpresentation (External Cephalic Version), and formulating a definitive delivery plan if the version fails or is contraindicated.

**1. Initial Antenatal Assessment and Investigations**

- **Identification of Complicating Factors:** Detailed clinical examination supplemented by ultrasonography (USG) is mandatory.
- **Role of USG:** USG is the gold standard for decision making. It is used to detect congenital malformations of the fetus, precise location of the placenta, adequate amniotic fluid volume, and congenital anomalies of the uterus.

**2. External Cephalic Version (ECV)** If there are no contraindications, ECV is the primary intervention to bring the favorable cephalic pole into the lower uterine pole.

- **Timing:** ECV is considered from 36 weeks in primigravidae and 37 weeks in multigravidae.
- **Benefits:** A successful ECV (success rate ~65%) reduces the incidence of breech presentation at term, decreases the need for cesarean delivery by 50%, and minimizes the maternal and fetal hazards associated with breech delivery.
- **Prerequisites and Monitoring:**
    - The procedure must be performed in a labor suite where facilities for emergency cesarean section are available.
    - A reactive Non-Stress Test (NST) or Cardiotocography (CTG) must precede and follow the maneuver to ensure fetal wellbeing.
    - Real-time ultrasound scanning is used during the procedure.
- **Procedure:**
    - Tocolytic drugs (e.g., terbutaline 0.25 mg SC) may be administered to relax an irritable uterus and increase the success rate.
    - The fetus is rotated using a "forward roll" movement with intermittent, firm pressure.
    - The procedure is abandoned if there is significant fetal distress or after a maximum of 4 failed attempts.
- **Post-Procedure Care:** The patient is observed for 30-60 minutes for FHR stability, vaginal bleeding, or PROM. Rh-negative, nonimmunized mothers must be given Anti-D immunoglobulin (100 µg).

**3. Management if ECV Fails or is Contraindicated** If the breech presentation persists, the case is reassessed based on maternal age, associated complicating factors, estimated fetal weight, and pelvic capacity. Induction of labor is generally not recommended. The delivery plan is formulated as follows:

**A. Elective Cesarean Section (CD)** Planned cesarean delivery is recommended at or after 38 weeks for cases with unfavorable factors.

- **Indications for Elective CS:**
    - Estimated fetal weight > 3.5 kg (macrosomia) or < 1.5 kg (severe prematurity/IUGR).
    - Hyperextended fetal head ("stargazing fetus").
    - Footling presentation (due to high risk of cord prolapse).
    - Suspected fetopelvic disproportion or inadequate pelvis.
    - Prior cesarean delivery or other obstetric/medical complications.
    - Absence of a skilled obstetrician experienced in vaginal breech delivery or upon patient request.

**B. Trial of Vaginal Breech Delivery** Spontaneous onset of labor is awaited. Vaginal delivery can be allowed if strict criteria are met, as it carries a higher risk of perinatal mortality and morbidity compared to cesarean delivery.

- **Selection Criteria for Vaginal Delivery:**
    - Frank or complete breech presentation.
    - Average estimated fetal weight between 2.0 kg and 3.5 kg (or 1.5 kg - 3.5 kg).
    - Flexed or neutral fetal head.
    - Clinically adequate maternal pelvis.
    - Zatuchni-Andros score $\ge$ 4 (a score < 4 predicts a poor prognostic index for vaginal delivery).
    - Informed consent obtained, explaining the risks.
    - Availability of continuous electronic fetal monitoring, an experienced obstetrician, an anesthetist, a neonatologist, and facilities for an immediate rapid cesarean section.
- **Conduction of Delivery:** If allowed, delivery must be conducted as an **Assisted Breech Delivery**, where the obstetrician assists the natural expulsion of the fetus from the umbilicus onwards to prevent head entrapment and trauma. Routine cesarean section is performed in labor if there is an arrest of progress, fetal distress, or cord prolapse.


16. Causes of puerperal sepsis?

**DEFINITION** Puerperal sepsis (puerperal infection) is defined as an infection of the genital tract that occurs as a complication of delivery. Any puerperal pyrexia (fever) is considered to be due to genital tract infection unless proved otherwise.

**CAUSATIVE MICROORGANISMS** Most of the infections in the genital tract are polymicrobial, involving a mixture of aerobic and anaerobic organisms. The commonly isolated pathogens include:

**1. Aerobic Organisms**

- **Gram-positive cocci:** Group A beta-hemolytic _Streptococcus_, Group B beta-hemolytic _Streptococcus_, Methicillin-Resistant _Staphylococcus aureus_ (MRSA), and Methicillin-Sensitive _S. aureus_ (MSSA).
- **Gram-negative bacilli:** _Escherichia coli_, _Klebsiella_, _Proteus_, and _Pseudomonas_.
- **Gram-variable:** _Gardnerella vaginalis_.
- **Others:** _Chlamydia_ and _Mycoplasma_.

**2. Anaerobic Organisms**

- **Cocci:** _Peptostreptococcus_ and _Peptococcus_. Anaerobic _Streptococcus_ is the most predominant pathogen in endogenous infections.
- **Bacilli:** _Bacteroides_ group (e.g., _B. fragilis_, _B. bivius_), _Fusobacteria_, _Mobiluncus_, and _Clostridia_ (e.g., _Clostridium welchii_).

**MODES OF INFECTION** Puerperal sepsis is essentially a wound infection, with organisms gaining a foothold in the raw placental site, cervical/vaginal lacerations, or cesarean section wounds. The infection occurs via three primary routes:

- **Endogenous:** Caused by organisms normally present in the genital tract before delivery (e.g., Anaerobic _Streptococcus_).
- **Autogenous:** Organisms present elsewhere in the mother's body (e.g., skin, throat) migrate to the genital organs via the bloodstream or by the patient herself (e.g., Beta-hemolytic _Streptococcus_, _E. coli_, _Staphylococcus_).
- **Exogenous:** Infection is contracted from outside sources, such as the hospital environment or healthcare attendants (e.g., Beta-hemolytic _Streptococcus_, _Staphylococcus_, _E. coli_).

**PREDISPOSING FACTORS** Under normal circumstances, the vaginal flora remains dormant and harmless. However, the pathogenicity of these organisms is triggered by the raw placental site, cervicovaginal mucosal damage, and retained blood clots which serve as excellent growth media. The major predisposing high-risk factors are:

**1. Antepartum Factors:**

- Anemia.
- Premature Rupture of Membranes (PROM) and prolonged rupture of membranes ($\ge$ 18 hours).
- Preterm labor.
- Maternal medical comorbidities: Diabetes and immunocompromised states (e.g., HIV).

**2. Intrapartum Factors:**

- Repeated vaginal examinations during labor.
- Traumatic vaginal delivery and operative interferences.
- Cesarean delivery.
- Prolonged or obstructed labor.
- Hemorrhage (Antepartum or Postpartum).
- Retained bits of placental tissue or fetal membranes.
- Dehydration and ketoacidosis during labor.

17. How will you diagnose ectopic pregnancy?

**DIAGNOSIS OF ECTOPIC PREGNANCY**

The diagnosis of an ectopic pregnancy demands a high index of suspicion. It is based on the clinical presentation (which varies depending on whether the ectopic is acute/ruptured, unruptured, or subacute/chronic) supported by specific investigations.

**I. CLINICAL DIAGNOSIS (History and Examination)**

**Symptoms**

- **Classic Triad:** The classic triad of symptoms for disturbed tubal pregnancy includes:
    1. **Abdominal Pain (100%):** Most constant feature. It can be acute, agonizing, colicky, or a vague soreness in the lower abdomen. Shoulder tip pain (referred pain from diaphragmatic irritation due to hemoperitoneum) may be present.
    2. **Amenorrhea (75%):** Usually a short period of 6–8 weeks, or a history of a delayed period.
    3. **Vaginal Bleeding (70%):** Usually slight, continuous, and dark. Expulsion of a decidual cast may occur.
- **Syncope (10%):** Dizziness or fainting attacks may be the initial presentation, usually due to intra-abdominal bleeding following a rupture.

**Signs**

- **General Signs:** The patient may look blanched and exhibit varying degrees of pallor. In acute rupture, features of hypovolemic shock (rapid and feeble pulse, cold clammy extremities, hypotension) are evident. In chronic ectopic, a persistently high pulse is a conspicuous finding.
- **Abdominal Signs:** Extreme tenderness, muscle guarding, and rebound tenderness in the lower abdomen. A tender mass may be palpated.
- **Pelvic (Vaginal) Examination:** Extreme cervical motion tenderness is a hallmark. The uterus may feel soft and "float" as if in water (due to hemoperitoneum). An ill-defined, boggy, and extremely tender mass may be felt through the posterolateral fornix or extending to the Pouch of Douglas.

---

**II. SPECIFIC INVESTIGATIONS (High-Yield)**

In an acute, hemodynamically unstable case, time should not be wasted on extensive investigations other than Hb% and blood grouping; immediate resuscitation and laparotomy are indicated. For stable or unruptured cases, the following diagnostic modalities are used:

**1. Estimation of $\beta$-hCG**

- **Urine Pregnancy Test:** ELISA test is sensitive (10-50 mIU/mL) and is positive in 95% of ectopic pregnancies.
- **Quantitative Serum $\beta$-hCG:** A single value confirms pregnancy but not its location. The characteristic finding in ectopic pregnancy is a lower concentration of $\beta$-hCG compared to a normal intrauterine pregnancy, and the titer fails to double every 48 hours.

**2. Transvaginal Sonography (TVS) & Color Doppler**

- TVS is the imaging modality of choice and can detect 90% of tubal ectopics.
- **Diagnostic Features:**
    - Empty uterine cavity in the presence of a positive pregnancy test.
    - Presence of an adnexal mass clearly separated from the ovary.
    - Echogenic fluid (blood) in the Pouch of Douglas.
    - Rarely, a gestational sac containing a yolk sac or an embryo with cardiac motion is directly visualized in the adnexa.
- **Color Doppler (TV-CDS):** Demonstrates enhanced vascular blood flow outside the uterine cavity, characteristically seen as the **"ring-of-fire" pattern**.

**3. The "Discriminatory Zone" (hCG + USG)**

- The discriminatory zone is the lowest $\beta$-hCG level at which a normal intrauterine gestational sac should be consistently visible on USG.
- If the serum $\beta$-hCG is **> 1,500 IU/L** and TVS shows an empty uterine cavity, an ectopic pregnancy is highly likely. (For Transabdominal Sonography, the threshold is 6,000 IU/L).

**4. Laparoscopy**

- This is the "gold standard" for diagnosis when the clinical picture is confusing, provided the patient is hemodynamically stable.
- It allows direct visualization of the unruptured/ruptured tube and hemoperitoneum, and offers the advantage of concurrent therapeutic surgical management.

**5. Dilatation and Curettage (D&C)**

- Uterine curettage helps differentiate an ectopic from an incomplete abortion.
- Identification of decidua _without_ chorionic villi (often showing the characteristic adenomatous change known as the **Arias-Stella reaction**) is highly suggestive of an ectopic pregnancy.

**6. Culdocentesis**

- An older, alternative test used if TVS/Laparoscopy is unavailable.
- Aspiration of non-clotting blood with a hematocrit >15% from the Pouch of Douglas (via the posterior vaginal fornix) signifies a ruptured ectopic pregnancy.

**7. Serum Progesterone**

- A value < 5 ng/mL suggests an abnormal intrauterine or ectopic pregnancy, whereas a value > 25 ng/mL strongly suggests a viable intrauterine pregnancy.

**8. Routine Blood Tests**

- Hemoglobin, hematocrit, ABO and Rh grouping (mandatory for potential transfusion and Anti-D administration), total WBC count (may show varying degrees of leukocytosis), and ESR.

**9. Laparotomy**

- If the clinical condition is critical (hemodynamically unstable), the axiom **'open and see'** holds good. Diagnostic laparotomy is justified to prevent eventual fatality.


18. Explain post molar pregnancy follow up?
**OBJECTIVE OF FOLLOW-UP** Routine follow-up is mandatory for all cases of molar pregnancy for at least 6 months. The primary objective is the early detection of persistent gestational trophoblastic disease (GTN) or choriocarcinoma, which occurs in 15–20% of complete mole cases and mostly within the first 6 months.

**FOLLOW-UP SCHEDULE AND INTERVALS**

- **Initial Phase:** Check-ups and serum $\beta$-hCG estimations are done at weekly intervals until the $\beta$-hCG level becomes undetectable (negative). Normalization usually occurs by 4 to 8 weeks.
- **Subsequent Phase:** Once the hCG level is negative (within 56 days), the patient is followed up at monthly intervals for a minimum of 6 months.

**CLINICAL EVALUATION AT EACH VISIT**

- **History:** The patient must be specifically enquired about relevant symptoms that suggest persistent disease or metastasis, such as irregular vaginal bleeding, continuing amenorrhea, persistent cough, breathlessness, or hemoptysis.
- **Abdominovaginal Examination:** Performed to confirm the involution of the uterus, the regression of bilateral theca lutein cysts (which usually regress within 2 months), and to rule out malignant deposits in the anterior vaginal wall.

**INVESTIGATIONS**

- **Serum $\beta$-hCG Assays:** This is the mainstay of monitoring. Because the hCG molecule exhibits structural heterogeneity in GTN (different isoforms, nicked free beta, $\beta$ core fragments), the assay used must be highly sensitive and capable of detecting all isoforms of hCG.
- **Ultrasonography (USG):** Pelvic USG or examination is typically done one week after molar evacuation.
- **Chest Radiograph:** If the pre-evacuation chest X-ray was normal, it does not need to be repeated routinely; it is only repeated if the $\beta$-hCG titer plateaus or rises.

**CONTRACEPTIVE ADVICE** It is critical that the patient does not become pregnant during the follow-up period, as a new pregnancy will elevate hCG levels, masking the diagnosis of persistent GTN.

- **Duration:** The woman should strictly avoid pregnancy for 1 year following the treatment of a molar pregnancy.
- **Recommended Methods:** Barrier methods can be safely used. Combined Oral Contraceptive (COC) pills are recommended, but they must only be started _after_ the serum $\beta$-hCG values have returned to normal. Injectable DMPA or progestin implants are also safe. Surgical sterilization can be done if the family is complete.
- **Contraindicated Methods:** Intrauterine Devices (IUDs) are strictly contraindicated due to the high risk of uterine perforation and because they frequently cause irregular vaginal bleeding, which can confuse the diagnosis of choriocarcinoma.

**DIAGNOSTIC CRITERIA FOR POSTMOLAR GTN (FIGO)** During the follow-up period, GTN is diagnosed if any of the following occur:

1. **Plateauing hCG:** $\ge$ Four values of plateaued hCG ($\pm10%$) over at least 3 weeks' time (Days 1, 7, 14, 21).
2. **Rising hCG:** A rise in hCG of $>10%$ for $>3$ values over at least 2 weeks' time.
3. **Prolonged hCG:** Persistence of hCG beyond 6 months of molar evacuation.
4. **Histology:** Histologic diagnosis of choriocarcinoma. _Note: Any plateau, re-elevation, or persistent elevation requires a full metastatic survey (X-ray chest, CT/MRI of brain and liver) and initiation of chemotherapy_.

19.What are the criteria to diagnose preterm labour?

**CRITERIA TO DIAGNOSE PRETERM LABOR (PTL)**

Preterm labor is evaluated and diagnosed based on a combination of clinical symptoms, documented uterine contractions, cervical changes, and specific biomarker tests.

**1. Clinical Symptoms and Signs**

- Presence of pelvic pressure, backache, and/or vaginal discharge or bleeding (show).
- Palpable uterine contractions evaluated for frequency, duration, and intensity (firmness and tenderness).
- Continuous monitoring using tocodynamometry and CTG to assess uterine contractions and fetal wellbeing.

**2. Specific Diagnostic Criteria** The diagnosis is established when there are **regular uterine contractions** with or without pain ($\ge$ 4 contractions every 20 minutes OR >8 contractions in 60 minutes) **AND** any of the following cervical changes:

- Cervical dilatation $\ge$ 2 cm.
- Cervical effacement $\ge$ 80%.

**3. Ultrasonographic (TVS) Criteria** Transvaginal Sonography (TVS) is highly valuable for assessing cervical length and dilatation:

- **PTL is likely** if the cervical length is $\le$ 2.5 cm (25 mm) along with 80% effacement and dilatation $\ge$ 2 cm.
- **Alternative criteria:** Cervical length $\le$ 20 mm coupled with a positive fetal fibronectin (fFN) test.
- **PTL is unlikely** if the cervical length is $\ge$ 30 mm, regardless of the frequency of uterine contractions.

**4. Biomarkers and Biochemical Tests**

- **Fetal Fibronectin (fFN):** This is a glycoprotein that binds fetal membranes to the decidua. The presence of fFN (>50 ng/mL) in the cervicovaginal discharge between 24 and 34 weeks is a strong predictor of preterm labor. Conversely, a negative fFN test strongly reassures that delivery will not occur within the next 7 days.
- **Other Biomarkers:** Placenta Associated $\alpha$-Macroglobulin-1 (PAMG-1), Insulin Like Growth Factor Binding Protein-1 (IGFBP-1), and $\beta$-hCG.
- **Serum Markers:** Corticotropin-Releasing Hormone (CRH), C-Reactive Protein (CRP), and ALF.
- **Amniocentesis:** May be utilized to evaluate amniotic fluid for fetal lung maturity (L/S ratio) and to rule out intra-amniotic infection/chorioamnionitis.



20. Explain DIPSI guidelines for screening diabetes in Pregnancy.

**DIPSI GUIDELINES FOR SCREENING DIABETES IN PREGNANCY**

**Introduction**

- **DIPSI** stands for the Diabetes in Pregnancy Study Group in India (also referred to as Diabetes in Pregnancy Societies of India).

**Screening and Diagnostic Procedure**

- **Method:** DIPSI recommends a simplified **1-step procedure** for screening and diagnosing Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM).
- **Glucose Load:** The pregnant woman is administered a **75 g oral glucose** load.
- **Patient Status:** The test is performed **without regard to the time of the last meal**, meaning it does not require the patient to be in a fasting state.

**Diagnostic Criteria**

- **Timing of Blood Draw:** Blood is drawn exactly 2 hours after the ingestion of the glucose load.
- **Cut-off Value:** A 2-hour venous plasma glucose value of **more than 140 mg/dL** is diagnosed as Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM).

**Current Status**

- The DIPSI criteria for the diagnosis of GDM currently awaits reconsiderations.

21. How will you differentiate between atonic and traumatic post partum hemorrhage?
**DIFFERENTIATION BETWEEN ATONIC AND TRAUMATIC POSTPARTUM HEMORRHAGE (PPH)**

The most reliable clinical method to differentiate between atonic and traumatic postpartum hemorrhage is the assessment of the state of the uterus by abdominal palpation.

|**Parameters**|**Atonic PPH**|**Traumatic PPH**|
|:--|:--|:--|
|**Incidence**|It is the most common cause, accounting for about 80% of primary PPH cases.|It accounts for about 20% of primary PPH cases.|
|**Feel of the Uterus (Per Abdomen)**|The uterus is found flabby, soft, and lacks tone.|The uterus is found firm, well-contracted, and hard.|
|**Response to Uterine Massage**|The flabby uterus responds to massage by becoming hard and contracted.|The uterus is already hard; massage does not significantly alter its firm state.|
|**Relationship of Bleeding to Uterine Tone**|Bleeding occurs because the torn uterine sinuses cannot be effectively compressed due to imperfect contraction and retraction.|Excessive vaginal bleeding continues as a steady trickle or flow despite the uterus being hard and well-retracted.|
|**Source of Bleeding**|Bleeding originates from the placental implantation site within the uterine cavity.|Bleeding originates from injuries or lacerations in the cervix, vagina, perineum, paraurethral region, or from a ruptured uterus.|
|**Common Predisposing Factors**|Grand multiparity, overdistension of the uterus (multiple pregnancy, hydramnios, big baby), prolonged labor, uterine fibroids, profound anesthesia, and mismanaged third stage of labor.|Operative or instrumental delivery (forceps, ventouse, breech extraction), precipitate labor, episiotomy extensions, and destructive operations.|
|**Diagnostic Confirmation**|Confirmed by palpating a flaccid fundus that regains tone upon massaging and administration of oxytocics.|Confirmed by exploring the uterovaginal canal under good light with a speculum to directly visualize the bleeding lacerations or tears.|

**Important Clinical Note:** Both atonic and traumatic causes of hemorrhage can coexist in the same patient. Even if the primary cause of bleeding is traumatic (e.g., from an injured area), the subsequent massive blood loss and resulting state of shock can eventually cause the uterus to lose its tone and become atonic. Therefore, if bleeding continues even after the uterus has been made hard by massage, the presence of a genital tract injury must be strongly suspected and systematically excluded.



22. What is modified Bishops score?

**MODIFIED BISHOP'S PREINDUCTION CERVICAL SCORING SYSTEM**

**Introduction and Purpose** The Bishop's score (developed by Edward Harry Bishop) is a clinical preinduction cervical scoring system used to assess the readiness ("ripeness") of the cervix for the induction of labor. It is a highly reliable predictor of the success of labor induction.

**The Modification (1991)** The original Bishop's score evaluated five parameters, including cervical effacement measured in percentages (0-30% for score 0; 40-50% for score 1; 60-70% for score 2; and $\ge$80% for score 3). The **modified Bishop's score (1991)** replaces the parameter of _cervical effacement (%)_ with the measurement of _cervical length in centimeters (cm)_.

**Parameters and Scoring** The modified system evaluates five specific pelvic and cervical parameters. Each parameter is assigned a score ranging from 0 to 3. The maximum possible total score is 13.

|**Parameters**|**Score 0**|**Score 1**|**Score 2**|**Score 3**|
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
|**Cervical Dilatation (cm)**|Closed|1 - 2|3 - 4|5+|
|**Cervical Length (cm)** _(Modified)_|> 4|2 - 4|1 - 2|< 1|
|**Cervical Consistency**|Firm|Medium|Soft|-|
|**Cervical Position**|Posterior|Midline|Anterior|-|
|**Station of Head**|-3|-2|-1, 0|+1, +2|

_Note: In the original score, effacement % was used instead of Cervical length in cm._

**Clinical Interpretation and Management**

- **Favorable Score (6 to 13):** A score of 6 or more indicates a favorable or "ripe" cervix. This is a positive predictive factor for successful induction of labor. When the preinduction score is favorable, methods such as low rupture of membranes (amniotomy) and oxytocin infusion are highly effective.
- **Unfavorable Score (0 to 5):** A score of 5 or less indicates an unfavorable or "unripe" cervix. When the score is unfavorable (e.g., long, firm cervix with a closed os), amniotomy cannot be employed. In such cases, cervical ripening is required prior to induction. Prostaglandins (such as PGE2 gel/tablet or Misoprostol/PGE1) have a distinct advantage over oxytocin and are the preferred first-line agents to improve the cervical score.
