**DEFINITION OF INFERTILITY** Infertility is defined as the inability to conceive within one or more years of regular unprotected coitus.

- **Primary infertility:** Denotes patients who have never conceived.
- **Secondary infertility:** Indicates previous pregnancy but failure to conceive subsequently.

**OVARIAN CAUSES OF INFERTILITY** Ovarian factors (ovulatory dysfunctions) are responsible for 30–40% of female infertility cases. The primary ovarian causes include:

**1. Anovulation or Oligo-ovulation:** Absence or infrequent ovulation, primarily due to a disturbed hypothalamo-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis, thyroid, or adrenal dysfunction. Important conditions include:

- **Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome (PCOS):** The most common endocrine disorder causing anovulation due to hyperandrogenism and altered LH:FSH ratio.
- **Hypogonadotropic Hypogonadism (WHO Group I):** Includes stress-related amenorrhea, Kallmann’s syndrome, and anorexia nervosa.
- **Hyperprolactinemia:** Elevated prolactin disrupts GnRH pulsatility and inhibits gonadotropin secretion.

**2. Decreased Ovarian Reserve / Premature Ovarian Insufficiency (POI):** Accelerated depletion or absence of ovarian follicles before age 40, leading to hypergonadotropic hypogonadism. Causes include chromosomal anomalies (Turner's syndrome), autoimmune disorders, or iatrogenic factors (radiation, chemotherapy).

**3. Resistant Ovarian Syndrome (Savage’s Syndrome):** Follicles are present in the ovary, but the FSH receptors are either absent or resistant to gonadotropins.

**4. Luteal Phase Defect (LPD):** Inadequate growth and function of the corpus luteum, leading to insufficient progesterone secretion and a shortened lifespan of the corpus luteum (<10 days). This results in an inadequate secretory endometrium that hinders implantation. Causes include subclinical hypothyroidism, hyperprolactinemia, pelvic endometriosis, and older age.

**5. Luteinized Unruptured Follicle (LUF) Syndrome (Trapped Ovum):** The ovum is trapped inside the follicle, which becomes luteinized without rupturing. It is often associated with pelvic endometriosis or hyperprolactinemia.

---

**DIAGNOSIS OF OVARIAN INFERTILITY**

The diagnostic workup is aimed at confirming ovulation, assessing ovarian reserve, and identifying specific ovulatory defects.

**A. Diagnosis of Ovulation** **1. Indirect (Presumptive) Evidences:**

- **Menstrual History:** Regular normal menstrual loss, midcycle pain (Mittelschmerz), or premenstrual syndrome (PMS) strongly suggest ovulatory cycles.
- **Basal Body Temperature (BBT):** A biphasic pattern with a temperature rise of 0.5–1°F (0.2–0.5°C) following ovulation due to the thermogenic effect of progesterone.
- **Cervical Mucus Study:** Disappearance of the typical 'fern pattern' and loss of spinnbarkeit (stretchability) in the second half of the cycle due to progesterone.
- **Vaginal Cytology:** Shift of the Maturation Index (MI) to the left.
- **Serum Progesterone:** Evaluated on Day 8 and Day 21 of a 28-day cycle. A level >6 ng/mL on Day 21 is suggestive of ovulation.
- **Serum LH and Urinary LH:** Daily midcycle estimation detects the LH surge. Ovulation typically occurs 34–36 hours after the onset of the surge and 10–12 hours after the LH peak. Urinary LH kits predict ovulation within 14–24 hours of color change.
- **Endometrial Biopsy (D21–D23):** Demonstrates secretory changes. Subnuclear vacuolation of the glandular epithelium is the earliest histological evidence, appearing 36–48 hours after ovulation.
- **Transvaginal Sonography (TVS):** Serial midcycle folliculometry. A mature Graafian follicle measures 18–20 mm. Post-ovulatory signs include a collapsed follicle and fluid in the Pouch of Douglas (POD).

**2. Direct Evidence:**

- **Laparoscopy:** Visualization of a recent corpus luteum or detection of an ovum in aspirated POD fluid.

**3. Conclusive Evidence:**

- **Pregnancy**.

**B. Diagnosis of Specific Ovarian Defects**

- **Decreased Ovarian Reserve:** Evaluated on Day 3 of the cycle. Indicators of poor reserve include: Serum FSH >10–15 IU/L, Estradiol >60–80 pg/mL, Serum Anti-Müllerian Hormone (AMH) <0.5 ng/mL, and Antral Follicle Count (AFC) <10 on TVS. Clomiphene citrate challenge test (CCCT) may also show elevated FSH.
- **Luteal Phase Defect (LPD):** Diagnosed by a slow rise in BBT sustaining for <10 days, serum progesterone <10 ng/mL on the 8th postovulatory day, and an endometrial biopsy showing a lag of >3 days (must be proved in two consecutive cycles).
- **LUF Syndrome:** Presumptive signs of ovulation are present, but TVS shows persistence of an echo-free dominant follicle >36 hours post-LH peak, and laparoscopy shows no ovulation stigma.

**MANAGEMENT OF OVARIAN INFERTILITY**

The cornerstone of managing ovarian infertility is the induction of ovulation and correction of underlying endocrinopathies.

**1. General Measures & Adjuvant Therapy:**

- **Weight Reduction:** Essential in obese patients (PCOS), as weight loss of 2–5% improves insulin resistance, hyperandrogenemia, and ovulatory rates.
- **Insulin Sensitizers (Metformin):** Indicated in PCOS women with BMI >25 kg/m² and hyperinsulinemia. It significantly increases ovulation rates when combined with clomiphene.
- **Thyroxine:** For women with overt or subclinical hypothyroidism.
- **Dopamine Agonists (Bromocriptine, Cabergoline):** For hyperprolactinemia, restoring cyclic menses and ovulation in the majority of cases.
- **Corticosteroids (Dexamethasone):** 0.5 mg daily for 10 days to suppress pre-existing or induced elevated androgens.

**2. Medical Induction of Ovulation (Pharmacotherapy):**

- **Clomiphene Citrate (CC):** The first-line drug for normogonadotropic-normoprolactinemic anovulation and PCOS. Dose: 50 mg daily for 5 days (from Day 2 or 5). Blocks hypothalamic estrogen receptors, increasing GnRH and gonadotropin pulse amplitude.
- **Letrozole:** An aromatase inhibitor that prevents E2 production, removing negative feedback and increasing FSH. Given as 2.5–5 mg daily from Day 3 to 7. Highly effective in clomiphene-resistant PCOS and has fewer anti-estrogenic side effects on the endometrium.
- **Gonadotropins (hMG, uFSH, rFSH):** Indicated for hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, clomiphene-resistant cases, and unexplained infertility. Administered via step-up or step-down regimens. Requires rigorous monitoring with TVS folliculometry and serum estradiol to prevent Ovarian Hyperstimulation Syndrome (OHSS).
- **hCG Trigger:** 5000–10,000 IU of hCG is given IM to trigger ovulation when the leading follicle reaches 18–20 mm.
- **GnRH Therapy:** Pulsatile IV or SC administration via a pump for hypothalamic amenorrhea or isolated gonadotropin deficiency (Kallmann's syndrome).

**3. Surgical Management:**

- **Laparoscopic Ovarian Drilling (LOD) / Laser Vaporization:** Indicated for clomiphene-resistant, hyperandrogenic PCOS women. 4–6 punctures (2–4 mm deep) are made in each ovary using diathermy or laser. Reduces intraovarian androgens and restores spontaneous ovulation.
- **Bariatric Surgery:** Recommended for morbidly obese anovulatory women to restore normal HPO axis function.

**4. Management of Specific Conditions:**

- **Luteal Phase Defect (LPD):** Natural micronized progesterone (100 mg vaginal suppositories thrice daily) starting from the day of ovulation. hCG injections or clomiphene (to improve primary folliculogenesis) can also be used.
- **Luteinized Unruptured Follicle (LUF):** Treated with optimally timed IM hCG (5000–10,000 IU) or ovulation-inducing drugs followed by hCG. Bromocriptine is used if hyperprolactinemia is present.
- **Premature Ovarian Insufficiency (POI) / Decreased Reserve:** Fertility potential is extremely low. The definitive option for these women is Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART) via **In Vitro Fertilization (IVF) with donor oocytes** and exogenous hormonal support.

**DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS OF LOWER ABDOMINAL MASS / PELVIC LUMP**

The differential diagnosis for an abdominopelvic lump varies significantly with the patient's age and reproductive status. In the childbearing period, amenorrhea with a lower abdominal lump must be provisionally diagnosed as pregnancy until proven otherwise.

The common causes classified by age group are:

**1. Toddlers (< 5 years)**

- Ovarian tumor
- Mucocolpos
- Full bladder

**2. 5 Years of Age to Puberty**

- Ovarian tumor
- Full bladder
- Hematocolpos

**3. Childbearing Period**

- **Physiological:** Pregnancy, Full urinary bladder.
- **Uterine:** Fibroid uterus, Adenomyosis.
- **Ovarian/Tubal:** Ovarian tumor (functional or neoplastic), Chocolate cyst (endometrioma), Tubo-ovarian (TO) mass.
- **Infective/Inflammatory:** Pelvic abscess, Encysted peritonitis, Pelvic hematocele.
- **Psychosomatic:** Pseudocyesis.
- **Other Adnexal Masses:** Ectopic pregnancy, Hydrosalpinx, Appendicular mass, Diverticulitis, Pelvic kidney.

**4. Postmenopausal**

- Ovarian tumor (genital malignancy must be excluded).
- Pyometra.
- Sarcoma of the uterus.

**MANAGEMENT OF A 26-YEAR-OLD NULLIPAROUS WOMAN WITH AN 18-WEEK FIBROID UTERUS**

**A. Case Analysis & Principles of Management**

- **Patient Profile:** 26 years old (young), Nulliparous (strong desire for future fertility/childbearing).
- **Clinical Finding:** Fibroid uterus corresponding to 18 weeks size (large myoma, >12 weeks size).
- **Treatment of Choice:** **Myomectomy** (enucleation of myomata leaving behind a potentially functioning organ capable of future reproduction).
- **Indication for Surgery:** Size >12 weeks in a woman desirous of having a baby is a definitive indication for myomectomy, even if asymptomatic.

**B. Preoperative Evaluation & Work-up**

- **Confirmation of Diagnosis & Mapping:** Transvaginal and abdominal ultrasonography (USG) or Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) to document the location, size, and number of leiomyomas, and to exclude pregnancy.
- **Fertility Assessment (Prerequisites):**
    - Hysteroscopy or Hysterosalpingography (HSG) to exclude any submucous fibroid, polyp, or concurrent tubal block.
    - Examination of the husband (semen analysis) from a fertility point of view.
- **Cervical Cytology/Endometrial Biopsy:** To rule out associated cervical or endometrial pathology.

**C. Preoperative Medical Therapy**

- **GnRH Agonists (e.g., Goserelin, Leuprorelin):** Administered for 3 months prior to surgery.
- **Benefits:** Reduces the size (up to 50%) and vascularity of the tumor, making the surgery technically easier and minimizing intraoperative blood loss. It also improves preoperative hemoglobin levels by inducing amenorrhea.

**D. Definitive Surgical Management (Myomectomy)**

- **Route:** Laparotomy (Abdominal Myomectomy) is generally preferred for a markedly enlarged (18 weeks) uterus, though advanced laparoscopic myomectomy is an option for skilled surgeons if criteria are met.
- **Counseling:** The patient must be counseled that myomectomy is a risky operation, and consent for a possible emergency hysterectomy (life-saving) must be obtained in case of uncontrollable hemorrhage.
- **Operative Steps:**
    1. **Incision:** A single anterior midline (linear or elliptical) incision on the uterine wall is preferred to avoid injury to the tubes/ovaries and to prevent posterior adhesions.
    2. **Enucleation:** The incision is deepened through the myometrium and pseudocapsule. The myoma is grasped with a single-toothed vulsellum and intracapsular enucleation is done by blunt and sharp dissection.
    3. **Obliteration of Dead Space:** The myoma bed (deep space) must be obliterated completely using interrupted mattress or figure-of-eight sutures (Vicryl 1-0). Multiple layers ("tire stitch") may be required for a large 18-week fibroid bed.
    4. **Serosal Closure:** The serous coat is approximated with thin delayed absorbable sutures (4-0). Adhesion barriers (e.g., oxidized cellulose/Interceed) can be placed over the incision.

**E. Intraoperative Measures to Minimize Blood Loss** Due to the massive size (18 weeks), blood loss can be profuse. The following measures are employed:

- **Vasoconstrictive Agents:** Injection of diluted synthetic Vasopressin (20 U diluted in 20-100 mL of normal saline) between the myoma capsule and myometrium.
- **Mechanical Tourniquets/Clamps:** Application of a Bonney's myomectomy clamp or a rubber tourniquet around the lower uterine segment/infundibulopelvic ligaments to temporarily curtail uterine blood supply.

**F. Postoperative Prognosis & Counseling**

- **Fertility Outcome:** The pregnancy rate following myomectomy is approximately 40-60%.
- **Recurrence:** The patient must be informed about a 30-50% risk of recurrence of fibroids in the future.
- **Mode of Delivery:** Future pregnancies must have mandatory hospital monitoring. Due to the extensive myometrial dissection required for an 18-week fibroid, elective Cesarean section is often recommended to avoid the risk of uterine scar rupture during labor.



3.	Management of secondary amenorrhoea.

**DEFINITION**
Leukorrhea is strictly defined as an excessive normal vaginal discharge. It is considered leukorrhea when it fulfills the following criteria:
*   The excess secretion causes persistent vulvar moistness or staining of the undergarments (which turns brownish-yellow on drying), or necessitates the use of a vulvar pad.
*   It is nonpurulent and nonoffensive.
*   It is nonirritant and never causes pruritus.

**CLINICAL CHARACTERISTICS**
*   **Physical:** The discharge is watery, white in color, and nonodorous, with a pH of around 4.0.
*   **Microscopic:** It contains squamous epithelial cells, a few bacteria (predominantly Lactobacilli or Doderlein bacilli), a few gram-negative bacteria, and anaerobes. Crucially, it is devoid of white or red blood cells.

**ETIOLOGY (CAUSES)**
Normal vaginal secretion increases predominantly in conditions where estrogen levels are high or there is pelvic congestion.

**1. Physiologic Excess**
*   **Puberty:** Increased levels of endogenous estrogen lead to a marked overgrowth of the endocervical epithelium. This may encroach onto the ectocervix, producing congenital ectopy (erosion) and resulting in increased secretion.
*   **Menstrual Cycle:** 
    *   *Around ovulation:* A peak rise in estrogen causes an increase in the secretory activity of the cervical glands.
    *   *Premenstrual phase:* Pelvic congestion occurs along with increased mucus secretion from the hypertrophied endometrial glands.
*   **Pregnancy:** Hyperestrinism combined with increased vascularity leads to an increase in both vaginal transudate and cervical gland secretion.
*   **Sexual Excitement:** Produces abundant secretion from the Bartholin’s glands.

**2. Cervical Causes (Non-infective)**
Noninfective cervical lesions can produce excessive secretion that pours out of the vulva. These include cervical ectopy, chronic cervicitis, mucous polyps, and ectropion (where cervical glands are exposed to the vagina).

**3. Vaginal Causes**
Increased vaginal transudation occurs in states of increased pelvic congestion. Common conditions include uterine prolapse, acquired retroverted uterus, chronic pelvic inflammation, the use of oral contraceptive ‘pills’, and vaginal adenosis.

**4. Ill Health**
General ill health is a significant cause as it produces excessive exfoliation of the superficial vaginal cells.

**DIAGNOSIS AND INVESTIGATIONS**
Leukorrhea is primarily a diagnosis of exclusion to rule out infective or neoplastic causes.
*   **History:** Detailed inquiry regarding the duration of the discharge, prior episodes, contraceptive practices, and any associated symptoms like dysuria, dyspareunia, or pelvic pain.
*   **Examination:** 
    *   *General & Abdominal:* Assessment of general health and checking for any abdominal mass or tenderness.
    *   *Pelvic:* Vulvar inspection, speculum examination to detect cervical or vaginal pathology, and bimanual examination to check for adnexal mass, tenderness, or foreign bodies.
*   **Investigations:** Microbiological study of the discharge, Pap smear, urine routine examination and culture sensitivity (RE and CS), and PCR/NAAT tests to rule out specific pathogens.

**MANAGEMENT**
*   **General Measures:** The foundation of treatment involves the improvement of general health, maintaining meticulous local hygiene, and providing a sympathetic attitude and assurance to the patient, as physiologic leukorrhea often subsides on its own.
*   **Medication Adjustments:** In oral contraceptive pill users, the pill may be temporarily stopped if the discharge is highly annoying.
*   **Surgical Management:** Cervical factors (like ectopy) may require surgical interventions such as electrocautery, cryosurgery, or trachelorrhaphy.
*   **Targeted Therapy:** Any underlying pelvic lesions producing the discharge or any incidentally discovered specific infections require appropriate targeted medical or surgical therapy.


4. Write short notes on Imperforate hymen.
**IMPERFORATE HYMEN**

**Definition and Etiopathogenesis:**

- Imperforate hymen is the most common congenital cause of cryptomenorrhea (hidden menstruation).
- **Embryological Basis:** It is caused by the failure of disintegration of the central cells of the Müllerian eminence that projects into the urogenital sinus.
- **Incidence:** The overall incidence is approximately 1 in 1,000 live-born females.

**Pathophysiology:**

- The condition almost always remains unnoticed until the girl reaches puberty (typically 13–16 years of age).
- Because the uterus functions normally, shed menstrual blood becomes pent up inside the vagina behind the imperforate hymen (cryptomenorrhea).
- **Sequence of blood accumulation:**
    1. **Hematocolpos:** The accumulated blood first progressively distends the vagina.
    2. **Hematometra:** The uterus is subsequently involved, and the uterine cavity becomes dilated.
    3. **Hematosalpinx:** In late and neglected cases, the fallopian tubes become distended with blood after the fimbrial ends are sealed by adhesions.
- **Pressure Effects:** Hematocolpos produces marked elongation of the urethra, which commonly leads to retention of urine.
- **In Newborns:** Accumulated mucus behind the imperforate membrane, caused by maternal estrogen stimulation, can result in the clinical entity of mucocolpos (usually within one week of birth).

**Clinical Features:**

- **Symptoms:**
    - The patient is usually a young girl aged 13–16 years presenting with primary amenorrhea.
    - She complains of periodic lower abdominal pain, which may eventually become continuous.
    - Urinary symptoms such as dysuria, frequency, or acute retention of urine (retention is often the main presenting feature).
- **Signs:**
    - **Abdominal examination:** Reveals a uniform, globular suprapubic swelling. This could be the distended uterus or a full bladder (prior catheterization differentiates and reveals the true state).
    - **Vulvar inspection:** Pathognomonic finding of a tense, bulging membrane of bluish coloration at the introitus.
    - **Rectal examination:** Confirms the fullness of the distended vagina and the uterine mass.

**Investigations:**

- **Ultrasonography (USG):** Readily confirms the diagnosis by visualizing hematometra and hematocolpos.

**Management:**

- **Surgical Treatment:** The definitive treatment is a **cruciate incision** made into the hymen to drain the accumulated blood.
- **Operative details:**
    - The quadrants of the hymen are partially excised, ensuring the cuts are not made too close to the vaginal mucosa.
    - The dark, tarry-colored blood must be allowed to escape spontaneously.
- **Strict Intraoperative Precautions:**
    - **No pressure** should be applied from above the abdomen.
    - **No internal (vaginal) examination** should be performed at the time of surgery to prevent introducing ascending infection.
- **Postoperative Care:**
    - The patient is put to bed with the head end raised to facilitate continuous gravitational drainage.
    - Systemic antibiotics must be administered to prevent secondary infection.
    - If there is any residual pelvic pathology, it should only be assessed by internal examination after the completion of her next normal menstrual period.


5. Write short note on Turners syndrome.
**TURNER'S SYNDROME**

**Definition and Genetics** Turner's syndrome is the most common variety of gonadal dysgenesis and is classified as a sex chromosome Disorder of Sexual Development (DSD).

- **Karyotype:** The most common karyotype is 45, XO (accounting for >50% of cases), resulting from the nondisjunction of the maternal chromosome. Mosaic patterns, such as 46, XX/45, XO, are also seen.
- **Sex Chromatin:** Sex chromatin (Barr body) study is negative in classical 45, XO cases.

**Pathology**

- **Gonads:** The germ cells either fail to develop or fail to migrate to the gonadal ridge. Consequently, the gonads are reduced to bilateral white "streaks" of fibrous tissue devoid of follicles or germ cells, possessing no potential to produce hormones. This results in chromosomally incompetent ovarian failure (hypergonadotropic hypogonadism).
- **Müllerian Structures:** The vagina, uterus, and fallopian tubes are present but remain infantile. The uterus is, however, highly responsive to exogenous estrogen.

**Clinical Features** Patients are phenotypically female but typically present with:

- **Gynecological:** Primary amenorrhea with absent or delayed development of secondary sexual characteristics (e.g., poor breast development).
- **Somatic Features:**
    - Short stature (height < 150 cm).
    - Webbing of the neck with a low hairline on the neck.
    - Broad, shield-like chest with widely spaced nipples.
    - Cubitus valgus (increased carrying angle at the elbow).
    - Micrognathia and a high arched palate.
    - Short fourth metatarsals, skin nevi, and lymphedema.
    - Mental retardation may occasionally be present.

**Associated Anomalies**

- **Cardiovascular:** Coarctation of the aorta is commonly associated.
- **Renal:** Horseshoe kidneys and other renal anomalies.
- **Autoimmune Disorders:** High association with multiglandular autoimmune disorders like Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, Addison’s disease, hypothyroidism, and diabetes.

**Investigations**

- **Karyotyping:** Confirms the 45, XO chromosomal pattern.
- **Hormone Profile:** Serum Estradiol (E2) levels are characteristically very low. Serum gonadotropins (FSH and LH) are markedly elevated due to the lack of negative feedback from the ovaries.
- **Autoantibody Screening:** Autoantibodies (e.g., antithyroid) may be present and should be checked.
- **Laparoscopy:** Visualizes the characteristic bilateral streak gonads.

**Mosaic Turner's Syndrome (46, XX/45, XO)** In the mosaic variety, the classic somatic features of Turner's syndrome may be absent, and the individual may be of average height. The streak ovaries may contain a few follicles. These patients can occasionally menstruate for a few cycles until the follicles deplete, and spontaneous pregnancy has rarely been recorded.

**Management**

- **Somatic Growth:** Exogenous Growth Hormone (GH) therapy can be administered to increase the patient's adult height.
- **Hormone Replacement Therapy (HRT):** To induce the development of secondary sexual characteristics, breasts, and to bring about withdrawal bleeding, HRT is initiated after 13 years of age. A standard regimen involves low-dose estrogen (e.g., conjugated equine estrogen 0.625 mg) taken orally daily for 25 days, with a progestin (e.g., medroxyprogesterone acetate 5 mg) added daily for the last 10 days of the cycle.
- **Fertility:** Achieving pregnancy is possible through Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART), specifically In Vitro Fertilization (IVF) utilizing donor oocytes, since the patient's uterus can support a pregnancy when primed with exogenous hormones.
- **Prophylactic Gonadectomy:** If karyotyping reveals the presence of a 'Y' chromosome (e.g., in mosaic variants), prophylactic surgical removal of the streak gonads (gonadectomy) is strictly indicated to prevent the development of malignant gonadal tumors such as dysgerminoma, seminoma, or gonadoblastoma.


6. What are causes and management of puberty menorrhagia?
**PUBERTY MENORRHAGIA: CAUSES AND MANAGEMENT**

Menstrual abnormalities are common in adolescents. Initially, the periods may be heavy, irregular, or scanty, but eventually, the majority of these teenage girls establish a normal cycle. Puberty menorrhagia requires careful evaluation, especially if the menstrual interval is <22 days or >44 days, lasts longer than one week, or if the bleeding is so heavy that the patient develops anemia.

**CAUSES OF PUBERTY MENORRHAGIA** The causes of puberty menorrhagia can be broadly classified into functional, endocrinal, hematological, and structural/organic pathologies:

- **Dysfunctional Uterine Bleeding (DUB) (95% of cases):** This is the most common cause. It is primarily due to anovulatory cycles, which lead to unopposed estrogen stimulation. This results in endometrial hyperplasia, ultimately causing prolonged and heavy menstrual periods.
- **Endocrine Dysfunction:** Conditions include Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS), and thyroid dysfunctions such as hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism.
- **Hematological Disorders:** Bleeding diatheses such as Idiopathic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (ITP), Von Willebrand’s disease, and Leukemia.
- **Pelvic Tumors:** Neoplasms including fibroid uterus, sarcoma botryoides, and estrogen-producing ovarian tumors.
- **Pregnancy Complications:** Such as abortion, must always be kept in mind and excluded.

---

**INVESTIGATIONS AND DIAGNOSTIC WORKUP** Diagnosis is established by meticulous history taking and thorough clinical examination. If heavy bleeding continues, the patient should be admitted for a comprehensive workup:

- **Hematological Tests:** Estimation of Hemoglobin (Hb%), peripheral blood film, bleeding time, clotting time, and platelet count.
- **Coagulation Profile:** Prothrombin time (PT), partial thromboplastin time (PTT), Factor VIII, and von Willebrand factor (VWF) assays.
- **Endocrinal Tests:** Thyroid profile (TSH, T3, T4).
- **Imaging:** Pelvic ultrasonography or MRI to rule out pelvic pathology such as tumors and polyps.
- **Surgical Evaluation:** Examination Under Anesthesia (EUA) and uterine curettage may be necessary to exclude pregnancy complications and to obtain endometrial tissue for histopathological study.

---

**MANAGEMENT OF PUBERTY MENORRHAGIA**

The management protocol depends on the severity of the bleeding and the underlying etiology.

**1. General and Supportive Measures:**

- **Rest and Reassurance:** The young girl requires adequate explanation, reassurance, and psychological support. Rest is advised.
- **Correction of Anemia:** Hematinics (iron supplements) are prescribed. In cases of severe acute blood loss or shock, blood transfusion is administered.

**2. Management of Primary DUB (Majority of cases):** When secondary causes are ruled out, the bleeding is managed medically to arrest the acute episode and regulate the cycle.

- **Progestin Therapy (First-line):** Progestogens such as medroxyprogesterone acetate (10–20 mg/day) or norethisterone 5 mg thrice daily are given. This usually controls the bleeding within 3–7 days. The medication is then continued for 21 days. If responsive, the treatment is continued for 2–3 cycles, after which normal menstrual cycles typically resume.
- **Emergency Estrogen Therapy:** In refractory cases or emergency situations with unremitting heavy bleeding, conjugated equine estrogen is administered intravenously at a dose of 20–40 mg every 6–8 hours. Once the bleeding is successfully controlled, the therapy is replaced with combined oral contraceptive pills containing 50 µg of estrogen.
- **GnRH Analogs:** These can be used for short-term management.
- **Surgical Intervention:** If the patient remains unresponsive to aggressive medical therapy (including IV estrogens), an Examination Under Anesthesia (EUA) and Dilatation & Curettage (D&C) is performed, and an endometrial biopsy is taken.

**3. Management of Secondary Causes:** If investigations reveal an underlying organic or systemic cause, appropriate specific therapy must be instituted. This includes specific management for thyroid dysfunction, immune thrombocytopenic purpura, leukemia, Von Willebrand's disease, anatomical disorders, neoplasms, or pregnancy complications. Regular menstrual cycles are generally established once the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis fully matures.


7. Write short notes on medical management of endometriosis.

**MEDICAL MANAGEMENT OF ENDOMETRIOSIS**

**Introduction and Principles of Therapy** Endometriosis is an estrogen-dependent disease that typically regresses with natural or surgical menopause. The primary aim of medical management is to induce atrophy of the endometriotic implants, providing symptom relief. Hormonal treatment is considered suppressive rather than curative, as the recurrence rate of endometriosis following medical therapy is high (5–15% in the first year, rising to 40–50% within 5 years).

**Mechanism of Action** The hormonal treatments induce endometrial atrophy through three main mechanisms:

- **Pseudopregnancy:** Induced by combined oral contraceptive pills.
- **Pseudomenopause:** Induced by Danazol and Gestrinone.
- **Medical Oophorectomy:** Induced by Gonadotropin-Releasing Hormone (GnRH) agonists. All these drugs are typically administered continuously to produce a state of amenorrhea, and doses require individualization.

**Classes of Drugs Used**

**1. Nonsteroidal Anti-inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs) & Expectant Management**

- NSAIDs (e.g., Ibuprofen 800–1200 mg/day or Mefenamic acid 150–600 mg/day) are used empirically to relieve pelvic pain and dysmenorrhea, especially in mild cases.

**2. Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs)**

- Low-dose combined estrogen-progestogen pills prescribed continuously or cyclically for 6–12 months cause endometrial decidualization and atrophy.
- They are highly effective in relieving dysmenorrhea and improve symptoms in about 80% of women.

**3. Progestogens**

- These agents cause decidualization followed by atrophy of the endometrium. High doses suppress ovulation and induce amenorrhea.
- **Oral formulations:** Medroxyprogesterone acetate (10 mg thrice daily), Dydrogesterone (10–20 mg daily), Norethisterone (10–30 mg daily), or Dienogest (2 mg daily) given for 3–9 months.
- **Injectable:** Depot medroxyprogesterone acetate 150 mg every 3 months.
- **Levonorgestrel-releasing IUCD (LNG-IUS):** Significantly reduces pelvic pain, dyspareunia, and menorrhagia, and is especially useful for rectovaginal endometriosis.

**4. Danazol**

- An isoxazole derivative that acts as an androgen agonist and strictly an antigonadotropin, producing a 'pseudomenopause' state.
- **Dose:** 400–600 mg orally in divided doses for 6–9 months, starting from day 5 of the cycle.
- While it causes resolution of lesions in about 80% of cases, it has high androgenic side effects (e.g., hirsutism, weight gain, voice changes) which can be intolerable. Barrier contraception must be used to prevent virilization of a female fetus in case of accidental pregnancy.

**5. Gestrinone**

- A 19-nortestosterone derivative that acts as an androgen agonist and a progesterone agonist-antagonist.
- **Dose:** 1.25 or 2.5 mg twice a week for 6–9 months. It produces 'pseudomenopause' and has similar but better-tolerated side effects compared to Danazol.

**6. Gonadotropin-Releasing Hormone (GnRH) Analogs**

- **GnRH Agonists (e.g., Leuprolide, Goserelin, Triptorelin):** Used continuously, they cause a profound hypoestrogenic state ('medical oophorectomy') and amenorrhea. They maintain serum estrogen at a reduced level (30–45 pg/mL) to suppress the growth of endometriosis. They provide the quickest relief of symptoms and reduction in lesion volume.
- **Add-back therapy:** To combat severe hypoestrogenic side effects (vasomotor symptoms, vaginal atrophy, and bone demineralization), low-dose estrogen and progestin are co-administered during long-term therapy (>6 months).
- **GnRH Antagonists (e.g., Elagolix):** Administered orally (150–250 mg/day). They are as effective as Leuprolide acetate but do not cause an initial 'flare' effect, and result in less bone demineralization and hypoestrogenic symptoms.

**7. Aromatase Inhibitors**

- Drugs like Letrozole (2.5 mg) and Anastrozole (1 mg) inhibit the aromatase enzyme, directly reducing estrogen levels in the blood as well as within the endometriotic tissues themselves. This significantly reduces pain symptoms.

**8. Other / Newer Medications**

- **Progesterone antagonists:** Mifepristone 50 mg/day for 6 months reduces pelvic pain and disease spread.
- **Selective progesterone-receptor modulators (SPRMs):** Asoprisnil induces endometrial atrophy and amenorrhea.
- **Statins and others:** Simvastatin (inhibits cell proliferation) and Rosiglitazone (reduces pain symptoms).

**Role of Medical Therapy as an Adjunct to Surgery**

- **Preoperative:** Hormonal therapy given for 3–6 months prior to surgery aims to reduce the size and vascularity of the endometriotic lesions, thereby facilitating surgical excision.
- **Postoperative:** Medical therapy for 3–6 months after surgery helps to destroy residual lesions and control pain, though it does not improve fertility outcomes.

8. How will you manage Pelvic Tuberculosis?
**MANAGEMENT OF PELVIC (GENITAL) TUBERCULOSIS**

The management of pelvic tuberculosis requires a comprehensive approach, primarily relying on multidrug antitubercular chemotherapy, with surgery reserved for specific unresponsive cases.

**1. GENERAL MANAGEMENT**

- **Hospitalization:** Pelvic tuberculosis per se does not require hospitalization or strict bed rest unless there is an acute exacerbation or active pulmonary tuberculosis is present.
- **Supportive Care:** Due attention is paid to improving the patient's body resistance, providing an adequate diet, and correcting anemia.
- **Prevention of Transmission:** Until the infection is fully controlled, the husband must use a condom during intercourse to prevent the possibility of contracting urogenital tuberculosis.

**2. MEDICAL MANAGEMENT (CHEMOTHERAPY)** Antitubercular chemotherapy is the treatment of first choice. Treatment should be started without waiting for culture results if clinical features and histology are consistent with tuberculosis, and must be continued even if initial culture results are negative.

According to the Revised National TB Control Program (RNTCP, Government of India) and WHO National Strategic Plan, regimens are classified as:

- **New Cases:**
    - _Initial Phase (Bactericidal):_ 4 drugs daily for 2 months (2HRZE) – Isoniazid (H), Rifampicin (R), Pyrazinamide (Z), and Ethambutol (E). This reduces the bacterial population and prevents drug resistance.
    - _Continuation Phase:_ 3 drugs daily for 4 months (4HRE) – Isoniazid, Rifampicin, and Ethambutol,.
- **Previously Treated Cases (Relapse/Failure):**
    - _Initial phase:_ 4 drugs daily for 2 months (2HRZE).
    - _Continuation phase:_ 3 drugs daily for 4 months (4HRE),.
- **Multidrug-Resistant (MDR) Tuberculosis:**
    - Defined as infection resistant to two or more agents including Isoniazid.
    - Treated with a 6-drug regimen: Kanamycin (IM), Ofloxacin (PO), Ethionamide (PO), Pyrazinamide (PO), Ethambutol (PO), and Cycloserine (PO).
    - Duration: Initial phase for 6–9 months and continuation phase for 18 months.
- **Extremely Drug-Resistant (XDR) Cases:** Treated with combinations including Capreomycin, Moxifloxacin, Ethionamide, Cycloserine, Linezolid, or Clofazimine for 24–30 months.
- **GTB with HIV positive:** Options include completing TB treatment then starting ARV, or treating TB (with Rifampicin) + Efavirenz + 2 NRTIs.

**Follow-up of Medical Therapy:**

- After about a year of treatment, a diagnostic endometrial curettage is performed.
- If histology and/or bacteriology are positive, treatment is continued. If negative, the endometrium is re-examined at 6-month intervals.
- A patient is considered cured if at least two consecutive reports (including histological and bacteriological examinations) are negative,.

**3. SURGICAL MANAGEMENT** Surgery is rarely the primary mode of therapy and is strictly reserved for specific indications.

- **Indications for Surgery:**
    - Unresponsiveness of the disease despite adequate antitubercular chemotherapy.
    - Tubercular pyosalpinx, ovarian abscess, or pyometra.
    - Persistent menorrhagia and/or chronic pelvic pain causing deteriorating health status.
- **Contraindications for Surgery:**
    - Presence of active tuberculosis in an extragenital site.
    - Favorable response to antimicrobial therapy with diminishing size of the pelvic mass.
    - Accidental discovery of a tubercular tubo-ovarian mass on laparotomy in a young patient (abdomen is closed after taking a tissue biopsy).
- **Surgical Precautions:** Full-dosage antitubercular drug therapy must be instituted for at least 6 weeks prior to surgery and continued postoperatively.
- **Type of Surgery:**
    - The ideal surgery is **Total Abdominal Hysterectomy with Bilateral Salpingo-oophorectomy (TAH + BSO)**.
    - In young women, at least one apparently healthy ovary should be preserved.
    - Isolated excision of a tubo-ovarian mass, drainage of pyometra, or fistula repair may be done in highly selected cases.

**4. MANAGEMENT OF INFERTILITY IN GENITAL TUBERCULOSIS** The prognosis for future reproduction is extremely unfavorable. The pregnancy rate is rare (5–10%), and if conception occurs, the chance of ectopic pregnancy is very high (40%), along with an increased risk of miscarriage and preterm delivery,.

- **Tubal Reconstructive Surgery:** Surgery on the fallopian tubes is strictly withheld/contraindicated in women with pelvic tuberculosis,.
- **Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART):** In Vitro Fertilization and Embryo Transfer (IVF-ET) is the preferred treatment option and may be considered only _after_ successful chemotherapy and once the endometrium is proven completely free from the disease,.
- **Gestational Surrogacy:** If the endometrium is extensively destroyed (synechiae formation) leading to implantation failure, or if the ovaries are resistant to gonadotropin stimulation, gestational surrogacy is an indicated option,,.

**5. CONTRACEPTIVE ADVICE DURING TREATMENT**

- **Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs) should be strictly avoided** in women receiving antitubercular therapy. Rifampicin is a hepatic enzyme inducer (P-450 cytochrome) and significantly accelerates the metabolism of oral contraceptives, leading to a high rate of contraceptive failure,. Alternative barrier methods (like condoms) are recommended.


9. Write short note on Sling surgeries for Nulliparous prolapse uterus.
**SLING SURGERIES FOR NULLIPAROUS PROLAPSE UTERUS (CERVICOPEXY / PURANDARE'S OPERATION)**

**Introduction & Indications** Nulliparous or congenital prolapse of the uterus occurs in young women primarily due to an inherent, congenital weakness of the supporting structures holding the uterus in position. It typically involves the herniation of the uterus along with the inverted upper vagina, usually without an associated cystocele. Before surgical intervention, occult spina bifida and associated neurological abnormalities must be excluded.

The definitive surgical management for a young woman with congenital or nulliparous prolapse (where the uterus is without vaginal wall prolapse) is Cervicopexy, also known as the Sling or Purandare's operation.

**Principle of the Operation** The surgery is performed via the abdominal route. The core principle is to mechanically pull up and suspend the cervix by passing strips of the rectus sheath from either side extraperitoneally, which are then stitched to the anterior surface of the uterine cervix (isthmus).

**Principal Steps of the Procedure**

- **Incision:** A transverse abdominal incision is made through the skin and subcutaneous fat.
- **Preparation of Sling:** Two fascial strips (from the rectus sheath), each measuring about 1.5 cm wide, are dissected off. The lateral attachments of these strips at the lateral border of the rectus muscle are kept intact to provide a strong anchor.
- **Abdominal Entry & Dissection:** The peritoneal cavity is opened in the midline. The bladder peritoneum is dissected off, and the bladder is mobilized downwards to adequately expose the uterine isthmus.
- **Routing the Sling:** The free medial ends of the fascial strips are brought down extraperitoneally between the leaves of the broad ligament to reach the site of the uterine isthmus. This specific routing technique is anatomically similar to that described in Gilliam's operation.
- **Fixation:** The uterus is manually adjusted into its correct, elevated anatomical position. The free edges of the fascial strips are then securely fixed to the anterior surface of the uterine isthmus using sturdy bites with silk sutures.
- **Closure:** The bladder peritoneum is repaired over the surgical site, and the abdomen is closed in layers.

**Modern Modifications & Concurrent Procedures**

- **Synthetic Tapes:** Instead of harvesting autologous fascial strips from the rectus sheath, contemporary procedures currently utilize nonabsorbable synthetic tapes, such as Marlex or Gore-Tex, to create the supporting sling.
- **Moskowitz Procedure:** If an enterocele is present or anticipated, the sling operation may be combined with the Moskowitz procedure, which involves obliterating the pouch of Douglas with concentric sutures to prevent the herniation of the bowel.

10. Staging of Carcinoma Cervix.
**STAGING OF CARCINOMA CERVIX (FIGO 2018)**

**General Rules & Principles of Staging**

- Staging of cervical cancer is based principally on clinical examination.
- Final staging cannot be changed once therapy has begun.
- When doubt exists as to the correct stage, the lower staging should be assigned.
- Imaging (CT, MRI, PET) and pathology can be used, where available, to supplement clinical findings with respect to tumor size and extent, in all stages.
- Notations of **r** (imaging) and **p** (pathology) are added to indicate the findings that are used to allocate the case (e.g., Stage IIIC1r or Stage IIIC1p).
- The involvement of vascular/lymphatic spaces does not change the staging.
- The lateral extent of the lesion is no longer considered in the updated staging.

---

**STAGE I: Tumor confined to the cervix** (Extension to the uterine corpus should be disregarded).

- **Stage IA:** Invasive carcinoma that can be diagnosed only by microscopy, with a maximum depth of invasion <5 mm.
    - **IA1:** Measured stromal invasion <3 mm in depth.
    - **IA2:** Measured stromal invasion ≥3 mm and <5 mm in depth.
- **Stage IB:** Invasive carcinoma with measured deepest invasion ≥5 mm (greater than stage IA), lesion limited to the cervix uteri.
    - **IB1:** Invasive carcinoma ≥5 mm depth of stromal invasion, and <2 cm in greatest dimension.
    - **IB2:** Invasive carcinoma ≥2 cm and <4 cm in greatest dimension.
    - **IB3:** Invasive carcinoma ≥4 cm in greatest dimension.

**STAGE II: Tumor invades beyond the uterus, but has not extended onto the lower third of the vagina or to the pelvic wall**.

- **Stage IIA:** Involvement limited to the upper two-thirds of the vagina without parametrial involvement.
    - **IIA1:** Invasive carcinoma <4 cm in greatest dimension.
    - **IIA2:** Invasive carcinoma ≥4 cm in greatest dimension.
- **Stage IIB:** With parametrial involvement but not up to the pelvic wall.

**STAGE III: Tumor involves the lower third of the vagina and/or extends to the pelvic wall and/or causes hydronephrosis or nonfunctioning kidney and/or involves pelvic and/or para-aortic lymph nodes**.

- **Stage IIIA:** The carcinoma involves the lower third of the vagina, with no extension to the pelvic wall.
- **Stage IIIB:** Extension to the pelvic wall and/or hydronephrosis or nonfunctioning kidney (unless known to be due to another cause).
- **Stage IIIC:** Involvement of pelvic and/or para-aortic lymph nodes, irrespective of tumor size and extent (with _r_ and _p_ notations).
    - **IIIC1:** Pelvic lymph node metastasis only.
    - **IIIC2:** Para-aortic lymph node metastasis.

**STAGE IV: Tumor has extended beyond the true pelvis or has involved (biopsy proven) the mucosa of the bladder or rectum**. _(Note: A bullous edema, as such, does not permit a case to be allotted to stage IV)_.

- **Stage IVA:** Spread to adjacent pelvic organs.
- **Stage IVB:** Spread to distant organs.



11. Write short notes on Colposcopy.
**COLPOSCOPY**

**Definition and Introduction**

- Colposcopy is an _in situ_ clinical examination procedure using a low-power binocular microscope (colposcope) mounted on a stand.
- It was devised by Hinselmann in 1925.
- It provides a magnified view (usually 6–16 times, up to 40 times) of the surface epithelium of the vaginal part of the cervix, including the entire transformation zone.

**Principle**

- While exfoliative cytology identifies patients with cervical neoplasia by evaluating the morphological changes of exfoliated cells, colposcopy identifies the exact site for taking a targeted biopsy.
- It primarily evaluates the changes in the terminal vascular network of the cervix, which reflect the underlying biochemical and metabolic changes of the tissue.
- It is complementary to, and not a substitute for, cervical cytology.

**Indications**

- Abnormal cytology reports: High-grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion (HSIL), Atypical Squamous Cells cannot exclude HSIL (ASC-H), and Atypical Squamous Cells of Undetermined Significance (ASC-US) with HPV positivity.
- Clinically abnormal or suspicious cervix (e.g., contact bleeding), even if the cytology smear is normal.
- Women with persistently positive high-risk HPV-DNA test results.
- Unexplained postcoital bleeding.
- Evaluation of vulvar (vulvoscopy) or vaginal (vaginoscopy) neoplasia.

**Procedure**

1. **Preparation:** The patient is placed in the lithotomy position. A Cusco's bivalve speculum is introduced to visualize the cervix.
2. **Cleaning:** The cervix is cleared of mucus using a swab soaked in normal saline, and examined under low magnification. A green filter is used to better visualize the vascular patterns.
3. **Acetic Acid Test:** 3–5% acetic acid is gently applied, and the cervix is examined after 30–60 seconds. Acetic acid acts as a mucolytic agent and causes coagulation of nuclear proteins (which are high in Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia, CIN). This prevents light transmission, making the dysplastic areas appear white (**Acetowhite epithelium**).
4. **Schiller’s / Lugol’s Iodine Test:** Lugol's iodine solution is applied. Mature normal squamous epithelial cells contain abundant glycogen and stain dark brown. Dysplastic cells lack glycogen and remain unstained (yellowish/iodine negative).
5. **Biopsy:** A targeted biopsy is taken from the most suspicious acetowhite or iodine-negative areas.

**Colposcopic Findings**

- **Normal Findings:** Normal squamous epithelium, columnar epithelium, and normal transformation zone.
- **Abnormal Findings (Atypical Transformation Zone):**
    - **Acetowhite epithelium:** Areas turning white after acetic acid application.
    - **Punctuation:** Dilated capillaries appearing as stippled dots on the surface (end-on view of vessels).
    - **Mosaicism:** Capillaries encircling polygonal-shaped blocks of epithelial cells.
    - **Keratosis (Leukoplakia):** White epithelium present even before applying acetic acid.
    - **Atypical vessels:** Blood vessels with irregular diameters, shapes, and branching (strongly suggestive of invasive carcinoma).
    - **Iodine negativity**.
- **Suspected Frank Invasive Cancer:** Exophytic, ulcerated, or irregular surface contours with marked friability and atypical vessels.

**Types of Colposcopy**

- **Satisfactory Colposcopy:** The entire transformation zone and the original squamocolumnar junction (SCJ) are fully visualized.
- **Unsatisfactory Colposcopy:** The entire transformation zone is not seen because it extends up into the endocervical canal, or the extent of the abnormal tissue cannot be determined. In such cases, an Endocervical Curettage (ECC) is mandatory.

**Reid Colposcopic Index** This index correlates colposcopic findings with histology by scoring four features:

1. Margin of the lesion
2. Color
3. Vascular pattern
4. Lugol solution staining effect. Each feature is scored 0, 1, or 2 (maximum score of 8) to objectively predict the severity of the lesion.

12.Management of secondary amenorrhoea.
**DEFINITION** Secondary amenorrhea is the absence of menstruation for 6 months or more in a woman in whom normal menstruation has previously been established.

**PREREQUISITE TO MANAGEMENT** Pregnancy must be excluded first, irrespective of the marital status of the woman (married, unmarried, widow, divorced, or separated).

---

**MANAGEMENT PROTOCOL** The management is divided into two broad categories: cases where no abnormality is detected and cases with a specific detectable cause.

### **A. MANAGEMENT WHEN NO ABNORMALITY IS DETECTED**

In many cases, no specific pathology is found. Treatment depends on the patient's anxiety and desire for fertility.

- **Patient not anxious about amenorrhea/fertility:** No treatment is required; provide reassurance. Spontaneous resumption of menstruation occurs in about 60% of cases within 6 months to a year.
- **Patient anxious for amenorrhea (but not fertility):**
    - _With normal endogenous estrogen:_ Combined oral contraceptive (COC) pills are prescribed for at least 3 cycles.
    - _With low endogenous estrogen:_ Ethinyl estradiol 0.02 mg or Conjugated Equine Estrogen (CEE) 1.25 mg is given daily for 25 days, with Medroxyprogesterone acetate 10 mg daily added from day 16–25.
- **Patient desires fertility:** The couple requires a complete infertility evaluation (including semen analysis and tubal patency). Ovulation is then induced using Clomiphene citrate, Letrozole, or Gonadotropins.

---

### **B. MANAGEMENT OF SPECIFIC CAUSES**

**1. Hypothalamic & General Factors**

- **Stress & Anxiety:** Managed by assurance, psychotherapy, and lifestyle modification.
- **Exercise-induced & Weight-related Amenorrhea:** Cured by limitation of strenuous activity and appropriate diet/weight gain.
- **Systemic Illness & Malnutrition:** Improvement of nutritional status and appropriate medical therapy for the underlying systemic illness (e.g., antitubercular drugs for tuberculosis).
- **Anorexia Nervosa:** Requires a multidisciplinary team approach including behavioral therapy and psychiatric consultation. Hospitalization may be necessary in severe cases.

**2. Pituitary Factors (Hyperprolactinemia / Prolactinoma)**

- **Medical Management (First-line):** Dopamine agonists are highly effective. Cabergoline (0.25 or 0.5 mg once/twice weekly) is preferred as it is long-acting, has greater potency, and is better tolerated with fewer side effects. Bromocriptine is an alternative, starting at a low dose to minimize side effects like nausea and orthostatic hypotension. Menses and ovulation return when prolactin levels normalize.
- **Surgical Management:** Transnasal-transsphenoidal microsurgical resection (adenectomy) is indicated if medical therapy fails or for macroadenomas causing compression symptoms.
- **Radiation Therapy:** Rarely preferred; used for large residual tumors post-surgery.

**3. Ovarian Factors**

- **Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome (PCOS):** Management is individualized based on symptoms.
    - _Obesity & Metabolic Syndrome:_ Weight reduction (first-line) and lifestyle changes. Metformin is used as an insulin sensitizer.
    - _Subfertility:_ Ovulation induction with Letrozole or Clomiphene (± Metformin or Gonadotropins). Laparoscopic ovarian drilling is an option for refractory cases.
    - _Abnormal Bleeding/Hirsutism:_ COCs are the treatment of choice to regulate cycles, prevent endometrial hyperplasia, and manage body hair along with antiandrogens.
- **Premature Ovarian Insufficiency (POI) / Ovarian Failure:**
    - _Hormone Replacement Therapy (HRT):_ Essential to prevent postmenopausal health hazards (osteoporosis, cardiovascular issues) and to treat hypoestrogenic symptoms.
    - _Autoimmune cases:_ Corticosteroids may be of help.
    - _Fertility:_ Best achieved via IVF with donor oocytes.
    - _Genetic cases:_ If a 'Y' chromosome is present, gonadectomy is mandatory to avoid gonadal malignancy.
- **Ovarian Tumors (Masculinizing/Feminizing):** Appropriate surgical excision of the tumor.

**4. Uterine Factors (Uterine Synechiae / Asherman's Syndrome)**

- **Surgical:** Hysteroscopic adhesiolysis (using scissors or electrocautery) followed by the insertion of an Intrauterine Contraceptive Device (IUCD) to keep the uterine walls apart.
- **Medical:** High-dose estrogen and progestin therapy is given for 3 months post-surgery to promote endometrial regeneration and withdrawal bleeding.

**5. Thyroid Dysfunction**

- **Hypothyroidism:** Substitution therapy with Thyroxine (eltroxin) 1.6 µg/kg/day.
- **Hyperthyroidism:** Treated with antithyroid drugs (propylthiouracil or carbimazole).

**6. Adrenal Disorders**

- **Cushing's Disease (Pituitary ACTH-producing tumor):** Treated by transsphenoidal microsurgical resection or external beam radiation.
- **Adrenal Tumor:** Managed by simple adrenalectomy.
- **Medical Adrenalectomy:** Enzyme inhibitors like Ketoconazole, Aminoglutethimide, or Metyrapone are used to block excess cortisol.
- **Adult-onset Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia:** Dexamethasone replacement therapy (0.25–0.5 mg/day) to suppress endogenous ACTH and androgen levels.


13.	How will you manage acute pelvic inflammatory disease?
**MEDICAL MANAGEMENT OF FIBROID UTERUS**

Medical therapy for uterine fibroids has established a firm place in gynecological practice. It is primarily used as a temporary palliation to minimize blood loss, correct anemia, or reduce tumor size prior to surgery, though in rare cases, it serves as an alternative to surgery.

**OBJECTIVES OF MEDICAL TREATMENT**

- **Preoperative optimization:** To improve menorrhagia and correct anemia prior to surgery.
- **Facilitation of surgery:** To minimize the size and vascularity of the tumor, making operations (like laparoscopic or hysteroscopic myomectomy/hysterectomy) technically easier with reduced blood loss.
- **Alternative to surgery:** In perimenopausal women or women with high medical risk factors for surgery.
- **Postponement of surgery:** When temporary delay of surgical intervention is planned.
- **Fertility preservation:** In young women desirous of pregnancy or to facilitate selected cases of infertility treatments.

---

**DRUGS USED IN MEDICAL MANAGEMENT**

**1. Non-Hormonal Options**

- **Tranexamic Acid (TXA):** An antifibrinolytic agent that is highly useful for controlling myoma-related heavy menstrual bleeding.
- **Prostaglandin Synthetase Inhibitors (NSAIDs):** Drugs like Naproxen and Mefenamic acid are used primarily to relieve pain associated with fibroid degeneration or coexisting endometriosis. However, they do not improve menorrhagia caused by fibroids.

**2. Progesterone Modulators**

- **Antiprogesterones (Mifepristone / RU486):** Given in a daily dose of 5–10 mg. It effectively reduces the size of the fibroid (by about 50%) and significantly decreases bleeding, often producing amenorrhea. Long-term therapy is avoided due to the risk of inducing endometrial hyperplasia.
- **Selective Progesterone Receptor Modulators (SPRMs - Ulipristal Acetate):** Ulipristal reduces blood loss by 90% in women with heavy menstrual bleeding and reduces myoma size by 53%. Nearly 70% of women become amenorrheic within 10 days of treatment. It is used for 3 months and is as effective as GnRH agonists. Unlike mifepristone, it does not cause endometrial hyperplasia, though benign endometrial changes may be seen after short-term use.

**3. Gonadotropin-Releasing Hormone (GnRH) Analogs**

- **GnRH Agonists (Goserelin, Leuprorelin, Buserelin, Nafarelin):** They cause sustained pituitary down-regulation and suppress ovarian function. Administered for an optimal duration of 3 to 6 months, they reduce the tumor size by 50%, decrease vascularity, relieve pressure symptoms, and improve anemia.
    - _Disadvantages:_ Hypoestrogenic side effects (vasomotor symptoms, trabecular bone loss), high cost, and rapid regrowth of the myomas upon cessation of therapy. "Add-back" therapy (low dose estrogen/progestin) may be required to combat hypoestrogenic symptoms.
- **GnRH Antagonists (Cetrorelix, Ganirelix):** These cause immediate suppression of the pituitary and ovaries without the initial stimulatory "flare" effect seen with agonists. The onset of amenorrhea is rapid, and benefits are similar to agonists.

**4. Aromatase Inhibitors (AIs)**

- **Letrozole and Anastrazole:** Fibroids possess higher aromatase levels. AIs induce systemic hypoestrogenism and are effective in reducing fibroid volume (by 10–70%) and controlling heavy menstrual bleeding. Treatment is started in the luteal phase and used for 2–3 months.

**5. Other Hormonal Agents**

- **Danazol:** An antigonadotropin and androgen agonist. Administered at 200–400 mg daily in divided doses for 3 months. It slightly reduces fibroid volume, minimizes blood loss, and produces amenorrhea. Its use is restricted to 3–6 months due to androgenic side effects.
- **Levonorgestrel-Releasing Intrauterine System (LNG-IUS):** Effective in reducing menstrual blood loss and uterine size. However, it is contraindicated if the uterine size is >12 weeks or if the uterine cavity is anatomically distorted by submucous fibroids.
- **Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs):** Used to minimize blood loss and regulate cycles. They do not typically increase the size of pre-existing fibroids and can palliate bleeding symptoms.

14. What is the role of Laparoscopy in infertility?
**ROLE OF LAPAROSCOPY IN INFERTILITY**

Laparoscopy is considered the "gold standard" (definitive method) for the evaluation of tubal and peritoneal factors of infertility. It is an invaluable tool because it allows direct visualization of the pelvic organs and offers the distinct advantage of performing therapeutic interventions at the same sitting.

**Timing of the Procedure:** It is commonly performed in the proliferative (preovulatory) phase of the menstrual cycle.

**A. DIAGNOSTIC ROLE** **Indications for Diagnostic Laparoscopy:**

- **Abnormal HSG:** To further evaluate filling defects or tubal blocks seen on Hysterosalpingography (HSG).
- **Unexplained Infertility:** Failure to conceive after a reasonable period (e.g., 6 months) despite a normal HSG and normal basic workup.
- **Advanced Age:** Women aged >35 years.
- **Suspicion of Pelvic Pathology:** History of chronic pelvic pain, deep dyspareunia, previous pelvic surgery, or suspected pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) and endometriosis.

**Diagnostic Benefits & Findings:**

- **Tubal Assessment:** Laparoscopic chromopertubation (injecting dye transcervically) directly visualizes tubal patency. It accurately detects the site and side of a tubal block, tubal motility, fimbrial agglutination, and hydrosalpinx changes.
- **Ovarian Assessment:** Identifies features of Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome (PCOS), ovarian endometriosis (chocolate cysts), periovarian adhesions, and the presence of an ovulation stigma (confirming recent ovulation).
- **Peritoneal Assessment:** Highly sensitive for detecting minimal pelvic endometriosis (e.g., 'powder burn' spots), pelvic adhesive disease, and pelvic tuberculosis.
- **Uterine Assessment:** Detects external uterine anatomical abnormalities such as Müllerian anomalies (bicornuate/unicornuate uterus) and subserous fibroids.

**B. THERAPEUTIC (OPERATIVE) ROLE**

- **Tubal Reconstructive Surgery:** Performed for peritubal adhesiolysis (salpingo-ovariolysis), fimbrioplasty (opening agglutinated fimbriae), and tubotubal anastomosis (reversal of sterilization).
- **Management of Endometriosis:** Excision, fulguration, or laser vaporization of endometriotic implants and drainage/cystectomy of endometriomas, which significantly improves the fertility outcome.
- **Ovarian Surgery:** Laparoscopic Ovarian Drilling (LOD) using electrodiathermy or laser. Making 4–6 punctures in the ovaries is highly effective for clomiphene-resistant, hyperandrogenic PCOS women to restore spontaneous ovulation.
- **Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART):** Facilitates advanced procedures like Gamete Intrafallopian Transfer (GIFT) and Zygote Intrafallopian Transfer (ZIFT).
- **Incidental Therapeutic Effect:** The process of flushing dye during chromopertubation may mechanically wash out small, flimsy adhesions or mucus plugs obstructing the tubal lumen, occasionally leading to spontaneous pregnancy shortly after the procedure.

**C. LIMITATIONS & DRAWBACKS**

- **Invasiveness:** It is more invasive than HSG and requires surgical expertise and anesthesia.
- **Incomplete Luminal Assessment:** While it shows the external pelvic anatomy perfectly, it cannot detect abnormalities within the uterine cavity (like synechiae or submucous fibroids) or inside the tubal lumen. Thus, HSG/Hysteroscopy and laparoscopy are complementary procedures, not substitutes for one another.


15. Complications of benign ovarian cyst.
**COMPLICATIONS OF BENIGN OVARIAN CYSTS**

The complications of benign ovarian cysts (tumors) can lead to acute abdominal emergencies or long-term morbidities. The major complications include:

**1. Torsion of the Pedicle (Axial Rotation)**

- **Incidence:** This is the most common complication, found in about 10–15% of cases at operation.
- **Tumor Characteristics:** It is especially common in tumors having a moderate size (preferably with a round contour), moderate weight (e.g., dermoid cysts due to high fat content), free mobility, and a long pedicle.
- **Predisposing Factors:** Trauma, violent physical movements, contractions of a pregnant uterus, pulsation of pelvic major vessels, and intestinal peristalsis.
- **Site:** Torsion of the right ovary is 2–3 times more common than the left, because the mobility of the left ovary is limited by the sigmoid colon.
- **Clinical Presentation:** Patients present with acute hypogastric pain and a lump. Examination reveals a tense, tender, cystic mass in the hypogastrium arising from the pelvis.
- **Management:** Urgent laparoscopy or laparotomy. Detorsion of the adnexa followed by cystectomy is recommended if vascularity is maintained. Ovariotomy (salpingo-oophorectomy) is the definitive surgery if the structures have become gangrenous. Oophoropexy is done to prevent repeat torsion.

**2. Intracystic Hemorrhage**

- This complication is more common in serous cystadenomas, particularly the papillary varieties.
- It frequently occurs following venous congestion due to the axial torsion of the pedicle or secondary to malignant changes.

**3. Infection**

- Infection of the cyst is common following torsion.
- The offending microorganisms are usually derived from the adjacent intestines or the uterine tubes when they become adherent to the cyst wall.

**4. Rupture**

- Spontaneous rupture usually occurs in big, tense cysts where a part of the cyst wall has undergone degeneration.
- Rupture can also be triggered by intracystic hemorrhage, direct trauma, in papillary varieties of cysts, or in the presence of malignancy.

**5. Pseudomyxoma Peritonei**

- **Pathogenesis:** Spontaneous leakage of a mucinous cyst adenoma leads to the implantation of cells of low-grade malignancy onto the peritoneum. The peritoneal mesothelium is converted to high columnar epithelium with secretory activity, which continues to secrete mucin even after the primary ovarian tumor is removed.
- **Associations:** It is often associated with a mucocele of the appendix, mucocele of the gallbladder, and intestinal malignancy.
- **Prognosis and Treatment:** The prognosis is poor due to risks of inanition, infection, and intestinal obstruction. Recommended treatment is total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, removal of mucin and peritoneal implants, along with an appendicectomy.

**6. Malignant Change**

- The malignant potential varies depending on the type of benign cyst.
- It is highest in the papillary variety of serous cyst adenomas, which can transform into adenocarcinoma.
- The risk of malignancy is the lowest (1–2%) in mature cystic teratomas (dermoid cysts), which typically give rise to squamous cell carcinoma.

**7. Complications Related to Pregnancy, Labor, and Puerperium**

- **During Pregnancy:** Increased chances of torsion of the pedicle (typically around 8–10 weeks of pregnancy or in the early puerperium due to a lax abdominal wall), intracystic hemorrhage, malpresentation, and non-engagement of the fetal head at term.
- **During Labor:** If the tumor is impacted in the pelvis (Pouch of Douglas), it can cause obstructed labor. Rupture of the cyst can also occur due to impaction during labor.
- **During Puerperium:** Infection of the cyst is more common following abortion and delivery, as the physiological events of thrombosis invite sepsis.

16. DMPA.

**DEPOT MEDROXYPROGESTERONE ACETATE (DMPA)**

**Definition and Nature** DMPA is a highly effective, long-acting, reversible, injectable progestin-only contraceptive. Chemically, it is a pregnane progestogen derivative that does not alter carbohydrate metabolism.

**Dose and Route of Administration**

- **Intramuscular (IM):** Administered in a dose of 150 mg every 3 months (WHO recommends 4 months) or 300 mg every 6 months. It is injected deep into the deltoid or gluteus muscle using the Z-tract technique, within the first 5 days of the menstrual cycle. The injection site must not be massaged.
- **Subcutaneous (SC) (Depo-SubQ Provera 104 / SAYANA PRESS):** Contains 104 mg of DMPA. It is injected subcutaneously into the anterior thigh or abdomen every 3 months (12–14 weeks).

**Mechanism of Action**

1. **Inhibition of ovulation:** It suppresses the midcycle Luteinizing Hormone (LH) peak, thereby preventing ovulation.
2. **Cervical mucus alteration:** It renders the cervical mucus thick and viscid, which acts as a barrier and prevents sperm penetration.
3. **Endometrial atrophy:** It causes the endometrium to become atrophic, which prevents the implantation of the blastocyst.

**Advantages and Non-Contraceptive Benefits**

- **High Efficacy:** It has a very low failure rate of 0–0.3 per 100 woman-years.
- **Convenience:** It eliminates the need for daily pill intake.
- **Lactation:** It is safe for nursing mothers as it does not alter breast milk composition and may increase milk secretion.
- **No Estrogen Side Effects:** Devoid of estrogenic risks like thromboembolism, making it safer than combined oral contraceptives (COCs).
- **Menstrual Benefits:** It significantly reduces dysmenorrhea and menorrhagia (often inducing amenorrhea, which is beneficial for women with heavy menstrual bleeding).
- **Protective Effects:** It decreases the risk of endometrial cancer, ovarian cancer, pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), ectopic pregnancy, and iron deficiency anemia.
- It is the contraceptive of choice for women with sickle cell disease.

**Disadvantages and Side Effects**

- **Menstrual Irregularities:** Characterized by unpredictable irregular bleeding and eventual amenorrhea.
- **Delayed Fertility Return:** Following discontinuation, the return of fertility is typically delayed by 6 to 12 months.
- **Bone Mineral Density (BMD) Loss:** Long-term use is associated with a decrease in BMD. However, this is reversible, and most bone mass is restored within 5 years of stopping the injections.
- **Other Side Effects:** Weight gain, depression, and headache. Overweight, insulin-resistant women may develop diabetes.

**Contraindications**

- Women with high-risk factors for osteoporosis or breast cancer.

**Therapeutic Uses (Non-Contraceptive)**

- **Dysfunctional Uterine Bleeding (DUB):** Used to control heavy bleeding and induce amenorrhea.
- **Endometriosis:** Used in a dose of 400–800 mg orally or IM weekly for 1 year to induce endometrial atrophy and regress endometriotic lesions.

17. What are the common sites of ureteric injuries during abdominal hysterectomy?
**COMMON SITES OF URETERIC INJURIES DURING ABDOMINAL HYSTERECTOMY**

Due to the close anatomical association between the ureter and the female genital organs, ureteric injury is a known complication during gynecological surgeries, accounting for 75% of ureteral injuries. During an abdominal hysterectomy, the ureter is most vulnerable to injury at the following specific anatomical locations:

**1. At the Infundibulopelvic Ligament** The ureter runs parallel and in close proximity to the ovarian vessels at this site. Injury commonly occurs while clamping and ligating the infundibulopelvic ligament.

**2. By the Side of the Cervix (Level of Internal Cervical Os)** The ureter lies approximately 1.5 cm lateral to the supravaginal cervix, where the uterine artery crosses the ureter anteriorly from above. Injury frequently occurs here during the clamping of the cardinal ligament and the descending cervical artery, or if a clamp slips and requires blind reclamping.

**3. At the Vaginal Angle (Anterior Vaginal Fornix)** The ureter can be injured as it traverses over the anterior vaginal fornix within the ureteric tunnel of the cardinal ligament (tunnel of Wertheim), where it turns anteriorly and medially to enter the bladder.

**4. During Pelvic Peritonization** The ureter lies in the posterior leaf of the broad ligament peritoneum, making it susceptible to being kinked or caught in sutures during the closure of the pelvic peritoneum.

**5. Deep in the Pelvis (Below the Ischial Spine)** Injury can occur deep in the pelvis, just below the level of the ischial spine, where the ureter lies lateral to the peritoneum of the uterosacral ligament.

**6. At the Intravesical Portion** The ureter can be damaged where it traverses obliquely through the musculature of the bladder wall.

---

**PREDISPOSING FACTORS FOR URETERIC INJURY (HIGH YIELD)** The chances of ureteric injury are significantly increased when the normal pelvic anatomy is distorted by underlying pathologies. These high-risk conditions include:

- **Pelvic Tumors:** Cervical fibroids, low corporeal fibroids, or broad ligament tumors.
- **Inflammatory & Adhesive Disease:** Pelvic endometriosis, tubo-ovarian masses, pelvic inflammatory disease, and severe pelvic adhesions.
- **Malignancy:** Gynecological malignancies requiring radical surgeries.
- **Surgical Mishaps:** Reapplication of a clamp to a bleeding pedicle (e.g., uterine artery) following its initial slip is a highly common cause of injury compared to primary clamping.
- **Congenital Anomalies:** Presence of a duplex ureter or aberrant ureteric anatomy.


18. Name eligibility criteria for undergoing tubectomy (permanent method of sterilization).

**ELIGIBILITY CRITERIA AND INDICATIONS FOR TUBECTOMY (FEMALE STERILIZATION)**

Female sterilization (tubectomy) is a permanent method of contraception. The eligibility criteria and indications for undergoing this procedure are classified as follows:

**1. Family Planning & Demographic Criteria**

- **Limitation of Family Size:** This is the principal indication in most developing countries.
- **Two-Child Formula:** It is generally recommended that the couple should have at least two living children who have been fully immunized. The decision must be individualized, keeping in mind the perinatal and infant mortality rates.

**2. Socioeconomic Indications**

- The method is adopted by an individual or couple who have successfully achieved their desired number of children and want to prevent any future pregnancies due to socioeconomic constraints.

**3. Medicosurgical Indications (Maternal Risk Factors)** Sterilization is strictly indicated when future pregnancies pose a severe threat to the mother's health or life.

- **Medical Disorders:** Presence of chronic systemic illnesses that are likely to worsen with repeated pregnancies, such as severe heart disease, diabetes mellitus, chronic renal disease, and hypertension.
- **Obstetric/Surgical Factors:** It is highly recommended during a third consecutive (repeat) cesarean section, or following the surgical repair of pelvic organ prolapse, to avoid grave future complications like scar rupture or recurrence of prolapse.

**4. Prophylactic / Risk-Reducing Indications**

- **Risk-Reducing Salpingectomy:** Tubal sterilization (specifically bilateral total salpingectomy) serves as a prophylactic measure to significantly reduce the risk of serous ovarian cancer and peritoneal cancer, especially in high-risk women (e.g., BRCA 1 or BRCA 2 mutation carriers).

**5. Essential Prerequisites and Counseling Criteria** For a woman to be eligible for tubectomy, the following mandatory protocols must be fulfilled:

- **Informed Consent:** The woman must give voluntary, informed written consent.
- **Adequate Counseling:** The couple must be thoroughly counseled and must clearly understand the absolute permanency of the procedure. They must be informed about its occasional failure rate, potential risks, and the availability of equally effective, Long-Acting Reversible Contraceptive (LARC) alternatives.
- **Reversibility Awareness:** They must be explicitly informed that while microsurgical reversal (tubotubal anastomosis) is possible, the success and subsequent pregnancy rates are not guaranteed, and the risk of ectopic pregnancy increases.

19. What is red degeneration of fibroid?
**RED DEGENERATION OF FIBROID (CARNEOUS DEGENERATION)**

**Definition** Red degeneration (also known as carneous degeneration) is a specific type of secondary degenerative change in a uterine fibroid, essentially characterized as a hemorrhagic infarction. Partial recovery is possible, which is why it is also referred to as necrobiosis.

**Incidence and Timing** It predominantly occurs in large fibroids mainly during the **second half of pregnancy** or during the **puerperium** (affecting 5–10% of such cases).

**Etiology**

- The exact cause remains unknown, but it is considered to be **vascular in origin**.
- **Infection does not play any part** in its pathogenesis.

**Pathology**

- **Gross (Macroscopic) Appearance:** The tumor exhibits dark red areas. On cut section, it reveals a characteristic **"raw-beef" appearance** and often contains cystic spaces. It frequently emits a **fishy odor** due to the presence of fatty acids. The distinct red coloration is due to the presence of hemolyzed red blood cells and hemoglobin within the tissue.
- **Microscopic Appearance:** Cellular details reveal clear evidence of necrosis. The blood vessels are thrombosed, but actual extravasation of blood into the surrounding tissue is unlikely.

**Clinical Features** The condition usually presents as an acute abdomen. The classic signs and symptoms include:

- Acute onset of focal pain localized over the tumor.
- Constitutional symptoms such as malaise and a rise in temperature (fever).
- Rapid pulse (tachycardia).
- Constipation.
- Marked tenderness and rigidity over the tumor on abdominal palpation.
- Blood investigations show **leukocytosis**.

**Differential Diagnosis** Because of the acute presentation, it is most commonly confused with:

- Acute appendicitis.
- Twisted ovarian tumor. _(Note: Due to diagnostic confusion, the exact diagnosis is sometimes only made upon laparotomy.)_

**Management Protocol** The cornerstone of managing red degeneration, especially during pregnancy, is strictly **conservative**.

1. **Bed Rest:** The patient is admitted and put to bed.
2. **Symptomatic Relief:** Analgesics and sedatives are frequently administered for pain management.
3. **Antibiotics:** Ampicillin 500 mg capsule thrice daily for 7 days is given prophylactically.
4. **Prognosis:** The symptoms typically clear up spontaneously within 10 days.
5. **Surgical Intervention:** Laparotomy or myomectomy is generally **avoided** during pregnancy due to the severe risk of profuse hemorrhage. There is no need for laparotomy in the vast majority of cases, though an isolated pedunculated subserous fibroid may occasionally be removed if necessary.

20.How will you diagnose Adenomyosis?
**DIAGNOSIS OF ADENOMYOSIS**

Adenomyosis is defined as the ingrowth of the functioning endometrium (both glandular and stromal components) directly into the myometrium, also known as endometriosis interna.

The diagnosis is primarily based on a detailed clinical evaluation (history and pelvic examination) and is confirmed by imaging modalities and histopathology.

**1. PATIENT PROFILE**

- **Age and Parity:** Commonly seen in parous women (90%) who are typically older than 40 years of age (80%).

**2. CLINICAL DIAGNOSIS (SYMPTOMS & SIGNS)** **Symptoms:**

- **Menorrhagia (70%):** Heavy menstrual bleeding is a predominant symptom. It occurs due to the increased uterine cavity size, associated endometrial hyperplasia, and inadequate, abnormal subendometrial myometrial contractions.
- **Dysmenorrhea (30%):** Progressively increasing, severe colicky pain. Characteristically, the pain often begins a week before the onset of menstruation and continues even after the period is over.
- **Dyspareunia and Urinary Frequency:** Occurs due to the enlarged, bulky, and tender uterus.
- **Infertility and Miscarriage:** Women have a higher incidence of infertility due to abnormal subendometrial myometrial function.

**Signs (Physical Examination):**

- **Abdominal Examination:** May reveal a hypogastric mass occupying the midline, but the uterine size rarely exceeds that of a 14-week pregnant uterus.
- **Bimanual Pelvic Examination:** Pathognomonic finding is a **diffusely and uniformly enlarged uterus**. In contrast to the firm, non-tender feel of a fibroid, the adenomyotic uterus feels **soft, boggy, and tender**.

**3. IMAGING INVESTIGATIONS** **A. Transvaginal Ultrasonography (TVS) & Color Doppler:**

- **Myometrial changes:** Shows heterogeneous myometrial echotexture with multiple small hypoechoic cysts, giving a characteristic **"honeycomb" or "Swiss cheese" appearance**.
- **Vascularity:** Increased vascularity is noted within the myometrium on color Doppler.
- **Endometrium:** Shows an ill-defined endometrial echo.

**B. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** MRI is superior to USG for diagnosing adenomyosis and provides definitive diagnostic criteria. The specific MRI diagnostic criteria include:

- **Junctional Zone Thickening:** An endometrial-myometrial junctional zone (JZ) thickness of **$\ge$ 12 mm is highly diagnostic**.
- **Asymmetrical Thickness:** The uterine walls show asymmetrical thickening, typically with the posterior wall being thicker than the anterior wall.
- **Cystic Spaces:** Presence of small myometrial hypoechoic cysts (honeycomb appearance).
- **Striations:** Striated projections extending from the endometrium into the myometrium.
- **Uterine Contour:** The uterus is globally enlarged, and the endometrial echo is poorly defined.

**4. PATHOLOGICAL AND HISTOLOGICAL DIAGNOSIS (GOLD STANDARD)** Definitive diagnosis is often made retrospectively on the post-hysterectomy specimen.

- **Macroscopic (Cut Section):** The uterus shows diffuse, symmetrical enlargement with disproportionate thickening (especially the posterior wall). Unlike a fibroid, **a capsule is entirely absent**. The cut surface presents a diffuse trabeculated appearance with visible cystic dark spaces and hemorrhagic spots.
- **Microscopic (Histology):** Reveals the proliferation and extension of endometrial glands and stroma deep within the hypertrophied myometrium. Large phagocytic cells laden with hemosiderin pigment are characteristically present.


21. What are steps in staging laparotomy for ovarian malignancy?
**AIMS OF STAGING LAPAROTOMY** The primary objectives of a staging laparotomy in ovarian malignancy are to accurately stage the disease to allow for a better choice of adjuvant therapy, to effectively assess the prognosis, and to perform maximum surgical removal (cytoreduction) of the tumor.

**STEPS IN STAGING LAPAROTOMY** The procedure must be systematic and thorough. The standard practical guidelines and steps include the following:

**1. Abdominal Incision**

- A liberal vertical (midline or paramedian) incision is made.
- _Rationale:_ This minimizes the chance of accidental rupture of the tumor during delivery and facilitates adequate exposure and exploration of the upper abdomen.

**2. Fluid Collection and Cytology**

- Upon opening the peritoneal cavity, the character of any ascitic fluid is immediately noted, and a sample is collected for malignant cell cytology.
- If appreciable ascitic fluid is not available, a peritoneal wash is performed by pouring 100 mL of normal saline into the subdiaphragmatic area and paracolic gutters, and the washings are collected for cytological examination.

**3. Systematic Exploration**

- **Abdominal Exploration:** A meticulous visual and manual exploration is performed in a clockwise fashion, starting from the cecum. This includes the systematic palpation of the liver, gastrointestinal tract, subdiaphragmatic area, omentum, and para-aortic lymph nodes.
- **Pelvic Exploration:** Careful assessment of the nature of the primary tumor, the extent of any adhesions, the condition of the contralateral ovary, the uterus, and the fallopian tubes, along with the palpation of pelvic lymph nodes.

**4. Biopsies and Lymph Node Sampling**

- **Targeted Biopsies:** Any suspicious or metastatic deposits over the peritoneal surfaces, omentum, and the undersurface of the diaphragm must be biopsied.
- **Blind/Random Biopsies:** In the absence of any gross metastatic disease, multiple peritoneal biopsies and scrapings from the diaphragm for cytology must be taken, as occult metastasis is found in 10–40% of early-stage (Stage I and II) epithelial ovarian cancers.
- **Lymph Node Sampling:** Selective pelvic and retroperitoneal (para-aortic) lymph node sampling must be done.

**5. Definitive Surgical Resection and Cytoreduction**

- **Standard Resection:** The standard operative procedure includes a Total Abdominal Hysterectomy (TAH) combined with Bilateral Salpingo-Oophorectomy (BSO).
- **Fertility-Sparing Surgery (Exception):** In young women strongly desiring to preserve fertility, and strictly presenting with early-stage disease (Stage IA, Grade 1, capsule intact, negative washings), a conservative unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy may be performed.
- **Omentectomy:** A complete (or infracolic) omentectomy is performed. Removal of the omental cake improves the results of subsequent chemotherapy or radiotherapy.
- **Appendectomy:** This is specifically indicated and performed in cases of mucinous ovarian cancer.
- **Maximum Cytoreductive (Debulking) Surgery:** In advanced-stage disease (Stage III and IV), extensive surgical debulking is aimed at reducing the residual tumor load to ≤1–2 cm in diameter (ideally <1 cm, as lesser residual tumor yields better survival). To achieve optimal cytoreduction, the surgeon may need to perform resection of a segment of the bowel, bladder, or lymph nodes, as well as diaphragmatic stripping, splenectomy, or hepatic resection for parenchymal metastatic disease.


22. How will you manage acute pelvic inflammatory disease?

**MANAGEMENT OF ACUTE PELVIC INFLAMMATORY DISEASE (PID)**

**PRINCIPLES OF THERAPY**

- To control the infection.
- To prevent infertility and late sequelae.
- To prevent reinfection.
- Follow-up examination of women within 48 to 72 hours for evaluation of therapeutic response.

**OUTPATIENT MANAGEMENT** Patients with mild to moderate PID can be treated on an outpatient basis. Empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics are initiated immediately to cover _N. gonorrhoeae_, _C. trachomatis_, _streptococci_, _E. coli_, and anaerobes.

- **Regimen (CDC 2015):**
    - Ceftriaxone 250 mg IM single dose OR Cefoxitin 2g IM single dose with probenecid 1 g PO single dose OR Injection Cefotaxime IV.
    - **PLUS:** Doxycycline 100 mg PO twice daily for 14 days.
    - **WITH OR WITHOUT:** Metronidazole 500 mg PO twice daily for 14 days.
- **Follow-up:** All patients treated in the outpatient setting must be evaluated after 48 hours; if there is no optimal response, they must be hospitalized.

**INDICATIONS FOR HOSPITALIZATION (INPATIENT THERAPY)** The patient should be admitted for inpatient therapy if the following conditions (CDC 2015 criteria) are present:

- Suspected tubo-ovarian abscess.
- Severe illness, nausea, vomiting, or temperature >38°C.
- Uncertain diagnosis, where surgical emergencies (e.g., acute appendicitis) cannot be excluded.
- Unresponsive to outpatient oral therapy for 48 hours.
- Intolerance or inability to take outpatient oral antibiotics.
- Coexisting pregnancy.
- Patient is known to have an HIV infection.

**INPATIENT MANAGEMENT**

- **General Measures:** The patient is urged to take complete bed rest. Oral feeding is restricted. Dehydration and acidosis are corrected by intravenous (IV) fluids.
- **Parenteral Antibiotic Therapy (CDC 2015):** Intravenous therapy is recommended for at least 48 hours but may be extended to 4 days if necessary.
    - **Parenteral Regimen A:** Cefoxitin 2 g IV every 6 hours for 7 days **PLUS** Doxycycline 100 mg PO twice daily for 14 days.
    - **Parenteral Regimen B:** Clindamycin 900 mg IV every 8 hours **PLUS** Gentamicin 2 mg/kg IV (loading dose), followed by 1.5 mg/kg IV (maintenance dose) every 8 hours. _(Note: Aztreonam 2 g IV is similar to aminoglycosides, lacks renal toxicity, and can be used as an alternative)_.
    - **Alternative Parenteral Regimen:** Ampicillin-sulbactam 3 g IV every 6 hours for 3–5 days **PLUS** Doxycycline 100 mg PO twice daily for 14 days.
- **Monitoring Response:** Improvement is evidenced by remission of temperature, improvement of pelvic tenderness, a normal white blood cell count, and negative bacteriological reports.

**SURGICAL MANAGEMENT** Surgical intervention is indicated in unequivocal complications where medical therapy alone is insufficient. The unequivocal indications include:

1. Generalized peritonitis.
2. Pelvic abscess.
3. Tubo-ovarian abscess that does not respond (within 48–72 hours) to antimicrobial therapy or if it ruptures.
4. Life-threatening infections.

**PREVENTION AND COUNSELING** To prevent reinfection, the following must be rigidly observed:

- Educating the patient about the potential hazards and ways to avoid reinfection.
- Warning the patient against multiple sexual partners.
- Contact tracing and simultaneous treatment of the sexual partner.
