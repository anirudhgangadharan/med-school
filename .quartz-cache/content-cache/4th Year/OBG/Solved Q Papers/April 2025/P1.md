1. Diagnosis and management of Diabetes Mellitus complicating pregnancy.

 **DIABETES MELLITUS COMPLICATING PREGNANCY**

**I. DEFINITION & CLASSIFICATION**
*   **Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM):** Defined as carbohydrate intolerance of variable severity with onset or first recognition during the present pregnancy. It accounts for 90% of diabetic pregnancies.
*   **Overt Diabetes:** Pre-existing diabetes or diabetes detected for the first time during pregnancy with a fasting plasma glucose $\ge$ 126 mg/dL, 2-hour post-glucose $\ge$ 200 mg/dL, and/or HbA1c $\ge$ 6.5%.

**II. ETIOPATHOGENESIS**
Pregnancy is a diabetogenic state characterized by chronic low-grade inflammation and increased levels of anti-insulin hormones (hPL, estrogen, progesterone, cortisol, prolactin, TNF-$\alpha$, leptin). This leads to decreased peripheral tissue sensitivity to insulin (insulin resistance). 
*   **Pedersen Hypothesis:** Maternal hyperglycemia $\to$ Fetal hyperglycemia $\to$ Fetal pancreatic islet cell hyperplasia and hypertrophy $\to$ Increased fetal insulin secretion $\to$ Excessive fetal growth/adiposity (Macrosomia).

**III. RISK FACTORS FOR GDM**
*   Positive family history of diabetes (parents or siblings).
*   Previous birth of a macrosomic baby ($\ge$ 4 kg).
*   Previous history of GDM, unexplained perinatal loss, or stillbirth.
*   Obesity (BMI > 30), maternal age > 30 years, and PCOS.
*   Presence of polyhydramnios or recurrent vaginal candidiasis in the current pregnancy.
*   Hypertension ($\ge$ 140/90 mm Hg).

**IV. SCREENING AND DIAGNOSIS**
Universal screening for GDM is recommended between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation. High-risk women should be screened early.
*   **DIPSI Criteria (India):** A 1-step procedure using 75 g oral glucose irrespective of the last meal. A 2-hour venous plasma glucose value $>140$ mg/dL diagnoses GDM.
*   **IADPSG/WHO Criteria (75 g OGTT):** Diagnosis is made if one or more thresholds are met or exceeded: Fasting $\ge$ 92 mg/dL, 1-hour $\ge$ 180 mg/dL, 2-hour $\ge$ 153 mg/dL.
*   **O'Sullivan/Mahan Criteria:** 50 g oral glucose challenge test (GCT). A 1-hour plasma glucose $\ge$ 140 mg/dL is the cut-off to proceed to a 100 g 3-hour OGTT.

**V. COMPLICATIONS**
**A. Maternal Complications:**
*   *During Pregnancy:* Increased incidence of pre-eclampsia (25%), polyhydramnios (25–50%), recurrent infections (UTI, vulvovaginitis), abortion, and preterm labor (26%). Worsening of diabetic retinopathy and nephropathy.
*   *During Labor:* Prolonged labor, shoulder dystocia, increased rate of cesarean delivery (approx. 50%), and postpartum hemorrhage.
*   *Long-term:* 50% risk of developing overt Type 2 DM within 5–20 years, obesity, and cardiovascular complications.

**B. Fetal and Neonatal Complications:**
*   *Fetal:* Macrosomia (>4 kg) due to fetal hyperinsulinemia, congenital malformations (6-10% in pregestational DM, notably VSD, neural tube defects, and sacral agenesis), intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) if maternal vasculopathy is present, and unexplained intrauterine fetal death (IUFD).
*   *Neonatal:* Respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), hypoglycemia, polycythemia, hyperbilirubinemia, hypocalcemia, and hypomagnesemia.

**VI. MANAGEMENT OF DIABETES IN PREGNANCY**

**1. Preconceptional Counseling (For Overt DM):**
*   Achieve tight glycemic control with an HbA1c $\le$ 6.5% to reduce congenital malformations. Pregnancy is strongly discouraged if HbA1c > 10%.
*   Start Folic acid 5 mg/day to prevent neural tube defects.

**2. Antenatal Management:**
*   **Target Blood Glucose Levels:** Fasting $\le$ 95 mg/dL; 1-hour postprandial $\le$ 140 mg/dL; 2-hour postprandial $\le$ 120 mg/dL; HbA1c $\le$ 6%.
*   **Medical Nutrition Therapy (MNT):** Diet should provide 30 kcal/kg for normal weight, 24 kcal/kg for overweight, and 12 kcal/kg for morbidly obese women. Macronutrient distribution: 40-50% complex carbohydrates, 20% protein, 30-40% fat. Divided into 3 meals and 3 snacks.
*   **Exercise:** Brisk walking for 30 mins/day, 5 days a week, or 10-15 mins after each meal helps control postprandial glucose.
*   **Pharmacotherapy:** Initiated if diet and exercise fail to meet targets. 
    *   *Insulin* is the primary medication as it does not cross the placenta. Regimen includes twice-daily basal insulin (e.g., NPH) and rapid-acting insulin with meals.
    *   *Oral Hypoglycemics:* Metformin and glibenclamide are safe and effective first-line alternatives, although 46% of women on metformin may eventually require supplemental insulin.
*   **Fetal Surveillance:** 
    *   NT scan and serum screening at 11–14 weeks.
    *   Detailed anomaly scan and fetal echocardiography at 20–22 weeks.
    *   Serial USG for fetal growth (macrosomia/IUGR) every 2-3 weeks.
    *   Biophysical profile (BPP), Non-Stress Test (NST), and Daily Fetal Movement Count (DFMC) starting from 28 weeks.

**3. Obstetric Management (Timing & Mode of Delivery):**
*   **Timing:** 
    *   Well-controlled GDM on diet: Deliver at 39 to 40 weeks.
    *   Controlled on insulin/medication: Deliver at 39 weeks.
    *   Suboptimal control or vascular complications: Deliver at 37 to 38 weeks.
*   **Mode of Delivery:** Vaginal delivery is preferred for uncomplicated cases. Cesarean delivery is indicated for macrosomia (>4 kg), severe pre-eclampsia, fetal compromise, previous stillbirth, or unfavorable cervix.

**4. Intrapartum Glycemic Control Protocol:**
*   Schedule delivery/induction in the early morning.
*   Omit the morning dose of insulin and breakfast.
*   Start an IV infusion of normal saline.
*   Once active labor begins or glucose falls <70 mg/dL, change to 5% dextrose at 100 mL/hour to maintain a glucose level of ~100 mg/dL.
*   Monitor capillary glucose hourly. If it exceeds 110 mg/dL, administer regular (short-acting) insulin via IV infusion. 

**5. Postpartum Management:**
*   Insulin requirements fall dramatically immediately after delivery.
*   Encourage early breastfeeding (within 30-60 mins) to prevent neonatal hypoglycemia; lactating mothers require an extra 500 kcal/day.
*   **Follow-up:** Perform a 75 g, 2-hour OGTT at 4–12 weeks postpartum to evaluate for overt Type 2 DM. Repeat testing every 3 years is recommended.

2. Define PPH. What are the causes of PPH. Discuss the management of Atonic PPH.
**DEFINITION OF POSTPARTUM HEMORRHAGE (PPH)**

**Quantitative Definition:** PPH is arbitrarily defined as the amount of blood loss in excess of 500 mL following the birth of the baby, according to the WHO. **Clinical Definition (More Practical):** Any amount of bleeding from or into the genital tract following the birth of the baby up to the end of the puerperium, which adversely affects the general condition of the patient (evidenced by a rise in pulse rate and falling blood pressure), is called postpartum hemorrhage. **ACOG Definition (2017):** Postpartum hemorrhage is defined as excessive bleeding (>500 mL in vaginal delivery or >1000 mL in cesarean delivery) accompanied by signs and symptoms of hypovolemia.

Based on the amount of blood loss, PPH is categorized as:

- **Minor:** <1 L.
- **Major:** >1 L.
- **Severe:** >2 L.

**Classification Based on Timing:**

- **Primary (Early) PPH:** Hemorrhage occurs within 24 hours following the birth of the baby (the majority occur within two hours). It is further divided into Third-stage hemorrhage (before expulsion of the placenta) and True postpartum hemorrhage (subsequent to the expulsion of the placenta).
- **Secondary (Late) PPH:** Hemorrhage occurs beyond 24 hours and within the puerperium.

---

**CAUSES OF PRIMARY PPH**

The four basic pathologies are easily remembered as the "Four Ts" (Tone, Tissue, Trauma, Thrombin).

**1. Tone (Atonic Uterus) - 80%:** Atonicity of the uterus is the most common cause of PPH. Following placental separation, the torn uterine sinuses cannot be compressed effectively due to imperfect contraction and retraction of the uterine musculature. Risk factors leading to atonicity include:

- **Overdistension of the uterus:** Multiple pregnancy, hydramnios, and macrosomia (big baby >4 kg) lead to imperfect retraction and a large placental site.
- **Grand multiparity:** Frequent adherent placenta and inadequate retraction contribute to PPH.
- **Malnutrition and Anemia (<9.0 g/dL):** Even a slight amount of blood loss may precipitate clinical manifestations of PPH.
- **Antepartum Hemorrhage:** Placenta previa and abruptio placentae.
- **Prolonged Labor (>12 hours):** Poor retraction, dehydration, and infection (amnionitis) play a role.
- **Mismanaged Third Stage of Labor:** Rapid delivery of the baby, premature attempts to deliver the placenta, kneading the uterus, and pulling the cord cause irregular contractions and partial placental separation.
- **Anesthesia and Drugs:** Deep anesthesia using ether or halothane, or the use of tocolytic drugs (ritodrine, MgSO4, nifedipine) cause uterine relaxation.
- **Oxytocin misuse:** Initiation or augmentation of delivery by oxytocin without continuing it for at least one hour post-delivery.
- **Other Factors:** Uterine fibroids (mechanical interference), morbidly adherent placenta, precipitate labor, obesity (BMI >35), age >40 years, and previous history of PPH.

**2. Trauma (Genital Tract Injury) - 20%:** Traumatic bleeding occurs following operative delivery or even spontaneous delivery. Trauma typically involves the perineum (episiotomy wounds and lacerations), vagina, cervix, paraurethral region, and rarely, rupture of the uterus. Bleeding is usually revealed but can be concealed, such as in vulvovaginal or broad ligament hematomas. Note that traumatic and atonic causes can coexist.

**3. Tissue (Retained Products) - 5-10%:** Bits of placenta, membranes, or blood clots left behind in the uterine cavity cause PPH due to imperfect uterine retraction.

**4. Thrombin (Blood Coagulopathy):** Acquired or congenital blood coagulation disorders are less common causes. Conditions triggering this include abruptio placentae, severe pre-eclampsia, eclampsia, HELLP syndrome, amniotic fluid embolism, intrauterine death, jaundice in pregnancy, and thrombocytopenic purpura.

---

**MANAGEMENT OF ATONIC PPH**

The management of atonic PPH requires a simultaneous multidisciplinary approach focusing on communication, resuscitation, monitoring, and arresting the bleeding. The "Golden 1 hour" is critical, and effective resuscitation must begin immediately to ensure the best chance of survival. The initial step is to palpate the fundus; a flabby uterus confirms atonic hemorrhage.

**General Resuscitation and Immediate Measures:**

- Call for extra help and involve the senior obstetric registrar.
- Site two large-bore (14-gauge) intravenous cannulas, keep the patient flat and warm.
- Rapidly infuse 2 liters of normal saline or Haemaccel until blood is available. Volume replacement should be quick and balanced with crystalloids and colloids.
- Send blood for cross-matching, coagulation screening (including fibrinogen level), and order at least 2 units of blood. Massive transfusion with packed RBCs, FFP, platelets, and cryoprecipitate may be necessary.
- Catheterize the bladder using a Foley catheter to monitor output.
- Assign staff to continuously monitor pulse, blood pressure, temperature, respiratory rate, SpO2, fluid intake, and urine output every 15-30 minutes.

**Step-I: Initial Pharmacological and Mechanical Measures**

- Massage the uterus to make it hard and express any accumulated blood clots.
- Administer injection methergine 0.2 mg intravenously slowly.
- Start an oxytocin drip (10 to 40 units in 1 L of normal saline) at a rate of 40-60 drops per minute. Alternatively, Carbetocin (a longer-acting oxytocin derivative) 100 µg IV can be used.
- Examine the expelled placenta and membranes for missing cotyledons or pieces.

**Step-II: Secondary Pharmacological Therapy and Exploration**

- If the uterus fails to contract, explore the uterovaginal canal under general anesthesia to rule out retained tissues or unrecognized traumatic injuries.
- Administer injection 15-methyl PGF2a (Carboprost) 250 µg IM in the deltoid muscle every 15 minutes, up to a maximum of 2 mg.
- Misoprostol (PGE1) 1000 µg administered rectally is highly effective.
- Tranexamic acid 1 g IV is recommended by WHO when other uterotonics have failed and can be repeated in 30 minutes if needed.

**Step-III: Uterine Tamponade**

- Perform bimanual compression of the uterus.
- **Balloon Tamponade:** Considered the first-line surgical intervention for most women with atonic PPH, avoiding hysterectomy in 78% of cases. A Foley catheter, Bakri balloon, Condom catheter, or Sengstaken-Blakemore tube is inserted into the uterine cavity, inflated with 200-500 mL of normal saline, and kept in place for 4-6 hours.
- Uterine packing with a continuous strip of gauze or using a non-pneumatic antishock garment can be employed as temporary measures, especially if the patient is being transferred to a tertiary center.

**Step-IV: Uterine Artery Embolization (UAE)**

- If available, UAE under fluoroscopy by an interventional radiologist using gelatin sponge (gelfoam) particles can be performed. It controls bleeding in over 90% of cases while preserving the uterus and future fertility.

**Step-V: Surgical Interventions (Uterine Devascularization/Compression Sutures)**

- **Compression Sutures:** The B-Lynch compression suture or multiple square sutures can be applied; these work by applying continuous mechanical tamponade to the uterus.
- **Stepwise Uterine Devascularization:** Successive ligation procedures include: (i) unilateral/bilateral ligation of uterine arteries, (ii) low uterine artery ligation, (iii) utero-ovarian artery ligation, and (iv) bilateral ligation of the anterior division of the internal iliac arteries.

**Step-VI: Hysterectomy**

- Subtotal or total hysterectomy is the definitive treatment of last resort.
- It should be performed sooner rather than later to prevent death from exsanguination. Proceeding to hysterectomy is reasonable after 3 doses of failed PGF2a.

3. Antenatal care.
**ANTENATAL CARE**

**Definition** Antenatal (prenatal) care is the systematic supervision (examination and advice) of a woman during pregnancy. It is a care in continuum that starts before pregnancy and ends at delivery and the postpartum period.

**Aims and Objectives**

- **To screen the 'high-risk' cases** and ensure continued risk assessment.
- To prevent, detect, and treat any complications at the earliest.
- To educate the mother about the physiology of pregnancy and labor through mothercraft classes, improving psychology and removing fear.
- To discuss the place, time, and mode of delivery, and to motivate the couple about family planning.
- **Objective:** To ensure a normal pregnancy with the delivery of a healthy baby from a healthy mother.

**Schedule of Antenatal Visits**

- **Standard Schedule:** Checkups are done at intervals of 4 weeks up to 28 weeks, 2 weeks up to 36 weeks, and weekly thereafter until delivery.
- **WHO Recommendation (for developing countries):** At least 4 visits. First visit around 16 weeks, second between 24-28 weeks, third at 32 weeks, and the fourth at 36 weeks.

---

**PROCEDURE AT THE FIRST VISIT** The first visit should ideally not be deferred beyond the second missed period.

**1. History Taking**

- **Vital Statistics:** Age (elderly primigravida is $\ge$ 35 years; teenage and elderly are at high obstetric risk).
- **Gravida and Parity:** A nulligravida is pregnant for the first time, while a grand multipara has had four or more births.
- **Period of Gestation:** Duration is expressed in completed weeks, counting from the first day of the Last Normal Menstrual Period (LNMP).
- **Calculation of Expected Date of Delivery (EDD):** Calculated using Naegele's formula by adding 9 calendar months and 7 days to the first day of the LNMP. For IVF, 266 days are added to the date of embryo transfer.
- **Obstetric & Medical History:** Past medical illness, previous surgical history, family history of twins/anomalies, and personal history (smoking, contraceptive practice) are recorded. Minimum spacing between births should be 2 years.

**2. General Physical & Systemic Examination**

- **Build, Nutrition & Height:** Short stature ($\le$ 139.7 cm in India) indicates a likely small pelvis.
- **Weight:** Baseline weight should be taken. Regular weight checking is vital.
- **Pallor & Jaundice:** Check lower palpebral conjunctiva, tongue, and nail beds for pallor. Check bulbar conjunctiva for jaundice.
- **Blood Pressure:** BP is measured in the sitting position, with Korotkoff 5 sound used to determine diastolic pressure.
- **Systemic Exam:** Heart, lungs, liver, spleen, and breast examination (to check for cracked/depressed nipples).

**3. Obstetric Examination**

- Abdominal examination notes the tone of abdominal muscles, scars, and fundal height (palpable above the symphysis pubis at 12 weeks). Routine vaginal examination before 12 weeks is generally replaced by ultrasound.

**4. Routine & Special Investigations**

- **Blood:** Hemoglobin, hematocrit, ABO and Rh grouping, blood glucose, VDRL, and serology for Rubella, HBV, and HIV.
- **Urine:** Routine and culture (checked for protein and sugar via dipstick at every visit).
- **Ultrasonography:**
    - _First Trimester (11-14 weeks):_ To confirm gestational age, viability, detect multiple fetuses, and screen for aneuploidy (combined screening).
    - _Second Trimester (18-22 weeks):_ Detailed anomaly survey and placental localization.
- **Immunization:** Two doses of Tetanus Toxoid (0.5 mL IM) given 6 weeks apart, starting between 16-24 weeks. A booster is given if previously immunized.

---

**PROCEDURE AT SUBSEQUENT VISITS** **Objectives:** To assess fetal well-being, presentation, amniotic fluid volume, and screen for pre-eclampsia or anemia.

- **History:** Elicit any new symptoms (e.g., headache, dysuria) and note the date of 'quickening' (perception of fetal movements).
- **General Exam:** Monitor weight gain, pallor, edema, and blood pressure at every visit.
- **Abdominal Exam:** Check Symphysis Fundal Height (SFH) or fundal height in relation to landmarks, external ballottement, fetal movements, and auscultate the fetal heart sounds (FHS). Beyond 30 weeks, measure the abdominal girth.
- **Vaginal Exam:** Not recommended routinely in later months (beyond 37 weeks) for pelvic assessment unless in labor or before induction.

---

**ANTENATAL ADVICE** **1. Diet and Nutrition**

- **Calories:** Increased requirement of 300 kcal/day in the second half of pregnancy. Total daily recommendations: 2500 kcal, 71 g protein, 1000 mg calcium, 40 mg iron, and 600 $\mu$g folic acid.
- **Weight Gain:** Women with a normal BMI should aim to gain an optimal 11 kg. Obese women must restrict weight gain to prevent complications.
- **Supplements:** Dietetic iron is insufficient; oral iron supplementation (60 mg elemental iron) and vitamins are given from 16-20 weeks onwards.

**2. Rest, Sleep, and Hygiene**

- **Rest:** Normal activities can be continued, but excessive/strenuous work must be avoided. On average, 10 hours of sleep (8 hours at night, 2 hours at noon) is recommended, especially in the last 6 weeks.
- **Bowel & Dental Care:** Prevent constipation through diet. Good oral hygiene is essential, and necessary dental fillings can be done in the second trimester.

**3. Travel and Coitus**

- **Travel:** Avoid jerky travel (e.g., rough bus routes) and prolonged sitting due to the risk of venous stasis. Air travel is safe up to 36 weeks but contraindicated in severe anemia, placenta previa, or pre-eclampsia.
- **Coitus:** Generally not restricted, but avoided in women at risk of miscarriage or preterm labor because prostaglandins in semen can cause uterine activity.
- **Addictions:** Smoking and alcohol consumption must be drastically curtailed or stopped to prevent fetal growth restriction.

**4. Warning Signs** The woman must be counseled to report to the hospital immediately if she experiences:

- Painful uterine contractions indicative of preterm labor.
- Sudden gush of watery fluid from the vagina (membrane rupture).
- Active vaginal bleeding (Antepartum hemorrhage).
- Severe headache, visual changes, or epigastric pain (Pre-eclampsia).
- Decrease or loss in fetal movements.

---

**VALUES OF ANTENATAL CARE**

- It is vital to screen high-risk cases and rectify risk factors early.
- Proper care drastically reduces maternal mortality and morbidity (by about one-seventh) and perinatal mortality (by about one-fifth).
- Antenatal care acts as a strategy, while intranatal care is the tactic; both are complementary for a successful obstetric outcome.


4. Diagnosis of cephaloplevic disproportion (CPD) and its management
**DEFINITION** Cephalopelvic disproportion (CPD) refers to a disparity in the relation between the fetal head and the maternal pelvis, which is mainly caused by abnormal fetal size (macrosomia), reduced maternal pelvic capacity, or more commonly, a combination of both. From a clinical standpoint, the fetal head is considered the best pelvimeter.

**DIAGNOSIS OF CEPHALOPELVIC DISPROPORTION AT THE BRIM** The presence and degree of CPD at the pelvic brim (inlet) can be ascertained through clinical history, clinical palpation methods, and imaging techniques.

**1. Clinical History**

- **Multigravida:** A past history of an uncomplicated, spontaneous vaginal delivery of an average-sized baby reasonably rules out a contracted pelvis.
- **Primigravida:** The nonengagement of the fetal head even at the onset of labor should immediately raise the suspicion of disproportion.

**2. Clinical Methods** **A. Abdominal Method** The patient is placed in a dorsal position with her thighs slightly flexed and separated. The examiner grasps the fetal head with the left hand and places the index and middle fingers of the right hand above the symphysis pubis to assess any overlapping of the parietal bone when the head is pushed downwards and backwards.

- _No disproportion:_ The head can be pushed down into the pelvis without the parietal bone overlapping the symphysis pubis.
- _Moderate disproportion:_ The head can be pushed down slightly, but there is a slight overlapping of the parietal bone (about 0.5 cm, equal to the thickness of the symphysis pubis) felt on the undersurface of the examiner's fingers.
- _Severe disproportion:_ The head cannot be pushed down, and the parietal bone overhangs the symphysis pubis, displacing the examiner's fingers.
- _Limitations:_ This method can serve as a screening tool but is difficult to elicit in cases involving a thick abdominal wall, a deflexed fetal head, an irritable uterus, or a high floating head.

**B. Abdominovaginal Method (Muller-Munro Kerr)** This bimanual method is superior to the abdominal method as it allows for simultaneous pelvic assessment. Two vaginal fingers are placed at the level of the ischial spines to assess descent, while the thumb of the same hand rests on the symphysis pubis to check for overlapping. The external hand pushes the fetal head downwards and backwards into the pelvis.

- _No disproportion:_ The head reaches the level of the ischial spines without the parietal bone overlapping the symphysis pubis.
- _Slight/Moderate disproportion:_ The head descends slightly but fails to reach the ischial spines, accompanied by slight overlapping of the parietal bone.
- _Severe disproportion:_ The head cannot be pushed down, and the parietal bone overhangs the symphysis pubis, displacing the examiner's thumb.
- _Limitations of Clinical Methods:_ These tests only determine CPD at the brim in the anteroposterior plane. They cannot detect midpelvic or outlet contractions, nor can they effectively evaluate transverse inlet contractions.

**3. Cephalometry and Imaging**

- **Ultrasonography (USG):** USG provides superior information by accurately measuring the biparietal diameter (BPD), which averages 9.4–9.8 cm at term, to evaluate its relation to pelvic diameters. Intrapartum USG is highly valuable for assessing pelvic dimensions (pelvimetry), fetal presentation, position, and descent during labor.
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** MRI accurately assesses pelvic capacity at various planes, fetal size, fetal head volume, and the maternal soft tissues involved in dystocia without radiation risks.
- **X-ray & Computed Tomography (CT):** X-ray pelvimetry has limited value for routine CPD diagnosis but may be useful in cases of pelvic fractures. CT provides greater accuracy than conventional X-ray but carries radiation exposure risks.

**DEGREES OF DISPROPORTION**

- **Severe Disproportion:** The obstetric conjugate is <7.5 cm (rarely seen in modern practice).
- **Borderline Disproportion:** The obstetric conjugate is between 9.5 cm and 10 cm. When both the anteroposterior (<10 cm) and transverse (<12 cm) diameters of the inlet are reduced, the risk of dystocia is significantly higher.

---

**MANAGEMENT OF CEPHALOPELVIC DISPROPORTION**

The management strategy is formulated based on the degree of contraction, the plane of the pelvis involved, and associated complicating factors.

**1. Management of Contracted Pelvis (Inlet Contraction)**

- **Severe Disproportion:** Handled by elective Cesarean Section (CS).
- **Minor Degrees of Contraction:** Often do not pose significant problems, and patients usually achieve spontaneous vaginal delivery at term.
- **Minor to Moderate Disproportion:** Managed by either induction of labor, elective CS at term, or a Trial of Labor.
    - **Trial of Labor:** This is the conduction of spontaneous labor in a woman with a minor degree of CPD, strictly under institutional supervision with partographic monitoring. The goal is to reduce unnecessary cesarean sections while ensuring the delivery of a healthy baby.
    - _Contraindications to Trial of Labor:_ Associated midpelvic and outlet contraction, elderly primigravida, malpresentation (e.g., brow), postmaturity, prior cesarean scar, pre-eclampsia, and maternal medical disorders (e.g., heart disease, diabetes).
    - _Successful Outcome Depends On:_ The specific degree of contraction, the shape of the pelvis (a flat pelvis is more favorable than android), a favorable vertex presentation with minimal parietal obliquity, intact membranes, and effective uterine contractions.
    - _Termination of Trial:_ The trial must be terminated via cesarean delivery if ominous features appear, such as arrest of labor, incoordinate uterine action, fetal distress, or excessive caput and molding.

**2. Management of Midpelvic and Outlet Disproportion** Isolated outlet contraction without concurrent midpelvic contraction is a rarity; hence, they are clinically considered together. Diagnosis is generally made only after the head has sufficiently descended into the pelvis.

- **Elective Cesarean Section:** Indicated when both the transverse and anteroposterior diameters of the midpelvis are contracted. CS is also the method of choice if the outlet is contracted and/or the baby is macrosomic, or if there is an arrest of the breech at or above the level of the ischial spines.
- **Vaginal Delivery (Watchful Expectancy):** In uncomplicated cases with minor contractions, vaginal delivery is allowed under strict supervision. The natural molding of the fetal head and "give way" of the pelvis may allow the head to pass the contracted zone. Delivery can be assisted using forceps or a ventouse, accompanied by a deep episiotomy to prevent severe perineal tears, especially if the pubic arch is narrow.
- **Labor Monitoring:** The progress of labor must be mapped with a partograph to detect dysfunctional labor early. If the active phase of labor shows no cervical dilatation or descent of the fetal head after 2 hours, it signifies an arrest disorder, and cesarean delivery becomes the indicated option.


5. Eclampsia management.

**MANAGEMENT OF ECLAMPSIA**

**Aims and Objectives:**

1. To control convulsions and prevent recurrence.
2. To stabilize hypertension and prevent complications.
3. To deliver the fetus safely in optimal time.
4. To restore maternal health postpartum.

**I. First Aid and Transport (Outside the Hospital)**

- Shift the patient urgently to a referral/tertiary care hospital equipped with NICU and ICU facilities.
- Sedate the patient using Midazolam 1-2 mg IV (may repeat in 5-10 mins) or Diazepam 10 mg IV slowly.
- Administer a loading dose of Magnesium Sulfate (4 g IV + 10 g IM) and Labetalol 20 mg IV to control hypertension before transfer.
- The patient must be accompanied by a doctor or trained midwife in an ambulance equipped to clear airways and prevent injury.

**II. General and Supportive Management (Hospital Care)**

- **Position & Nursing:** Admit to an intensive care facility with a railed cot; place the patient in a left lateral decubitus position to avoid aspiration and aortocaval compression.
- **Airway & Breathing:** Insert a mouth gag/tongue blade during the premonitory stage to prevent tongue bite. Remove oral secretions by frequent suctioning and administer oxygen (8-10 L/min) via a face mask.
- **Monitoring:** Insert a Foley catheter to record hourly urine output. Monitor pulse, BP, respiratory rate, and SpO2 (>95%) continuously.
- **Fluid Balance:** Start an IV line with Ringer's solution. Total fluids should not exceed the previous 24 hours' urinary output plus 1000 mL (insensible loss), usually restricted to <2 Liters in 24 hours at a rate of 1 mL/kg/h to prevent pulmonary edema.
- **Antibiotics:** Administer Ceftriaxone 1 g IV twice daily to prevent infection.

**III. Specific Management**

**A. Anticonvulsant Therapy** Magnesium Sulfate ($MgSO_4$) is the drug of choice as it acts as a membrane stabilizer and neuroprotector, reduces the rate of recurrent seizures, and significantly lowers maternal mortality (3%).

**Regimens for $MgSO_4$:**

1. **Pritchard Regimen (IM):**
    - _Loading Dose:_ 4 g (20% solution) IV slowly over 3-5 minutes, followed by 10 g (50% solution) deep IM (5 g in each buttock).
    - _Maintenance Dose:_ 5 g (50% solution) deep IM every 4 hours in alternate buttocks.
2. **Zuspan Regimen (IV):**
    - _Loading Dose:_ 4-6 g IV slowly over 15-20 minutes.
    - _Maintenance Dose:_ 1-2 g/hour continuous IV infusion.

**Monitoring for $MgSO_4$ Toxicity:** Repeat doses are given ONLY if:

1. Patellar reflex (knee jerk) is present.
2. Respiratory rate is >12 breaths/minute.
3. Urine output exceeds 30 mL/hour. _Therapeutic serum magnesium level is 4-7 mEq/L (4.8-8.4 mg/dL)_.

**Management of Toxicity:**

- Stop the magnesium therapy immediately.
- Administer the antidote: **10% Calcium Gluconate**, 10 mL IV slowly over 5 minutes.
- Initiate fluid loading, forced diuresis, and oxygenation.

_Duration:_ $MgSO_4$ must be continued for 24 hours after the last seizure or delivery, whichever is later. _Status Eclampticus:_ If fits persist despite $MgSO_4$, Midazolam 1-2 mg IV or Thiopentone sodium 0.5 g IV slowly can be used under expert anesthetic supervision.

**B. Antihypertensive Therapy** If BP remains $\ge$160/110 mm Hg, antihypertensives are administered to maintain a target SBP of 140-160 mm Hg and DBP of 90-100 mm Hg.

- **Labetalol:** 10-20 mg IV, repeated every 10-15 minutes (maximum 300 mg).
- **Hydralazine:** 5-10 mg IV, repeated every 20-30 minutes if necessary.
- **Nifedipine:** 10-20 mg orally, repeated in 30 minutes if needed.

**IV. Obstetric Management (Timing and Mode of Delivery)** Delivery is the definitive treatment for eclampsia. The decision is made once the maternal condition is stabilized.

**1. Fits Controlled:**

- **$\ge$34 weeks:** Delivery is expedited. If the cervix is favorable, perform low rupture of membranes (ARM) $\pm$ Oxytocin infusion. If the cervix is unfavorable, use $PGE_2$ gel for ripening followed by ARM.
- **<34 weeks:** Administer corticosteroid therapy for fetal lung maturity and deliver in a setting with NICU facilities.
- **Indications for Cesarean Section (CS):** Eclampsia before 30 weeks with an unfavorable cervix, fetal distress, malpresentation, or lack of progress.

**2. Fits Not Controlled:**

- Termination of pregnancy must be done within 6-8 hours irrespective of gestational age or fetal maturity. CS is preferred if vaginal delivery is not imminent.

**Intrapartum Care:**

- Epidural analgesia can be used safely during labor.
- Curtail the second stage of labor using prophylactic forceps or ventouse.
- Give Oxytocin 10 IU IM or IV slowly following the delivery of the baby to prevent PPH. **Avoid IV Ergometrine/Methergine** as it can precipitate a sudden rise in BP or eclamptic fits.

**V. Postpartum Management**

- Observe the patient closely in a high dependency unit/ICU for at least 48 hours, monitoring for recurrent seizures, pulmonary edema, or HELLP syndrome.
- Continue $MgSO_4$ for 24 hours postpartum.
- Continue antihypertensive medications (e.g., oral Nifedipine or Labetalol) to keep BP within safe limits. Avoid Methyldopa due to the risk of postpartum depression.


6. Cervical incompetence.

**CERVICAL INCOMPETENCE (CERVICAL INSUFFICIENCY)**

**Definition** Cervical incompetence is a condition where the retentive power of the cervix (internal os) is impaired functionally and/or anatomically, representing the ultimate manifestation of cervical weakness. It is considered a biological continuum of spontaneous preterm birth syndrome.

**Etiology** The weakness of the cervix may be due to primary anatomic defects, trauma, or a complex pathology involving infection and inflammation of the decidua/membranes. The specific causes include:

- **Congenital:** Müllerian fusion defects (e.g., bicornuate or septate uterus) and other congenital uterine anomalies.
- **Acquired (Iatrogenic - most common):** Trauma to the cervix following D&C operations, induced abortion by D&E (10%), vaginal operative delivery through an undilated cervix, amputation of the cervix, cone biopsy, trachelectomy, or loop excision of the cervix.
- **Other Factors:** Multiple gestations and a history of prior preterm birth.

**Clinical Features** The classic clinical presentation is recurrent, painless mid-trimester cervical dilatation without an apparent cause. This is followed by the sudden escape of liquor amnii and the painless expulsion of the products of conception.

**Diagnosis and Investigations** The condition is primarily a clinical diagnosis, but can be evaluated in two phases:

- **During the Interconceptional Period:**
    - **Clinical Examination:** Bimanual examination may reveal a unilateral or bilateral tear and/or gaping of the cervix up to the internal os.
    - **Hegar Dilator Test:** The painless passage of a No. 6–8 Hegar dilator beyond the internal os without resistance, and the absence of an internal os "snap" upon withdrawal (especially during the premenstrual phase) indicates incompetence.
    - **Premenstrual Hysterocervicography:** Reveals a funnel-shaped shadow of the internal os and cervical canal, which should normally be tight during the luteal phase due to progesterone.
- **During Pregnancy:**
    - **Clinical (Speculum) Examination:** Detection of painless cervical shortening and dilatation, sometimes with visible herniation of the fetal membranes.
    - **Ultrasonography (TVS):** Mid-trimester transvaginal sonography is diagnostic if it shows a short cervix (<25 mm) and funneling of the internal os (>1 cm). The incompetent cervix may appear Y-shaped, V-shaped, or U-shaped.

**Management** The definitive treatment is surgical, although conservative measures exist. A fetal growth and anomaly (aneuploidy) scan must be done prior to any operative intervention.

**1. Surgical Management (Cervical Cerclage)**

- **Principle:** The procedure reinforces the weak cervix using a nonabsorbable tape (e.g., Mersilene, Dacron, or Ethibond) placed around the cervix at the level of the internal os.
- **Types of Vaginal Cerclage:** The two standard procedures are the Shirodkar technique (submucosal tape placement) and the McDonald technique (purse-string suture), both boasting an 80–90% success rate.
- **Timing and Indications:**
    - _History-indicated cerclage (Prophylactic):_ Offered between 12–14 weeks (or at least 2 weeks earlier than the lowest period of previous wastage, as early as 10 weeks) for women with a singleton pregnancy and a history of three or more mid-trimester miscarriages or preterm births (<34 weeks).
    - _Ultrasound-indicated cerclage:_ Offered between 16–24 weeks for women with a history of spontaneous second-trimester loss and a sonographically short cervix (<25 mm).
    - _Emergency (Rescue) cerclage:_ Performed between 16–27+6 weeks when the cervix is actively dilating with bulging membranes, in order to delay birth. The membranes must be gently reduced into the uterine cavity beforehand.
- **Transabdominal Cerclage:** A Mersilene tape is placed at the level of the isthmus between the uterine wall and vessels via laparotomy or laparoscopy at 11–13 weeks. This is reserved for cases with a hypoplastic cervix or where prior vaginal cerclage has failed.

**Contraindications to Cerclage**

- Intrauterine infection (chorioamnionitis).
- Ruptured membranes.
- Presence of vaginal bleeding.
- Severe uterine irritability or contractions.
- Cervical dilatation greater than 4 cm.
- Fetal death or major congenital defect.

**Complications of Cerclage**

- Slipping or cutting through of the suture.
- Chorioamnionitis and rupture of membranes.
- Abortion or preterm labor.
- Cervical lacerations during delivery.
- Cervical scarring causing secondary cervical dystocia, which may require cesarean delivery.

**Postoperative Care and Suture Removal**

- Bed rest for at least 2–3 days.
- Tocolytics (e.g., Isoxsuprine 10 mg tablet thrice daily) to avoid uterine irritability.
- Weekly injections of 17 alpha-hydroxyprogesterone caproate (500 mg IM) in women with a history of prior preterm delivery.
- Advice on discharge includes avoiding intercourse and rough journeys.
- The cerclage stitch is generally removed after 36 completed weeks of pregnancy.

**2. Nonsurgical Alternatives** If surgery is not feasible or declined, management includes bed rest to avoid pressure on the cervix, the use of a vaginal pessary (when the cervix is found short on ultrasound), and weekly IM injections of 17 alpha-hydroxyprogesterone caproate (500 mg) to address the preterm birth syndrome continuum.


7. Janani Suraksha Yojana.
**JANANI SURAKSHA YOJANA (JSY) & JANANI SHISHU SURAKSHA KARYAKRAM (JSSK)**

**Introduction**

- **Janani Suraksha Yojana (JSY)** is a major maternal and child health care scheme launched by the Government of India in 2007.
- It operates under the umbrella of the **National Rural Health Mission (NRHM) / National Health Mission (NHM)**.

**Primary Objective**

- The core aim of JSY is to promote and increase the rate of **institutional deliveries** among pregnant women.
- By shifting the trend from home deliveries to institutional births, the scheme aims to ensure safe childbirth and significantly reduce maternal mortality and morbidity.

**Evolution: Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram (JSSK)**

- The JSY initiative was further expanded and strengthened as the **Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram (JSSK)** in 2011 (with continued programmatic updates like JSSK-2017).
- JSSK is implemented across all States and Union Territories (UTs) in India to ensure comprehensive care for both the mother and the newborn.

**Target Population and Focus Areas**

- While applicable nationwide, JSSK places a special, targeted focus on **Low-Performing States (LPS)** which historically have low rates of institutional deliveries.
- These focused LPS include: Uttar Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Bihar, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Assam, Rajasthan, Odisha, and Jammu & Kashmir.

**Impact and Significance**

- **Rise in Institutional Births:** The financial and structural support of the JSY scheme has led to a significant and measurable rise in institutional deliveries across India.
- **Reduction in Maternal Deaths:** Along with other complementary government initiatives—such as the Safe Motherhood Programme (1992) and the Reproductive and Child Health (RCH) Programme (1997)—JSY and JSSK have been highly successful stakeholders in substantially reducing maternal deaths and improving obstetric outcomes in the country.

8. Hematological changes in pregnancy.
**HEMATOLOGICAL CHANGES IN PREGNANCY**

Pregnancy induces profound anatomical and physiological adaptations to meet the demands of the growing fetus, which includes significant alterations in the maternal hematological system.

**1. Blood and Plasma Volume**

- **Total Blood Volume:** Markedly raised by 30–40% (an increase of approximately 1500 mL, from 4000 mL to 5500 mL). The increase begins around the 6th week, expands rapidly to peak at 30–34 weeks, and remains static until delivery.
- **Plasma Volume:** Increases by 40–50% (an addition of about 1250 mL, reaching 3750 mL near term). It starts rising at 6 weeks and plateaus around 30 weeks. The increase is greater in multigravidae, multiple pregnancies, and mothers carrying large babies.

**2. Red Blood Cells (RBC) and Hemoglobin (Hb)**

- **RBC Mass:** Increases by 20–30% (about 350 mL), beginning around 10 weeks and continuing without plateauing until term. This increase is mediated by elevated erythropoietin levels, and iron supplementation can further increase the RBC mass by 30%.
- **Hemoglobin Mass:** Total hemoglobin mass increases by 18–20% (from 475 g to 560 g). Reticulocyte count also increases by 2%.
- **Physiological Hemodilution (Physiological Anemia):** Because the expansion of plasma volume outpaces the increase in RBC mass, a state of relative hemodilution occurs. This leads to a drop in the hematocrit from 38% to 32% and an apparent fall in hemoglobin concentration by about 2 g/dL at term.
- **Significance:** This relative hemodilution lowers blood viscosity, which ensures optimal fetomaternal gaseous exchange, protects against adverse postural effects, and protects the mother from the effects of blood loss during delivery.

**3. Leukocytes and Immune System**

- **WBC Count:** Neutrophilic leukocytosis occurs, with white cell counts increasing to 8,000/mm³ normally, and potentially reaching up to 20,000/mm³ during labor. This is attributed to elevated estrogen and cortisol levels.
- **Immune Modulation:** The maternal immune system modulates away from cell-mediated cytotoxic responses toward enhanced humoral and innate immune responses to protect the fetus.

**4. Plasma Proteins**

- **Total Protein:** Increases from 180 g to 230 g at term.
- **Protein Concentration:** Due to hemodilution, plasma protein concentration decreases from 7 g/dL to 6 g/dL.
- **Albumin-Globulin Ratio:** Albumin levels fall by about 30% (from 4.3 to 3 g/dL), while globulin levels slightly increase (from 2.7 to 3 g/dL). Consequently, the normal nonpregnant Albumin:Globulin ratio of 1.7:1 drops to 1:1. This results in reduced colloid osmotic tension.

**5. Blood Coagulation Factors**

- **Hypercoagulable State:** Pregnancy is a hypercoagulable state with an increased risk of thromboembolic disease.
- **Fibrinogen (Factor I):** Levels increase by 50%, rising from 200–400 mg/dL to 300–600 mg/dL.
- **Other Coagulation Factors:** Factors VII, VIII, IX, and X are markedly increased. Factors II, V, and XII remain unchanged or mildly increased, while Factors XI and XIII slightly decrease.
- **Coagulation Inhibitors:** Fibrinolytic activity is depressed until 15 minutes postpartum, and Protein S levels are markedly decreased (by 40%).
- **Platelets:** The platelet count remains static or shows a slight reduction (by 15%) down to 1,60,000–2,00,000/mm³ due to hemodilution and low-grade intravascular consumption.
- **Clotting Time:** Remains unaffected. These collective procoagulant changes protect the mother against postpartum hemorrhage.

**6. Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR)**

- ESR undergoes a marked, four-fold increase during pregnancy (e.g., rising from 10 mm/h to 40 mm/h). Therefore, ESR has very little diagnostic value during pregnancy.


9. Iron therapy during pregnancy.
**IRON THERAPY DURING PREGNANCY**

**INTRODUCTION AND RATIONALE**

- **Total Iron Requirement:** The total iron requirement during pregnancy is approximately 1,000 mg (300 mg for the fetus and placenta, 500 mg for expanded red cell mass, and 200 mg obligatory loss).
- **Increased Demand:** The iron demand is mostly limited to the second half of pregnancy, especially the last 12 weeks, where the daily requirement increases to 6-7 mg.
- **Physiological Anemia:** The disproportionate increase in plasma volume relative to RBC mass produces a state of hemodilution. This, combined with a negative iron balance, necessitates iron therapy to prevent and treat anemia.

**PROPHYLACTIC IRON THERAPY**

- **Indications:** Most women should be given iron supplementation during pregnancy, as only 20% have the minimum essential iron stores of 500 mg, and about 40% have virtually no iron stores.
- **Regimen:** Daily administration of 200 mg of ferrous sulfate (containing 60 mg of elemental iron) along with 1 mg folic acid is highly effective.
- **Timing:** Supplementary iron therapy is needed for all pregnant mothers from 16 weeks onwards. If hemoglobin is above 10 g%, 1 tablet of ferrous sulfate a day is enough.
- **Dietary Advice:** A balanced diet rich in iron and protein (liver, meat, egg, green vegetables, figs, beans) should be prescribed. Tea should be avoided within 1 hour of taking the iron tablet.

**CURATIVE IRON THERAPY** The choice of therapy depends on the severity of the anemia, the duration of pregnancy (time available before delivery), and any associated complicating factors.

**1. Oral Iron Therapy**

- **Preparations:** Iron is best absorbed in the ferrous form. Common preparations include ferrous sulfate, ferrous fumarate, and ferrous gluconate. Fersolate tablet contains 325 mg ferrous sulfate (60 mg elemental iron). Iron polymaltose (ferric iron with maltol) contains 100 mg elemental iron, has fewer gastrointestinal side effects, and better absorption with food.
- **Dosage:** Initial dose is one tablet thrice daily, 30 minutes before meals. In cases of intolerance, start with one tablet daily and gradually increase.
- **Maintenance:** Treatment is continued until the blood picture normalizes, followed by a maintenance dose of 1 tablet daily for at least 100 days postpartum to replenish stores.
- **Side Effects:** Gastric irritation, constipation, diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, epigastric pain, and metallic taste.
- **Response to Therapy:** Evidenced by a sense of wellbeing, increased appetite, and reticulocytosis within 7-10 days. Hemoglobin should rise by 2.0 g/dL every 3-4 weeks.

**2. Parenteral Iron Therapy**

- **Indications:** Intolerance to oral iron, non-compliance, defective absorption (gastrointestinal disorders), or severe anemia detected in advanced pregnancy (last 8-10 weeks) where rapid replenishment is needed.
- **Advantages:** Certainty of administration, bypassing gastrointestinal intolerance, and avoiding malabsorption issues. The expected rise in hemoglobin is 0.7-1 g/100 mL/week.

**A. Intravenous Route (Total Dose Infusion - TDI)**

- **Compounds:** Iron sucrose, sodium ferric gluconate, iron carboxymaltose, and iron isomaltose. Iron sucrose is safe, effective, and has fewer side effects.
- **Dose Calculation:** Total iron dose (mg) = Body weight (kg) × [Target Hb - Actual Hb (g/L)] × 0.24 + 500 mg (to replenish stores).
- **Administration:**
    - _Iron Sucrose:_ 200 mg maximum single dose in 200 ml 0.9% saline over 30 minutes (up to 3 times/week).
    - _Ferric Carboxymaltose:_ 1000 mg maximum single dose in 250 ml 0.9% saline over 15 minutes.
- **Advantages over IM:** Eliminates painful repeated injections, completes treatment quickly, and is cost-effective.
- **Prerequisites:** Correct diagnosis of iron deficiency, adequate supervision, and facilities to manage anaphylactic reactions.

**B. Intramuscular Route**

- **Compounds:** Iron dextran (Imferon, 50 mg/mL), iron sucrose, and sodium ferric gluconate.
- **Procedure:** An initial test dose of 1 mL is given. Then, 2 mL is injected daily or on alternate days using a 2-inch needle.
- **'Z' Technique:** The skin and subcutaneous tissue are pulled to one side before needle insertion. This deep injection into the upper outer quadrant of the buttock prevents dark staining of the skin and minimizes pain. Air or saline is injected down the needle before withdrawal to prevent the solution from tracking back.
- **Drawbacks:** Painful injections, risk of abscess formation, skin discoloration, pyrexia, lymphadenopathy, headache, nausea, allergic reactions, hypotension, and arthralgia.

**3. Blood Transfusion**

- **Indications:** Severe anemia seen in later months (beyond 36 weeks) to quickly improve oxygen-carrying capacity before labor, refractory anemia resistant to iron therapy, anemia due to acute blood loss (APH or PPH), and associated infections.
- **Effect:** Transfusion of 500 mL of fresh blood raises the hemoglobin level by approximately 12.5 mg/100 mL and increases hematocrit by 3-4%.
- **Drawbacks:** Risk of initiating premature labor, circulatory overload leading to cardiac failure (pulmonary edema), and severe transfusion reactions. In cases with severe anemia, diuretics like furosemide (20 mg IV) may be used during transfusion to avoid volume overload.


10. Meconium aspiration syndrome.
**MECONIUM ASPIRATION SYNDROME (MAS)**

**Introduction and Incidence**

- Meconium Aspiration Syndrome (MAS) typically occurs in term or post-term babies who are small for gestational age (IUGR).
- The overall incidence of Meconium-Stained Amniotic Fluid (MSAF) varies between 10% and 15%.
- While not all infants with meconium aspiration develop MAS, features of respiratory distress develop immediately after birth in 5–10% of these infants.

**Etiopathogenesis**

- Meconium is toxic to the respiratory system and can be aspirated by the fetus in-utero or during the first breath.
- Chronic placental insufficiency leads to intrauterine hypoxia, which triggers meconium passage.
- **Vicious Cycle of Meconium Aspiration:** Placental insufficiency $\rightarrow$ oligohydramnios $\rightarrow$ cord compression $\rightarrow$ hypoxia $\rightarrow$ increased vagal response $\rightarrow$ increased peristaltic activity and relaxation of anal sphincter $\rightarrow$ passage of thick meconium $\rightarrow$ gasping breath $\rightarrow$ meconium aspiration.

**Pathophysiology** The pathophysiology of MAS involves several mechanisms:

1. **Airway obstruction and lung atelectasis:** Leads to hypoxia and increased pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR).
2. **Chemical pneumonitis:** Inflammatory response to the aspirated meconium.
3. **Pulmonary inflammation:** Release of cytokines causes airway edema and subsequent hypoxia.
4. **Surfactant dysfunction:** Meconium inactivates endogenous surfactant.
5. **Persistent Pulmonary Hypertension of the Newborn (PPHN):** Develops secondary to chronic hypoxia and vascular resistance.

**Clinical Features & Diagnosis** Diagnosis is based on clinical and radiological findings:

- **Clinical Presentation:** The infant manifests with signs of respiratory distress including tachypnea, nasal flaring, intercostal retractions, and cyanosis.
- **Aspiration Evidence:** Aspiration of meconium from the trachea at birth.
- **Radiological Findings:** X-ray shows hyperinflated lung fields, a flattened diaphragm, and coarse, patchy infiltration.

**Management**

- **Intrapartum Care:** Careful intrapartum fetal monitoring is required. Amnioinfusion in the presence of oligohydramnios may reduce cord compression, fetal gasping, and intrapartum aspiration.
- **General Supportive Care:**
    - Maintenance of a thermoneutral environment.
    - Circulatory support using normal saline or whole blood.
    - Correction of metabolic abnormalities, particularly hypoxia, acidosis, hypoglycemia, and hypocalcemia.
- **Respiratory Support:**
    - Liberal oxygen supply with continuous SpO2 monitoring.
    - Assisted ventilation and/or Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP) depending on the severity.
    - Mechanical ventilation is required if PO2 is less than 50 mm Hg and PCO2 is above 50 mm Hg.
    - Arterial blood gas analysis is mandated in severe cases.
- **Medical Therapy:**
    - **Antibiotics:** Broad-spectrum antibiotic coverage is required as meconium invites infection.
    - **Surfactant:** Surfactant therapy is beneficial (corticosteroids are not routinely used).
- **Advanced Modalities:** Inhaled Nitric Oxide (iNO) and Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO) may be needed for cases of refractory respiratory failure.

**Complications**

- Air leak syndromes (e.g., pneumothorax).
- Persistent Pulmonary Hypertension of the Newborn (PPHN).
- Bronchopulmonary dysplasia or chronic lung diseases.
- Chemical pneumonitis and atelectasis.

**Prognosis**

- With modern therapeutic modalities, the mortality rate has been reduced to <5%.
- Affected infants require long-term follow-up, as MAS may be associated with severe neurodevelopmental delay, cerebral palsy, and mental retardation.


11. Abruptio placenta.

**ABRUPTIO PLACENTAE (ACCIDENTAL HEMORRHAGE)**

**I. DEFINITION** Abruptio placentae is a form of antepartum hemorrhage where the bleeding occurs due to premature separation of a normally situated placenta. The bleeding occurs from or into the genital tract after the 20th week of pregnancy but before the birth of the baby.

**II. INCIDENCE & SIGNIFICANCE**

- **Incidence:** It occurs in about 1 in 100 deliveries.
- **Significance:** It contributes to nearly 30% of all antepartum hemorrhage cases, with the majority (60%) occurring in the third trimester. It is a significant cause of maternal mortality (2-5%) and perinatal mortality (10-15%).

**III. CLINICAL VARIETIES**

1. **Revealed:** Following separation, the blood tracks down between the membranes and the decidua, escaping out of the cervical canal to be visible externally. This is the most common type.
2. **Concealed:** The blood collects behind the separated placenta or between the membranes and the decidua, but does not escape out of the cervix. This is rare (10-20%).
3. **Mixed:** A part of the blood collects inside (concealed) and a part is expelled out (revealed). This is quite common.

**IV. ETIOLOGY & RISK FACTORS** The exact etiology is obscure in the majority of cases, but the following are established risk factors:

- **Prior abruption:** Risk of recurrence varies from 5-17%.
- **Hypertension in pregnancy:** The most important risk factor (10-50% association), including pre-eclampsia, gestational hypertension, and essential hypertension. It causes spasm of the decidual spiral arteries leading to anoxic endothelial damage and vascular rupture.
- **General factors:** High parity ($\ge$5), advancing maternal age, poor socioeconomic condition, low nutritional state, and smoking (causes vasospasm).
- **Trauma:** External cephalic version, road traffic accidents, direct blow to the abdomen, or needle puncture during amniocentesis.
- **Sudden uterine decompression:** Occurs following the delivery of the first twin, sudden escape of liquor in polyhydramnios, or premature rupture of membranes.
- **Other factors:** Short umbilical cord, supine hypotension syndrome, circumvallate placenta, folic acid deficiency, uterine anomalies (septate uterus, submucous fibroid), cocaine abuse, and inherited or acquired thrombophilias (e.g., hyperhomocysteinemia).

**V. PATHOGENESIS & COUVELAIRE UTERUS**

- **Pathogenesis:** Premature separation is initiated by hemorrhage into the decidua basalis, forming a retroplacental hematoma. Changes in spiral arteries lead to degeneration, infarction, and necrosis of the decidua basalis. Thrombin is generated, which triggers matrix metalloproteinases, inflammatory cytokines, and the coagulation cascade, leading to consumptive coagulopathy.
- **Couvelaire Uterus (Uteroplacental Apoplexy):** Seen in severe concealed abruption, there is massive intravasation of blood into the uterine musculature up to the serous coat. The uterus takes on a dark port-wine color, which can only be definitively diagnosed upon laparotomy.

**VI. CLINICAL CLASSIFICATION (GRADES OF ABRUPTION)**

- **Grade 0:** Clinical features are absent; diagnosis is made retrospectively after inspecting the placenta post-delivery.
- **Grade 1 (40%):** Slight vaginal bleeding, irritable uterus, minimal or absent tenderness, unaffected maternal BP and fibrinogen levels, and reassuring fetal heart sounds (FHS).
- **Grade 2 (45%):** Mild to moderate vaginal bleeding, distinct uterine tenderness, elevated maternal pulse with maintained BP, decreased fibrinogen level, absence of maternal shock, but fetal distress or even fetal death occurs.
- **Grade 3 (15%):** Moderate to severe (or concealed) bleeding, marked uterine tenderness/rigidity, pronounced maternal shock, fetal death is the rule, and it is often complicated by coagulation defects or anuria.

**VII. CLINICAL FEATURES** The presentation depends largely on the variety (revealed vs. concealed/mixed):

- **Symptoms:** In the revealed type, there is abdominal discomfort or pain followed by slight vaginal bleeding. In the concealed/mixed type, there is acute, intense abdominal pain followed by slight or no visible bleeding.
- **Signs:**
    - _Revealed:_ Patient's general condition and anemia are proportionate to the visible blood loss. Uterus has normal feel with localized tenderness. Fetal parts are easily identified, and FHS is usually present.
    - _Concealed/Mixed:_ Shock is out of proportion to the visible blood loss. Uterus is tense, tender, rigid, and "woody hard". Fetal parts are very difficult to make out, and FHS is usually absent (indicating fetal death) or shows severe hypoxia.

**VIII. INVESTIGATIONS**

- **Clinical Diagnosis:** Diagnosis is primarily clinical, though Ultrasonography (USG) or MRI can be helpful to confirm or rule out placenta previa.
- **Laboratory Tests:** Hemoglobin and hematocrit (markedly lowered in concealed types), ABO and Rh grouping, urine for protein, and a complete Coagulation Profile. Coagulation defects manifest as prolonged clotting time, low fibrinogen (<150 mg/dL), low platelet count, and elevated FDP/D-dimer levels.

**IX. COMPLICATIONS** **A. Maternal:**

- Hemorrhagic shock and hypovolemia.
- Blood coagulation disorders (Consumptive Coagulopathy / DIC).
- Acute kidney injury/failure (Oliguria/anuria) due to hypovolemia, acute tubular necrosis, or cortical necrosis.
- Postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) due to uterine atony and coagulopathy.
- Ischemic pituitary necrosis leading to Sheehan's syndrome.
- Maternal mortality ranges from 2-8%.

**B. Fetal/Neonatal:**

- Fetal death is exceptionally high (50-100% in concealed types) due to prematurity and anoxia from placental separation.
- Neonatal complications include prematurity, hypoxia-induced periventricular leukomalacia, and sudden infant death syndrome.

**X. MANAGEMENT** **1. Emergency Resuscitation (In Hospital):**

- Start an IV infusion with Ringer's solution using a wide-bore cannula.
- Send blood for cross-matching, Hb%, and coagulation profile; arrange for immediate blood transfusion.
- Monitor central venous pressure (CVP), urinary output (via Foley catheter), maternal vitals, and fetal status continuously.

**2. Definitive Treatment (Delivery):** _Expectant management is an exception and rarely done, considered only for small, chronic abruptions <34 weeks with a reassuring CTG, utilizing betamethasone and MgSO4 tocolysis._

- **If the Patient is in Labor:**
    - Accelerate labor by performing a low rupture of the membranes (amniotomy). Amniotomy initiates contraction, expedites delivery, aids in compression of spiral arteries to arrest hemorrhage, and reduces thromboplastin entry into maternal circulation.
    - Oxytocin drip may be added if required. Vaginal delivery is favored if the abruption is limited, FHR is reassuring, or the fetus is dead.
- **If the Patient is NOT in Labor:**
    - _Induction:_ If conditions are favorable, perform amniotomy +/- oxytocin. Labor usually starts soon and completes within 4-6 hours.
    - _Cesarean Section:_ Strongly indicated for severe abruption with a live, viable fetus, appearance of adverse maternal features (falling fibrinogen, oliguria), unfavorable cervix preventing amniotomy, or failure of amniotomy to arrest bleeding/progress labor.

**3. Management of Complications:**

- **PPH:** Administer oxytocics (e.g., Oxytocin 10 IU IV/IM or Methergine 0.2 mg) immediately upon delivery of the baby to maintain uterine tone, alongside adequate blood transfusion.
- **DIC:** Administer fresh blood, fresh frozen plasma (FFP), and platelets to restore fibrinogen levels to >150 mg/dL.
- **Fetomaternal Hemorrhage:** Administer 300 $\mu$g of anti-D immunoglobulin to all Rh-negative women.



12. Partogram.
**PARTOGRAPH (PARTOGRAM)**

**I. DEFINITION AND INTRODUCTION** A partograph is a composite graphical record of the progress of labor in terms of cervical dilatation and descent of the fetal presenting part against the duration of labor in hours. It also gives simultaneous information about the fetal and maternal conditions, all recorded on a single sheet of paper. It was originally devised by Friedman (1954) and later modified by Philpott and Castle (1972).

**II. RECENT UPDATES: WHO LABOR CARE GUIDE (2020)** The partograph has been recently updated by the WHO into the "Labor Care Guide" to ensure a positive childbirth experience. Key changes include:

- **Active Phase Onset:** The active phase of labor is now considered to start from **5 cm** of cervical dilatation (previously 4 cm).
- **Removal of Alert/Action Lines:** The fixed 1 cm/hour 'alert line' and the corresponding 'action line' have been removed. They are replaced with evidence-based time limits at each centimeter of cervical dilatation, allowing for natural variability in the rate of progress between women.
- **Second Stage:** Incorporates a section for intensified monitoring during the second stage of labor.

**III. COMPONENTS OF THE PARTOGRAPH** The modern WHO Labor Care Guide is divided into specific sections for structured recording:

**1. Patient Information & Supportive Care**

- Name, parity, risk factors, labor onset, and status of ruptured membranes.
- **Supportive care records:** Presence of a birth companion, pain relief, oral fluid intake, and maternal posture (e.g., supine, mobile).

**2. Fetal Assessment (Baby)**

- **Fetal Heart Rate (FHR):** Monitored every 30 minutes in the first stage and every 15 minutes in the second stage.
- **Amniotic Fluid Status:** Recorded as 'I' (intact), 'R' (ruptured), 'C' (clear), 'M' (meconium stained), or 'B' (blood stained).
- **Molding & Caput:** Progressive increase is assessed and recorded to identify cephalopelvic disproportion.

**3. Labor Progress**

- **Cervical Dilatation:** Recorded in centimeters and plotted with an **'X'**.
- **Descent of the Head:** Assessed abdominally in 'fifths' palpable above the pelvic brim and plotted with an **'O'**.
- **Uterine Contractions:** Assessed every half hour for frequency (number of contractions per 10 minutes) and duration in seconds.
    - _Mild:_ <20 seconds.
    - _Moderate:_ 20–60 seconds.
    - _Strong:_ >60 seconds.
    - _(Note: The strength of contractions is no longer quantified clinically in the new guide as it is difficult to quantify accurately)_.

**4. Maternal Condition (Woman)**

- **Pulse:** Recorded every 30 minutes.
- **Blood Pressure (BP):** Recorded every 1 hour.
- **Temperature:** Recorded every 2 hours.
- **Urine:** Output volume, and tested for protein and acetone.

**5. Medications & Shared Decision Making**

- **Medications:** Record of Oxytocin (U/L, drops/min), IV fluids, and any other drugs given.
- **Shared Decision Making:** A section to document the overall assessment by the caregiver and the care plan formulated in discussion with the woman.

**IV. ADVANTAGES OF USING A PARTOGRAPH**

1. Provides a comprehensive overview of necessary maternal, fetal, and labor information at a single glance.
2. Eliminates the need to repeatedly record labor events in lengthy clinical notes.
3. **Early Prediction of Abnormalities:** It can predict deviations from the normal progress of labor early (e.g., protracted active phase, secondary arrest), allowing for appropriate steps to be taken in time.
4. Facilitates a smooth and accurate handover procedure between shift changes of healthcare personnel.
5. **Improved Outcomes:** Its introduction has significantly reduced the incidence of prolonged labor and unnecessary cesarean section rates, leading to an overall improvement in maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality.



13.Complication of forceps delivery.
**COMPLICATIONS OF FORCEPS DELIVERY**

The complications of a forceps operation are mostly related to faulty technique and the underlying indication for which the forceps were applied, rather than the instrument itself. The complications are broadly classified into maternal and fetal, which are further divided into immediate and remote (late) effects.

**I. MATERNAL COMPLICATIONS**

**A. Immediate Complications**

- **Trauma to the Genital Tract:** Lacerations of the vagina or vaginal sulcus tears, cervical tears, and the extension of an episiotomy to involve the vaginal vault. There is also a significant risk (about 10%) of Obstetric Anal Sphincter Injury (OASI).
- **Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH) & Shock:** Hemorrhage may be traumatic (due to lacerations), atonic, or a combination of both. This excessive blood loss may require blood transfusion and can lead to maternal shock.
- **Nerve Injuries:** Compression or injury to the femoral nerve (L2, L3, L4) or the lumbosacral trunk (L4, L5), which is particularly associated with mid-forceps delivery.
- **Anesthetic Hazards:** Complications arising from the administration of local, regional, or general anesthesia required for the procedure.
- **Infection and Thromboembolism:** Increased risk of puerperal sepsis contributing to overall maternal morbidity, and an elevated risk of Venous Thromboembolism (VTE score 1).

**B. Remote (Late) Complications**

- **Pelvic Floor Dysfunction:** Weakening of pelvic supports leading to genital (pelvic organ) prolapse.
- **Sphincter and Bladder Issues:** Stress urinary incontinence, urinary retention, and anal sphincter dysfunction (fecal incontinence) resulting from perineal and sphincter injuries.
- **Chronic Pain:** Painful perineal scars leading to dyspareunia, and chronic low backache.

---

**II. FETAL AND NEONATAL COMPLICATIONS**

**A. Immediate Complications**

- **Soft Tissue Injuries:** Facial and scalp lacerations, abrasions, and bruising caused by the pressure of the metallic blades.
- **Nerve Injuries:** Peripheral facial nerve (cervical nerve VII) palsy. This occurs when the nerve, which remains unprotected after exiting the stylomastoid foramen, is compressed by the direct pressure of the forceps blades or affected by surrounding hemorrhage and edema.
- **Intracranial Injuries:** High risk of intracranial hemorrhage, specifically including the rupture of the great vein of Galen.
- **Bone and Joint Injuries:** Skull fractures, typically linear or depressed fractures of the cranial vault (frontal or parietal bones). Cervical spine injuries can also occur, particularly with the use of rotational forceps.
- **Other Neonatal Morbidities:** Birth asphyxia, neonatal jaundice, and cephalhematoma (though cephalhematoma is more frequently associated with ventouse/vacuum extraction).

**B. Remote (Late) Complications**

- **Neurological Deficits:** Cerebral palsy or spastic palsy resulting from residual cerebral injury, though this is a rare late sequela.

14. Classification of Anemia in pregnancy.

**CLASSIFICATION OF ANEMIA IN PREGNANCY**

Anemia in pregnancy is broadly classified into two main categories: physiological and pathological. For practical clinical management, a simplified classification based on the underlying etiology is highly useful.

**I. Physiological Anemia of Pregnancy** This is a normal physiological response occurring due to a disproportionate increase in plasma volume relative to the red blood cell mass, leading to a state of relative hemodilution.

**II. Pathological Anemia** Pathological anemia is further subdivided into the following categories based on the causative factors:

**A. Deficiency Anemia (Isolated or Combined)**

- **Iron deficiency:** The most common cause, accounting for 95% of cases.
- **Folic acid deficiency.**
- **Vitamin B12 deficiency.**
- **Protein deficiency.**

**B. Hemorrhagic Anemia**

- **Acute:** Occurs following bleeding in early months of pregnancy or due to Antepartum Hemorrhage (APH).
- **Chronic:** Results from continuous, low-grade blood loss such as hookworm infestation or bleeding piles.

**C. Hereditary (Hemoglobinopathies & Hemolytic)**

- Thalassemias.
- Sickle cell hemoglobinopathies.
- Other hemoglobinopathies.
- Hereditary hemolytic anemias (e.g., RBC membrane defects, spherocytosis).

**D. Bone Marrow Insufficiency**

- Bone marrow hypoplasia or aplasia caused by exposure to radiation or drugs (e.g., aspirin, indomethacin).

**E. Anemia of Infection**

- Secondary to chronic or acute parasitic/bacterial infections such as malaria, tuberculosis, or kala-azar.

**F. Chronic Disease or Neoplasm**

- Anemia resulting from chronic medical conditions (e.g., chronic renal disease) or neoplasms.

**G. Hematologic Malignancy**

- Blood cancers such as leukemias and lymphomas.

**H. Acquired Hemolytic Anemia**

- Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE).
- HELLP syndrome (Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelet count).
- Autoimmune hemolysis.
- Drug-induced G6PD (Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase) deficiency.


15. Bio physical profile.

**BIOPHYSICAL PROFILE (BPP)**

**I. DEFINITION AND PRINCIPLE** The Fetal Biophysical Profile (BPP) is a comprehensive non-invasive screening test used primarily for the assessment of uteroplacental insufficiency and fetal wellbeing.

- **Principle:** Fetal biophysical activities are initiated, modulated, and regulated by the fetal central nervous system (CNS). The fetal CNS is highly sensitive to diminished oxygenation. The cascade of pathophysiology is: Hypoxia $\rightarrow$ metabolic acidosis $\rightarrow$ CNS depression $\rightarrow$ observable changes (decrease or absence) in fetal biophysical activities.
- It is a widely accepted concept that the fetal biophysical activities that appear first during development are the last to disappear in the presence of fetal hypoxia. BPP correlates strongly with the fetal acid-base status.

**II. INDICATIONS AND TEST FREQUENCY**

- **Indications:** High-risk pregnancies (such as suspected intrauterine growth restriction, post-dated pregnancies, pre-eclampsia, etc.) and in cases of a non-reactive Non-Stress Test (NST).
- **Frequency:** It is typically performed weekly following a normal NST, and twice weekly after an abnormal test. In cases like post-dated pregnancy, BPP is recommended twice a week.

**III. COMPONENTS AND SCORING SYSTEM** The profile uses real-time ultrasonography and Cardiotocography (for NST) to evaluate five biophysical variables. According to the Manning modified scoring (1992), each parameter is observed over a 30-minute period and is awarded a score of 2 if normal, or 0 if abnormal (equivocal scores of 1 are not used).

**Table: Biophysical Profile Scoring Criteria**

|Parameters|Minimal Normal Criteria (Score = 2)|Abnormal Criteria (Score = 0)|
|:--|:--|:--|
|**1. Non-Stress Test (NST)**|Reactive pattern (at least 2 accelerations in a 20-minute period).|Non-reactive pattern.|
|**2. Fetal Breathing Movements**|$\ge$ 1 episode lasting > 30 seconds.|Absent or no episode of >30 seconds.|
|**3. Gross Body Movements**|$\ge$ 3 discrete body/limb movements.|< 3 discrete movements.|
|**4. Fetal Muscle Tone**|$\ge$ 1 episode of active extension (limb or trunk) with a return to flexion; opening and closing of the hand is considered normal.|Slow extension with no return to flexion, or absent movement.|
|**5. Amniotic Fluid Volume**|$\ge$ 1 pocket of fluid measuring 2 cm in two perpendicular planes ($2 \times 2$ cm pocket).|No pocket or a pocket < 2 cm.|

**IV. INTERPRETATION AND MANAGEMENT** BPP using real-time ultrasonography has a high predictive value. The false-negative rate of a normal BPP is exceptionally low at less than 0.1%.

- **Score 10/10 or 8/10:** Normal. Indicates a low risk of fetal asphyxia (about 1 per 1000). The current management protocol can be continued with routine monitoring.
- **Score 6/10:** Equivocal or suspicious. The test should be repeated, and management must be individualized based on the clinical scenario.
- **Score $\le$ 4/10:** Abnormal. Highly predictive of fetal asphyxia, warranting urgent intervention or delivery depending on fetal maturity.

**V. MODIFIED BIOPHYSICAL PROFILE**

- **Definition:** It is an abbreviated version of the standard BPP, consisting only of the Non-Stress Test (NST) and ultrasonographically determined Amniotic Fluid Index (AFI) or pocket measurement.
- **Clinical Value:** It takes much less time to perform compared to the full BPP. The modified BPP has a lower false-positive rate than using NST alone and possesses a high positive predictive value.
- **Abnormal Finding:** A modified BPP is considered abnormal if the NST is non-reactive or if there is oligohydramnios (amniotic fluid pocket < 2 cm and AFI $\le$ 5 cm), which often indicates the need for induction of labor or delivery.

16. Classical cesarean section.
**CLASSICAL CESAREAN SECTION**

**I. DEFINITION** A classical cesarean section is an operative procedure where the baby is extracted through a vertical (longitudinal) incision made in the upper segment of the uterus. In present-day obstetrics, its use is strictly limited to specific, forced circumstances.

**II. INDICATIONS** The indications for a classical cesarean section are restricted to situations where the lower uterine segment (LUS) approach is either difficult, risky, or impossible:

- **Lower Segment Approach is Difficult:**
    - Dense pelvic adhesions limiting access to the LUS.
    - Severe contracted pelvis (osteomalacic or rachitic) with a pendulous abdomen.
    - Preterm delivery (e.g., 26-28 weeks) where the lower segment is poorly formed or undeveloped.
- **Lower Segment Approach is Risky:**
    - Presence of a large fibroid in the lower segment (myomectomy during CS may lead to uncontrollable hemorrhage and hysterectomy).
    - Carcinoma of the cervix (to prevent the dissemination of the growth and postoperative sepsis).
    - Complete anterior placenta previa with engorged vessels in the lower segment, increasing the risk of massive hemorrhage.
    - Morbidly adherent placenta / Placenta Accreta Spectrum (PAS) where incising the placenta could cause massive hemorrhage.
- **Specific Fetal & Maternal Conditions:**
    - Transverse lie, particularly with a dorso-inferior position.
    - Planned cesarean hysterectomy.
    - Perimortem cesarean section (done within 5 minutes of maternal cardiopulmonary arrest to save the infant).
    - Repair of a high vesicovaginal fistula (VVF).

**III. ADVANTAGES (Over Lower Segment Cesarean Section)**

- The operation can be performed much more quickly.
- The baby can be delivered without disturbing the placenta, completely eliminating the risk of fetal exsanguination.
- In cases of placenta accreta, the placenta can be left _in situ_ if there is no bleeding, allowing for uterine preservation.

**IV. DISADVANTAGES & COMPLICATIONS**

- **Intraoperative:** Blood loss is significantly higher compared to LSCS.
- **Wound Healing:** The thick upper segment muscle layer is difficult to appose perfectly. The upper segment actively contracts and retracts, causing sutures to loosen, leading to imperfect healing and "gutter" formation on the inner surface.
- **Postoperative:** Perfect peritonization is not possible, leading to more subsequent intra-abdominal adhesions and bands. There is a higher risk of peritonitis if uterine sepsis is present.
- **Future Pregnancy:** The classical scar is inherently weak. The risk of scar rupture is 4-9% (5-10 times higher than an LSCS scar). Unpredictable rupture can occur not only during labor but also during the later months of pregnancy.

**V. SURGICAL STEPS**

1. **Abdominal Incision:** A longitudinal (paramedian) incision of about 15 cm (6 inches) is made, with one-third of the incision extending above the umbilicus.
2. **Uterine Incision:** A longitudinal incision of about 12.5 cm (5 inches) is made in the midline of the anterior wall of the uterus, starting below the fundus. The incision is deepened until the membranes are exposed and then punctured.
3. **Delivery of the Baby:** The baby is most commonly delivered by breech extraction.
4. **Delivery of the Placenta:** Following the delivery of the baby, intravenous Oxytocin 5 IU or Methergine 0.2 mg is administered. The uterus is eventrated (drawn out of the abdomen), and the placenta is extracted by cord traction or manual removal.
5. **Suturing the Uterus:** The thick upper segment is closed in three distinct layers:
    - _First Layer:_ A continuous suture using chromic catgut No. '0' or vicryl taking deep muscles, aiming to exclude the decidua.
    - _Second Layer:_ Interrupted sutures (1 cm apart) using chromic catgut No. '1' or vicryl, taking the entire depth of the superficial muscles down to the first layer.
    - _Third Layer:_ A continuous suture taking the serous coat and adjacent muscles using chromic catgut No. '0' with a round-bodied needle.
6. **Closure:** The uterus is returned to the abdominal cavity, peritoneal toileting is performed, packs are removed, and the abdomen is closed in layers.


7. Role of ante natal corticosteroids.
**ROLE OF ANTENATAL CORTICOSTEROIDS**

**I. INTRODUCTION AND RATIONALE** Antenatal corticosteroids (glucocorticoids) are administered to pregnant women at risk of preterm delivery to accelerate fetal organ maturation, primarily the lungs. The administration of these steroids significantly improves neonatal survival and reduces serious morbidities associated with prematurity.

**II. MECHANISM OF ACTION**

- **Surfactant Synthesis:** Cortisol acts directly on the type II pneumocytes in the fetal lung alveoli to stimulate the synthesis of pulmonary surfactant (phospholipids like phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylglycerol).
- **Alveolar Stabilization:** Surfactant lowers the surface tension of the lung fluid at the air-water interface, ensuring the alveoli can be opened easily when the newborn starts breathing, thereby preventing widespread atelectasis and hypoxia.
- **Systemic Maturation:** Apart from the lungs, antenatal corticosteroids also accelerate the maturation of the fetal cardiovascular system, gastrointestinal tract, and central nervous system (CNS).

**III. INDICATIONS AND TIMING**

- **Gestational Age:** Antenatal corticosteroids are highly recommended for pregnant women when preterm delivery is anticipated before 34 weeks of gestation.
- **Obstetric Conditions:** It is indicated in various high-risk conditions threatening early delivery, including:
    - Spontaneous preterm labor.
    - Prelabor Rupture of Membranes (PROM / PPROM).
    - Severe pre-eclampsia and HELLP syndrome.
    - Antepartum hemorrhage (Placenta previa, Placental abruption).
    - Fetal Growth Restriction (FGR).
    - Multiple pregnancies (twins) facing preterm delivery.
    - Red cell alloimmunization (Rh-incompatibility) needing early termination.

**IV. DRUG REGIMENS AND DOSAGE** Both Betamethasone and Dexamethasone cross the placenta effectively and possess identical biological activity. Betamethasone is generally considered the steroid of choice.

1. **Betamethasone (Betnesol):** 12 mg administered intramuscularly (IM) every 24 hours for a total of two doses.
2. **Dexamethasone:** 6 mg administered intramuscularly (IM) every 12 hours for a total of four doses.

- **Rescue Therapy:** A "rescue course" of corticosteroids should be administered if the woman remains pregnant before 34 weeks and her first course was given more than two weeks earlier.

**V. BENEFITS OF ANTENATAL CORTICOSTEROIDS** **A. Fetal and Neonatal Benefits:**

- **Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS):** Reduces the incidence of neonatal RDS significantly (by up to 70%).
- **Intracranial Hemorrhage:** Markedly reduces the risk of Periventricular Hemorrhage (PVH) and Intraventricular Hemorrhage (IVH).
- **Gastrointestinal:** Decreases the incidence of Necrotizing Enterocolitis (NEC).
- **Other Morbidities:** Reduces the risks of Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia (BPD) and Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA).
- **Overall Mortality:** Leads to a significant decrease in overall neonatal mortality.

**B. Maternal Benefits (Condition-Specific):**

- In women with HELLP syndrome, corticosteroid administration improves maternal outcomes by increasing the thrombocyte (platelet) count and urinary output.

**VI. TIMEFRAME OF EFFICACY**

- The therapeutic benefits of antenatal corticosteroids are obtained after 24 hours of initiating the therapy.
- The maximum protective effect continues for 7 days after administration.

**VII. RISKS, SIDE EFFECTS, AND PRECAUTIONS**

- **Maternal Diabetes Mellitus:** Steroids induce insulin resistance and can cause hyperglycemia. Diabetic mothers require strict monitoring and insulin dose readjustment. Furthermore, fetal hyperinsulinism can block the maturational action of cortisol on the fetal lungs.
- **Infection:** Corticosteroids possess immunosuppressive properties. Any pre-existing maternal infection may flare up following administration. However, it does not increase the background risk of maternal or neonatal sepsis in cases of PROM.
- **Fetal Activity:** May cause a transient reduction in fetal breathing and body movements.
- **Maternal Hematology:** Produces a transient increase in maternal White Blood Cell (WBC) count for about 48 hours following administration.


18. Ischial spine and its importance in obstetrics.

**ISCHIAL SPINE AND ITS IMPORTANCE IN OBSTETRICS**

**ANATOMY** The ischial spines are bony prominences extending from the ischial bones into the pelvic cavity. They represent a critical anatomical landmark delineating the midpelvic plane. The distance between the tips of the two ischial spines forms the **bispinous (interspinous) diameter**, which measures 10.5 cm and is the shortest diameter of the pelvis in the transverse plane.

**OBSTETRICAL IMPORTANCE**

**1. Determination of Fetal Station (Engagement and Descent)**

- The level of the ischial spines is situated exactly halfway between the pelvic inlet and the pelvic outlet.
- This level is designated as **"Station Zero (0)"**.
- The progressive descent of the fetal head during labor is assessed in relation to the ischial spines. It is stated in minus figures if the presenting part is above the spines (e.g., -1 cm, -2 cm) and in plus figures if it is below the spines (e.g., +1 cm, +2 cm).
- When the lower pole of the unmolded fetal head reaches the level of the ischial spines (station '0'), the head is clinically considered to be **engaged**.

**2. Landmark for Internal Rotation**

- The level of the ischial spines corresponds to the plane of least pelvic dimensions (the narrow pelvic plane) and the obstetrical outlet.
- It is specifically at this plane that the **internal rotation** of the fetal head occurs during the mechanism of normal labor.
- The obstetrical pelvic axis, through which the fetus negotiates the pelvis, is directed downward and backward up to the level of the ischial spines, after which it turns and is directed abruptly forward.

**3. Landmark for Pudendal Nerve Block**

- The pudendal nerve (S2-S4) leaves the pelvic cavity and re-enters the ischiorectal fossa by winding around the ischial spine.
- The ischial spine serves as the primary palpable landmark for administering a transvaginal pudendal nerve block (used for outlet forceps, ventouse, or episiotomy). The operator's fingers are placed on the tip of the ischial spine, and the needle is guided to pierce the sacrospinous ligament just above it.

**4. Clinical Pelvimetry and Midpelvic Contraction**

- During clinical assessment of the pelvis in early labor, the ischial spines are palpated.
- Normally, they are smooth and everted. However, if the spines are prominent, they encroach upon the pelvic cavity and severely diminish the available space in the midpelvis, acting as a major indicator of midpelvic contraction.

**5. Diagnosis of Cephalopelvic Disproportion (CPD)**

- In the **Muller-Munro Kerr (abdominovaginal) method** for evaluating CPD, the examining vaginal fingers are placed exactly at the level of the ischial spines to determine if the externally pushed fetal head can descend to this level. If the head can be pushed down to the spines without overlapping the symphysis pubis, there is no disproportion.

**6. Indicator for Operative Intervention**

- In breech presentations, if there is an arrest of the breech at or above the level of the ischial spines, it indicates fetopelvic disproportion or a big baby, making it a definitive indication for delivery by Cesarean section.
- Deep transverse arrest (DTA) of the fetal head typically occurs at the level of prominent ischial spines.

**7. Uterovaginal Support**

- The pericervical ring and uterosacral ligaments attach to the cervix and stabilize it posteriorly exactly at the level of the ischial spines, playing a crucial role in maintaining uterine position and preventing pelvic organ prolapse.

19. Classification of placenta previa.
**CLASSIFICATION OF PLACENTA PREVIA**

**I. BASIS OF CLASSIFICATION** Placenta previa is classified based on the degree of extension of the placenta into the lower uterine segment and its relationship to the internal cervical os. With the advent of modern imaging, transvaginal ultrasonography (TVS) provides the most precise and accurate placental localization.

**II. REVISED CLASSIFICATION (ACOG, ACR, 2014)** The contemporary, universally accepted classification simplifies the condition into two main types based on ultrasound findings:

- **True Placenta Previa:** The placenta completely covers the internal cervical os.
- **Low-Lying Placenta:** The placental edge lies within 2 cm of the internal cervical os but does not completely cover it. _(Note: If the placental edge is >2 cm from the undilated internal cervical os on transvaginal ultrasound, it is no longer classified as placenta previa)._

**III. DANGEROUS PLACENTA PREVIA (TYPE II POSTERIOR)** In traditional grading systems (Types I through IV), a specific anatomical variant holds immense clinical and surgical significance:

- **Definition:** It is the name given to a "Type II Posterior" placenta previa, where the major thickness of the placenta (about 2.5 cm) overlies the sacral promontory.
- **Obstetric Significance:** Due to the curved nature of the birth canal, the thick placental mass diminishes the anteroposterior diameter of the pelvic inlet.
- **Complications:** This reduction in diameter prevents the engagement of the fetal presenting part. Consequently, the fetal head cannot descend to effectively compress the separated placenta against the pelvic tissues, which hinders the mechanical control of bleeding and leads to profuse, life-threatening maternal hemorrhage.

**IV. CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS OF CLASSIFICATION ON DELIVERY**

- **Cesarean Delivery:** Women diagnosed with True Placenta Previa or those with a low-lying placenta (where the placental edge remains within 2 cm of the internal os) must be delivered by Cesarean section.
- **Vaginal Delivery:** A trial of vaginal delivery may only be considered if sonography clearly demonstrates that the placental edge is more than 2 cm away from the internal cervical os.


20. Anti D prophylaxis.
**ANTI-D PROPHYLAXIS**

**I. DEFINITION AND PRINCIPLE** Anti-D prophylaxis refers to the administration of Rh anti-D immunoglobulin (IgG) to an unimmunized Rh-negative mother to prevent active alloimmunization against the Rh-D antigen. Active immunization occurs when Rh-positive fetal red blood cells enter the maternal circulation during pregnancy or delivery.

**II. MECHANISM OF ACTION** The mode of action is through Antibody-Mediated Immune Suppression (AMIS). The proposed mechanisms to prevent maternal antibody formation are:

- The administered anti-D antibody blocks the Rh-antigen sites on the fetal red blood cells.
- The intact antibody-coated fetal red cells are rapidly recognized and removed from the maternal circulation by the reticuloendothelial system, such as the spleen or lymph nodes.
- Central inhibition occurs where the coated fetal red cells interfere with the production of IgG from the maternal B cells.

**III. INDICATIONS FOR ANTI-D IMMUNOGLOBULIN** Anti-D immunoglobulin is indicated for Rh-negative, unimmunized women during scenarios associated with potential fetomaternal hemorrhage. These indications include:

- **Routine Postpartum:** Following the delivery of an Rh-positive infant, provided the baby's Direct Coombs' test is negative.
- **Routine Antenatal Anti-D Prophylaxis (RAADP):** Administered universally at around 28 weeks of gestation.
- **Early Pregnancy Complications:** Miscarriage, medical termination of pregnancy (MTP), ectopic pregnancy, and hydatidiform mole.
- **Invasive Diagnostic or Therapeutic Procedures:** Genetic amniocentesis, chorion villus biopsy (CVS), cordocentesis, and embryo reduction.
- **Antepartum Sensitizing Events:** Placenta previa with bleeding, external cephalic version (ECV), abdominal trauma, and intrauterine fetal death (IUFD).
- **Other Scenarios:** After inadvertent transfusion of Rh-D-positive blood, or in women presenting with a "weak D phenotype".

**IV. DOSAGE AND ADMINISTRATION** The administration of anti-D immunoglobulin is via the intramuscular (IM) route.

- **Postpartum Prophylaxis:** The standard dose is 300 $\mu$g (1500 IU) IM administered within 72 hours (preferably earlier) following childbirth. This standard dose protects against a fetomaternal hemorrhage of up to 30 mL of fetal whole blood or 15 mL of fetal red cells. If inadvertently omitted, it may be given as late as 28 days postpartum to prevent sensitization.
- **First Trimester (< 12 weeks):** A lower dose of 50 $\mu$g IM is administered within 72 hours following induced or spontaneous abortion, ectopic/molar pregnancy, or CVS.
- **Second and Third Trimester Complications (> 12 weeks):** The full dose of 300 $\mu$g must be administered.
- **Routine Antenatal Prophylaxis (RAADP):** A prophylactic dose of 300 $\mu$g is given around 28 weeks. Since the half-life of Rh-IG is about 16-24 days, the maternal antibody titer will be very low at term; thus, another 300 $\mu$g dose must be given postpartum within 72 hours of delivery.

**V. ESTIMATION OF MASSIVE FETOMATERNAL HEMORRHAGE**

- **Kleihauer-Betke Test:** To accurately calculate the dose in cases of suspected massive fetomaternal hemorrhage, the volume of fetal blood in the maternal circulation is estimated using the Kleihauer-Betke test (acid elution technique). Fetal erythrocytes contain HbF, which resists acid elution and appears red, whereas adult RBCs appear as clear "ghosts".
- **Dose Calculation:** If the fetomaternal hemorrhage volume is determined to be greater than 30 mL of whole blood, the required dose of Rh-immune globulin is calculated as 10 $\mu$g for every 1 mL of fetal whole blood.
- An Indirect Coombs test can be performed after administration; a positive result indicates there is an adequate excess of anti-D immunoglobulin in the maternal serum, confirming the given dose was sufficient.

**VI. EFFICACY**

- Standard postpartum administration of anti-D immunoglobulin within 72 hours reduces the alloimmunization rate by 90%.
- The addition of Routine Antenatal Anti-D Prophylaxis (RAADP) at 28 weeks further reduces the background risk of third-trimester alloimmunization from 2% down to just 0.1%.

21. Advantages of breast feeding.

**ADVANTAGES OF BREASTFEEDING**

Breastfeeding is considered the "Gold standard" for infant feeding, offering numerous biological superiorities over artificial (formula) milk. The advantages can be broadly categorized into benefits for the infant and benefits for the mother.

**I. NUTRITIONAL ADVANTAGES (COMPOSITION)** Breast milk is an ideal food that is readily available at body temperature and free of cost. It provides easy digestion with a low osmotic load.

- **Carbohydrates:** Mainly consists of lactose, which stimulates the growth of intestinal flora and produces organic acids necessary for the synthesis of vitamin B.
- **Fats:** Contains smaller fat globules that are better emulsified and easier to digest.
- **Proteins:** Rich in lactalbumin and lactoglobulin, with a lower casein content.
- **Minerals:** Has a low osmotic load of electrolytes ($K^+$, $Ca^{2+}$, $Na^+$, $Cl^-$), which places less burden on the immature neonatal kidneys.
- **Vitamins:** Supplies essential nutrients and vitamins, including Vitamin D, which promotes bone growth and protects the baby against rickets.

**II. IMMUNOLOGICAL AND PROTECTIVE BENEFITS (FOR THE INFANT)** Breast milk contains potent anti-infective and anti-inflammatory properties.

- **Antibodies:** Rich in immunoglobulins (Secretory IgA, IgM, IgG) which provide passive immunological defense against infections.
- **Cellular and Humoral Factors:** Contains leukocytes, macrophages, lymphocytes, lysozyme, lactoferrin, interferon, and lactoperoxidase which inhibit the growth of infective agents.
- **Disease Reduction:** Significantly reduces the incidence and severity of pneumonia, otitis media, gastroenterocolitis, and necrotizing enterocolitis.
- **Allergies:** Reduces the risk of allergic diseases, atopic dermatitis, and atopic eczema.
- **Other Protective Effects:** Has a natural laxative action, lessens the incidence of sore buttocks, and reduces the risk of scurvy.

**III. LONG-TERM DEVELOPMENTAL BENEFITS (FOR THE INFANT)**

- **Neurological Development:** Contains long-chain omega-3 fatty acids that are essential for optimal neurological development. It also improves the Intelligent Quotient (IQ), especially in premature infants.
- **Somatic Growth:** Enhances and promotes optimal somatic growth.
- **Chronic Disease Prevention:** Lowers the long-term risks of Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS), childhood obesity, childhood asthma, and childhood type 1 diabetes.

**IV. MATERNAL BENEFITS**

- **Natural Contraception:** Acts as a natural method of family planning known as the Lactational Amenorrhea Method (LAM). Exclusive breastfeeding on demand (including night nursing) provides 98% contraceptive protection for the first 6 months postpartum.
- **Uterine Involution:** The release of oxytocin during suckling helps in the faster involution of the puerperal uterus.
- **Psychological Benefits:** Promotes excellent mother-child bonding, cuddling, and emotional attachment.
- **Reduced Cancer Risk:** The hormonal changes associated with lactation (low levels of estrogen and progesterone combined with hyperprolactinemia) reduce the long-term risk of female reproductive cancers, including breast cancer.
- **Metabolic Health:** Associated with less insulin resistance and a decreased risk of developing diabetes mellitus.
- **Economic:** It is a highly cost-effective and convenient method of infant feeding.



22. USG in first trimester of pregnancy.
**ULTRASONOGRAPHY (USG) IN FIRST TRIMESTER OF PREGNANCY**

**I. INTRODUCTION & MODALITY** Ultrasonography is the most valuable and safe diagnostic tool in early pregnancy. Transvaginal Sonography (TVS) using a 5–7 MHz high-frequency probe is superior to Transabdominal Sonography (TAS) for first-trimester evaluation because the uterus remains a pelvic organ during this time, allowing for better image resolution.

**II. INDICATIONS** The common indications for first-trimester USG include:

- Confirmation of intrauterine pregnancy and fetal viability.
- Accurate dating of gestational age.
- Evaluation of vaginal bleeding or suspected early pregnancy failure.
- Diagnosis of suspected ectopic or molar pregnancy.
- Determination of chorionicity and amnionicity in multiple pregnancies.
- Screening for fetal aneuploidy (11–14 weeks) and gross fetal anomalies (e.g., anencephaly).
- Evaluation of concomitant pelvic or adnexal masses.

**III. CHRONOLOGICAL APPEARANCE OF FETAL STRUCTURES (ON TVS)**

- **4 weeks:** Choriodecidual thickness, chorionic sac.
- **4.5 – 5 weeks:** Gestation sac is visible (Mean Sac Diameter ~5 mm).
- **5.5 weeks:** Yolk sac appears.
- **6 weeks:** Fetal pole with visible cardiac activity (CRL $\ge$ 4–5 mm).
- **7 weeks:** Embryonic movements, midgut herniation, lower limb buds.
- **8 weeks:** Upper limb buds, stomach.
- **9 weeks:** Spine and choroid plexus.
- **10 weeks:** Fetal heart rate reliably picked up by Doppler.

**IV. KEY PARAMETERS EVALUATED**

**1. Gestational Sac (GS):**

- Appears as an eccentrically located anechoic space within the endometrium.
- **Double Decidua Sign:** The interface between the decidua and chorion appears as two distinct layers, confirming a true gestational sac and differentiating it from the centrally located "pseudogestational sac" of an ectopic pregnancy.
- GS diameter increases by approximately 1.1 mm per day.

**2. Yolk Sac:**

- It is the first structure normally seen within the GS.
- **Significance:** It confirms an intrauterine pregnancy, differentiates a true GS from a pseudosac, and plays a role in early embryonic nutrition and hematopoiesis. The number of yolk sacs corresponds to the number of amniotic sacs, aiding in determining amnionicity in twins.

**3. Crown-Rump Length (CRL):**

- Measured from the top of the head (vertex) to the bottom of the rump (coccyx), excluding the limbs.
- **Significance:** It is the most accurate sonographic parameter for estimating gestational age and dating the pregnancy, with a minimal variability of $\pm$ 5 days.

**4. Embryonic Cardiac Activity:**

- The tubular embryonic heart starts beating at 36–37 days and is almost always visualized when the CRL is > 5 mm.
- Normal embryonic heart rate is > 100 beats per minute (bpm). FHR < 80 bpm is a sign of poor prognosis.

**V. DIAGNOSTIC APPLICATIONS IN PATHOLOGICAL CONDITIONS**

**1. Early Pregnancy Failure (Miscarriage):** USG criteria diagnostic of early pregnancy failure include:

- **Anembryonic Sac (Blighted Ovum):** Mean Sac Diameter (MSD) $\ge$ 25 mm with no visible embryo.
- **Missed Miscarriage:** CRL > 7 mm with no visible cardiac motion.
- Other ominous signs include a collapsing sac with an irregularly marginated amnion, or yolk sac calcification.

**2. Ectopic Pregnancy:**

- Diagnostic criteria include an empty uterine cavity alongside a positive $\beta$-hCG test.
- Presence of an adnexal mass separate from the ovary, or echogenic fluid (blood) in the Pouch of Douglas.
- **Color Doppler:** Reveals a "ring-of-fire" pattern (enhanced blood flow) around the extrauterine gestational sac.

**3. Aneuploidy Screening (11–14 Weeks):**

- **Nuchal Translucency (NT):** Measurement of the fluid-filled space at the back of the fetal neck. An NT thickness of $\ge$ 3 mm is abnormal and acts as a strong soft marker for chromosomal anomalies (Trisomy 21, 18, 13), cardiac defects, and skeletal dysplasia.
- **Nasal Bone:** Absence of the nasal bone at 10-14 weeks is highly associated with Down syndrome (Trisomy 21).

**4. Multiple Pregnancy:**

- Identifies the number of fetuses and is the best time to determine **chorionicity**, which dictates the obstetric outcome.
- **Dichorionic Diamniotic (DCDA):** Thick intertwin membrane (>2 mm) forming a "Twin peak" or "Lambda" sign.
- **Monochorionic Diamniotic (MCDA):** Thin intertwin membrane (<2 mm) forming a "T sign".

**5. Molar Pregnancy (Hydatidiform Mole):**

- Presents as an echogenic mass with multiple anechoic cystic spaces filling the uterine cavity.
- Produces a characteristic **"snowstorm" appearance** on USG. Fetal parts and amniotic sac are absent in a complete mole, but a fetus may be present in a partial mole.
