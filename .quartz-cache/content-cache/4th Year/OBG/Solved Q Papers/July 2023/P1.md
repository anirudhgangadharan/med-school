1. Define APH (Antepartum Hemorrhage). Discuss the causes, clinical features, diagnosis and management of placenta previa.
**ANTEPARTUM HEMORRHAGE (APH)**

**Definition** Antepartum hemorrhage is defined as bleeding from or into the genital tract after the 20th week of pregnancy but before the birth of the baby (includes the first and second stages of labor). The 28th week is arbitrarily taken as the lower limit of fetal viability. It complicates about 3% of all hospital deliveries.

**Causes of APH**

- **Placental bleeding (70%):** Placenta previa (35%) and Abruptio placentae (35%).
- **Unexplained or Indeterminate (25%):** Vasa previa, bleeding from a succenturiate lobe.
- **Extra-placental causes (5%):** Local cervicovaginal lesions (cervical polyp, carcinoma cervix, varicose veins, local trauma, cervical ectopy).

---

**PLACENTA PREVIA**

**Definition** Placenta previa is a condition where the placenta is implanted partially or completely over the lower uterine segment (over and adjacent to the internal os).

**Etiopathogenesis and Risk Factors** The exact cause is unknown, but the following theories are postulated:

- **Dropping down theory:** The fertilized ovum drops down and implants in the lower segment due to poor decidual reaction in the upper segment.
- **Persistence of chorionic activity** in the decidua capsularis coming in contact with the decidua vera of the lower segment.
- **Defective decidua:** Chorionic villi spread over a wide area for nourishment, encroaching onto the lower segment.
- **Hyperplacentosis:** A large surface area of the placenta (e.g., in multiple pregnancies) encroaches onto the lower segment.

**High-Risk Factors:**

- Multiparity and Advanced maternal age (>35 years).
- Prior Cesarean Delivery (CD) or uterine surgery (myomectomy, hysterotomy).
- Prior placenta previa or prior curettage.
- Multiple pregnancy and pregnancy following Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART).
- Smoking (causes placental hypertrophy to compensate for hypoxemia).

**Clinical Features** **Symptoms:**

- **Vaginal Bleeding:** The classical triad of bleeding in placenta previa is **sudden onset, painless, apparently causeless, and recurrent**.
- **Warning Hemorrhage:** A history of a slight initial bout of bleeding is present in about one-third of cases.
- The blood is bright red in color.
- Bleeding is unassociated with pain unless labor starts simultaneously.

**Signs:**

- **General Examination:** General condition, shock, and pallor are proportionate to the visible blood loss.
- **Abdominal Examination:**
    - Uterine size is proportionate to the period of gestation.
    - The uterus feels soft, relaxed, and elastic without any localized tenderness.
    - Malpresentations (breech, transverse, unstable lie) are common.
    - The presenting part (head) is high and floating, and cannot be pushed down into the pelvis.
    - Fetal Heart Sound (FHS) is usually present. _Stallworthy’s sign_ (slowing of FHR on pressing the head down into the pelvis) may be seen.
- **Vulval Inspection:** Bright red blood is visible.
- **Vaginal Examination:** **Strictly contraindicated** in a diagnosed or suspected case of placenta previa, as it can provoke severe and fatal hemorrhage.

**Diagnosis** Diagnosis is primarily clinical (painless bleeding) and confirmed by imaging:

- **Transvaginal Sonography (TVS):** The diagnostic technique of choice. It is safe, highly accurate (virtually 100%), and superior to transabdominal ultrasound as it provides better resolution and avoids the discomfort of a full bladder.
- **Transabdominal Sonography (TAS):** Accuracy after the 30th week is high, but false positives can occur due to a full bladder or myometrial contractions.
- **Transperineal Sonography (TPS):** Internal os visualized in 97-100% of cases.
- **Color Doppler / 3D Power Doppler:** Used to detect hypervascularity and rule out morbidly adherent placenta (Placenta Accreta Spectrum).
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** Superior to USG for diagnosing posterior placenta previa and assessing the depth of myometrial/bladder invasion in placenta accreta.
- **Clinical Confirmation:** Double set-up vaginal examination in the OT (rarely done now) or direct visualization during cesarean section.

**Management** **1. Immediate Attention (At Home & Transfer):**

- Put the patient to bed immediately.
- Assess blood loss by inspecting soaked clothing.
- **Do NOT perform a vaginal examination**.
- Transfer the patient to a well-equipped tertiary hospital (with blood bank and NICU) accompanied by an IV drip of Ringer's solution.

**2. On Admission to Hospital:**

- Assess hemodynamic status (pulse, BP, pallor).
- Draw blood for grouping, cross-matching, hemoglobin, hematocrit, and coagulation profile.
- Start a large-bore IV cannula with crystalloids.
- Confirm diagnosis via Ultrasound and evaluate fetal status (FHR, CTG).

**3. Formulation of the Line of Treatment:** Management depends on the gestational age, extent of hemorrhage, and maternal/fetal status.

**A. Expectant Management (Macafee and Johnson Regime):**

- _Objective:_ To prolong pregnancy for fetal lung maturity without compromising maternal health.
- _Prerequisites:_ Blood must be available, and 24-hour emergency Cesarean section facilities must be present.
- _Selection Criteria:_ Mother is hemodynamically stable, pregnancy is <37 weeks, no active vaginal bleeding, and reassuring fetal status.
- _Conduct:_ Strict bed rest, periodic inspection of vulval pads, correct anemia (hematinics/transfusion), administer prophylactic Anti-D immunoglobulin if the mother is Rh-negative, and administer Corticosteroids (Betamethasone) to accelerate fetal lung maturity if <34 weeks.

**B. Active (Definitive) Management:**

- _Indications:_ Pregnancy is ≥37 weeks, patient is in active labor, exsanguinating hemorrhage on admission, continuing moderate bleeding, or nonreassuring fetal status/dead fetus.
- _Mode of Delivery:_
    - **Cesarean Delivery (CD):** The method of choice for almost all cases, especially if the placental edge is within 2 cm of the internal os, or if the placenta is posterior or thick. A vertical uterine incision may be preferred if the placenta is anteriorly situated to prevent massive fetal and maternal bleeding.
    - **Vaginal Delivery:** May be considered only if the placental edge is clearly 2-3 cm away from the internal cervical os. Can be facilitated by Low Rupture of Membranes (ARM) with or without oxytocin.

**Management of Intraoperative Complications (PPH):**

- Because the lower segment is poorly retractile, Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH) is a major risk.
- Administer aggressive oxytocics (Oxytocin, Methergine, Carbetocin).
- Bleeding sinuses on the placental bed may need to be oversewn.
- In cases of intractable hemorrhage or Placenta Accreta Spectrum (PAS), uterine packing (Bakri balloon), bilateral internal iliac artery ligation, uterine artery embolization, or emergency Cesarean Hysterectomy must be performed.

2. Define preterm labor. Explain the etiology and management of preterm labor.

Here is a comprehensive, high-yield overview of preterm labor formatted for RGUHS examinations.

### **PRETERM LABOR**

**DEFINITION**

- Preterm labor (PTL) is defined as labor that starts after the period of viability (20–28 weeks) but before the 37th completed week (<259 days) of gestation, calculated from the first day of the last menstrual period (LMP).
- The lower limit of viability varies globally; it is typically 20 weeks in developed countries and 26–28 weeks in low- and middle-income (LMI) countries.

---

**ETIOLOGY AND HIGH-RISK FACTORS** The exact cause of preterm labor is unknown in about 50% of cases and is often multifactorial. The etiopathogenesis involves fetal HPA axis activation, pathologic uterine distension, decidual/membrane activation, and infection.

The high-risk factors can be classified as follows:

**1. Maternal Factors:**

- **Past Obstetric History:** Previous history of preterm delivery, induced or spontaneous abortion, or pregnancy following Assisted Reproductive Techniques (ART).
- **Pregnancy Complications:** Pre-eclampsia, antepartum hemorrhage (placenta previa or abruption), multiple pregnancy, polyhydramnios, and premature rupture of membranes (PROM).
- **Medical and Surgical Illnesses:** Acute fever, acute pyelonephritis, severe anemia, chronic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, nephritis, decompensated heart disease), periodontal disease, and low body mass index (BMI). Abdominal surgery or cervical operations (conization, amputation) during pregnancy.
- **Uterine Anomalies:** Cervical incompetence or uterine malformations (e.g., septate/bicornuate uterus).
- **Genital Tract Infections:** Bacterial vaginosis (causes a 4-fold rise in risk), Group B Streptococcus, Chlamydia, Bacteroides, and Mycoplasma.
- **Socio-demographic Factors:** Smoking habits, low socioeconomic and nutritional status, high maternal stress, and genetic predisposition (e.g., the mother herself was born preterm).

**2. Fetal Factors:**

- Multiple pregnancy, congenital malformations, and intrauterine fetal death (IUFD).

**3. Placental Factors:**

- Placental infarction, thrombosis, placenta previa, or abruptio placentae.

**4. Iatrogenic Causes:**

- Indicated preterm delivery due to severe medical complications (e.g., severe pre-eclampsia) or obstetric emergencies (e.g., APH).

**5. Idiopathic:**

- Unexplained premature effacement of the cervix, irritable uterus, and early engagement of the fetal head.

---

**MANAGEMENT OF PRETERM LABOR** The primary goals of management are to delay delivery for at least 48 hours to allow antenatal corticosteroids to enhance fetal lung maturity, to facilitate _in utero_ transfer to a center equipped with a Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU), and to prevent birth asphyxia and trauma.

**1. General Measures & Prevention:**

- **Bed Rest & Hydration:** Left lateral position to improve uteroplacental perfusion and adequate intravenous hydration.
- **Prophylactic Cerclage:** History-indicated cerclage (12–14 weeks) or ultrasound-indicated cerclage (16–24 weeks) for women with a short cervix (<25 mm).
- **Progesterone Supplementation:** Used prophylactically in high-risk cases with a short cervix to prolong pregnancy.

**2. Pharmacological Management:**

- **Tocolytic Agents:** Drugs (e.g., nifedipine, betamimetics, or progesterone) are used on a short-term basis (1–3 days) to arrest uterine contractions and buy time for corticosteroid administration. _Note: Tocolytics are contraindicated in uncontrolled diabetes, severe hypertension, cardiac disease, and APH_.
- **Antenatal Corticosteroids:** Indicated when gestational age is <34 weeks. Administer Betamethasone (12 mg IM, 2 doses 24 hours apart) or Dexamethasone (6 mg IM every 12 hours for 4 doses). This significantly reduces the risks of neonatal Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS), Intraventricular Hemorrhage (IVH), Necrotizing Enterocolitis (NEC), and Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA).
- **Neuroprotection:** Intravenous Magnesium Sulfate (MgSO4) is administered to the mother if delivery is expected before 32 weeks, to reduce the risk of neonatal cerebral palsy.
- **Antibiotics:** Prophylactic broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g., Ampicillin, Cefazoline, or Clindamycin) are given to prevent neonatal Group B Streptococcus (GBS) infection.

**3. Management in Labor:**

- **Place of Delivery:** Must occur in a facility with a well-equipped NICU.
- **Monitoring:** Continuous electronic fetal monitoring (EFM) is highly recommended due to the fetus's vulnerability to hypoxia.
- **Mode of Delivery:** Vaginal delivery is preferred unless there are specific obstetric indications for a Cesarean delivery (e.g., malpresentation, non-reassuring fetal status). Routine Cesarean delivery is not recommended for preterm labor alone.
- **Delivery Technique:** Ensure minimal trauma during expulsion. Episiotomy should be liberal, and prophylactic forceps/ventouse may be used to curtail the second stage of labor and prevent intracranial stress.
- **Post-Delivery:** Delayed cord clamping (60–90 seconds) is advised to give the neonate the benefit of a higher hematocrit and circulating blood volume. An experienced neonatologist must be present for immediate resuscitation.

3. Fetal circulation.
**FETAL CIRCULATION**

**INTRODUCTION AND UNIQUE FEATURES** The fetal circulation is distinctly adapted for intrauterine life where the lungs are non-functional for gas exchange and the placenta acts as the primary organ for respiration, nutrition, and excretion.

Five unique structures (shunts) exist in the fetal circulation that bypass the liver and lungs:

1. **Umbilical Vein:** Carries oxygenated blood from the placenta to the fetus.
2. **Ductus Venosus:** Bypasses the hepatic circulation, connecting the umbilical vein directly to the Inferior Vena Cava (IVC).
3. **Foramen Ovale:** Shunts highly oxygenated blood from the right atrium directly into the left atrium.
4. **Ductus Arteriosus:** Shunts deoxygenated blood from the pulmonary trunk into the descending aorta, bypassing the lungs.
5. **Umbilical Arteries (two):** Carry deoxygenated blood from the fetal descending aorta back to the placenta.

---

**COURSE OF FETAL CIRCULATION**

**1. Placenta to the Fetal Heart:**

- Oxygenated blood (80% saturated) from the placenta enters the fetus through the single **umbilical vein** at the umbilicus.
- It runs along the free margin of the falciform ligament to the liver. In the liver, it gives off branches to the left lobe and receives deoxygenated blood from the portal vein.
- The greater portion of this well-oxygenated blood short-circuits the liver through the **ductus venosus** and empties directly into the Inferior Vena Cava (IVC), where it mixes with deoxygenated blood returning from the lower limbs.
- The IVC empties this mixed blood into the right atrium of the heart.

**2. Intracardiac Shunting:**

- In the right atrium, most of the well-oxygenated blood (about 75%) from the ductus venosus is preferentially directed by the valve of the IVC and the _crista dividens_ through the **foramen ovale** into the left atrium.
- In the left atrium, it mixes with a small amount of venous blood returning from the non-functioning lungs via the pulmonary veins. It then passes through the mitral valve into the left ventricle.
- The remaining 25% of IVC blood mixes with completely deoxygenated blood entering the right atrium from the Superior Vena Cava (SVC) (draining the head and upper limbs) and the coronary sinus. This blood passes through the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle. The right ventricular blood is 15-20% less saturated with oxygen compared to the left ventricular blood.

**3. Arterial Distribution:**

- During ventricular systole, the left ventricle pumps the highly oxygenated blood into the ascending aorta and the arch of the aorta, preferentially supplying the heart, head, neck, brain, and arms.
- The right ventricle pumps its deoxygenated blood into the pulmonary trunk. Because the pulmonary vascular resistance is very high during fetal life, the main portion (90%) of this blood bypasses the lungs and passes directly through the **ductus arteriosus** into the descending aorta.
- In the descending aorta, this blood mixes with the blood from the proximal aorta and is distributed to the abdominal viscera and the lower half of the body.

**4. Return to the Placenta:**

- About 70% of the total cardiac output is carried by the ductus arteriosus to the descending aorta.
- From the descending aorta, the deoxygenated blood leaves the fetal body via two **umbilical arteries** (branches of the internal iliac arteries) to reach the placenta.
- In the placenta, it gets oxygenated and is ready for recirculation. Approximately 40% of the combined fetal cardiac output goes to the placenta through the umbilical arteries.

---

**FETAL HEMODYNAMICS AND BLOOD VALUES**

- **Mean Cardiac Output:** It is comparatively high in the fetus, estimated to be 350 mL/kg/min.
- **Umbilical Vein:** $PO_2$ is 30–40 mm Hg, and $O_2$ saturation is 70–80%.
- **Umbilical Arteries:** $PO_2$ is 20–25 mm Hg, and $O_2$ saturation is 50–60%.
- **Fetal Hemoglobin (HbF):** It has a greater affinity for oxygen due to lower binding of 2,3-diphosphoglycerate (2,3-DPG), facilitating oxygen transfer from the mother.

---

**CHANGES IN FETAL CIRCULATION AT BIRTH**

Profound hemodynamic changes occur immediately after birth due to the cessation of placental blood flow and the initiation of independent respiration.

**1. Closure of the Umbilical Arteries:**

- _Functional closure:_ Almost instantaneous due to reflex spasm of the well-developed muscular coat in the arteries, preventing fetal blood from draining out.
- _Anatomical closure:_ Takes about 2–3 months. The distal parts form the **lateral umbilical ligaments**, and the proximal parts remain open as the **superior vesical arteries**.

**2. Closure of the Umbilical Vein and Ductus Venosus:**

- _Functional closure:_ Occurs slightly later than the arteries, allowing the fetus to receive an extra 80–100 mL of blood from the placenta (if cord clamping is delayed).
- _Anatomical Remnants:_ The umbilical vein obliterates to form the **ligamentum teres**, and the ductus venosus collapses to become the **ligamentum venosum**.

**3. Closure of the Ductus Arteriosus:**

- _Functional closure:_ Occurs within a few hours of respiration. The muscle wall contracts in response to the rising oxygen tension of the blood flowing through it (mediated via prostaglandin action).
- _Anatomical closure:_ Takes about 1–3 months and becomes the **ligamentum arteriosum**.

**4. Closure of the Foramen Ovale:**

- _Functional closure:_ Occurs soon after birth. Initiation of respiration decreases pulmonary vascular resistance, which increases pulmonary blood flow and left atrial pressure. Simultaneously, right atrial pressure decreases due to the cessation of umbilical venous return. This pressure gradient functionally closes the flap of the foramen ovale.
- _Anatomical closure:_ Complete anatomical closure takes about 1 year. During the first few days of life, this closure may be reversible, which is clinically evidenced by transient cyanosis when the baby cries (causing temporary right-to-left shunting).

4. Stages of labor.
**STAGES OF LABOR**

Labor is defined as the series of events that take place in the genital organs in an effort to expel the viable products of conception (fetus, placenta, and the membranes) out of the womb through the vagina into the outer world. Conventionally, the events of normal labor are divided into four stages.

**1. FIRST STAGE OF LABOR**

- **Definition:** It starts from the onset of true labor pain and ends with full dilatation of the cervix (10 cm). It is also known as the 'cervical stage' of labor.
- **Duration:** The average duration is 12 hours in primigravidae and 6 hours (WHO- 10 hours) in multiparae.
- **Main Events:** This stage is chiefly concerned with the preparation of the birth canal, specifically the dilatation and effacement (taking up) of the cervix, and the full formation of the lower uterine segment.
- **Phases:**
    - **Latent Phase:** This is a period characterized by painful uterine contractions and variable changes of the cervix, including some degree of effacement and slower progression of dilatation up to 5 cm (WHO) or 6 cm (ACOG). The normal duration of the latent phase is widely variable, averaging about 8.6 hours (up to 20 hours) in a primigravida and 5.3 hours (up to 14 hours) in a multipara.
    - **Active Phase:** This period is characterized by regular painful uterine contractions, a substantial degree of cervical effacement, and more rapid cervical dilatation from 5 cm (or 6 cm) until full dilatation.
- **Clinical Course:** Pains are felt anteriorly with simultaneous hardening of the uterus, and they gradually increase in frequency, intensity, and duration. Expulsion of the cervical mucus plug mixed with blood, known as 'show', occurs. A 'bag of waters' forms due to the herniation of membranes through the dilating cervical canal, which helps in dilatation and usually ruptures near full dilatation.

**2. SECOND STAGE OF LABOR**

- **Definition:** It starts from the full dilatation of the cervix (not from the rupture of the membranes) and ends with the expulsion of the fetus from the birth canal.
- **Duration:** The average duration is 2 hours (WHO- 3 hours) in primigravidae and 30 minutes (WHO- 2 hours) in multiparae.
- **Main Events:** This stage is concerned with the descent and delivery of the fetus through the birth canal.
- **Phases:**
    - **Propulsive (Passive) Phase:** Starts from full dilatation up to the descent of the presenting part to the pelvic floor.
    - **Expulsive (Active) Phase:** Distinguished by the mother experiencing an involuntary, irresistible urge to bear down and push, triggered by a nerve reflex (Ferguson reflex) due to stretching of the vagina by the presenting part. This phase ends with the delivery of the baby.
- **Clinical Course:** Uterine contractions become stronger, more frequent (every 2-3 minutes), and last longer. The volume of the uterine cavity is reduced as liquor escapes, and the uterus elongates during contractions. The presenting part descends and stretches the vulval outlet until the maximum diameter of the fetal head (biparietal diameter) stretches the perineum without any recession after the contraction passes off, a phenomenon called 'crowning'. The head is then born by extension, followed by restitution, external rotation, and the delivery of the shoulders and trunk by lateral flexion.

**3. THIRD STAGE OF LABOR**

- **Definition:** It begins immediately after the expulsion of the fetus and ends with the expulsion of the placenta and membranes (afterbirths).
- **Duration:** The average duration is about 15 minutes in both primigravidae and multiparae. However, this duration is reduced to about 5 minutes with the active management of the third stage of labor (AMTSL).
- **Main Events:** It comprises three physiological phases: (1) Separation of the placenta, primarily caused by the marked reduction in the surface area of the placental attachment following successive uterine retractions; (2) Descent of the separated placenta into the lower segment and vagina; and (3) Finally, its expulsion to the outside.
- **Hemostasis:** Bleeding is controlled by the firm retraction of interlacing uterine muscle fibers, which act as "living ligatures" to clamp the torn vessels, and is facilitated by the hypercoagulable state of pregnancy promoting thrombosis.
- **Clinical Signs of Separation:** The uterus becomes globular, firm, and ballottable. The fundal height slightly rises, there is a slight gush of vaginal bleeding, and permanent lengthening of the umbilical cord occurs.

**4. FOURTH STAGE OF LABOR**

- **Definition:** It is the stage of observation for at least 1 hour after the expulsion of the afterbirths.
- **Main Events & Management:** The primary purpose is to ensure that both the mother and baby are well and hemodynamically stable. During this critical hour, the mother is continuously monitored for vital signs (pulse and blood pressure), the tone of the uterus (ensuring it is well retracted), and any abnormal vaginal bleeding to prevent or promptly manage postpartum hemorrhage.


5. Physiology of lactation.

**PHYSIOLOGY OF LACTATION**

Although lactation formally starts following delivery, the preparation for effective lactation begins during pregnancy. The physiological basis of lactation is divided into four interdependent phases:

1. Mammogenesis (Preparation of breasts)
2. Lactogenesis (Synthesis and secretion from the breast alveoli)
3. Galactokinesis (Ejection of milk)
4. Galactopoiesis (Maintenance of lactation).

---

**1. MAMMOGENESIS (Preparation of Breasts)**

- Pregnancy is associated with a remarkable growth and development of both the ductal and lobuloalveolar systems.
- **Hormonal Control:** Hypertrophy and proliferation of the ducts are primarily due to estrogen, while the development of the lobuloalveolar system is due to the combined action of estrogen and progesterone.
- The maturation of the breast components is also accelerated by growth hormone, adrenal hormones, thyroid hormone, prolactin, and insulin.
- An intact nerve supply is not essential for the growth of the mammary glands during pregnancy.

**2. LACTOGENESIS (Synthesis and Secretion of Milk)**

- The alveolar epithelial cells are the principal sites for the production of milk.
- While some secretory activity (colostrum) is evident during pregnancy and accelerates immediately following delivery, actual milk secretion typically starts on the 3rd or 4th postpartum day.
- **Hormonal Control:** During pregnancy, despite high prolactin levels, milk secretion is kept in abeyance because circulating steroids (estrogen and progesterone) make the breast tissues unresponsive to prolactin.
- Following delivery, the sudden withdrawal of estrogen and progesterone allows prolactin to begin its milk secretory activity in the fully developed mammary glands.
- Prolactin, insulin, growth hormone, and glucocorticoids are the most important hormones in this stage, and the secretory activity is further enhanced by thyroxine.
- Notably, nursing effort is not essentially required for the initial milk secretion to occur.

**3. GALACTOKINESIS (Ejection of Milk)**

- Oxytocin is the major galactokinetic hormone.
- Discharge of milk from the mammary glands depends not only on the suction exerted by the baby during suckling but importantly on the contractile mechanism which expresses milk from the alveoli into the ducts.
- **Milk Ejection Reflex (Let-Down Reflex):**
    - During suckling, a conditioned reflex is set up.
    - **Afferent Arc:** Ascending tactile impulses from the nipple and areola pass via the thoracic sensory (T4, T5, and T6) afferent neural arc to the paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei of the hypothalamus.
    - **Efferent Arc:** The hypothalamus synthesizes oxytocin, which is transported to the posterior pituitary and released into the bloodstream.
    - **Action:** Oxytocin causes the contraction of the myoepithelial cells that surround the alveolar epithelium, forcing the milk down into the ampulla of the lactiferous ducts (where it is sucked out by the baby).
    - A sensation of a rise in pressure in the breasts experienced by the mother at the beginning of suckling is called a 'draught'.
- **Inhibition:** The milk ejection reflex can be inhibited by adverse psychic conditions such as pain, anxiety, breast engorgement, or depression.

**4. GALACTOPOIESIS (Maintenance of Lactation)**

- Prolactin appears to be the single most important galactopoietic hormone.
- For the maintenance of effective and continuous lactation, the frequency of suckling (more than 8 times in 24 hours) is essential.
- Distension of the alveoli by retained milk (due to failure of suckling or lack of milk transfer) causes a decrease in milk secretion by the alveolar epithelium. Thus, periodic breastfeeding and complete emptying of the ducts and alveoli are necessary to prevent lactation failure.

6. Methods of 2nd trimester abortion.
**METHODS OF SECOND TRIMESTER ABORTION (13–24 WEEKS)**

The termination of a midtrimester pregnancy is inherently more difficult and carries higher risks compared to first-trimester methods,. The available methods are broadly classified into medical and surgical approaches.

**1. MEDICAL METHODS** Medical regimens are extensively used as the primary modality. Prostaglandins (PGs) and their analogs are highly effective, with PGEs being preferred due to their selective myometrial action and fewer side effects,.

- **Mifepristone and Misoprostol (Combination Regimen):**
    - **Protocol:** Mifepristone 200 mg orally, followed 36–48 hours later by misoprostol 800 µg vaginally. This is followed by misoprostol 400 µg orally every 3 hours for up to four doses.
    - **Advantage:** Pretreatment with mifepristone significantly reduces the induction-to-abortion interval (median 6.5 hours) compared to misoprostol alone, with a success rate of 97%.
- **Misoprostol (PGE1 Analog) Alone:**
    - **Protocol:** 600–800 µg of misoprostol is administered vaginally, followed by 400 µg vaginally or buccally every 3–4 hours.
    - **Advantage:** It achieves a 100% success rate with a mean induction-abortion interval of 11–12 hours.
- **Dinoprostone (PGE2 Analog):**
    - **Protocol:** 20 mg vaginal suppository inserted every 3–4 hours (maximum of 4–6 doses).
    - **Drawbacks:** It is thermolabile (requires refrigeration) and expensive. When combined with osmotic dilators, the mean interval to abortion is 17 hours.
- **Letrozole and Misoprostol:** Can be used as an alternative combination medical regimen.
- **Concentrated Oxytocin:** High-dose oxytocin as a single agent can be utilized.
    - **Protocol:** 50 units of oxytocin in 500 mL normal saline infused over 3 hours, followed by 1 hour of diuresis (without oxytocin). The dose is then sequentially escalated to 150, 200, 250, and 300 units, each administered in 500 mL of normal saline,.

**2. SURGICAL METHODS**

- **Dilatation and Evacuation (D&E):**
    - **Cervical Priming:** Wide mechanical cervical dilatation is necessary. Softening of the cervix MUST be achieved prior to the procedure to prevent cervical trauma. This is done using intracervical osmotic dilators (tents), vaginal/buccal misoprostol 400 µg given 3–4 hours prior, or mifepristone given 48 hours prior,.
    - **Procedure:** Ideally performed under ultrasound guidance to reduce complication risks. The amniotic sac is punctured and the fluid is drained using an 11 to 16 mm suction cannula. The fetus is then extracted in parts. Finally, a large-bore cannula is used to evacuate the placenta and any remaining tissue,.
    - **Antibiotic Prophylaxis:** Doxycycline 100 mg orally 1 hour prior to surgery and 200 mg orally 2 hours post-procedure.
    - **Complications:** Uterine perforation, cervical laceration, severe bleeding, and postabortion infection.
- **Abdominal Hysterotomy:**
    - **Definition:** An operative extraction of the products of conception through incisions on the abdominal and uterine walls. It is rarely performed in modern practice.
    - **Indications:** Prior failed medical termination, or when D&E is deemed unsafe (e.g., lower uterine segment fibroids, uterine anomalies, or cases with a repeatedly scarred uterus and placenta accreta/percreta),. It is often combined with concurrent tubal sterilization.
    - **Complications:** Hemorrhage, shock, anesthetic hazards, peritonitis, scar endometriosis (1%), incisional hernia, and a risk of scar rupture in future pregnancies.

**3. INTRA-AMNIOTIC INSTILLATION (THERAPEUTIC AMNIOCENTESIS)**

- In the first half of pregnancy, therapeutic amniocentesis can be used to induce abortion via the direct intra-amniotic instillation of chemicals such as hypertonic saline, urea, or prostaglandins,.

**POST-ABORTION PROPHYLAXIS** For any nonimmunized Rh-negative woman undergoing a midtrimester termination, 100 µg of anti-D immunoglobulin must be administered intramuscularly within 72 hours of the abortion procedure to prevent alloimmunization.


7. Complication of twin pregnancy.
**COMPLICATIONS OF TWIN PREGNANCY**

The complications of multifetal pregnancy are significantly higher compared to a singleton pregnancy and are broadly classified into maternal and fetal complications.

**I. MATERNAL COMPLICATIONS**

**A. During Pregnancy**

- **Nausea and vomiting:** Occurs with increased frequency and severity.
- **Anemia:** Increased due to higher iron and folate requirements by the two fetuses. Folic acid deficiency leads to an increased incidence of megaloblastic anemia.
- **Pre-eclampsia (25%):** Incidence is increased three times over singleton pregnancy, likely due to exposure to a superabundance of chorionic villi.
- **Hydramnios (Polyhydramnios) (10%):** More common in monozygotic twins and usually involves the second sac.
- **Antepartum Hemorrhage (APH):** Increased incidence of both placenta previa (due to a larger placenta encroaching on the lower segment) and placental abruption (due to pre-eclampsia, sudden escape of liquor, or folic acid deficiency).
- **Malpresentation:** Very common; the second baby is more prone to malpresentation.
- **Preterm Labor (50%):** Mean gestational period for twins is 37 weeks. It is caused by overdistension of the uterus, polyhydramnios, and premature rupture of membranes (PROM).
- **Mechanical Distress:** Palpitation, dyspnea, varicosities, and hemorrhoids are increased compared to a singleton pregnancy.

**B. During Labor**

- **Early Rupture of Membranes & Cord Prolapse:** Cord prolapse is five times more common than in singleton pregnancies, particularly in relation to the second baby due to malpresentation.
- **Increased Operative Interference:** Higher rates of cesarean delivery (CD) and forceps/vacuum deliveries due to malpresentation and related complications.
- **Intrapartum Bleeding:** May occur following the birth of the first baby due to the separation of the placenta following the reduction of the placental site.
- **Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH):** A major danger due to uterine atony from overdistension, longer time taken for the big placenta to separate, larger placental surface area exposing more sinuses, and lower segment implantation.
- **Locked Twins:** A rare but severe complication causing dystocia.

**C. During Puerperium**

- **Subinvolution:** Due to the bigger size of the uterus.
- **Infection/Sepsis:** Risk is elevated due to increased operative interference, pre-existing anemia, and blood loss.
- **Lactation Failure:** Needs reassurance and additional support.
- **Thromboembolism:** Risk is significantly increased.

**II. FETAL COMPLICATIONS**

- **Miscarriage:** Rate is increased, especially with monozygotic twins.
- **Prematurity (80%):** Very much increased, leading to hazards of low birth weight and neonatal complications.
- **Discordant Twin Growth (20%):** True pathological discordance (estimated weight difference of >25%) can result from twin-twin transfusion syndrome, placental insufficiency, IUGR, or structural anomalies.
- **Vanishing Twin (21%):** Loss of one fetus in the first trimester, which simply "vanishes" by resorption.
- **Fetus Papyraceous (Compressus):** If fetal death occurs in the second trimester, the dead fetus becomes flattened and mummified.
- **Intrauterine Fetal Death (3-7%):** More common in monozygotic twins. If death occurs late in pregnancy, it can cause cerebral palsy, microcephaly, or DIC in the surviving co-twin.
- **Fetal Anomalies:** Increased by 2-4% (more in monozygotic twins), including neural tube defects, cardiac anomalies, and Down's syndrome.
- **Asphyxia and Stillbirth:** Increased due to pre-eclampsia, malpresentation, placental abruption, and prematurity. The second baby is more at risk.

**III. COMPLICATIONS SPECIFIC TO MONOCHORIONIC TWINS** Complications are significantly higher in monochorionic compared to dichorionic twins.

- **Twin-Twin Transfusion Syndrome (TTTS) (10-15%):** The donor twin bleeds into the recipient via placental vascular anastomoses, causing the recipient to become polyuric, hypervolemic, and develop polyhydramnios (macrosomia), while the donor becomes anemic, oliguric, and develops oligohydramnios (IUGR/"stuck" twin).
- **Twin Anemia-Polycythemia Sequence (TAPS):** Chronic hemoglobin discordance due to small arteriovenous anastomoses allowing slow transfusion.
- **Dead Fetus Syndrome (2-7%):** Death of one twin causes acute hypotension and thromboplastin liberation, transferring to the living twin and risking cerebral palsy or DIC.
- **Twin Reversed Arterial Perfusion (TRAP):** Characterized by an "acardiac perfused twin" receiving blood from a normal co-twin, often leading to heart failure in the donor.
- **Monoamniotic Complications (1-2% of MZ twins):** High perinatal mortality (50%) primarily due to cord entanglement.
- **Conjoined Twins:** Extremely rare incomplete cleavage, resulting in thoracopagus, craniopagus, or other fusions with high mortality.
- **Selective Growth Restriction (sGR):** Estimated fetal weight difference >20%, seen in over 50% of monochorionic twins complicated by TTTS.


8. Deep Transverse Arrest.
**DEEP TRANSVERSE ARREST (DTA)**

**Definition** Deep transverse arrest (DTA) is a condition during labor where the fetal head is deep into the pelvic cavity, the sagittal suture is placed in the transverse bispinous diameter, and there is no progress in descent of the head even after ½–1 hour following full dilatation of the cervix. It typically occurs at the plane of least pelvic dimensions, which corresponds to the level of the ischial spines. DTA is an arrested position that results either from the incomplete anterior rotation (1/8th of a circle) of an oblique occiput posterior position, or from the nonrotation of a primary occiput transverse position.

**Causes** The failure of the occiput to rotate and descend can be attributed to maternal, fetal, or uterine factors:

- **Faulty Pelvic Architecture:** Android pelvis, mid-pelvic contraction, flat sacrum, prominent ischial spines, or convergent side walls.
- **Fetal Factors:** Deflexion of the fetal head, a big baby, or immobility of the fetal trunk (which may happen secondary to the drainage of liquor amnii).
- **Uterine Factors:** Weak uterine contractions.
- **Soft Tissue Factors:** Laxity or weakness of the pelvic floor muscles.

**Diagnosis** The diagnosis is made clinically via vaginal examination during the second stage of labor, characterized by the following findings:

- The fetal head is engaged.
- The sagittal suture lies exactly in the transverse bispinous diameter.
- The anterior fontanel is palpable, which indicates a deflexed head.
- Faulty pelvic architecture may be detected on clinical pelvimetry.

**Management** The management protocol is strictly guided by the fetal condition and a careful assessment of pelvic adequacy. Operative vaginal delivery for DTA should only be performed by a highly skilled obstetrician; otherwise, cesarean delivery is preferred.

**1. Cesarean Delivery (CD)**

- Cesarean section is the safest and most preferred method of delivery if the pelvis is inadequate (mid-pelvic contraction), the baby is big, or if manual/instrumental rotation is deemed unsuitable.

**2. Operative Vaginal Delivery** This is considered only if vaginal delivery is found safe (i.e., the pelvis is adequate, and the arrest is primarily due to weak uterine contractions or poor pelvic floor tone). The methods include:

- **Ventouse (Vacuum Extraction):** The vacuum cup is placed more towards the occiput (the flexion point) to promote flexion. Rotation of the head is expected to occur spontaneously during its descent on traction. Excessive traction force must not be used.
- **Manual Rotation and Forceps:** The fetal head is manually rotated to an occipito-anterior position, followed by the application of forceps for extraction.
- **Forceps Rotation and Delivery:** This is performed using specialized rotational forceps, such as Kielland's forceps, strictly in the hands of an expert. It is commonly used when DTA is associated with asynclitism of the fetal head, often utilizing the wandering method for the application of the anterior blade.

9. Pathogenesis of preeclampsia.
**PATHOGENESIS OF PRE-ECLAMPSIA**

The underlying basic pathology of pre-eclampsia is **endothelial dysfunction and intense vasospasm** resulting from abnormal placentation. It is a progressive, multisystem disorder where the exact etiopathogenesis is a cascade of events involving maternal, placental, and fetal factors.

**1. Abnormal Placentation (Failure of Trophoblast Invasion)**

- In a normal pregnancy, endovascular trophoblasts invade the spiral arterioles in two distinct waves (the decidual segment at 10–12 weeks and the myometrial segment at 16–18 weeks). This physiological change replaces the endothelial lining and muscular wall, converting spiral arterioles into a low-resistance, low-pressure, high-flow system.
- In pre-eclampsia, there is a **failure of the second wave** of endovascular trophoblast migration.
- Because the invasion fails to proceed beyond the decidua-myometrial junction, the musculoelastic media in the myometrial segment remains intact and highly responsive to vasoconstrictor stimuli, resulting in decreased uteroplacental blood flow.

**2. Placental Ischemia and Oxidative Stress**

- Poor cytotrophoblastic invasion in the spiral arteries leads to chronic hypoxia and ischemia of the placenta.
- This chronic hypoxia activates apoptotic pathways within the endoplasmic reticulum, increasing oxidative stress.
- The hypoxic placenta releases lipid peroxides, Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS), and superoxide anion radicals into the maternal circulation, which directly cause widespread maternal endothelial cell injury and dysfunction.

**3. Imbalance of Angiogenic and Antiangiogenic Proteins**

- A normal placenta secretes angiogenic proteins like Placental Growth Factor (PlGF) and Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF) to promote vascular development.
- In pre-eclampsia, the stressed trophoblastic tissue overproduces **antiangiogenic factors**, primarily **sFlt-1** (soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1) and **soluble endoglin**.
- sFlt-1 actively binds to and neutralizes PlGF and VEGF. This deprives the maternal endothelium of these essential maintenance factors, inducing severe endothelial dysfunction and preventing placental angiogenesis.

**4. Imbalance of Vasoactive Substances (Vasospasm)** The widespread endothelial injury leads to a critical imbalance between endogenous vasodilators and vasoconstrictors:

- **Deficiency of Vasodilators:** There is a relative or absolute deficiency of Prostacyclin (PGI2) and Nitric Oxide (NO) from the damaged vascular endothelium.
- **Excess of Vasoconstrictors:** There is increased synthesis of Thromboxane A2 (TXA2) from platelets and Endothelin-1 from endothelial cells. Additionally, the maternal vascular system exhibits an increased sensitivity to the pressor agent Angiotensin-II.
- **Net Effect:** Generalized and intense vasospasm occurs, which elevates systemic vascular resistance and results in maternal hypertension.

**5. Inflammatory Mediators and Immunological Dysregulation**

- **Cytokine Storm:** Activated leukocytes release high levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-a) and Interleukin-6 (IL-6), which further exacerbate endothelial injury.
- **Immune Maladaptation:** There is decreased placental HLA-G expression, leading to a faulty interaction between extravillous trophoblasts and uterine Natural Killer (NK) cells. The normal pregnancy shift from a Th-1 to a Th-2 immune response is insufficient, resulting in abnormally high levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-a, IL-6, IL-1B, IL-8) and lower anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10).

**6. Pathophysiological Consequences** The systemic endothelial dysfunction and vasospasm culminate in end-organ damage, manifesting as two distinct syndromes:

- **Maternal Syndrome:**
    - _Vasoconstriction:_ Leads directly to hypertension.
    - _Decreased Organ Perfusion:_ Causes oliguria (kidneys), ischemia leading to elevated liver enzymes or HELLP syndrome (liver), and seizures or PRES (brain).
    - _Increased Capillary Permeability (Capillary Leak):_ The leaky capillaries and decreased oncotic pressure lead to generalized edema, proteinuria, ascites, pulmonary edema, and hemoconcentration.
    - _Coagulation Activation:_ Widespread endothelial damage activates the coagulation cascade, leading to thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, and Disseminated Intravascular Coagulopathy (DIC).
- **Fetal Syndrome:**
    - Uteroplacental insufficiency and vasospasm restrict fetal nutrient and oxygen supply, leading to Fetal Growth Restriction (FGR), oligohydramnios, preterm delivery, and Intrauterine Fetal Death (IUFD).


10. Screening for gestational diabetes.

**SCREENING FOR GESTATIONAL DIABETES MELLITUS (GDM)**

**Definition** Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) is defined as carbohydrate intolerance of variable severity with onset or first recognition during the present pregnancy.

**Timing of Screening**

- **Universal Screening:** All pregnant women should be screened for GDM irrespective of risk factors. The ideal time for routine screening is between **24 and 28 weeks of gestation**.
- **Early Screening:** Women presenting with high-risk factors should be screened early, ideally at the first antenatal visit.

**High-Risk Factors (Indications for Early Screening)**

- Positive family history of diabetes (parents, siblings, grandparents, uncles, or aunts).
- Previous birth of an overweight/macrosomic baby (≥ 4 kg).
- Previous history of GDM or unexplained stillbirth (especially with pancreatic islet hyperplasia on autopsy).
- Unexplained perinatal loss.
- Presence of polyhydramnios or recurrent vaginal candidiasis in the present pregnancy.
- Persistent glycosuria (glycosuria on one occasion before the 20th week and on two or more occasions thereafter strongly indicates the need for an OGTT).
- Maternal age > 30 years and obesity.
- Medical conditions: Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome (PCOS) or Hypertension (≥140/90 mm Hg).
- High-risk ethnic group (e.g., East Asian, Pacific Island ancestry).

**Screening Strategies and Diagnostic Criteria**

**1. One-Step Procedure (DIPSI Guidelines)** _Note: The Diabetes in Pregnancy Study Group in India (DIPSI) criteria are highly recommended and widely followed in the Indian clinical setting._

- **Method:** A 75 g oral glucose load is administered to the pregnant woman irrespective of the time of day or her last meal (non-fasting state).
- **Diagnosis:** A venous plasma glucose value at 2-hours of **> 140 mg/dL** is diagnostic of GDM.

**2. Two-Step Procedure (ACOG Recommendation)**

- **Step 1 (Glucose Challenge Test - GCT):** A 50 g oral glucose load is given without regard to the time of day or the last meal.
    - _Cut-off:_ A plasma glucose value of ≥ 140 mg/dL (or whole blood of 130 mg/dL) at 1 hour is considered a positive screen.
- **Step 2 (Oral Glucose Tolerance Test - OGTT):** Screen-positive women undergo a 100 g (or 75 g) OGTT.

**3. IADPSG / WHO Criteria (75 g OGTT)**

- After overnight fasting, a 75 g OGTT is performed.
- **IADPSG Criteria:** Diagnosis of GDM is made if any _one_ or more of the following plasma glucose thresholds are met or exceeded:
    - Fasting: ≥ 92 mg/dL
    - 1 hour: ≥ 180 mg/dL
    - 2 hours: ≥ 153 mg/dL
- **WHO Criteria:** Fasting ≥ 5.6 mmol/L (100 mg/dL) or 2 hours plasma glucose ≥ 7.8 mmol/L (140 mg/dL).

**Role of HbA1c in Screening**

- HbA1c estimation is recommended for women with high-risk factors during early screening.
- An HbA1c value of **≥ 6.5%** suggests _overt diabetes_ rather than GDM. For these women with diabetes during embryogenesis, a comprehensive ultrasound must be done to detect any fetal malformations.

11. NYHA classification of cardiac disease in pregnancy.
**NEW YORK HEART ASSOCIATION (NYHA) FUNCTIONAL CLASSIFICATION OF HEART DISEASE**

The NYHA classification is used to grade the functional capacity and severity of symptoms in pregnant women with cardiac disease. It categorizes patients into four grades based on their exercise tolerance and physical limitations:

- **Grade-I (Uncompromised):**
    - No limitation of physical activity.
    - Ordinary physical activity does not cause any discomfort.
- **Grade-II (Slightly compromised):**
    - Slight limitation of physical activity.
    - The patient is comfortable at rest, but ordinary physical activity causes discomfort.
- **Grade-III (Markedly compromised):**
    - Marked limitation of physical activity.
    - The patient is comfortable at rest, but discomfort occurs with _less than_ ordinary physical activity.
- **Grade-IV (Severely compromised):**
    - Severely compromised cardiovascular function.
    - The patient experiences discomfort and symptoms of cardiac insufficiency _even at rest_.

**LIMITATIONS OF NYHA CLASSIFICATION IN PREGNANCY** While the NYHA classification is a standard clinical tool, it has specific limitations when applied to obstetric patients:

- **Symptom-based only:** It considers only the clinical symptoms of the patient and does not take into account the anatomical type or the exact severity of the underlying cardiac pathology.
- **Poor predictability:** It does not reliably predict the ultimate pregnancy outcome for the mother or the fetus. _(Note: Due to these limitations, the modified WHO classification of cardiovascular disease is also widely used in modern obstetrics to predict maternal mortality risks and guide management)._



12. Asymptomatic bacteriuria.
**ASYMPTOMATIC BACTERIURIA (ASB) IN PREGNANCY**

**1. DEFINITION** Asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB) is defined as the presence of actively multiplying bacteria within the urinary tract, characterized by a bacterial count of the same species exceeding $10^5$/mL in a midstream 'clean-catch' specimen of urine on two separate occasions, without any clinical symptoms of a urinary tract infection.

**2. INCIDENCE** The overall incidence of ASB during pregnancy ranges between 5% and 10%.

**3. ETIOLOGY / CAUSATIVE ORGANISMS**

- **Escherichia coli (_E. coli_):** The most common offending pathogen, responsible for over 90% of cases.
- **Other Pathogens:** _Klebsiella pneumoniae_ and _Proteus_ species.

**4. SCREENING AND DIAGNOSIS**

- **Routine Antenatal Screening:** Ideally, all pregnant women should undergo a urine culture at their first antenatal visit to detect pre-existing ASB.
- **Urine Dipstick Test:** Urine is rapidly screened for the presence of nitrites and leukocyte esterase. If either of these is positive, a confirmatory urine culture must be performed.

**5. COMPLICATIONS AND IMPACT ON PREGNANCY** If left untreated, ASB poses significant maternal and fetal risks:

- **Acute Pyelonephritis:** About 25% to 30% of women with untreated ASB will go on to develop acute pyelonephritis, typically during the third trimester.
- **Other Maternal Risks:** There is a high incidence of associated hypertension and anemia. ASB is also the most common cause of recurrent UTIs in pregnancy. Furthermore, women with ASB run a greater risk of developing chronic renal lesions later in life.
- **Fetal Risks:** ASB is associated with an increased incidence of premature (preterm) labor and intrauterine growth restricted (IUGR) babies, which is likely related to underlying chronic renal lesions.
- **Underlying Abnormalities:** Recurrent ASB is associated with a high incidence (20%) of congenital or acquired urinary tract abnormalities.

**6. MANAGEMENT** The goal of treating ASB is to prevent the progression to symptomatic pyelonephritis and to reduce the risk of preterm birth.

**A. Standard Antimicrobial Therapy** A 10 to 14-day course of appropriate antibiotics cures 70-100% of cases. Options include:

- **Nitrofurantoin:** 100 mg twice daily (BID). It is uniformly effective against common uropathogens. _Note: Nitrofurantoin should be avoided in the third trimester (at term) due to the risk of hemolysis in newborns with G6PD deficiency_.
- **Amoxicillin:** 500 mg three times daily (TID).
- **Cephalexin:** 500 mg three times daily (TID).

_(Note: While ampicillin and amoxicillin were traditionally used, many strains of E. coli, Klebsiella, and Proteus have developed resistance to them. Broad-spectrum penicillins can also alter normal bowel and vaginal flora, leading to diarrhea or monilial vulvovaginitis)_.

**B. Single-Dose Therapy** A single-dose regimen can also be effective and improves compliance. Options include Nitrofurantoin 0.2 g or Amoxicillin 3 g.

**C. Prophylaxis for Recurrent ASB / UTI** If the infection is recurrent, long-term suppressive antibiotic prophylaxis may need to be continued every night until delivery. Regimens include:

- Nitrofurantoin 50-100 mg at bedtime.
- Amoxicillin 250 mg at bedtime.
- Trimethoprim 100 mg at bedtime _(Avoided in the first trimester)_.

**D. Postpartum Follow-up** Women who experience recurrent UTIs or persistent ASB should undergo imaging of the upper urinary tract (such as renal USG) 3 months postpartum to rule out anatomical abnormalities like hydronephrosis or renal stones.


13. Hegar’s sign.
**HEGAR'S SIGN**

**1. Introduction and Incidence:**

- Hegar's sign is an objective clinical sign of early pregnancy characterized by the marked softening of the lower uterine segment.
- It is clinically present in approximately two-thirds of all pregnancy cases.

**2. Time of Appearance:**

- It is typically demonstrated between the 6th and 10th weeks of gestation.
- It can be elicited a little earlier in multiparous women compared to primigravidae.

**3. Anatomical Basis:** The sign is based on the distinct variation in the consistency of different parts of the uterus during early pregnancy. It relies on three anatomical facts:

- The upper part of the body of the uterus is enlarged due to the presence of the growing fetus.
- The lower part of the uterine body is empty and becomes extremely soft.
- The cervix is comparatively firm in relation to the lower uterine segment.

**4. Method of Elicitation:**

- The sign is elicited through a bimanual pelvic examination.
- Two fingers of the examining hand are placed in the anterior fornix of the vagina, while the fingers of the other hand are placed abdominally behind the uterus.
- Because of the extreme softness of the empty lower uterine segment, the abdominal and vaginal fingers seem to appose (meet) each other below the body of the uterus.

**5. Clinical Precaution:**

- The examination to elicit Hegar's sign must be conducted with utmost gentleness to prevent any risk of provoking an abortion.

14. Uses of Ultrasonography in 1st trimester.
**USES OF ULTRASONOGRAPHY IN THE FIRST TRIMESTER**

Transvaginal Sonography (TVS) is the diagnostic imaging modality of choice during the first trimester as it provides superior resolution and earlier visualization of embryonic structures compared to Transabdominal Sonography (TAS).

The clinical indications and uses of ultrasonography in the first trimester (up to 12 weeks) are:

**1. Confirmation and Localization of Pregnancy**

- **Intrauterine Pregnancy:** TVS can identify an intradecidual Gestational Sac (GS) as early as 4.5 to 5 weeks (or 29–35 days of menstrual age). The sequence of appearance is:
    - Gestational sac at 5 weeks.
    - Yolk sac at 5.5 weeks.
    - Fetal pole and cardiac motion at 6 weeks.
- **Ectopic Pregnancy:** To rule out extrauterine gestation. TVS can detect 90% of tubal ectopic pregnancies by identifying an empty uterine cavity, an adnexal mass ("tubal ring" or "ring of fire" on color Doppler), or fluid in the pouch of Douglas.

**2. Estimation of Gestational Age (Dating Scan)**

- Accurate dating is crucial for subsequent fetal growth assessment.
- **Crown-Rump Length (CRL)** is the single most accurate biometric parameter for calculating gestational age in the first trimester, with an error margin of only ±5 days.

**3. Assessment of Fetal Viability**

- Confirmation of embryonic cardiac activity ensures fetal viability. Normal embryonic cardiac activity (>100 bpm) is typically visualized when the CRL is greater than 5 mm.
- It reliably diagnoses **Early Pregnancy Failure** (missed abortion or blighted ovum). An anembryonic sac is diagnosed when the Mean Sac Diameter (MSD) is ≥25 mm with no visible embryo.

**4. Evaluation of Multiple Pregnancy**

- USG accurately determines the number of gestational sacs, yolk sacs, and fetal poles.
- **Determination of Chorionicity and Amnionicity:** The first trimester (11–14 weeks) is the ideal time to establish chorionicity, which dictates obstetric management.
    - _Dichorionic diamniotic:_ Identified by a thick dividing septum (>2 mm) and the "lambda" or "twin peak" sign.
    - _Monochorionic diamniotic:_ Identified by a thin dividing membrane (<2 mm) and the "T" sign.

**5. First Trimester Screening for Aneuploidy (11–14 Weeks)**

- **Nuchal Translucency (NT):** Measurement of the fluid-filled space at the back of the fetal neck. An NT thickness of ≥3 mm is a strong soft marker for chromosomal anomalies like Trisomy 21 (Down's syndrome), 18, and 13.
- **Nasal Bone:** Absence of the fetal nasal bone at 11–14 weeks is highly associated with Down's syndrome.
- USG markers are combined with maternal serum biochemistry (free β-hCG and PAPP-A) for the "Combined Test".

**6. Evaluation of Bleeding in Early Pregnancy**

- To differentiate between threatened, inevitable, and incomplete miscarriage.
- **Gestational Trophoblastic Disease (Molar Pregnancy):** Diagnosed by the characteristic "snowstorm" appearance (echogenic mass with many anechoic cystic spaces) and the absence of a fetus in a complete mole.

**7. Early Detection of Gross Fetal Anomalies**

- Major structural malformations such as anencephaly (absence of cranial vault) or severe abdominal wall defects can be identified by 10–14 weeks.

**8. Evaluation of Concomitant Pelvic Pathology**

- To assess and monitor associated pelvic masses during early pregnancy, such as uterine fibroids, ovarian cysts (e.g., corpus luteum or dermoid cysts), or congenital uterine malformations (e.g., bicornuate uterus).

**9. Adjunct to Invasive Diagnostic Procedures**

- Real-time ultrasound guidance is mandatory for performing Chorionic Villus Sampling (CVS) between 10 and 13 weeks or early amniocentesis, significantly reducing the risks of maternal or fetal injury.


15. Modified biophysical profile.
**MODIFIED BIOPHYSICAL PROFILE (MBPP)**

**Definition and Components** The modified biophysical profile is a primary method of antenatal fetal surveillance used to assess fetal wellbeing. It is a simplified and quicker version of the full biophysical profile and consists of two specific components:

1. **Non-Stress Test (NST):** A continuous electronic monitoring of the fetal heart rate to assess its reactivity.
2. **Amniotic Fluid Index (AFI):** Ultrasonographic measurement and estimation of the amniotic fluid volume.

**Advantages**

- It is commonly performed because it takes less time to complete compared to a full, detailed biophysical profile.
- It has a lower false-positive rate than using the Non-Stress Test alone and possesses a high positive predictive value for determining fetal wellbeing.

**Interpretation and Clinical Action**

- **Abnormal (Nonreassuring) Result:** A modified BPP is considered abnormal or nonreassuring if the NST is non-reactive and/or the AFI is < 5 cm.
- **Significance of Oligohydramnios:** An ultrasonographic finding of an amniotic fluid pocket < 2 cm or an AFI $\le$ 5 cm during the test strongly indicates fetal compromise and warrants the immediate induction of labor or delivery.

**Clinical Applications in Obstetrics**

- **Algorithm Step-up:** In standard fetal surveillance protocols, if an initial daily fetal movement count or NST yields a non-reactive or abnormal result, a modified BPP is immediately performed to assess the need for further management or Doppler flow velocimetry studies.
- **Prolonged / Post-dated Pregnancy:** In cases of post-term pregnancy where an expectant management approach (selective induction) is adopted to await spontaneous labor, fetal surveillance must be rigorously maintained using the modified biophysical profile at least twice a week.

16. Methods of delivery of aftercoming head in breech.
Here is a comprehensive, high-yield overview of the methods of delivery of the aftercoming head in a breech presentation, formatted for RGUHS examinations.

### **METHODS OF DELIVERY OF AFTERCOMING HEAD IN BREECH**

**INTRODUCTION** The delivery of the aftercoming head is the most crucial stage of a breech delivery. The time interval between the delivery of the umbilicus to the delivery of the mouth should preferably be between 5 to 10 minutes. The principal aim is to maintain the flexion of the head and avoid hasty traction from below, which can cause severe fetal injuries (e.g., cervical spine injury, intracranial hemorrhage).

The common methods employed to deliver the aftercoming head are:

---

**1. BURNS-MARSHALL METHOD** This is a widely used and relatively simple technique.

- **Principle:** The baby is allowed to hang by its own weight to promote descent and flexion of the head.
- **Procedure:**
    - The baby's trunk is allowed to hang downward.
    - An assistant applies firm suprapubic pressure in a downward and backward direction (directed more towards the sinciput) to promote flexion of the head.
    - When the nape of the neck (subocciput) is visible under the pubic arch, the obstetrician grasps the baby by the ankles with a finger placed between the two ankles.
    - Maintaining steady traction, the baby's trunk is swung in an upward and forward direction, forming a wide arc of a circle towards the mother's abdomen.
    - Simultaneously, the left hand guards the perineum, slipping it successively over the baby's face and brow.
    - Once the mouth is cleared, mucus is quickly aspirated using a mucus sucker, and the trunk is then depressed to deliver the rest of the head (vertex and occiput).

**2. MALAR FLEXION AND SHOULDER TRACTION (MODIFIED MAURICEAU-SMELLIE-VEIT TECHNIQUE)** This technique provides excellent control over the flexion and descent of the head.

- **Procedure:**
    - The baby is placed resting on the operator's supinated left forearm, with the limbs hanging on either side.
    - **Left Hand (Flexion):** The middle and index fingers of the left hand are placed over the fetal malar bones on either side (This is a modification; the original method placed a finger inside the baby's mouth) to maintain flexion.
    - **Right Hand (Traction):** The pronated right hand is placed over the baby's back. The ring and little fingers are placed on the child's right shoulder, the index finger on the left shoulder, and the middle finger rests on the suboccipital region.
    - Downward and backward traction is applied until the nape of the neck is visible under the pubic arch. An assistant simultaneously applies suprapubic pressure to maintain head flexion.
    - Finally, the fetus is carried in an upward and forward direction towards the mother's abdomen, releasing the face and brow, and the trunk is depressed to release the vertex and occiput.

**3. FORCEPS DELIVERY (PIPER FORCEPS)** Forceps can be used routinely or when the above methods fail, provided the head is in the pelvic cavity.

- **Instrument:** **Piper forceps** are specially designed for this purpose. They have an absent pelvic curve, a reverse pelvic curve, and long parallel shanks that allow the baby's body to rest against them during the delivery. Ordinary long-curved forceps (like Das's) can also be used.
- **Advantages:**
    - Traction force is applied directly to the head and not transmitted through the vulnerable cervical spine/neck.
    - Flexion of the head is better maintained.
    - Mucus can be sucked out from the mouth effectively while the forceps are in place.
- **Procedure:** An assistant elevates the baby's trunk to facilitate the introduction of the blades from below. Too much elevation must be avoided to prevent head extension. The head must be delivered slowly (over 1 minute) to reduce compression-decompression forces, which could lead to intracranial bleeding.

---

**MANAGEMENT OF ARRESTED AFTERCOMING HEAD (Complications)**

**1. Arrest of Head due to Incompletely Dilated Cervix:**

- Occurs due to prematurity, a macerated baby, or hasty extraction.
- **Management:** "Shoe-horn method" (pushing the cervix up while applying traction via malar flexion and shoulder traction). If this fails, **Dührssen's incisions** are made on the cervix at the 2 o'clock and 10 o'clock positions (and 6 o'clock if needed) to release the entrapped head.

**2. Occiput Posterior Position of the Aftercoming Head (Head Extended):**

- **Modified Prague Maneuver:** One hand of the operator supports the shoulders from below, while the other hand gently lifts the baby's trunk upwards towards the maternal abdomen. This flexes the head within the birth canal, allowing the occiput to deliver slowly over the perineum.

**3. Arrest at the Brim or Cavity (Due to contracted pelvis, deflexed head, or hydrocephalus):**

- **At the brim:** If due to deflexion, use malar flexion and shoulder traction along with suprapubic pressure. If due to contracted pelvis or dead hydrocephalic fetus, **craniotomy** (perforation of the suboccipital region to decompress the head) is performed. Forceps should not be applied to a high head.
- **In the cavity:** Delivery of the head by forceps is the best management.


17. Bishops score.
**BISHOP'S PREINDUCTION CERVICAL SCORING SYSTEM**

**1. DEFINITION AND PURPOSE** Bishop's score (devised by Edward Harry Bishop) is a preinduction cervical scoring system used to assess the "ripeness" or favorability of the cervix. It is primarily used to predict the likelihood of a successful induction of labor.

**2. COMPONENTS OF THE SCORE** The scoring system evaluates five clinical parameters via vaginal examination. Each parameter is awarded a score ranging from 0 to 3. The five parameters are:

1. **Cervical Dilatation** (in cm)
2. **Cervical Effacement** (in %)
3. **Cervical Consistency**
4. **Cervical Position**
5. **Station of the Fetal Head**

**3. THE SCORING TABLE** The scores are assigned based on the clinical findings as follows:

|Parameters|Score 0|Score 1|Score 2|Score 3|
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
|**Dilatation (cm)**|Closed|1–2 cm|3–4 cm|5+ cm|
|**Effacement (%)**|0–30%|40–50%|60–70%|$\ge$80%|
|**Consistency**|Firm|Medium|Soft|-|
|**Position**|Posterior|Midline|Anterior|-|
|**Station of Head**|-3|-2|-1, 0|+1, +2|

_Note on Modification (1991):_ A modification to this original scoring system replaces cervical effacement (%) with cervical length measured in centimeters: >4 cm (Score 0), 2–4 cm (Score 1), 1–2 cm (Score 2), and <1 cm (Score 3).

**4. INTERPRETATION AND CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE**

- **Total Maximum Score:** 13.
- **Favorable Score ($\ge$ 6):** A score between 6 and 13 is considered favorable. It indicates a "ripe" cervix, predicting a high success rate for labor induction. In these cases, there is very little to choose between oxytocin and prostaglandins for induction.
- **Unfavorable Score ($\le$ 5):** A score between 0 and 5 is considered unfavorable ("unripe" cervix). In such instances, prostaglandins have a distinct advantage over oxytocin for cervical ripening.
- **Most Important Parameter:** Out of the five parameters, the dilatation of the cervix is the most important predictor for successful induction of labor.

18. Pre requisites for forceps delivery.
**PREREQUISITES FOR FORCEPS DELIVERY (OPERATIVE VAGINAL DELIVERY)**

Before the application of obstetric forceps, certain essential criteria must be strictly fulfilled to ensure the safety of both the mother and the fetus. These prerequisites are categorized as follows:

**1. MATERNAL AND FETAL CRITERIA**

- **Cervix:** The cervix must be fully dilated.
- **Membranes:** The amniotic membranes must be ruptured.
- **Fetal Head Station:** The fetal head must be engaged (i.e., head $\le$ 1/5th palpable per abdomen).
- **Fetal Head Position:** The exact position of the fetal head must be known.
- **Caput and Moulding:** Caput succedaneum and moulding should not be more than +2. Excessive caput and moulding can mislead the assessment of the actual station of the head.
- **Pelvic Adequacy:** The maternal pelvis must be deemed clinically adequate for the fetus.
- **Bladder:** The maternal urinary bladder must be emptied (usually via catheterization) prior to the procedure.
- **Analgesia:** Adequate maternal analgesia must be administered (e.g., pudendal block for low/outlet forceps, or regional block for mid-cavity delivery).
- **Consent:** Informed consent (verbal or written) must be obtained from the patient following a clear explanation of the procedure.

**2. OPERATOR AND INSTITUTIONAL CRITERIA**

- **Experienced Operator:** The procedure must be performed by an experienced and skilled obstetrician.
- **Asepsis:** Strict aseptic techniques must be followed during the procedure.
- **Back-up Plan:** There must be a back-up plan and ready facilities to perform an immediate emergency Cesarean Delivery in case of failed forceps.
- **Neonatal Care:** A neonatologist must be present to attend to the baby immediately after birth.
- **Willingness to Abandon:** The operator must have the clinical judgment and willingness to abandon the procedure if difficulties are faced or if the head fails to descend with traction.

19. Lochia
**LOCHIA**

**1. DEFINITION** Lochia is the vaginal discharge that occurs for the first fortnight during the puerperium. The discharge originates from the uterine body, cervix, and vagina.

**2. CHARACTERISTICS**

- **Odor:** It has a peculiar offensive fishy smell.
- **Reaction:** It is alkaline initially, tending to become acidic toward the end.
- **Amount:** The average amount of discharge for the first 5–6 days is estimated to be approximately 250 mL.
- **Duration:** The normal duration may extend up to 3 weeks.

**3. TYPES OF LOCHIA (Based on Color and Duration)** Depending upon the variation in the color of the discharge, it is classified into three types:

**A. Lochia Rubra (Red)**

- **Duration:** 1–4 days.
- **Composition:** It consists of blood, shreds of fetal membranes and decidua, vernix caseosa, lanugo, and meconium.

**B. Lochia Serosa (Yellowish / Pink / Pale Brownish)**

- **Duration:** 5–9 days.
- **Composition:** It consists of fewer Red Blood Cells (RBCs) but more leukocytes, wound exudate, mucus from the cervix, and microorganisms (anaerobic streptococci and staphylococci).
- _Note:_ The presence of bacteria in this stage is not pathognomonic of infection unless it is associated with clinical signs of sepsis.

**C. Lochia Alba (Pale White)**

- **Duration:** 10–15 days.
- **Composition:** It contains plenty of decidual cells, leukocytes, mucus, cholesterin crystals, fatty and granular epithelial cells, and microorganisms.

**4. CLINICAL IMPORTANCE / ABNORMALITIES** The character of the lochial discharge provides vital information regarding any abnormal puerperal state. Vulval pads must be inspected daily to note the following deviations:

- **Odor:** If the lochia is malodorous, it indicates infection. The possibility of a retained plug or cotton piece inside the vagina should also be kept in mind.
- **Amount:**
    - _Scanty or absent:_ Signifies infection or lochiometra.
    - _Excessive:_ Indicates infection.
- **Color:** The persistence of the red color (lochia rubra) beyond the normal limit signifies subinvolution or retained bits of the conceptus.
- **Duration:** The persistence of lochia alba beyond 3 weeks suggests a local genital lesion.


20. Caput succedaneum.
**CAPUT SUCCEDANEUM**

**1. Definition** Caput succedaneum is a localized, diffuse, and boggy swelling on the fetal scalp formed due to the effusion of serum and stagnation of fluid in the layers of the scalp beneath the girdle of contact. The girdle of contact is usually the dilating cervix or the vulval ring.

**2. Mechanism of Formation**

- As the fetal head descends during labor, it presses against the dilating cervix or the vulval ring.
- The area of the scalp directly over the cervical opening is free from pressure, whereas the tissues in contact with the full circumference of the girdle of contact are highly compressed.
- This compression causes an obstruction to the venous return and lymphatic drainage from the unsupported area of the scalp.
- This obstruction leads to the stagnation of fluid, resulting in scalp edema and the appearance of a swelling.
- Caput succedaneum usually occurs after the rupture of the amniotic membranes.
- An artificial caput succedaneum, known as a "chignon," is produced when a Ventouse (vacuum extractor) cup is applied to the fetal scalp during operative vaginal delivery.

**3. Clinical Features and Characteristics**

- **Time of Appearance:** It is present immediately at birth.
- **Feel:** The swelling is soft, boggy, and compressible.
- **Extent:** It is diffuse and is not limited by the skull bones; it characteristically crosses and spreads over the suture lines.
- **Position:** It is not fixed to one site; its position can change and move to the dependent part of the head.
- **Resolution:** The swelling gradually reduces in size and disappears spontaneously within 24 hours (often within a few hours) after birth without requiring any active treatment.

**4. Clinical Importance**

- The presence of a caput signifies that the fetal head has been held in a static position for a long period of time against the dilating cervix.
- The exact location of the caput provides valuable information regarding the position the head occupied in the maternal pelvis and the degree of head flexion achieved during labor.
- In a left-sided position, the caput is typically placed on the right parietal bone.
- In a right-sided position, the caput forms on the left parietal bone.
- With increasing flexion of the fetal head during descent, the caput forms more posteriorly.

**5. Differential Diagnosis (Differentiation from Cephalhematoma)** Caput succedaneum must be clinically differentiated from a cephalhematoma.

- While caput is present at birth, crosses suture lines, and resolves within hours, a cephalhematoma typically appears hours after birth, is strictly limited to one bone (does not cross suture lines), and takes weeks or months to disappear.
- Caput is formed by an effusion of serum (scalp edema), whereas a cephalhematoma is a hemorrhage occurring under the periosteum of the skull bone.



21. Oral iron therapy.
**ORAL IRON THERAPY IN PREGNANCY**

Oral iron supplementation is the primary and most widely used modality for the prevention and treatment of iron deficiency anaemia during pregnancy. Iron is best absorbed in its ferrous form.

**1. PROPHYLACTIC THERAPY**

- **Indications:** Most women have inadequate iron stores (only 20% have the minimum 500 mg required for pregnancy), making routine supplementation essential.
- **Regimen:** Supplementary iron therapy is needed for all pregnant mothers from 16 weeks of gestation onwards.
- **Dose:** Daily administration of 200 mg of ferrous sulphate (which contains 60 mg of elemental iron) along with 1 mg of folic acid is highly effective. If the haemoglobin level is above 10 g%, one tablet daily is sufficient, but the dose should be proportionately increased with lower haemoglobin levels.

**2. CURATIVE THERAPY**

- **Objectives:** To raise the haemoglobin level as near to normal as possible and to replenish the iron stores before the patient goes into labour.
- **Regimen:** The initial dose is one tablet given three times a day, 30 minutes before meals. If a larger dose is necessary, it can be stepped up gradually over 3–4 days to a maximum of six tablets per day.
- **Duration:** Treatment must be continued until the blood picture returns to normal. Thereafter, a maintenance dose of one tablet daily should be continued for at least 100 days postpartum to replenish the depleted iron stores.

**3. COMMON ORAL IRON PREPARATIONS** Various ferrous preparations are available in tablet or capsule forms. Despite claims of superiority of one over another, ferrous sulphate remains the most widely used.

- **Ferrous sulphate:** 300 mg yields 60 mg of elemental iron. (A standard 'Fersolate' tablet contains 325 mg ferrous sulphate, yielding 60 mg elemental iron).
- **Ferrous sulphate (dried):** 200 mg yields 65 mg of elemental iron.
- **Ferrous fumarate:** 200 mg yields 65 mg of elemental iron.
- **Ferrous gluconate:** 300 mg yields 35 mg of elemental iron.
- **Iron polymaltose:** Contains 100 mg of elemental iron in the _ferric_ form. It is soluble at a neutral pH, possesses improved absorption when taken with food, and causes fewer gastrointestinal problems.

**4. FACTORS AFFECTING ABSORPTION**

- **Inhibitors:** Absorption is significantly reduced by antacids, milk, tannins (found in tea), oxalates, phytates, and phosphates. Tea should be avoided within 1 hour of taking the iron tablet.
- **Enhancers:** Ascorbic acid (Vitamin C), lactate, and various amino acids increase iron absorption.

**5. SIDE EFFECTS** Oral iron therapy can cause several side effects, which may lead to non-compliance.

- **Gastrointestinal:** Gastric irritation, nausea, vomiting, epigastric pain, constipation, and diarrhoea.
- **Others:** Metallic taste (often related to high doses or specific preparations).
- _Management of Intolerance:_ If intolerance (evidenced by epigastric pain) occurs, it is preferable to reduce the dose to one tablet daily and then gradually increase it back to the required dose.

**6. RESPONSE TO THERAPY** A positive response to oral iron therapy is indicated by the following clinical and haematological parameters:

- **Clinical:** A restored sense of wellbeing, increased appetite, and an improved general outlook of the patient.
- **Haematological:**
    - Reticulocytosis occurs within 7–10 days.
    - Haemoglobin levels should rise by approximately 2.0 g/dL every 3–4 weeks.
    - Haematocrit values progressively return to normal.
- If no significant clinical or haematological improvement is evident within 3 weeks, a diagnostic re-evaluation is necessary.

**7. CAUSES OF FAILURE OF IMPROVEMENT** If the haemoglobin fails to rise, the following causes must be investigated:

1. Improper typing of the anaemia (e.g., treating thalassaemia as iron deficiency).
2. Defective absorption due to associated gastrointestinal disorders.
3. Patient non-compliance (failure to take the prescribed iron).
4. Concurrent, ongoing blood loss (e.g., hookworm infestation or bleeding piles).
5. Inhibition of erythropoiesis by an underlying infection.
6. Co-existent folate deficiency.

**8. CONTRAINDICATIONS TO ORAL THERAPY** Oral iron should be avoided, and parenteral iron or blood transfusion considered, in the following situations:

- Intolerance to oral iron preparations.
- Severe anaemia diagnosed in advanced pregnancy, where the unpredictable absorption rate of oral iron will not correct the deficit in the short time available before delivery.



22. Janani Suraksha Yojana.
**JANANI SURAKSHA YOJANA (JSY)**

**Overview and Objective**

- Janani Suraksha Yojana (JSY) is a safe motherhood intervention scheme implemented under the National Rural Health Mission (NRHM) (2007).
- The primary objective of the scheme is to promote and increase **institutional deliveries** in order to reduce maternal and neonatal mortality.

**Impact**

- The support and implementation of the JSY scheme have led to a significant rise in the number of institutional deliveries across India.

**Related Initiative: Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram (JSSK)**

- As an extension of safe motherhood initiatives, the Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram (JSSK) was introduced under the umbrella of the National Health Mission (NHM).
- **Target Areas:** While it is implemented in all states and Union Territories (UTs), JSSK places a special focus on Low-Performing States (LPS) that historically have low institutional delivery rates.
- These Low-Performing States include Uttar Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Bihar, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Assam, Rajasthan, Odisha, and Jammu and Kashmir.
- Together with the Reproductive and Child Health (RCH) Programme and the Safe Motherhood Programme, these initiatives are key government strategies to combat obstetric emergencies, encourage deliveries by skilled birth attendants, and successfully reduce maternal deaths.
