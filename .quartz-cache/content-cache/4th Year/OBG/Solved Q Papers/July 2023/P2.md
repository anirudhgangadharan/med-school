1. 1. Enumerate causes of female infertility. Discuss the tests for ovulation and discuss the management of an Ovulatory Infertility.
**CAUSES OF FEMALE INFERTILITY**

Female factors are responsible for 40–55% of infertility cases. The causes are broadly classified into the following anatomical and physiological categories:

**1. Ovulatory Dysfunction (30–40%)**

- Anovulation or oligo-ovulation (e.g., Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome),.
- Luteal phase defect (LPD): Inadequate growth and function of the corpus luteum leading to insufficient progesterone secretion.
- Luteinized unruptured follicle (LUF) syndrome (trapped ovum): Often associated with endometriosis or hyperprolactinemia.
- Decreased ovarian reserve or premature ovarian failure,.

**2. Tubal and Peritoneal Factors (25–35%)**

- Tubal obstruction due to pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), commonly caused by Chlamydia, Gonococcus, or tuberculosis.
- Peritubal adhesions and endosalpingeal damage.
- Previous tubal surgery or tubal sterilization.
- Salpingitis isthmica nodosa or tubal endometriosis.
- Pelvic adhesions or minimal to severe pelvic endometriosis distorting pelvic anatomy or altering peritoneal fluid,.

**3. Uterine Factors (10%)**

- Congenital malformations of the uterus (e.g., septate or bicornuate uterus).
- Uterine synechiae (Asherman’s syndrome) or tubercular endometritis.
- Fibroid uterus, especially submucous fibroids that distort the cavity.
- Inadequate secretory endometrium (hindering nidation).

**4. Cervical Factors (5%)**

- Physiologic: Scanty or hostile cervical mucus following amputation, conization, or deep cauterization of the cervix.
- Anatomic: Congenital elongation of the cervix or second-degree uterine prolapse.
- Immunological: Presence of antisperm or sperm-immobilizing antibodies in the cervical mucus,.

**5. Vaginal and Combined Factors**

- Congenital vaginal atresia, transverse vaginal septum, septate vagina, or narrow introitus causing dyspareunia.
- Advanced maternal age (>35 years), infrequent intercourse, or use of spermicidal lubricants,.

---

**TESTS FOR OVULATION**

Diagnosis of ovulation is made through indirect, direct, and conclusive evidences.

**A. Indirect Evidences**

- **Menstrual History:** Regular, normal menstrual loss between 20–35 years of age is a strong presumptive evidence of ovulation. Associated midmenstrual pain (Mittelschmerz), primary dysmenorrhea, or premenstrual syndrome (PMS) suggest an ovulatory cycle.
- **Basal Body Temperature (BBT):** Daily charting reveals a "biphasic pattern". The temperature rises by 0.5–1°F (0.2–0.5°C) following ovulation due to the thermogenic effect of progesterone and norepinephrine, and sustains throughout the luteal phase.
- **Cervical Mucus Study:** Following ovulation, the stretchability (spinnbarkeit) is lost, and the classic fern tree pattern disappears after the 22nd day of the cycle due to progesterone action.
- **Vaginal Cytology:** The maturation index shifts to the left in the luteal phase, demonstrating a preponderance of folded intermediate cells (navicular cells) against a dirty background,.
- **Hormone Estimations:**
    - **Serum Progesterone:** Levels >6 ng/mL on day 21 (or mid-luteal phase) indicate successful ovulation and adequate luteal function,.
    - **Serum Luteinizing Hormone (LH):** Daily midcycle estimation detects the LH surge; ovulation occurs 10–12 hours after the LH peak or 34–36 hours after the onset of the surge.
    - **Urinary LH:** Kits detect the midcycle LH surge, predicting ovulation within 14–24 hours.
    - **Serum Estradiol:** Peak rise occurs approximately 24–36 hours prior to ovulation.
- **Endometrial Biopsy:** Performed on day 21–23. Secretory activity of the endometrial glands confirms ovulation. Subnuclear vacuolation is the earliest histologic evidence, appearing 36–48 hours post-ovulation.
- **Transvaginal Sonography (TVS):** Serial scans show the dominant follicle reaching 18–20 mm prior to ovulation. The collapse of the follicle and the appearance of free fluid in the pouch of Douglas provide evidence of recent ovulation.

**B. Direct Evidences**

- **Laparoscopy:** Visualization of a recent corpus luteum or detection of an ovum aspirated from the peritoneal fluid in the pouch of Douglas.

**C. Conclusive Evidence**

- **Pregnancy:** The surest and only conclusive evidence of ovulation.

---

**MANAGEMENT OF OVULATORY DYSFUNCTION (ANOVULATORY INFERTILITY)**

The management of dysovulatory infertility aims to correct underlying biochemical abnormalities and induce ovulation using general, medical, or surgical measures.

**1. General Measures**

- **Weight Reduction:** In obese anovulatory women (especially those with PCOS), weight reduction decreases hyperinsulinemia and hyperandrogenemia, thereby improving the response to ovulation-inducing drugs,.

**2. Pharmacological Induction of Ovulation**

- **Clomiphene Citrate (CC):** The first-line drug for normogonadotropic-normoprolactinemic anovulation and PCOS. It acts as an antiestrogen, blocking hypothalamic receptors to increase GnRH pulse amplitude, ultimately increasing FSH/LH secretion. The starting dose is 50 mg daily for 5 days (from day 2 to day 5 of the cycle), increased up to a maximum of 250 mg daily if needed. Ovulation occurs 5–7 days after the last dose.
- **Letrozole:** An aromatase inhibitor that prevents estrogen production, thus removing negative feedback and increasing FSH release. Dose is 2.5 mg or 5 mg daily for 5 days. It has no antiestrogenic adverse effects on the endometrium or cervical mucus, making it an excellent alternative in CC-resistant cases,.
- **Gonadotropins (hMG, uFSH, rFSH):** Indicated for hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, clomiphene-resistant PCOS, or unexplained infertility. Stimulation begins on cycle day 2 or 3 and is monitored rigorously with TVS folliculometry and serum estradiol. Once the leading follicle reaches 18–20 mm and E2 levels are optimal, ovulation is triggered with 5000–10000 IU of hCG.
- **GnRH Therapy:** Pulsatile administration via an infusion pump stimulates physiological gonadotropin secretion, used primarily in hypothalamic amenorrhea or Kallmann's syndrome,.
- **GnRH Analogs (Agonists/Antagonists):** Used alongside gonadotropins for controlled ovarian stimulation (COS) in Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART) to prevent a premature LH surge.

**3. Correction of Associated Biochemical Abnormalities**

- **Insulin Resistance:** Metformin (an insulin sensitizer) at 500 mg thrice daily is used alongside clomiphene in obese PCOS women to reduce hyperinsulinemia and improve ovulation rates,.
- **Hyperprolactinemia:** Dopamine agonists (Bromocriptine or Cabergoline) normalize prolactin levels, restoring normal GnRH pulsatility and ovulation,.
- **Hyperandrogenism:** Dexamethasone 0.5 mg daily may be given during the follicular phase to suppress elevated adrenal androgens.
- **Hypothyroidism:** Thyroid hormone replacement (Thyroxine/Eltroxin 0.1 mg) restores the ovulatory axis in subclinical or overt hypothyroidism.

**4. Surgical Management**

- **Laparoscopic Ovarian Drilling (LOD):** Indicated for CC-resistant PCOS. 4–6 punctures are made per ovary using diathermy or laser, which reduces intraovarian androgens and restores spontaneous ovulation with a lower risk of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS) and multiple pregnancy,.
- **Bariatric Surgery:** Considered for morbidly obese women (BMI >35) who fail to ovulate and have severe metabolic derangements.
- **Pituitary Surgery:** Transsphenoidal surgery may be indicated for refractory pituitary prolactinomas causing anovulation.

**5. Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART)**

- If standard induction protocols fail or if additional subfertility factors exist, Controlled Ovarian Stimulation (COS) with Intrauterine Insemination (IUI) is employed,.
- In refractory cases or those with premature ovarian failure, In Vitro Fertilization and Embryo Transfer (IVF-ET) or the use of donor oocytes is the ultimate management step,.


2. Mention causes of postmenopausal vaginal bleeding. Discuss the diagnosis and management of Endometrial Carcinoma Stage I and Stage II
**CAUSES OF POSTMENOPAUSAL VAGINAL BLEEDING**

Postmenopausal bleeding is defined as bleeding per vagina following established menopause. It is a highly significant symptom, as approximately one-third of cases are due to genital malignancy.

**1. Malignant Causes**

- **Carcinoma of the endometrium:** The most common malignant cause.
- **Carcinoma of the cervix:** Can present as bleeding or contact bleeding.
- **Other Genital Malignancies:** Carcinoma of the vagina, vulva, or fallopian tube.
- **Uterine Sarcoma**.
- **Estrogen-producing Ovarian Tumors:** Granulosa cell tumor or thecoma.

**2. Benign/Hyperplastic Causes**

- **Endometrial Hyperplasia:** Often secondary to unopposed estrogen.
- **Uterine and Cervical Polyps**.
- **Dysfunctional Uterine Bleeding (DUB)**.

**3. Atrophic and Inflammatory Causes**

- **Senile (Atrophic) Endometritis:** Due to loss of periodic shedding and estrogen deficiency.
- **Senile Vaginitis**.
- **Tubercular Endometritis**.
- **Decubitus Ulcer:** Secondary to pelvic organ prolapse.

**4. Iatrogenic and Other Causes**

- **Hormone Replacement Therapy (HRT):** Withdrawal bleeding following estrogen intake.
- **Retained Foreign Body:** Forgotten pessary or Intrauterine Contraceptive Device (IUCD).
- **Urethral Pathology:** Urethral caruncle, polyp, prolapsed mucosa, or carcinoma.
- **Idiopathic/Unknown:** In about 25% of cases, no cause is found.

---

**ENDOMETRIAL CARCINOMA: DIAGNOSIS**

Any case of postmenopausal bleeding must be considered endometrial carcinoma unless proved otherwise.

**Clinical Features**

- **Patient Profile:** Typically a nulliparous, postmenopausal woman (median age 60 years). Often associated with "corpus cancer syndrome" (obesity, hypertension, diabetes) and late menopause.
- **Symptoms:**
    - Postmenopausal bleeding (75%), which may be slight, irregular, or continuous.
    - Watery, foul-smelling, or purulent vaginal discharge (pyometra).
    - Colicky lower abdominal pain due to uterine contractions attempting to expel a polypoidal growth.
- **Signs:**
    - Pallor and obesity may be noted.
    - _Speculum Examination:_ Cervix often appears healthy, but blood or purulent offensive discharge escapes through the external os.
    - _Bimanual Examination:_ Uterus may be normal, atrophic, or enlarged (due to pyometra, associated fibroid, or tumor spread).

**Investigations**

- **Transvaginal Sonography (TVS) & Color Doppler:** Used as an initial adjunct. Findings suggestive of malignancy include endometrial thickness >4 mm, hyperechoic endometrium with an irregular outline, increased vascularity with low resistance, and intracavitary fluid. Saline infusion sonography (SIS) increases diagnostic accuracy.
- **Endometrial Sampling (Outpatient):** Using a soft, flexible plastic suction cannula (Pipelle) or Sharman curette. It has a diagnostic reliability of >90%.
- **Hysteroscopy and Directed Biopsy:** Allows direct visualization of the uterine cavity and targeted biopsy of suspicious lesions.
- **Fractional Curettage:** The definitive diagnostic method. Done under anesthesia with utmost gentleness to avoid uterine perforation.
- **Tumor Markers:** Serum CA 125 may be elevated in advanced disease or uterine papillary serous carcinoma (UPSC).
- **Imaging for Staging:**
    - **MRI:** Best for assessing the depth of myometrial invasion, cervical extension, and lymph node status.
    - **CT Scan / PET-CT:** Useful for detecting extrauterine spread, pelvic/para-aortic lymph node metastases, and distant metastases.

---

**ENDOMETRIAL CARCINOMA: MANAGEMENT OF STAGE I & STAGE II**

Staging is based on surgical-pathological evaluation (FIGO guidelines). Preoperative preparation includes optimizing comorbid conditions like diabetes and hypertension.

**Management of Stage I (Tumor confined to the corpus uteri)** Surgery is the absolute mainstay of treatment.

- **Primary Surgery:** Extrafascial Total Abdominal Hysterectomy (TAH) with Bilateral Salpingo-Oophorectomy (BSO). This can be performed via laparotomy (midline/paramedian incision), laparoscopy, or robotic-assisted surgery.
- **Surgical Steps & Staging:**
    - Peritoneal washings are taken immediately upon opening the abdomen for cytology.
    - Thorough exploration of the liver, diaphragm, omentum, and pelvic/para-aortic nodes.
    - The uterus must _not_ be held by a vulsellum (to prevent cell spillage); instead, traction is applied using clamps at the uterine cornu.
    - The extirpated uterus is cut open in the operating room to grossly evaluate tumor size, cervical extension, and myometrial invasion (often confirmed via frozen section).
- **Lymphadenectomy:** For "High-Risk" women (tumor >2 cm, G3 histology, deep myometrial invasion >50%, or cervical extension), systematic pelvic and para-aortic lymph node sampling/lymphadenectomy is indicated.
- **Adjuvant Radiotherapy:**
    - _Stage IA (G1, G2) without myometrial invasion:_ Observation only.
    - _Stage IA (G2) with <50% myometrial invasion:_ Vaginal vault brachytherapy (5000–6000 cGy).
    - _Stage IB (>50% myometrial invasion):_ Whole pelvis external beam radiation (4500–5000 cGy) plus a vaginal cuff boost.
- **Adjuvant Chemotherapy:** Indicated in all Stage I diseases (except Stage IA Grade 1). Typically, carboplatin and paclitaxel are given for six cycles.
- **Fertility-Sparing Surgery:** Strictly reserved for highly selected young women with Stage IA G1 disease without myometrial invasion. Managed with high-dose progestins (oral MPA or LNG-IUS) followed by strict surveillance and definitive surgery after childbearing.

**Management of Stage II (Tumor invades cervical stroma, but does not extend beyond uterus)** The presence of cervical stromal involvement necessitates a more radical approach. The management options include:

- **Option A (Primary Radical Surgery):** Radical Hysterectomy with Bilateral Salpingo-Oophorectomy and systematic pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy.
- **Option B (Combined Therapy):** Preoperative radiation (external beam and intracavitary) followed 6 weeks later by extrafascial Total Abdominal Hysterectomy and Bilateral Salpingo-Oophorectomy.
- **Option C (Surgery followed by Radiation):** Initial simple hysterectomy followed by external pelvic radiation and intravaginal brachytherapy.



3. Complications of Acute Pelvic Inflammatory Disease and its late sequele.
**COMPLICATIONS OF ACUTE PELVIC INFLAMMATORY DISEASE (PID) AND ITS LATE SEQUELAE**

Acute Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID) is a spectrum of infection and inflammation of the upper genital tract. If left untreated or inadequately treated, it can lead to severe acute complications and debilitating long-term consequences.

**A. IMMEDIATE COMPLICATIONS** The immediate risks associated with acute PID (often following delivery or abortion) include the spread of infection and systemic involvement:

- **Peritonitis:** Pelvic peritonitis or even generalized peritonitis due to the escape of purulent exudate into the peritoneal cavity.
- **Abscess Formation:** Development of a tubo-ovarian abscess or pelvic abscess.
- **Septicemia:** Gram-negative septicemia which can produce distant metastatic infections like arthritis or myocarditis.
- **Shock & Organ Failure:** Endotoxic shock, which can lead to oliguria or anuria.
- **Coagulopathy:** Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC).
- **Parametritis and Pelvic Cellulitis:** Direct spread of infection to the pelvic cellular tissues.
- **Vascular Complications:** Pelvic thrombophlebitis (typically appearing after 7–10 days) and subsequent pulmonary embolism.

**B. LATE SEQUELAE OF PID (High Yield)** Amongst all women with acute PID, about one in four will suffer from further long-term sequelae. The late complications include:

- **Infertility (Tubal Factor):** This is a major consequence caused by tubal damage, cornual block, or tubo-ovarian mass. The infertility rate strongly correlates with the number of PID episodes:
    - 12% after a single episode.
    - 25% after two episodes.
    - 50% after three episodes.
- **Ectopic Pregnancy:** The risk of ectopic pregnancy is increased 6-10 fold due to ciliary damage, tubal scarring, and impaired tubal motility.
- **Chronic Pelvic Pain & Dysmenorrhea:** Persistent lower abdominal pain and congestive dysmenorrhea are very common due to altered pelvic anatomy and inflammation.
- **Pelvic Adhesive Disease:** Formation of dense peritubal and pelvic adhesions binding the uterus, tubes, ovaries, and intestines.
- **Structural Tubal Damage:** Formation of hydrosalpinx, pyosalpinx, and chronic tubo-ovarian mass.
- **Dyspareunia:** Deep dyspareunia resulting from pelvic adhesions and chronic inflammation, often leading to marital disharmony.
- **Recurrent Infections:** Residual infection in the pelvis leading to periodic acute exacerbations (recurrent PID).
- **Intestinal Obstruction:** Secondary to dense pelvic adhesions involving the bowel loops.
- **Chronic Ill Health:** Long-standing pelvic inflammation leads to generalized weakness and chronic ill health.
- **Endometriosis:** Associated as a late sequela in some cases.
- **Surgical Endpoint:** In severe, intractable cases with chronic pelvic pain or frozen pelvis, the condition may ultimately require a hysterectomy.


4. Fother Gill’s operation.

**FOTHERGILL’S (MANCHESTER) OPERATION**

**Definition and Objectives** Fothergill’s or Manchester operation is a restorative surgical procedure designed to correct uterine descent associated with cystocele and rectocele, specifically in cases where preservation of the uterus is desirable.

**Indications**

- Women desirous of preserving their reproductive and menstrual functions.
- When symptoms are primarily due to vaginal prolapse associated with elongation of the (supravaginal) cervix.
- Used as an alternative to vaginal hysterectomy in cases of uterovaginal prolapse where the family is completed but the uterus is preserved.

**Component Parts of the Operation (High Yield)** The composite steps of Fothergill's operation include:

1. Preliminary Dilatation and Curettage (D&C).
2. Amputation of the cervix.
3. Plication of Mackenrodt's ligaments in front of the cervix.
4. Anterior colporrhaphy.
5. Colpoperineorrhaphy.

**Principal Steps of the Surgery**

- **Preliminary D&C:** The uterine sound provides an idea of cervical elongation. Dilatation facilitates the passage of sutures through the cervical canal during flap coverage, ensures adequate uterine drainage, and prevents cervical stenosis during healing. Curettage removes unhealthy endometrium.
- **Anterior Dissection:** The initial steps mimic an anterior colporrhaphy, proceeding up to the mobilization and pushing up of the bladder.
- **Cervical Dissection:** The posterior lip of the cervix is held with a vulsellum and drawn upwards. The anterior transverse incision is extended posteriorly across the posterior cervicovaginal junction, and the vaginal wall is dissected off the cervix.
- **Clamping the Ligaments:** Mackenrodt’s ligaments, along with the descending cervical arteries on either side, are clamped at a higher level, cut, and replaced by ligatures (e.g., chromic catgut No. '1').
- **Enterocele Management:** The pouch of Douglas is checked for an enterocele, which is repaired if detected.
- **Amputation of the Cervix:** The cervix is amputated at the calculated level. If future reproduction is desired, a low amputation is performed.
- **Covering the Stump:** The posterior lip of the amputated cervix is covered by the vaginal flap using a Sturmdorf suture or Bonney’s method.
- **Fothergill’s Stitch / Plication:** The cut ends of Mackenrodt's ligaments are sutured to the anterior surface of the cervix, or they are fixed using the classic Fothergill's stitch to make the uterus anteverted.
    - _Sequence of Fothergill's stitch:_ Vaginal skin (at Fothergill’s lateral point) $\rightarrow$ Mackenrodt’s ligament $\rightarrow$ cervical tissue (outside inwards) $\rightarrow$ cervical tissue (inside outwards) $\rightarrow$ Mackenrodt’s ligament (opposite side) $\rightarrow$ Vaginal skin (opposite side).
- **Closure:** The pubocervical fascia is approximated. The redundant portion of the vaginal mucosa is excised, and the cut margins are opposed with interrupted sutures.
- **Posterior Repair:** A posterior colpoperineorrhaphy is performed.
- **Postoperative Formalities:** Toileting of the vagina is done, an optional vaginal pack is inserted, and a self-retaining catheter is introduced.

**Complications** **1. During Operation:**

- Haemorrhage.
- Injury to the bladder and rectum.

**2. Postoperative (Immediate):**

- Retention of urine or cystitis.
- Primary or secondary haemorrhage.
- Infection.

**3. Late Complications:**

- Dyspareunia.
- Cervical stenosis leading to haematometra.
- Infertility.
- Cervical incompetency (leading to mid-trimester abortion).
- Cervical dystocia during subsequent labour.
- Recurrence of the prolapse.


5. Classification of causes of AUB.
**CLASSIFICATION OF CAUSES OF ABNORMAL UTERINE BLEEDING (AUB)**

**Definition:** Any uterine bleeding outside the normal volume, duration, regularity, or frequency is considered Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (AUB).

According to the **FIGO 2011 Classification**, all causes of AUB are subdivided into nine main categories using the acronym **PALM-COEIN**. It broadly divides AUB into structural and nonstructural causes.

**I. STRUCTURAL CAUSES (PALM GROUP)** These are structural or histological causes that can be diagnosed by imaging or histopathology.

- **P - Polyp (AUB-P):** Includes endometrial or endocervical polyps.
- **A - Adenomyosis (AUB-A):** Ingrowth of endometrium into the myometrium, diagnosed optimally by MRI or Transvaginal Sonography (TVS).
- **L - Leiomyoma (AUB-L):** Fibroid uterus. This category is further subdivided based on the relationship with the endometrial cavity:
    - **AUB-Lsm:** Submucosal myoma (affecting the endometrial cavity).
    - **AUB-Lo:** Other myomas (intramural or subserosal, not affecting the cavity).
- **M - Malignancy and Hyperplasia (AUB-M):** Includes endometrial hyperplasia and female genital tract cancers (vulva, vagina, cervix, endometrium, uterus, tubes, and ovary). It is observed in 4.5% of postmenopausal women presenting with bleeding symptoms.

**II. NONSTRUCTURAL CAUSES (COEIN GROUP)** These systemic or nonstructural causes are usually not diagnosed by imaging.

- **C - Coagulopathy (AUB-C):** Includes systemic bleeding disorders such as Von Willebrand disease, prothrombin deficiency, Hemophilia A and B, Idiopathic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (ITP), leukemia, and deficiency of clotting factors (V, VII, X, XI, XIII). Nearly 20% of adolescent girls presenting with AUB have underlying coagulation disorders. Women on anticoagulation therapy (Heparin, LMWH) are also included.
- **O - Ovulatory dysfunction (AUB-O):** Often seen in the extremes of reproductive age (premenarchal/adolescents and perimenopausal women). It is caused by an abnormality of neuroendocrine function (HPO axis), resulting in a steady state of anovulation. This leads to continued estrogenic endometrial stimulation without the opposing effect of progesterone.
- **E - Endometrial (AUB-E):** Arises from primary endometrial dysfunction in the presence of normal ovulatory cycles. Mechanisms include abnormal endometrial production of vasodilators (PGI2, excessive PGE) and deficiency of vasoconstrictors (PGF2a). Chronic inflammatory changes and subclinical infections like _Chlamydia trachomatis_ are also implicated.
- **I - Iatrogenic (AUB-I):** Mostly due to medical interventions or medications. Causes include breakthrough bleeding from combined oral contraceptives, erratic use of contraceptive steroids (vaginal rings), or the use of Intrauterine Contraceptive Devices (IUCDs) and Levonorgestrel Intrauterine Systems (LNG-IUS).
- **N - Not yet classified / Not otherwise specified (AUB-N):** Rare or poorly defined causes that do not fit into the other categories. This includes trauma and foreign bodies.

_(Note: When documenting a diagnosis, it is systematic to indicate the presence (1) or absence (0) of a condition. For example, AUB due to adenomyosis alone is written as AUB: P0 A1 L0 M0 – C0 O0 E0 I0 N0)_.

7. Dermoid cyst.


**DERMOID CYST (MATURE CYSTIC TERATOMA)**

**Introduction and Origin** A dermoid cyst, or mature cystic teratoma, is a benign germ cell tumor of the ovary that arises from a single germ cell arrested after the first meiotic division. The karyotype in the majority of cases is 46,XX. The term 'dermoid cyst' is technically a misnomer, as it contains tissues derived from all three embryonic germ cell layers, not just the ectoderm.

**Incidence**

- It is the most common mature teratoma, constituting about 97% of all teratomata.
- It accounts for 30–40% of all ovarian tumors and is the most common germ cell tumor (95%).
- It is bilateral in 15–20% of cases.
- It is the most common ovarian neoplasm in adolescence and makes up 20–40% of all ovarian tumors found during pregnancy.

**Pathology and Morphology**

- **Gross Appearance:** It is usually of moderate size (generally <10 cm) with a tense and smooth capsule.
- **Cut Section:** The cyst predominantly contains thick sebaceous material (sebum) and hair. "Butter balls" (aggregated sebum) may also be present.
- **Rokitansky’s Protuberance:** A characteristic solid area that projects into the cystic cavity. It is covered by skin containing sweat and sebaceous glands, and it is the site where teeth and bones are typically found. Histological sections should always be taken from this area to rule out malignancy.
- **Microscopic Features (Germ Layer Derivatives):**
    - _Ectodermal:_ Skin, hair, teeth, nerve tissue, glial tissue, and sebaceous material.
    - _Mesodermal:_ Bone, cartilage, and muscle.
    - _Endodermal:_ Thyroid tissue, salivary gland, bronchus, and intestine.
- **Special Variants:** _Struma ovarii_ (contains predominantly thyroid tissue and may cause hyperthyroidism) and _strumal carcinoids_ (can secrete serotonin and cause carcinoid syndrome).

**Clinical Features**

- About 50% of these tumors are symptomatic, typically presenting in the reproductive age group.
- **Bimanual Examination:** If an ovarian cyst is palpated lying anterior to the uterus, it is highly suggestive of a dermoid cyst.

**Diagnostic Investigations**

- **Ultrasonography (USG):** Characteristic features include the "tip of the iceberg" sign (caused by amorphous echogenic interfaces of fat, hair, and tissue that obscure structures behind them), fat-fluid or hair-fluid levels, and Rokitansky's protuberance appearing as a distinct echogenic region.
- **Plain X-ray Abdomen:** May reveal radio-opaque shadows of teeth or bones.
- **CT Scan:** Highly diagnostic, showing a mixture of low-density areas (fat), high-density areas (dental elements or calcification), and distinct fat-fluid levels.

**Complications**

- **Torsion of the Pedicle (Axial Rotation):** This is the most common complication, occurring in 15–20% of cases. The tumor is highly prone to torsion due to its moderate size, free mobility, long pedicle, and high fat content which makes it lighter.
- **Malignant Transformation:** The risk of malignancy is very low (1–2%), with squamous cell carcinoma being the most common type.
- **Rupture:** Rupture is uncommon (1%), but if it occurs, the spillage of highly irritant sebaceous material into the peritoneal cavity causes severe chemical (irritant) peritonitis.
- **Infection:** Can occasionally occur, often following torsion.

**Management**

- **Operative Treatment:** The procedure of choice in young women is an ovarian cystectomy with the preservation of normal, healthy ovarian tissue. In postmenopausal women, a total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy is justified.
- **Laparoscopic Approach:** Laparoscopic cystectomy using an endobag is highly recommended to prevent the spillage of contents.
- **Pelvic Wash:** Copious pelvic irrigation with normal saline is essential during surgery to remove any spilled sebum and prevent postoperative chemical peritonitis.


8. Adenomyosis.

**ADENOMYOSIS**

**DEFINITION** Adenomyosis is a condition characterized by the ingrowth of functioning endometrium (both glandular and stromal components) directly into the myometrium. It is also referred to as endometriosis interna.

**ETIOLOGY AND RISK FACTORS** The exact cause of such ingrowth is unknown, but several strongly associated factors include:

- **Age & Parity:** It is most commonly seen in parous women (90% of cases) who are typically older than 40 years of age (80% of cases).
- **Trauma:** It may be related to repeated childbirths or vigorous uterine curettage, which traumatizes the deeper endometrium.
- **Hormonal:** Excess estrogen effect is a known risk factor.
- **Associated Pathologies:** Pelvic endometriosis coexists in about 40% of cases. Nearly 60% of women with adenomyosis have coexistent pathologies like uterine myomas or endometrial hyperplasia.

**PATHOGENESIS** The condition develops due to the extension of endometrial glands and stroma beyond the endometrial-myometrial interface (EMI). Because the uterus lacks a submucosa, the endometrial glands lie in direct contact with the underlying myometrium, forming nests deep within it.

- **Junctional Zone (JZ) Dysfunction:** MRI studies reveal that a disturbance of the normal junctional zone (the innermost layer of the myometrium) predisposes to this secondary infiltration.
- **Myometrial Hyperplasia:** The presence of ectopic endometrial tissue induces surrounding myometrial hyperplasia.

**PATHOLOGY**

- **Gross/Naked Eye Appearance:** The uterus is diffusely and symmetrically enlarged (2-3 times its normal size), though the posterior wall is often thicker than the anterior wall. The uterine size rarely exceeds that of a 14-week gestation (the size of a large orange). Unlike a fibroid, the growth has no capsule. The cut surface presents a characteristic trabeculated appearance with visible dark cystic spaces and hemorrhagic blood spots.
- **Microscopic Appearance:** Histology reveals endometrial glandular tissue and stromal cells scattered within the myometrium. Phagocytic cells laden with hemosiderin pigment are also present.

**CLINICAL FEATURES** Approximately 50% of patients remain asymptomatic, with the condition discovered incidentally on histological examination. When symptomatic, women present with:

- **Menorrhagia (70%):** Excessive bleeding occurs due to an enlarged uterine cavity, associated endometrial hyperplasia, and abnormal/inadequate myometrial contractions resulting from the distorted junctional zone.
- **Dysmenorrhea (30%):** Patients experience progressively increasing colicky pain. Characteristically, the pain often begins a week before menstruation and continues even after the period is over. This is caused by retrograde uterine contractions, local tissue edema, and prostaglandin release.
- **Dyspareunia and Urinary Frequency:** These occur due to the enlarged and tender uterus.
- **Infertility and Miscarriage:** Adenomyosis reduces fertility due to abnormal subendometrial myometrial function, interference with sperm transport and blastocyst implantation, and an abnormal endometrial immune response.
- **Signs:** On bimanual pelvic examination, the uterus is uniformly enlarged, soft, and tender.

**DIAGNOSIS**

- **Ultrasonography (TVS & 3D) and Color Doppler:** Reveals a diffusely enlarged uterus with heterogeneous myometrial echotexture, hypoechoic myometrium with multiple small cysts (giving a "honeycomb" appearance), and increased vascularity within the myometrium. The endometrial echo is often ill-defined.
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** MRI is the superior and most accurate imaging modality. Diagnostic criteria include asymmetrical thickness of the uterine walls (posterior > anterior), honeycomb cysts, striated projections extending from the endometrium to the myometrium, and a thickened Junctional Zone (≥12 mm).

**TREATMENT** Medical treatment is often unsatisfactory, and the definitive treatment is surgical.

- **Medical Management:** Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are commonly used to control pain and bleeding. The Levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine system (LNG-IUS) or a Danazol-loaded IUD is highly effective in improving both menorrhagia and dysmenorrhea. Treatments with oral progestins or cyclic estrogen-progestin have demonstrated little benefit.
- **Surgical Management:**
    - _Conservative:_ Options include adenomyomectomy, laparoscopic or laparotomic uterine mass reduction, and Uterine Artery Embolization (UAE) to relieve symptoms in women wishing to retain their uterus.
    - _Definitive:_ Total hysterectomy is the definitive treatment of choice, provided it is appropriate for the woman (i.e., in parous women over the age of 40 who have completed their family).



9. Tubal patency tests.
**TUBAL PATENCY TESTS**

Tubal and peritoneal factors are responsible for 25–35% of all cases of female infertility. Testing the anatomical patency and functional integrity of the fallopian tubes is a fundamental step in the basic investigation of an infertile couple.

**General Prerequisites and Timing:**

- **Timing:** Tests are ideally performed in the postmenstrual (proliferative) phase, specifically between Day 7 and Day 10 of a regular menstrual cycle.
- **Rule out infection:** Tubal patency tests are strictly contraindicated in the presence of acute or active pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) to prevent the flaring up of infection.

The various tests employed to assess tubal patency include:

**1. HYSTEROSALPINGOGRAPHY (HSG)**

- **Principle:** A radiographic study where a radiopaque contrast medium is instilled transcervically to outline the uterine cavity and tubal lumen.
- **Contrast Media Used:** Water-soluble contrast (e.g., meglumine diatrizoate) or low-viscosity oil-based contrast (e.g., ethiodized oil). Water-soluble media are preferred as they permit rapid absorption, carry no risk of granuloma formation, cause negligible peritoneal irritation, and eliminate the risk of embolism.
- **Procedure:** Done in the radiology department without anesthesia. A Leech-Wilkinson cannula is used to inject 5–10 mL of dye. Tubal patency is confirmed by observing the free spillage of dye into the peritoneal cavity on the radiograph.
- **Advantages:**
    - Precisely identifies the exact side and site of the tubal block.
    - Identifies intrauterine abnormalities (e.g., unicornuate/bicornuate/septate uterus, synechiae/Asherman's syndrome, submucous fibroids, polyps).
    - Has a therapeutic effect, as flimsy intraluminal adhesions or mucus plugs may be flushed out, increasing the chances of conception in subsequent cycles.
- **Contraindications:** Pelvic infection, active bleeding, pregnancy, adnexal masses, or suspected pelvic tuberculosis.

**2. LAPAROSCOPY AND CHROMOPERTUBATION (GOLD STANDARD)**

- **Principle:** Direct endoscopic visualization of the pelvic organs while injecting methylene blue dye transcervically.
- **Diagnostic Value:** It is the definitive method (gold standard) for evaluating the tubal and peritoneal factors of infertility. It accurately detects tubal patency (dye spillage from the fimbrial ends), tubal motility, fimbrial agglutination, hydrosalpinx, and peritubal adhesions.
- **Added Advantages:** Diagnoses concomitant pelvic pathologies like minimal endometriosis or polycystic ovaries (PCOS), which HSG misses. Allows for simultaneous therapeutic surgical procedures (e.g., adhesiolysis, fulguration of endometriotic implants, ovarian drilling).
- **Drawbacks:** It is an invasive procedure requiring anesthesia. It cannot detect intratubal luminal debris or uterine cavity anomalies. Therefore, HSG and laparoscopy are complementary rather than substitutes for one another.

**3. SONOHYSTEROSALPINGOGRAPHY / SALINE INFUSION SONOGRAPHY (SIS)**

- **Principle:** Normal saline is pushed into the uterine cavity via a pediatric Foley catheter with an inflated balloon to prevent back-leak. Transvaginal Ultrasonography (TVS) tracks the fluid flowing through the tubes into the pouch of Douglas.
- **HyCoSy (Hysterosalpingo-contrast sonography):** Similar to SIS, but utilizes a specific ultrasound contrast medium.
- **Advantages:** Completely non-invasive with zero radiation exposure. Highly superior to HSG for evaluating focal intracavitary pathologies (e.g., endometrial polyps, submucous fibroids, septa) while simultaneously checking tubal patency.

**4. ENDOSCOPIC TUBAL EVALUATION**

- **Falloposcopy:** A fine, flexible fiberoptic device is passed transcervically via a hysteroscope into the tubal lumen. It allows direct visualization of the tubal ostia, mucosal pattern, intratubal polyps, or debris.
- **Salpingoscopy:** A rigid telescope is inserted retrogradely through the abdominal ostium (fimbrial end) of the tube during laparoscopy. It studies the physiology and anatomy of the tubal epithelium to accurately select patients suitable for reconstructive tubal surgery versus IVF.

**5. DILATATION AND INSUFFLATION TEST (RUBIN'S TEST)**

- **Principle:** Air or CO2 is pushed transcervically under pressure. Entry of gas into the peritoneal cavity signifies patency.
- **Observations for Patency:** (1) A sudden drop in pressure when raised to 120 mm Hg, (2) a hissing sound heard on auscultation over the iliac fossa, and (3) referred shoulder pain due to diaphragmatic irritation by the gas.
- **Drawbacks:** Extremely high false-negative rate (up to 33%) due to cornual tubal spasm. It cannot identify the specific side or anatomical site of the blockage. Because of these limitations, it is largely obsolete and rarely performed today.


10. Causes of Hirsuitism. Diagnosis and management of Hirsuitism in a 20 year old girl.
**HIRSUTISM: CAUSES, DIAGNOSIS, AND MANAGEMENT**

**Definition:** Hirsutism is the excessive growth of androgen-dependent sexual hair (terminal hair) in the facial and central parts of the body (male pattern) that worries a female. It is one of the early manifestations in the spectrum of hyperandrogenism and virilism.

---

### **CAUSES OF HIRSUTISM**

The causes of hirsutism are due to excess androgen production (from ovaries or adrenals) or increased sensitivity of the pilosebaceous unit to normal circulating androgens.

**1. Ovarian Causes**

- **Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome (PCOS):** The most common cause in a 20-year-old female.
- Androgen-secreting tumors: Sertoli-Leydig cell tumor, Hilus cell tumor, Lipoid cell tumor.
- Stromal hyperthecosis and luteoma of pregnancy.

**2. Adrenal Causes**

- Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia (CAH) or Late-onset CAH (due to 21-hydroxylase deficiency).
- Cushing’s syndrome.
- Adrenal tumor (Adenoma or Carcinoma).

**3. Pituitary Causes**

- Cushing’s disease (Excess ACTH).
- Acromegaly (Excess Growth Hormone).

**4. Obesity & Metabolic Causes**

- Android (central) obesity: Associated with insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, decreased SHBG, and excess free testosterone.
- HAIR-AN syndrome (Hyperandrogenism, Insulin Resistance, Acanthosis Nigricans).

**5. Iatrogenic (Exogenous Drugs)**

- Intake of androgens, anabolic steroids, danazol, phenytoin, diazoxide, or synthetic progestogens.

**6. Idiopathic Hirsutism**

- Normal serum androgens with normal menses. Caused by increased peripheral sensitivity of the pilosebaceous unit (PSU) to androgens and increased 5α-reductase activity converting testosterone to potent dihydrotestosterone (DHT).

---

### **DIAGNOSIS IN A 20-YEAR-OLD GIRL**

The aim is to differentiate benign/functional causes (like PCOS or idiopathic) from life-threatening androgen-secreting tumors.

**1. Detailed History:**

- **Age and Speed of Onset:** Gradual onset since menarche suggests PCOS or Idiopathic. Rapid, progressive onset with virilization strongly suggests an ovarian or adrenal tumor.
- **Menstrual History:** Irregular cycles, oligomenorrhea, or amenorrhea suggest PCOS or CAH. Regular menses usually points to idiopathic hirsutism.
- **Drug History:** Rule out intake of androgenic medications.
- **Family History:** Genetic predisposition to hirsutism or PCOS.

**2. Physical Examination:**

- **Ferriman-Gallwey Scoring:** Quantifies hair growth in 11 androgen-sensitive body areas (upper lip, chin, chest, back, abdomen, thighs, etc.). A score >8 (or >3 in Asian women) indicates hirsutism.
- **Signs of Virilism:** Check for deepening of voice, clitoromegaly, breast atrophy, temporal balding, or increased muscle mass. Presence mandates ruling out a tumor.
- **General Exam:** Calculate BMI (>25 kg/m² with abdominal distribution indicates android obesity). Look for acne and acanthosis nigricans (velvety pigmentation on the neck/axilla denoting insulin resistance).
- **Abdominal/Pelvic Exam:** Palpate for any adnexal or abdominal mass.

**3. Laboratory Investigations (Hormonal Profile):**

- **Serum Total Testosterone (T):**
    - Mildly elevated (≤150 ng/dL) typically indicates PCOS or idiopathic.
    - Markedly elevated (>200 ng/dL) is highly suspicious of an ovarian androgen-producing tumor.
- **Serum DHEA-S:** Marker of adrenal androgen. Markedly elevated (>700 µg/dL) suggests an adrenal tumor.
- **Serum 17-Hydroxyprogesterone (17-OHP):** Baseline >800 ng/dL is diagnostic of Late-Onset Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia (21-hydroxylase deficiency).
- **LH:FSH Ratio:** An elevated ratio (>3:1) is supportive of PCOS.
- **Insulin Resistance Testing:** Fasting insulin or Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT) if PCOS is suspected.

**4. Imaging:**

- **Pelvic USG (Transabdominal/TVS):** To identify polycystic ovaries or detect an ovarian solid mass.
- **CT / MRI (Abdomen & Pelvis):** Indicated if DHEA-S or Testosterone levels are extremely high to localize an adrenal or ovarian tumor.

---

### **MANAGEMENT IN A 20-YEAR-OLD GIRL**

The management principles include removing the source of excess androgen, neutralizing its action, and removing existing terminal hair. _Note: Medical therapy takes at least 6 months to show clinical benefits because the life cycle of a hair follicle is 6 months._

**1. Lifestyle Modifications (First-line for obese PCOS):**

- Weight reduction, diet, and regular exercise. Reducing BMI to <25 improves insulin resistance, lowers free testosterone (by increasing SHBG), and improves menses.

**2. Pharmacological Therapy (Antiandrogens & Ovarian Suppression):**

- **Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs):** The treatment of choice for young women with PCOS or idiopathic hirsutism who do not desire pregnancy.
    - _Mechanism:_ Progestin suppresses pituitary LH, decreasing ovarian androgen production. Estrogen increases SHBG, which binds free testosterone.
    - _Choice of Pill:_ Formulations containing less androgenic or anti-androgenic progestins (e.g., Drospirenone, Desogestrel, Cyproterone acetate) are highly preferred.
- **Antiandrogens (Used if COCs are inadequate or as combination):**
    - **Spironolactone:** (50–200 mg/day). An aldosterone antagonist that competitively blocks androgen receptors in the hair follicle and inhibits 5α-reductase activity. Highly effective for idiopathic hirsutism.
    - **Cyproterone Acetate:** A potent progestogen with antiandrogenic properties. Often combined with ethinyl estradiol (e.g., 2 mg cyproterone + 35 µg EE for 21 days) to ensure regular cycles and prevent pregnancy.
    - **Finasteride:** (5 mg/day). A 5α-reductase inhibitor that prevents the conversion of testosterone to potent DHT at the hair follicle level. Very effective with minimal side effects.
    - **Flutamide:** (250 mg/day). A nonsteroidal androgen receptor blocker. Less commonly used due to the risk of hepatotoxicity.
- **Insulin Sensitizers (Metformin):** Added in patients with PCOS and documented insulin resistance.
- **Glucocorticoids (Dexamethasone):** Used specifically for late-onset CAH to suppress ACTH and adrenal androgen output (0.25–0.75 mg/day at bedtime).

**3. Cosmetic & Local Hair Removal:** Antiandrogens prevent new hair growth but do not eliminate existing terminal hairs. Cosmetic removal is mandatory for immediate relief.

- **Temporary:** Shaving, plucking, bleaching, waxing, and depilatory creams.
- **Permanent:** Electrolysis (destroys individual follicles) and Laser therapy / Photo epilation (applies heat to pigmented hair follicles).
- **Topical Therapy:** **Eflornithine hydrochloride (13.9%) cream** inhibits the enzyme ornithine decarboxylase in the dermal papilla, slowing the rate of new hair growth.

**4. Surgical Management:**

- Surgical excision is strictly reserved for the removal of identified androgen-secreting ovarian or adrenal tumors.

11. Intrauterine Contraceptive device.

**INTRAUTERINE CONTRACEPTIVE DEVICES (IUCD)**

**Introduction and Classification** Intrauterine Contraceptive Devices (IUCDs) are safe, highly effective, and reversible methods of contraception, primarily used for spacing births. The efficacy of third-generation IUCDs is comparable to surgical sterilization.

- **Open / Non-Medicated Devices:** Lippes Loop.
- **Medicated (Copper-Bearing) Devices:**
    - **Cu-T 380A:** Contains 380 mm² of copper (stem wrapped with 314 mm², arms have 33 mm² each). Approved lifespan is 10 years.
    - **Multiload Cu-375:** Has 375 mm² surface area of copper wire. Replacement is every 5 years.
- **Hormone-Releasing Devices (LNG-IUS):**
    - **Mirena:** A T-shaped device containing 52 mg of levonorgestrel, releasing 20 µg/day. Replaced every 5 to 7 years.
    - **Skyla / Kyleena:** Smaller devices suitable for nulliparous women, releasing lower doses of LNG.

**Mechanism of Action** IUCDs primarily act in the uterine cavity and do not inhibit ovulation.

- **General Action:** They cause a non-specific inflammatory reaction and biochemical changes in the endometrium. Lysosomal disintegration from macrophages liberates toxic prostaglandins which phagocytose spermatozoa, impairing sperm transport and fertilization.
- **Copper Devices:** Ionized copper has an additional local gametotoxic and spermolytic effect. It prevents blastocyst implantation through enzymatic interference and releases cytotoxic cytokines.
- **LNG-IUS:** Induces strong, uniform suppression and atrophy of the endometrium. It alters the cervical mucus, making it thick and scanty, thereby impeding sperm motility and penetration.

**Timing of Insertion**

- **Interval:** Inserted in the interconceptional period, preferably 2–3 days after menses, though it can be inserted at any time in the cycle if pregnancy is confidently excluded.
- **Postpartum:** Can be inserted post-placental (within 10 minutes of placental delivery), intra-cesarean (before closing the uterine incision), within 48 hours of delivery, or extended postpartum (after 4 weeks).
- **Postabortal:** Inserted immediately following spontaneous abortion or termination of pregnancy (MTP/suction evacuation).
- **Emergency Contraception:** Cu-T 380A can be inserted up to 5 days following unprotected coitus as a highly effective emergency contraceptive.

**Technique of Insertion**

- Insertion is an outpatient procedure done without anesthesia, taking strict aseptic precautions. Ibuprofen may be given 30 minutes prior to reduce cramping.
- Bimanual examination is done to assess uterine size and position, followed by passing a uterine sound to measure the uterocervical length.
- A **"no-touch" technique** is strictly followed (the distal end of the inserter is not touched, nor does it touch the vaginal walls).
- **Cu-T 380A** is inserted using the **withdrawal technique**, where the inserter tube is withdrawn while keeping the plunger steady to release the device at the fundus.
- **Multiload Cu-375** is simply pushed up to the fundus through the cervical canal without the need for a plunger.

**Contraindications**

- **Absolute / General:** Suspected or confirmed pregnancy, undiagnosed genital tract bleeding, acute Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID) currently or within the last 3 months, distorted uterine cavity (fibroids or anomalies), severe dysmenorrhea, known/suspected cervical or uterine neoplasia, and trophoblastic disease.
- **Copper-Specific:** Wilson's disease and copper allergy.
- **LNG-IUS-Specific:** Active hepatic tumor/disease, current breast cancer, and severe arterial disease.

**Complications**

- **Immediate:** Cramp-like pain, syncopal attack (especially in nulliparous women), and partial or complete perforation of the uterus.
- **Remote:**
    - **Abnormal Menstrual Bleeding:** Menorrhagia, prolonged periods, and intermenstrual bleeding are common with copper devices and are the main cause for discontinuation.
    - **Pain:** Proportionate to myometrial distension.
    - **PID:** Risk is highest in the first month following insertion, often due to bacterial ascent during the procedure.
    - **Spontaneous Expulsion:** Approx 5%, usually occurs within the first month or during menstruation.
    - **Ectopic Pregnancy:** If a pregnancy occurs with an IUCD in situ, there is a relatively higher risk of it being ectopic.
    - **Missing Threads**.

**Non-Contraceptive Benefits (Primarily LNG-IUS)** Third-generation devices, particularly the LNG-IUS, offer significant gynecological benefits:

- Significant reduction in menstrual blood loss, providing an effective medical alternative to hysterectomy for dysfunctional uterine bleeding (DUB) and menorrhagia.
- Relief from dysmenorrhea and premenstrual tension syndrome (PMS).
- Therapeutic utility in managing endometrial hyperplasia, adenomyosis, and endometriosis.
- Reduction in the risk of endometrial and cervical carcinoma.

**Indications for Removal** An IUCD should be removed in cases of:

- Persistent excessive uterine bleeding or pain.
- Flaring up of acute PID.
- Uterine perforation or partial expulsion.
- Pregnancy occurring with the device in situ (to prevent abortion, sepsis, and prematurity).
- Missing threads (after evaluation).
- The woman's desire to conceive.
- Completion of the device's effective lifespan, or 1 year post-menopause.

**Management of Missing Threads (High Yield)** If threads are not visible, the possibilities include: thread coiled inside, torn thread, unnoticed expulsion, uterine perforation, or the device being pulled up by a growing pregnancy.

- **Diagnosis:** Exclude pregnancy first.
- **Imaging:** Transvaginal or transabdominal ultrasonography is the preferred method to locate the device. If USG is inconclusive, a plain X-ray of the pelvis (AP and lateral views) with a radio-opaque uterine sound inside the cavity will differentiate an intra-uterine device from a perforated (extra-uterine) one.
- **Removal:** Depending on location, it can be removed blindly with a loop hook, under hysteroscopic guidance, or via laparoscopy/laparotomy if perforated into the peritoneal cavity.



12. Puberty menorrhagia.
**PUBERTY MENORRHAGIA**

**Definition** Puberty menorrhagia refers to heavy, irregular, or prolonged menstrual bleeding in adolescent girls following menarche. Evaluation is specifically indicated if the menstrual interval is less than 22 days or more than 44 days, if bleeding lasts longer than one week, or if the bleeding is heavy enough to cause anemia. The initial periods following menarche are often excessive due to anovulation.

**Etiology (Causes)** The neurohormonal mechanism essential for normal menstruation takes 2–3 years to mature. During this time, abnormalities can occur. The important causes of puberty menorrhagia include:

- **Dysfunctional Uterine Bleeding (DUB) (95%):** The most common cause. Immaturity of the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis leads to anovulatory cycles. This results in unopposed estrogen stimulation, endometrial hyperplasia, and subsequent asynchronous, prolonged, and heavy shedding.
- **Endocrine Dysfunction:** Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), and thyroid disorders (hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism).
- **Hematological Disorders:** Idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP), Von-Willebrand’s disease, and leukemia.
- **Pelvic Tumors:** Fibroid uterus, sarcoma botryoides, and estrogen-producing ovarian tumors.
- **Pregnancy Complications:** E.g., abortion-related complications.

**Investigations** A careful history and thorough clinical examination are the first steps. If heavy bleeding continues despite conservative measures, the patient must be investigated systematically:

- **Hematological profile:** Hemoglobin percentage (Hb%), peripheral blood film, bleeding time, clotting time, and platelet count.
- **Coagulation screening:** Prothrombin time (PT), partial thromboplastin time (PTT), factor VIII, and von Willebrand factor (vWF) to rule out bleeding disorders.
- **Endocrine profile:** Thyroid profile (TSH, T3, T4).
- **Imaging:** Pelvic ultrasonography (USG) or MRI to exclude any organic pelvic pathology like tumors or polyps.
- **Surgical Evaluation:** In refractory cases that are unresponsive to medical therapy, Examination Under Anesthesia (EUA) and uterine curettage (D&C) may be needed. The curetted material is sent for histopathological study to exclude organic lesions.

**Management Protocol** The management approach depends on the severity of bleeding and the underlying cause.

**1. General Measures:**

- Adequate explanation, assurance, and psychological support are essential for the young girl.
- Bed rest.
- **Correction of Anemia:** Administration of hematinics (iron). In cases of severe anemia or heavy active bleeding, blood transfusion may be required.

**2. Medical Management for Primary DUB (Majority of cases):**

- **Progestin Therapy:** In cases responsive to oral medication, progestogens such as medroxyprogesterone acetate (10–20 mg/day) or norethisterone (5 mg thrice daily) are given until bleeding stops, which typically occurs within 3–7 days. The medication is continued for 21 days. This course is usually repeated for 2–3 cycles, after which normal cycles resume.
- **Acute/Emergency Bleeding (Unresponsive to Progestins):** Conjugated equine estrogen 20–40 mg is administered intravenously every 6–8 hours. Once the bleeding is controlled, therapy is replaced with combined oral contraceptive (COC) pills containing 50 µg of estrogen.
- **GnRH Analogs:** Can be used for short-term management in refractory cases.

**3. Specific Therapy for Secondary Causes:** If the bleeding is secondary to a specific cause (e.g., thyroid dysfunction, immune thrombocytopenic purpura, Von Willebrand's disease, leukemia, anatomical disorders, or neoplasms), appropriate targeted therapy is instituted.

**Prognosis** With appropriate management and time, a regular menstrual cycle will eventually be established once the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis matures.


13. Diagnostic criteria of Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome.
**DIAGNOSTIC CRITERIA OF POLYCYSTIC OVARIAN SYNDROME (PCOS)**

The diagnosis of Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome (PCOS) is based on the criteria established by the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) and the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE) in 2018.

**A. THE ASRM/ESHRE (ROTTERDAM) CRITERIA (High Yield)** The diagnosis is confirmed by the presence of **any two of the following three criteria**:

1. **Oligo-ovulation and/or anovulation**.
2. **Hyperandrogenism** (clinical and/or biochemical).
3. **Polycystic ovaries** (on ultrasonography).

_Note: Before confirming the diagnosis of PCOS, other specific etiologies such as congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH), thyroid dysfunction, hyperprolactinemia, and Cushing's syndrome must be strictly excluded_.

---

**B. DETAILED BREAKDOWN OF THE CRITERIA**

**1. Oligo-ovulation and/or Anovulation**

- **Clinical Presentation:** Manifests primarily as menstrual abnormalities, which are seen in about 70% of cases.
- **Patterns:** Patients typically present with oligomenorrhea, amenorrhea, or dysfunctional uterine bleeding (DUB) and subsequent infertility.

**2. Hyperandrogenism** Hyperandrogenism is considered the key feature of the syndrome and can be either clinical or biochemical.

- **Clinical Hyperandrogenism:** Manifests prominently as hirsutism and acne (present in 70% of cases). Frank virilism is rare.
- **Biochemical Hyperandrogenism:**
    - Elevated serum testosterone levels (>1.5 ng/mL) and raised dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS) levels.
    - Elevated Luteinizing Hormone (LH) level, or a raised LH:FSH ratio of >3:1 (although this is supportive, it is neither specific nor strictly diagnostic on its own).
    - Sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) levels are typically reduced.

**3. Polycystic Ovaries (Ultrasonographic Criteria)** Transvaginal sonography (TVS) is the preferred method for evaluating the ovaries, especially in obese patients. The diagnostic features include:

- **Ovarian Volume:** Enlarged ovarian volume of ≥10 cm³.
- **Follicle Count and Size:** Increased number (≥20) of peripherally arranged small follicles per ovary, measuring 2–9 mm in diameter.
- **Stromal Changes:** Increased ovarian stroma (stromal hyperthecosis).
- **Capsule:** The ovarian capsule is thickened and pearly white in color.

---

**C. ASSOCIATED SUPPORTING CLINICAL & BIOCHEMICAL FINDINGS** While not part of the strict diagnostic triad, the following are highly characteristic of PCOS and hold significant diagnostic value:

- **Obesity:** Central (android) obesity is common, with a Body Mass Index (BMI) typically >25 kg/m².
- **Insulin Resistance (IR):** Present in about 40-50% of cases. It is indicated biochemically by raised fasting serum insulin levels (>25 µIU/mL) and a fasting glucose/insulin ratio of <4.5.
- **Acanthosis Nigricans:** Thickened, gray-brown pigmented skin over the nape of the neck, inner thighs, groin, and axilla, serving as a clinical marker for profound insulin resistance.
- **HAIR-AN Syndrome:** A severe subset of PCOS characterized by **H**yperandrogenism, **I**nsulin **R**esistance, and **A**canthosis **N**igricans.

14. Complications of Fibroid uterus.

**COMPLICATIONS OF FIBROID UTERUS**

Uterine fibroids (leiomyomas) are generally benign but can lead to a variety of secondary changes, acute emergencies, and chronic gynecological or obstetrical complications.

**1. SECONDARY CHANGES (DEGENERATIONS)** As a fibroid outgrows its blood supply, it undergoes various degenerative changes.

- **Hyaline Degeneration (65%):** The most common type, affecting all sizes of fibroids except very tiny ones. The tumor loses its whorled appearance and becomes a homogeneous mass.
- **Cystic Degeneration:** Follows hyaline degeneration, commonly seen in interstitial fibroids after menopause, where liquefied areas form cystic spaces.
- **Myxomatous Degeneration (15%):** Smooth muscle cells undergo myxomatous changes, usually in the central part of the myoma.
- **Fatty Degeneration:** Occurs at or after menopause, with fat globules deposited in the muscle cells.
- **Calcific Degeneration (10%):** Usually involves subserous fibroids with a small pedicle or in postmenopausal women. Precipitation of calcium carbonate or phosphate occurs, occasionally converting the entire tumor into a calcified mass known as a "womb stone".
- **Red (Carneous) Degeneration (5–10%):** Predominantly occurs in large fibroids during the second half of pregnancy and puerperium. It presents with raw-beef appearance and fishy odor due to thrombosis and necrosis.
- **Atrophy:** Shrinkage of the tumor following menopause due to estrogen withdrawal.
- **Necrosis:** Central necrosis occurs due to circulatory inadequacy, typically in a submucous polyp or pedunculated subserous fibroid.

**2. ACUTE & LIFE-THREATENING COMPLICATIONS (High Yield)**

- **Torsion:** Axial rotation of a subserous pedunculated fibroid leading to acute abdominal pain.
- **Severe Intraperitoneal Hemorrhage:** Caused by the rupture of dilated surface veins over a subserous fibroid.
- **Severe Infection:** Leading to peritonitis or septicemia. Infection typically gains access through the thinned, sloughed surface of a submucous fibroid following delivery or abortion.
- **Severe Anemia:** Resulting from persistent menorrhagia, metrorrhagia, or continued vaginal bleeding.
- **Sarcomatous Change (Malignancy):** Rare, occurring in less than 0.1% of cases (usually leiomyosarcoma). It is suspected if there is rapid enlargement, postmenopausal bleeding, or a soft, yellowish cut section with hemorrhage.

**3. GYNECOLOGICAL COMPLICATIONS**

- **Menstrual Abnormalities:** Menorrhagia (30%) is the classic symptom due to increased surface area, congestion, and impaired uterine contractility. Metrorrhagia occurs due to ulceration of a submucous fibroid or polyp.
- **Infertility (30%):** Caused by distortion or elongation of the uterine cavity, cornual block, impaired sperm transport, defective nidation over a submucous fibroid, or associated congestion.
- **Pressure Symptoms:** A posterior fibroid impacted in the pelvis can cause constipation, dysuria, or retention of urine. A broad ligament fibroid can compress the ureter, leading to hydroureter, hydronephrosis, and pyelitis.
- **Extrusion / Polyp Formation:** A submucous fibroid may be extruded into the uterine cavity and acquire a pedicle, forming a fibroid polyp.
- **Chronic Uterine Inversion:** A submucous myomatous polyp arising from the fundus can exert traction and cause incomplete or complete chronic uterine inversion.

**4. OBSTETRICAL COMPLICATIONS (FIBROID IN PREGNANCY)** Fibroids grow rapidly during pregnancy and can cause significant complications across all stages.

- **During Pregnancy:** Increased risk of miscarriage, preterm labor, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), malpresentation, non-engagement of the presenting part, placental abruption, and red degeneration.
- **During Labor:** Uterine inertia, dystocia (especially with cervical or broad ligament fibroids that fail to pull up), obstructed labor, and a higher need for difficult cesarean section.
- **During Puerperium:** Increased risk of postpartum hemorrhage (due to uterine atony or morbidly adherent placenta over the myoma site), subinvolution, puerperal sepsis, secondary PPH, inversion of the uterus, lochiometra, and pyometra.


15. Mittellschmerz’s syndrome.
**MITTELSCHMERZ’S SYNDROME (OVULAR PAIN)**

**Definition** Mittelschmerz's syndrome, also known as ovular or intermenstrual pain, refers to the cyclical lower abdominal pain that occurs during the midmenstrual period. It serves as a strong indirect (presumptive) clinical evidence of ovulation.

**Etiology and Pathogenesis** The exact cause of the pain is not definitively known, but the probable factors involved include:

- **Follicular Tension:** Increased tension of the Graafian follicle just prior to its rupture.
- **Peritoneal Irritation:** Chemical irritation of the peritoneum by the spillage of follicular fluid following ovulation.
- **Muscular Contraction:** Spasmodic contractions of the fallopian tubes and the uterus.

**Clinical Features (High Yield)**

- **Timing:** Appears exclusively in the midmenstrual period, coinciding with ovulation.
- **Site of Pain:** The pain is usually situated in the hypogastrium or localized to either the right or left iliac fossa.
- **Laterality:** It is typically unilateral, depending entirely on which ovary is ovulating during that specific cycle, and characteristically does not change from side to side during a single episode.
- **Duration:** The pain is short-lived and rarely lasts for more than 12 hours.
- **Associated Symptoms:** It is frequently accompanied by slight vaginal bleeding (spotting) or an excessive mucoid vaginal discharge.
- **Negative Findings:** Nausea and vomiting are conspicuously absent.

**Diagnosis** The diagnosis is primarily clinical, based on the characteristic timing and nature of the pain. It is categorized as a cyclic cause of chronic pelvic pain.

**Management**

- **Conservative:** In the vast majority of cases, the condition is effectively treated with simple assurance and analgesics.
- **Hormonal Suppression:** In obstinate or refractory cases, the absolute cure is achieved by making the menstrual cycle anovular. This is typically managed by prescribing combined oral contraceptive (OC) pills. Other hormonal suppressive options for midmenstrual pain include progestogens, danazol, or gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) analogs.


16. Lymphatic drainage of cervix.

**LYMPHATIC DRAINAGE OF THE CERVIX**

The lymphatic drainage of the cervix is extensive and holds immense clinical significance, particularly concerning the spread of cervical carcinoma. The lymphatic vessels from the cervix course along the uterine veins to drain into specific regional lymph nodes.

The draining lymph nodes are systematically classified into primary and secondary groups:

**1. PRIMARY GROUP (First Echelon Nodes)** These lymph nodes receive direct lymphatic drainage from the cervix. On each side, the primary nodes include:

- **Parametrial (Paracervical) Nodes:** These are inconsistently present and lie in the parametrium near the crossing of the ureter with the uterine artery.
- **Internal Iliac (Hypogastric) Nodes:** A major drainage station for the cervical lymphatics.
- **Obturator Nodes:** Frequently involved in cervical lymphatic drainage.
- **External Iliac Nodes:** Specifically, the anterior and medial groups of the external iliac nodes.
- **Sacral Nodes:** Drain the posterior aspect of the cervix.

**2. SECONDARY GROUP (Second Echelon Nodes)** The efferent lymphatics from all the aforementioned primary groups ultimately drain into the secondary groups, which include:

- **Common Iliac Nodes**.
- **Superior Lumbar (Para-aortic) Nodes**.

**APPLIED ANATOMY (High Yield Clinical Significance)**

- **Spread of Cervical Malignancy:** In carcinoma of the cervix, the primary groups of lymph nodes involved by metastatic spread are the parametrial, internal iliac, obturator, external iliac, and sacral nodes.
- **Surgical Implication:** An intimate knowledge of this specific lymphatic pathway is crucial during radical hysterectomy and systematic pelvic lymphadenectomy for the surgical staging and treatment of cervical cancer.

17. Bacterial vaginosis.
**BACTERIAL VAGINOSIS (BV)**

**DEFINITION & ETIOLOGY**

- Bacterial vaginosis is the most common cause of symptomatic vaginitis (15–50% of cases).
- It is a polymicrobial infection characterized by a marked decrease in normal vaginal lactobacilli.
- The condition is caused by a synergistic action between _Gardnerella vaginalis_ (formerly _Haemophilus vaginalis_) and anaerobic organisms such as _Bacteroides_ species, _Peptococcus_ species, _Mobiluncus_, _Prevotella_, and _Mycoplasma hominis_.
- The term "vaginosis" is specifically used instead of "vaginitis" because there is an absence of vaginal inflammation on biopsy.

**RISK FACTORS**

- Multiple or new sexual partners.
- Early age of sexual intercourse.
- Cigarette smoking.
- Intrauterine Contraceptive Device (IUCD) use.
- Immunosuppression.

**CLINICAL FEATURES**

- **Vaginal Discharge:** The classic presentation is a malodorous, creamy, or grayish-white homogeneous vaginal discharge that is adherent to the vaginal wall.
- **Odor:** It characteristically produces a fishy smell.
- **Symptoms:** It is typically non-pruritic and unassociated with dysuria or inflammation.

**DIAGNOSIS (HIGH YIELD)** **1. Amsel’s Diagnostic Criteria:** The diagnosis requires the presence of at least three out of the following four criteria:

- Homogeneous vaginal discharge.
- Vaginal pH > 4.5 (tested via litmus paper).
- **Positive Whiff test:** A fishy (amine) odor is liberated when a drop of the vaginal discharge is mixed with 10% potassium hydroxide (KOH) solution.
- **Presence of Clue cells (>20% of cells):** On a normal saline wet mount microscopy, vaginal epithelial cells are seen heavily covered with coccobacilli, giving them a stippled or granular appearance that obscures their cell borders.

**2. Gram Stained Vaginal Smear (Hay/Ison Criteria):**

- Reveals a preponderance of _Gardnerella_ or _Mobiluncus_ morphotypes with few or entirely absent lactobacilli.

_Note:_ Bacterial vaginal culture has absolutely no role in the diagnosis of BV.

**COMPLICATIONS** Bacterial vaginosis is associated with significant gynecological and obstetrical morbidities:

- **Gynecological:** Recurrent infections leading to Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID), development of PID following abortion, and vaginal cuff cellulitis following hysterectomy.
- **Obstetrical:** Increased risk of second-trimester miscarriage (pregnancy loss before 20 weeks), preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), preterm labor/birth, and endometritis.
- **Infertility:** It is associated with a decreased success rate in In Vitro Fertilization (IVF) treatments.

**TREATMENT (CDC - 2015 GUIDELINES)**

- **Metronidazole:** 500 mg orally twice daily (BID) for 7 days is highly effective.
- **Tinidazole:** 2 g orally for 3 days.
- **Clindamycin:** 300 mg orally twice daily for 7 days, OR Clindamycin ovules 100 mg intravaginally, once at bedtime for 3 days.
- **Partner Management:** The sexual partner should be treated simultaneously.
- **Prognosis:** Following appropriate therapy, the cure rate is approximately 80%.


18. Contraindications for oral contraceptive pills.
**CONTRAINDICATIONS FOR COMBINED ORAL CONTRACEPTIVE PILLS (COCs)**

According to the WHO Medical Eligibility Criteria (MEC) for contraceptive use, the contraindications for Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs) are classified into absolute contraindications (WHO Category 4, where health risks are unacceptable) and relative contraindications (WHO Category 3, where theoretical or proven risks usually outweigh the advantages).

**A. ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATIONS (WHO Category 4)** These are conditions where the use of COCs is strictly prohibited:

**1. Circulatory and Vascular Diseases (Past or Present):**

- Arterial or venous thrombosis.
- Severe hypertension.
- History of stroke.
- Heart disease (valvular and ischemic).
- Diabetes mellitus with vascular complications.
- Migraine with focal neurologic symptoms (aura).

**2. Diseases of the Liver:**

- Active liver disease or jaundice.
- Liver adenoma or carcinoma.

**3. Hormone-Dependent Neoplasms:**

- Estrogen-dependent neoplasms, particularly current breast cancer.

**4. Other Medical and Physiological Conditions:**

- Pregnancy.
- Breastfeeding in the early postpartum period (within 6 weeks postpartum).
- Major surgery or conditions requiring prolonged immobilization (due to the high risk of thromboembolism).

**B. RELATIVE CONTRAINDICATIONS (WHO Category 3)** In these conditions, the risks of using COCs generally outweigh the benefits, and alternative methods should be considered:

- Unexplained or undiagnosed vaginal bleeding.
- Hyperlipidemia.
- Benign liver tumors.
- Breastfeeding between 6 weeks and 6 months postpartum.
- Heavy smoking (defined as >20 cigarettes per day), especially in women over the age of 35.
- Past history of breast cancer.

**C. INDICATIONS FOR IMMEDIATE WITHDRAWAL OF PILLS (High Yield)** While on COC therapy, the pills must be stopped immediately if the patient develops any of the following warning signs or complications:

- Severe migraine, visual or speech disturbances.
- Sudden chest pain, unexplained fainting attacks, or acute vertigo.
- Severe cramps and pains in the legs (suspicion of deep vein thrombosis).
- Excessive weight gain or severe depression.
- Prior to major surgery: COCs should be withheld for at least 6 weeks before surgery to minimize the risk of postoperative vascular complications.
- When the patient desires to become pregnant.



19. Medical management of Ectopic pregnancy.
**MEDICAL MANAGEMENT OF ECTOPIC PREGNANCY**

Medical management is a conservative approach primarily aimed at treating unruptured tubal ectopic pregnancies without surgical intervention. It involves the use of chemotherapeutic agents administered either systemically or via direct local injection (salpingocentesis) under sonographic or laparoscopic guidance.

**CRITERIA FOR MEDICAL THERAPY (High Yield)** Proper patient selection is paramount for the success of medical management. The patient must fulfill the following criteria:

- **Clinical Status:** The patient must be asymptomatic, hemodynamically stable, and compliant with follow-up.
- **β-hCG Levels:** Serum β-hCG level must be less than 3,000 IU/L.
- **Ultrasound Findings:** The tubal diameter (ectopic mass) must be less than 4 cm, and there must be no fetal cardiac activity.
- **Peritoneal Cavity:** There must be no evidence of intra-abdominal hemorrhage.

**DRUGS USED**

- **Systemic Therapy:** Methotrexate (MTX) is the standard drug of choice.
- **Local Therapy (Salpingocentesis):** Drugs injected directly into the ectopic sac include Methotrexate, Potassium chloride (KCl), Prostaglandin (PGF2a), Hyperosmolar glucose, or Actinomycin.

**MECHANISM OF ACTION (METHOTREXATE)** Methotrexate is a folic acid antagonist. It competitively blocks the enzyme dihydrofolate reductase, preventing the conversion of dihydrofolate to tetrahydrofolate (the active form of folic acid). This blocks the synthesis of DNA, RNA, and protein, effectively halting the proliferation of the rapidly dividing trophoblastic cells of the ectopic pregnancy.

**TREATMENT REGIMENS** **1. Single-Dose Regimen:**

- A single dose of Methotrexate 50 mg/m² is administered intramuscularly (IM).

**2. Multi-Dose Regimen:**

- This regimen is typically reserved for advanced gestations or cases where embryonic cardiac activity is present.
- Methotrexate 1 mg/kg IM is given on Days 1, 3, 5, and 7.
- Leucovorin (folinic acid rescue) 0.1 mg/kg IM is given on Days 2, 4, 6, and 8 to minimize systemic toxicity.

**MONITORING OF THERAPY** Rigorous follow-up is mandatory to ensure the resolution of the ectopic pregnancy.

- **Single-Dose Monitoring:** Serum β-hCG is measured on Day 4 and Day 7 post-treatment.
    - If the decline in β-hCG between Day 4 and Day 7 is **≥ 15%**, the patient is followed up weekly until the serum hCG drops to < 10 mIU/mL.
    - If the decline is **< 15%**, a second dose of MTX 50 mg/m² IM is administered on Day 7.
- **Multi-Dose Monitoring:** Serum β-hCG is monitored weekly until levels fall to < 5.0 µIU/mL.
- _Success Rate:_ The overall success rate of Methotrexate therapy is 90%.

**CONTRAINDICATIONS TO METHOTREXATE THERAPY** **Absolute Contraindications:**

- Breastfeeding.
- Hepatic, renal, or hematologic dysfunction.
- Significant anemia.
- Immunodeficiency state.
- Sensitivity to methotrexate.
- Active pulmonary disease.
- Peptic ulcer disease (often considered alongside systemic contraindications).

**Relative Contraindications:**

- Presence of embryonic cardiac motion.
- Gestational sac > 3.5 cm.

**SIDE EFFECTS & COMPLICATIONS** Side effects are generally more pronounced with the multi-dose regimen.

- **Gastrointestinal:** Flatulence (most common), mucositis, gastroenteritis, and hepatitis.
- **Hematological:** Anemia and bone marrow depression.
- **Separation Pain:** The patient may complain of abdominal pain following therapy. This is a common physiological response due to the death of trophoblast cells and the subsequent separation of the ectopic pregnancy from the tubal wall, which generally subsides on its own.



20. Types and causes of Dysmenorrhoea.

**DEFINITION** Dysmenorrhea literally means painful menstruation, but practically it is defined as painful menstruation of sufficient magnitude so as to incapacitate day-to-day activities.

**TYPES OF DYSMENORRHEA** Dysmenorrhea is broadly classified into two main types:

1. **Primary (Spasmodic) Dysmenorrhea:** Painful menstruation where there is no identifiable pelvic pathology.
2. **Secondary (Congestive) Dysmenorrhea:** Menstruation-associated pain occurring in the presence of a demonstrable pelvic pathology.

---

### **1. PRIMARY (SPASMODIC) DYSMENORRHEA**

This type is predominantly confined to adolescent girls, usually appearing within 2 years of menarche, and is almost always confined to ovulatory cycles. The pain usually begins a few hours before or just with the onset of menstruation and rarely lasts beyond 48 hours.

**Causes and Etiopathogenesis:** The exact mechanism of pain initiation is linked to multiple factors causing dysrhythmic uterine contractions and myometrial ischemia:

- **Role of Prostaglandins:** In ovulatory cycles, under the influence of progesterone, prostaglandins (PGF2α, PGE2) are synthesized and released from the secretory endometrium. PGF2α is a potent vasoconstrictor that causes myometrial ischemia (angina). Increased production or heightened myometrial sensitivity to PGF2α leads to intense contractions and pain.
- **Myometrial Hyperactivity & Junctional Zone (JZ) Dysperistalsis:** The subendometrial layer of the myometrium, known as the junctional zone (JZ), shows irregular thickening, hyperplasia of smooth muscle, and reduced vascularity in dysmenorrheic women. This leads to dysperistalsis and hyperactivity.
- **Role of Vasopressin:** There is an increased release of vasopressin during menstruation, which directly increases myometrial activity and stimulates prostaglandin synthesis, leading to hypoxia and pain.
- **Endothelins, Leukotrienes, and Platelet Activating Factors (PAF):** These are potent vasoconstrictors present in higher concentrations that stimulate myometrial smooth muscle contractions and aggravate local ischemia.
- **Imbalance in Autonomic Nervous Control:** Overactivity of the sympathetic nerves leads to hypertonicity of the circular muscle fibers at the isthmus and internal os.
- **Psychosomatic Factors:** Tension and anxiety during adolescence can lower the pain threshold, making the perception of pain more severe.

---

### **2. SECONDARY (CONGESTIVE) DYSMENORRHEA**

This type is typically seen in women in their thirties and is more often parous. The pain is dull, usually starts 3–5 days prior to the period, and is relieved with the onset of bleeding.

**Causes of Pain:** The pain is primarily related to increasing tension in the pelvic tissues due to premenstrual pelvic congestion or increased vascularity in the pelvic organs.

**Etiological Causes (Pelvic Pathologies):**

- **Endometriosis:** Endometriotic implants bleed locally during menstruation, causing peritoneal inflammation, tissue necrosis, adhesion formation, and nerve irritation.
- **Adenomyosis:** Ingrowth of the endometrium directly into the myometrium causes progressively increasing colicky pain due to retrograde uterine contractions, local tissue edema, and prostaglandin release.
- **Uterine Fibroids:** Especially submucous fibroids or polyps, where pain is spasmodic and associated with the uterus attempting to expel the polyp from the cavity.
- **Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (Chronic Pelvic Infection):** Residual infection and adhesions restrict organ mobility and cause congestive pain.
- **Intrauterine Contraceptive Device (IUCD) in utero:** Can cause mechanical irritation and myometrial distension.
- **Obstruction due to Müllerian Anomalies:** Conditions like bicornuate uterus or vaginal septum can obstruct menstrual flow (cryptomenorrhea) or cause cyclic pain.
- **Cervical Stenosis:** Narrows the outflow tract, causing increased intrauterine pressure during menstruation.
- **Pelvic Adhesions:** Following previous surgery, endometriosis, or PID.
- **Pelvic Congestion Syndrome:** Disturbance in the autonomic nervous system leading to gross vascular congestion and pelvic varicosities.

---

### **3. OVARIAN AND UNILATERAL DYSMENORRHEA (SPECIAL TYPES)**

- **Right Ovarian Vein Syndrome:** The right ovarian vein crosses the ureter at a right angle; premenstrual pelvic congestion causes venous engorgement which presses on the ureter, leading to stasis, infection, pyelonephritis, and unilateral dysmenorrhea.
- **Mittelschmerz's Syndrome (Ovulation Pain):** Pain appearing in the midmenstrual period due to increased tension of the Graafian follicle just prior to rupture, peritoneal irritation by follicular fluid, or tubal/uterine contractions.
- **Other Unilateral Causes:** Bicornuate uterus, unilateral pelvic endometriosis, small fibroid polyp near one cornu, or colonic/cecal spasm.

21. Complication of radiotherapy.

**COMPLICATIONS OF RADIOTHERAPY**

Radiation complications arise primarily due to the interaction of ionizing radiation with the normal tissues encountered in the radiation path. Complications can manifest during the treatment or may occur more than one year after the primary therapy.

They are broadly classified into early (acute) and late (chronic) reactions. Late reactions are predominantly caused by underlying vasculitis and fibrosis.

**I. EARLY REACTIONS** These occur during or immediately following the course of radiation and are usually managed symptomatically.

- **General Symptoms:** Anorexia, nausea, vomiting, lassitude, and occasionally fever.
    - _Management:_ Antiemetics and antihistaminics.
- **Gastrointestinal:** Diarrhea (radiation enteritis), colitis, and proctitis.
    - _Management:_ Intravenous fluid therapy to correct electrolyte imbalances, antispasmodics, analgesics, and antidiarrheal agents.
- **Hematological:** Anemia, leukopenia, and thrombocytopenia due to bone marrow depression.
    - _Management:_ Hematinics, blood transfusion, Granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), and erythropoietin (EPO).
- **Urinary:** Cystitis, pyelitis, and hematuria.
    - _Management:_ Urinary antiseptics and analgesics.
- **Dermatological/Skin:** Skin peeling and reactions, most often found in moist areas of the vulva.
    - _Management:_ Keep the area dry and apply 1% aqueous gentian violet. Note: This complication is almost completely avoided with modern megavoltage radiotherapy.

**II. LATE REACTIONS (High Yield)** Late complications are progressive and are principally due to radiation-induced vasculitis and subsequent tissue fibrosis.

- **Genitourinary Fistulae:** Vesicovaginal fistula (VVF) or rectovaginal fistula (RVF). These usually develop 1 to 2 years following the primary therapy. They result from ischemic necrosis caused by radiation-induced endarteritis obliterans. They are very difficult to treat locally, and colpocleisis may sometimes be required as an alternative.
- **Vaginal and Vulvar Changes:** Vaginal fibrosis and stricture (stenosis) which leads to severe dyspareunia. Atrophic changes of the vulvar skin also occur, though these are minimized with megavoltage therapy.
- **Intestinal Complications:** Bowel fibrosis, stricture, intestinal obstruction, bleeding per rectum, and bowel perforation. Proctosigmoiditis is common and is treated with steroid enemas and antidiarrheals.
- **Malabsorption Syndrome:** Chronic bowel damage can lead to malabsorption syndrome accompanied by megaloblastic anemia (requires folic acid administration).
- **Ovarian Damage:** Complete destruction of ovarian function leading to radiation menopause and early onset of menopausal symptoms.
- **Skeletal Complications:** Pathological fractures secondary to severe osteoporosis.
- **Radiation Fibrosis:** Dense fibrosis in the pelvis that is often clinically confused with the recurrence of the malignant growth.


22. Six causes of secondary Amenorrhoea.
**SECONDARY AMENORRHEA**

**Definition:** It is defined as the absence of menstruation for 6 months or more in a woman in whom normal menstruation has been established. _Note: Amenorrhea in a woman of reproductive age should always be considered as pregnancy (physiological) unless proved otherwise._

The pathological causes of secondary amenorrhea result from disruptions in the hypothalamo-pituitary-ovarian-uterine axis. Six major categories of causes include:

**1. HYPOTHALAMIC FACTORS (Most Common Pathological Cause)**

- **Stress and Psychogenic Shock:** Emotional stress alters dopamine metabolism and increases endogenous opioids, inhibiting the pulsatile release of GnRH.
- **Weight-Related (Anorexia Nervosa & Malnutrition):** Severe weight loss (BMI <19 kg/m²) or self-imposed starvation leads to hypothalamic dysfunction, reducing GnRH pulse frequency.
- **Exercise-Induced:** Strenuous exercise (e.g., in female athletes or ballet dancers) increases corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and endorphins, suppressing GnRH.

**2. OVARIAN FACTORS**

- **Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome (PCOS):** The most common endocrine disorder (20–25%) characterized by hyperandrogenism, hyperinsulinemia, and tonically elevated LH, leading to anovulation and amenorrhea.
- **Premature Ovarian Insufficiency/Failure (POF):** Depletion of ovarian follicles or resistance to gonadotropins before the age of 40. It leads to a hypergonadotropic hypogonadal state (FSH >40 mIU/mL and low estradiol).
- **Ovarian Tumors:** Hormone-producing tumors, such as masculinizing Sertoli-Leydig cell tumors (androgen excess) or feminizing granulosa cell tumors (hyperestrogenic state), suppress normal cyclical changes.

**3. PITUITARY FACTORS**

- **Prolactinoma (Pituitary Adenoma):** Excess prolactin secretion inhibits GnRH pulsatility and ovarian steroidogenesis, resulting in hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, amenorrhea, and often galactorrhea.
- **Sheehan’s Syndrome:** Postpartum pituitary necrosis secondary to severe postpartum hemorrhage or shock. It leads to panhypopituitarism, presenting with amenorrhea, failing lactation, and genital atrophy.
- **Cushing's Disease & Acromegaly:** Pituitary tumors secreting excess ACTH or Growth Hormone can respectively disrupt the menstrual cycle.

**4. UTERINE FACTORS**

- **Uterine Synechiae (Asherman’s Syndrome):** Formation of intrauterine adhesions, typically following overzealous postabortal or puerperal curettage, which destroys the basal layer of the endometrium.
- **Tubercular Endometritis:** Destruction of the endometrium or inhibition of ovarian function by tubercular toxins.
- **Surgical or Radiation Ablation:** Destruction or removal of the endometrium via laser/resectoscope or pelvic radiation.

**5. THYROID AND ADRENAL FACTORS**

- **Thyroid Dysfunction:** Hypothyroidism is a frequent cause. Elevated TRH directly stimulates prolactin release, leading to hyperprolactinemia, altered GnRH pulsatility, and anovulation. Hyperthyroidism can also disrupt the cycle.
- **Adrenal Disorders:** Late-onset congenital adrenal hyperplasia, adrenal tumors, or Cushing's syndrome produce excess androgens that oppose estrogen's effect on the endometrium.

**6. IATROGENIC AND PHARMACOLOGICAL FACTORS**

- **Post-Pill Amenorrhea:** Occurs in <1% of women after discontinuing combined oral contraceptive pills due to prolonged HPO axis suppression.
- **Psychotropic and Antihypertensive Drugs:** Drugs like phenothiazine derivatives (dopamine receptor blockers) or reserpine/methyldopa (dopamine depleting agents) increase prolactin levels, thereby suppressing GnRH release and causing amenorrhea.
