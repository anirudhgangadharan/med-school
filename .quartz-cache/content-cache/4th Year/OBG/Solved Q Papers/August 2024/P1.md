1. What is definition of placenta previa? Write about the types of placenta previa, clinical features, diagnosis and treatment of placenta previa Type II B.
**DEFINITION OF PLACENTA PREVIA** Placenta previa is defined as a condition when the placenta is implanted partially or completely over the lower uterine segment, over and adjacent to the internal os. The term "previa" (Latin: in front of) denotes the position of the placenta in relation to the presenting part of the fetus.

**CLASSIFICATION AND TYPES** **1. Traditional Classification (Degrees of Extension):** There are traditionally different types depending on the degree of extension of the placenta to the lower uterine segment.

- **Type II Posterior (also known as Type II B):** This is clinically termed "Dangerous placenta previa".

**2. Revised Classification (ACOG, ACR, 2014):** Currently, with ultrasound precision, it is classified more accurately based on the relationship to the internal os:

- **True placenta previa:** The placenta completely covers the internal os.
- **Low-lying placenta:** The placental edge lies within 2 cm of the internal os but does not cover it.

**CLINICAL FEATURES** **Symptoms:**

- **Vaginal Bleeding:** This is the only symptom. The classical features of bleeding are sudden onset, painless, apparently causeless, and recurrent.
- **Warning Hemorrhage:** In about one-third of cases, there is a history of a slight initial "warning hemorrhage".
- **Nature of onset:** Bleeding is unrelated to activity, often occurs during sleep, and the patient awakens in a pool of blood. Obvious causes of separation like trauma or hypertension are typically absent.

**Signs:**

- **General Condition:** Pallor and general condition are directly proportionate to the visible blood loss.
- **Abdominal Examination:**
    - _Uterine Size:_ The size of the uterus is proportionate to the period of gestation.
    - _Uterine Feel:_ The uterus feels soft, relaxed, and elastic without any localized tenderness.
    - _Fetal Presentation:_ Persistence of malpresentations (e.g., breech, transverse, or unstable lie) is more frequent.
    - _Fetal Head:_ The head is unengaged and floating; it cannot be pushed down into the pelvis.
    - _Fetal Heart Sound (FHS):_ Usually present.
    - _Stallworthy’s Sign:_ Slowing of the fetal heart rate upon pressing the floating head down into the pelvis, which recovers promptly when the pressure is released, is suggestive of a low-lying posterior placenta.
- **Vulval Inspection:** The blood is bright red in color because it escapes immediately from the torn uteroplacental sinuses close to the cervical opening.
- **Vaginal Examination:** It is strictly contraindicated and should NOT be done, as it can provoke severe and torrential hemorrhage.

**DIAGNOSIS** The diagnosis is mainly clinical but must be confirmed via imaging:

- **Ultrasonography (USG):** The diagnostic technique of choice.
    - _Transabdominal USG (TAS):_ Accuracy is nearly 100% after 30 weeks.
    - _Transvaginal USG (TVS):_ It is safe, highly accurate (virtually 100%), and superior for defining whether the placenta is within 2 cm of the internal os.
    - _Transperineal USG (TPS):_ Visualizes the internal os in 97-100% of cases.
    - _Color Doppler:_ Shows diffuse vascular lakes and turbulent flow, helpful for identifying morbidly adherent placenta.
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** Superior to USG for diagnosing posterior placenta previa and assessing placenta accreta spectrum (e.g., bladder invasion).
- **Double Set-up Examination:** Rarely done today; involves a vaginal examination in the operating theater with all preparations ready for an immediate cesarean section.

**TYPE II B (POSTERIOR PLACENTA PREVIA): SIGNIFICANCE & TREATMENT** Type II posterior placenta previa is specifically named **Dangerous Placenta Previa**.

**Why is it Dangerous?**

- Because of the curved nature of the birth canal, the major thickness of the placenta (about 2.5 cm) overlies the sacral promontory.
- This diminishes the anteroposterior diameter of the pelvic inlet, thereby preventing the engagement of the presenting part.
- Consequently, the unengaged fetal head fails to exert effective compression on the separated placenta to stop the bleeding, causing the patient to suffer from profuse, life-threatening hemorrhage.

**Treatment of Type II Posterior Placenta Previa:** Because the placenta is thick, posterior, and mechanically obstructs descent and tamponade, vaginal delivery is unsafe.

- **Definitive Delivery Mode:** Cesarean delivery is the treatment of choice and is indicated for all women where the placental edge is within 2 cm from the internal os, and is _especially_ indicated if the placenta is posterior or thick.
- **Immediate Resuscitation:** All patients with antepartum hemorrhage must be admitted. Initial management involves immediate assessment of blood loss, drawing blood for hemoglobin, ABO and Rh grouping, and establishing a wide-bore IV cannula for crystalloid infusion and blood transfusion if required.
- **Expectant Management (If Preterm & Stable):** If the pregnancy is less than 37 weeks, active bleeding has stopped, and the fetal cardiotocography (CTG) is reassuring, expectant management (bed rest, hematinics) is instituted to allow fetal maturation. Corticosteroids (e.g., betamethasone) are administered if the gestation is less than 34 weeks to promote fetal lung maturity.
- **Active Intervention / Emergency Cesarean:** If active bleeding continues, the patient is in labor, the patient is exsanguinated, or if there is fetal distress, an immediate Cesarean Section is performed regardless of gestational age.



2. Define abortion. What are the causes of abortion? How do you manage a case of recurrent mid trimester abortion?

**DEFINITION OF ABORTION** Abortion is defined as the expulsion or extraction of an embryo or fetus weighing 500 g or less from its mother, when it is not capable of independent survival. This 500 g developmental stage is reached at approximately 22 weeks (154 days) of gestation. The expelled embryo or fetus is termed an abortus, and the clinically recommended term for spontaneous abortion is miscarriage.

**CAUSES OF ABORTION** The causes of abortion are multifaceted and often overlap. They are broadly classified as follows:

- **Genetic Factors (50% of first-trimester abortions):** Parental chromosomal abnormalities are a proven cause. The most common chromosomal abnormality is autosomal trisomy, while Monosomy X (45,X) is the single most frequent abnormality in miscarriages. Structural rearrangements like balanced translocations, deletions, and inversions are also responsible.
- **Endocrine and Metabolic Factors (10-15%):**
    - **Luteal Phase Defect (LPD):** Deficient progesterone secretion causes inadequate support for implantation and placentation.
    - **Thyroid Abnormalities:** Overt hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism, and the presence of thyroid autoantibodies.
    - **Diabetes Mellitus:** Poorly controlled diabetes increases miscarriage risk.
    - **Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS):** Due to insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, and hyperandrogenemia.
- **Anatomical Abnormalities (3-38%, predominant in the second trimester):**
    - **Cervicouterine Factors:** Congenital or acquired cervical incompetence.
    - **Müllerian Fusion Defects:** Bicornuate or septate uterus, leading to reduced cavity volume and abnormal placentation.
    - **Uterine Fibroids:** Especially submucous fibroids that distort the uterine cavity and decrease vascularity.
    - **Intrauterine Adhesions (Synechiae/Asherman's syndrome):** Interfere with implantation and placentation.
- **Infections (5%):** Transplacental fetal infections from viral (Rubella, Cytomegalovirus, HIV), parasitic (Toxoplasma, malaria), and bacterial (Ureaplasma, Chlamydia, Brucella) agents.
- **Immunological Disorders (5-10%):**
    - **Antiphospholipid Antibody Syndrome (APAS):** Presence of lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin antibodies, causing thrombosis of the uteroplacental bed and fetal hypoxia.
    - **Immune Factors:** Predominance of Th1 (proinflammatory) cytokine response instead of the protective Th2 response.
- **Thrombophilias:** Inherited (Protein C/S deficiency, Factor V Leiden mutation, hyperhomocysteinemia) or acquired states causing intravascular coagulation and thrombosis.
- **Maternal Medical Illness:** Cyanotic heart disease and hemoglobinopathies.
- **Environmental Factors:** Cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, high-dose X-ray irradiation, use of IUDs, and exposure to anesthetic gases, arsenic, or lead.
- **Paternal Factors:** Sperm chromosomal anomalies (e.g., translocations).
- **Unexplained (40-60%):** In many cases, the exact cause remains obscure despite thorough investigation.

---

**MANAGEMENT OF RECURRENT MID-TRIMESTER ABORTION** Recurrent mid-trimester (second trimester) abortions are primarily caused by anatomic abnormalities (cervical incompetence, Müllerian defects, synechiae, fibroids), maternal medical illnesses, or infections. Management is highly systematic and divided into investigation, interconceptional care, and care during pregnancy.

**1. Investigations:**

- **Detailed History:** Analyze previous abortion processes, histology of the placenta, karyotyping of the conceptus, and any chronic maternal illnesses.
- **Blood Tests:** Fasting and postprandial glucose, VDRL, thyroid function tests, ABO and Rh grouping, and Toxoplasma antibodies (IgG/IgM).
- **Autoimmune & Thrombophilia Screening:** Lupus anticoagulant and anticardiolipin antibodies.
- **Imaging:** Ultrasonography (USG/TVS) to detect congenital uterine malformations, fibroids, or sonographic signs of cervical insufficiency (cervical length <25 mm and funneling of the internal os).
- **Advanced Imaging/Endoscopy:** Hysterosalpingography, MRI, hysteroscopy, or laparoscopy to confirm septate/bicornuate uterus or synechiae.
- **Microbiology:** Endocervical swab for Chlamydia, Mycoplasma, and bacterial vaginosis.

**2. Interconceptional Period Management:**

- **Psychological Support:** Reassurance and Tender Loving Care (TLC) alleviate anxiety and are highly effective.
- **Surgical Correction:** Hysteroscopic resection of uterine septa, synechiae, and submucous myomas improves outcomes. Metroplasty (uterine unification) is indicated for a bicornuate uterus.
- **Medical Therapy:** Appropriate antibiotics for specific genital tract infections (e.g., empirical doxycycline or erythromycin). Optimization of maternal medical diseases (e.g., tight glycemic control in diabetes).
- **Genetic Counseling:** If balanced translocations or inversions are found, preimplantation genetic diagnosis (PGD) may be advised.

**3. Management During Pregnancy:**

- **General Measures:** Adequate rest, avoidance of strenuous activities, traveling, and intercourse. Early USG to detect fetal viability. Reassurance and Tender Loving Care (TLC).
- **Progesterone Therapy:** Natural micronized progesterone (100 mg daily) can be given to induce an immunomodulatory shift from a Th1 to a Th2 cytokine response, supporting the pregnancy.
- **Management of Cervical Incompetence (Insufficiency):**
    - **Surgical (Cervical Cerclage):** Reinforces the weak cervix with a nonabsorbable tape using the Shirodkar or McDonald technique.
        - _Timing:_ Usually performed around 14 weeks of pregnancy.
        - _History-indicated cerclage:_ Offered between 12-14 weeks for women with three or more prior mid-trimester losses.
        - _Ultrasound-indicated cerclage:_ Offered between 16-24 weeks if serial USG reveals a short cervix (<25 mm).
        - _Removal:_ The stitch must be removed at 37 completed weeks, or immediately if labor pain starts or abortion features appear, to prevent uterine rupture or cervical tearing.
    - **Nonsurgical Alternatives:** Strict bed rest, use of a vaginal pessary, or weekly intramuscular injections of 17 alpha-hydroxyprogesterone caproate (500 mg).


3. Define IUGR. How do you manage a case of IUGR?
### **DEFINITION OF INTRAUTERINE GROWTH RESTRICTION (IUGR) / FETAL GROWTH RESTRICTION (FGR)**

Fetal Growth Restriction (FGR), also known as Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR), is a syndrome where the fetus fails to reach its optimal growth potential due to underlying maternal, fetal, or placental disorders.

Clinically and sonographically, Small for Gestational Age (SGA) is defined as an estimated fetal weight (EFW) below the 10th percentile for the gestational age. However, true FGR is defined by a combination of fetal size percentiles and Doppler abnormalities.

According to FIGO (2021), FGR is classified into:

1. **Early-onset FGR (<32 weeks):**
    - EFW or Abdominal Circumference (AC) <3rd percentile OR
    - Umbilical Artery (UA) with Absent End-Diastolic Velocity (AREDV) OR
    - EFW or AC <10th percentile combined with UA Pulsatility Index (PI) >95th percentile or Uterine Artery (UtA) PI >95th percentile.
2. **Late-onset FGR (≥32 weeks):**
    - EFW or AC <3rd percentile OR
    - At least two of the following: EFW or AC <10th percentile, EFW or AC crossing percentiles (>2 quartiles), Cerebroplacental Ratio (CPR) <5th percentile, or UA PI >95th percentile.

---

### **MANAGEMENT OF IUGR**

There is no proven definitive therapy to cure FGR once established. The cornerstone of management relies on accurate diagnosis, intensive fetal surveillance, treatment of underlying causes, and optimizing the timing and mode of delivery.

#### **1. General and Medical Management**

- **Rest:** Bed rest, especially in the left lateral position, is advised to improve uteroplacental perfusion.
- **Nutritional Support:** A balanced diet with 300 extra calories per day should be provided. Maternal hyperalimentation with amino acids may improve fetal growth only if the IUGR is strictly due to maternal malnutrition.
- **Avoidance of Toxins:** Absolute cessation of smoking, alcohol, and illicit drugs.
- **Low-Dose Aspirin:** Administration of 75–150 mg of aspirin daily, starting at 12–16 weeks, is helpful in selected high-risk cases (e.g., history of thrombotic disease, pre-eclampsia, or recurrent FGR).
- **Maternal Hyperoxygenation & Volume Expansion:** Administering 2.5 L/min of oxygen via nasal prongs may provide short-term prolongation of pregnancy. Maternal circulatory volume expansion may also improve placental perfusion.
- **Treat Underlying Pathology:** Appropriate therapy must be instituted for associated complicating factors like maternal hypertension or anemia.

#### **2. Antepartum Fetal Surveillance**

Once FGR is diagnosed, rigorous serial evaluation is mandatory to detect hypoxia and prevent intrauterine fetal death (IUFD).

- **Ultrasonography (USG):** Serial USG every 1–2 weeks to assess fetal biometry (BPD, HC, AC, FL), Estimated Fetal Weight (EFW), and Amniotic Fluid Index (AFI) or Single Deepest Vertical Pocket (SDVP).
- **Biophysical Monitoring:** Daily Fetal Movement Count (DFMCR), Non-Stress Test (NST), and Biophysical Profile (BPP) should be performed twice weekly.
- **Doppler Velocimetry:** Umbilical Artery (UA) Doppler is the primary surveillance tool. Additionally, Middle Cerebral Artery (MCA), Cerebroplacental Ratio (CPR), and Ductus Venosus (DV) flow patterns are monitored to determine the severity of fetal hypoxia and acidemia.

#### **3. Timing of Delivery**

The decision to deliver balances the risks of prematurity against the risks of ongoing intrauterine hypoxia.

- **Uncomplicated Mild FGR (Normal Doppler):** Delivery is planned between 37–39 weeks.
- **FGR with Early Doppler Changes / Oligohydramnios / Pre-eclampsia:** Deliver between 34–37 weeks. Monitor strictly with CTG/NST/BPP (weekly) and UA Doppler (twice weekly) until delivery.
- **FGR with UA Absent End-Diastolic Velocity (AEDV):** Deliver by 32–34 weeks.
- **FGR with UA Reversed End-Diastolic Velocity (REDV):** Deliver by 30 weeks.
- **FGR with Abnormal DV Doppler (Absent/Reversed A-wave):** Deliver by 26–30 weeks.
- **Absolute Indications for Immediate Delivery (Irrespective of Gestational Age):** Abnormal CTG/NST (reduced variability, repetitive late decelerations), severe pre-eclampsia with end-organ damage, or abnormal DV Doppler indicating severe acidemia.

#### **4. Pre-Delivery Preparations**

- **Antenatal Corticosteroids:** If delivery is anticipated before 34 weeks, administer Betamethasone or Dexamethasone to accelerate fetal lung maturation and reduce the risk of Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS), Intraventricular Hemorrhage (IVH), and Necrotizing Enterocolitis (NEC).
- **Magnesium Sulfate (MgSO4):** If delivery is planned before 32 weeks, MgSO4 must be administered for fetal neuroprotection (to reduce the risk of cerebral palsy).

#### **5. Mode of Delivery**

- FGR alone is not an absolute indication for Cesarean Delivery (CD).
- **Vaginal Delivery:** Can be attempted in late-onset cases (>34 weeks) with a favorable cervix and engaged head, using low rupture of membranes followed by oxytocin, provided continuous electronic fetal monitoring is available.
- **Cesarean Delivery:** Indicated for early-onset FGR, cases with UA AEDV/REDV, abnormal DV flow, abnormal CTG/NST/BPP, or associated obstetric indications.

#### **6. Immediate Neonatal Care and Postpartum Follow-up**

- Delivery must be conducted in a tertiary center equipped with a Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU).
- A pediatrician/neonatologist must be present during delivery to manage potential complications such as birth asphyxia, meconium aspiration, hypothermia, and hypoglycemia.
- The placenta should be sent for histopathological examination to aid in counseling for future pregnancies.
- Postpartum counseling should emphasize lifestyle modifications, optimal inter-pregnancy intervals, and early screening (aspirin use, BP, and growth monitoring) in subsequent pregnancies.


4. Internal Podalic version
**DEFINITION** Internal Podalic Version (IPV) is an obstetric manipulative procedure designed to change the lie or presentation of the fetus to a podalic (breech) pole, bringing it to the lower pole of the uterus. The conversion is performed principally by introducing one hand into the uterine cavity while the other hand assists externally over the maternal abdomen. Internal version is always a podalic version and is almost always completed immediately with the extraction of the fetus.

**INDICATIONS** In modern obstetric practice, IPV is rarely indicated in singleton pregnancies.

- **Primary Indication:** Transverse lie in the case of the second baby of twins.
- **Emergency Indications (where cesarean section facilities are lacking):** It may be employed in a singleton pregnancy to expedite delivery under adverse conditions such as:
    - Transverse lie with a fully dilated cervix.
    - Cord prolapse with a fully dilated cervix, where the lie is transverse or the head is high up, provided the baby is alive.

**CONDITIONS TO BE FULFILLED (PREREQUISITES)** Before attempting IPV, the following criteria must be strictly met:

1. The cervix must be fully dilated.
2. The liquor amnii (amniotic fluid) must be adequate to allow safe intrauterine fetal manipulation.
3. The fetus must be living.

**CONTRAINDICATIONS**

- It must **never** be attempted in neglected obstructed labor, even if the fetus is living.
- It is contraindicated in cases of tonic uterine contraction or the presence of a pathological retraction ring (Bandl's ring).

**STEPS OF THE PROCEDURE** Assessment of the lie, presentation, and fetal heart rate (FHR) must be made by an experienced obstetrician using abdominal palpation, vaginal examination, and transabdominal ultrasound, alongside continuous FHR monitoring. The procedure should be done under general or epidural anesthesia.

- **Step I (Preparation and Introduction of the Hand):** The patient is placed in the dorsal lithotomy position, followed by antiseptic cleaning, draping, and catheterization. If the podalic pole of the fetus is on the left side of the mother, the right hand is introduced, and vice versa. The hand is introduced in a cone-shaped manner, pushed up into the uterine cavity while keeping the back of the hand against the uterine wall, until the podalic pole is reached.
- **Step II (Grasping the Foot):** The internal hand passes up to the breech and then along the fetal thigh until a foot is grasped. The foot is identified by palpating the heel. It is advantageous to grasp the first foot that is encountered.
- **Step III (Version):** While the leg is brought down with steady traction (gripping in a cigarette-holding fashion), the cephalic pole is simultaneously pushed upwards towards the fundus using the external hand.
- **Step IV (Extraction):** After one leg is successfully brought down, there is usually no difficulty in delivering the other leg. Delivery is then completed via breech extraction, timed with uterine contractions.
- **Step V (Exploration):** Routine and methodical exploration of the uterovaginal canal is mandatory post-delivery to exclude rupture of the uterus or any other internal genital tract injury.

**COMPLICATIONS** **Maternal Complications:**

- Rupture of the uterus.
- Placental abruption.
- Increased maternal morbidity.
- Trauma to the genital tract and postpartum hemorrhage.

**Fetal Complications:**

- Asphyxia.
- Cord prolapse.
- Intracranial hemorrhage.
- Hazards of breech extraction, leading to a high perinatal mortality rate of about 50%.


5. Define maternal mortality. Discuss the causes and methods to reduce maternal mortality.
**DEFINITION OF MATERNAL MORTALITY** Maternal mortality is defined as the death of a woman while pregnant or within 42 days of the termination of pregnancy, irrespective of the duration and the site of pregnancy, from any cause related to or aggravated by the pregnancy or its management, but not from accidental or incidental causes.

It is typically expressed as the Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR), which is the number of maternal deaths per 100,000 live births.

**CAUSES OF MATERNAL MORTALITY** The causes of maternal deaths are broadly classified into Direct, Indirect, and Non-obstetric (fortuitous) causes:

**1. Direct Obstetric Deaths (75%)** These result from complications of pregnancy, delivery, or their management.

- **Hemorrhage (20–25%):** Predominantly Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH), but also includes Antepartum Hemorrhage (abruptio placentae, placenta previa), retained placenta, and ectopic pregnancy,. Hemorrhage is the major cause of maternal death.
- **Hypertensive Disorders (15–20%):** Severe pre-eclampsia and eclampsia,,.
- **Puerperal Sepsis/Infection (11–15%):** Associated with prolonged/obstructed labor, premature rupture of membranes, and unhygienic delivery practices,.
- **Unsafe Abortion (8–13%):** Often due to lack of access to safe abortion services and family planning,.
- **Obstructed Labor (8–9%):** Caused by cephalopelvic disproportion, abnormal lie, or malpresentation,.

**2. Indirect Obstetric Deaths (25%)** These include conditions present before or developed during pregnancy that are aggravated by the physiological effects of pregnancy and the strain of labor.

- **Anemia (15–20%):** The most important single indirect cause of maternal death in developing countries,. It is commonly due to dietary deficiency, hookworm, or malaria,.
- **Medical Disorders:** Cardiac disease, diabetes, thyroid disease, and systemic infections.
- **Viral Hepatitis:** Endemic in India, contributing to 5–10% of deaths, often presenting in the third trimester with hepatic coma or coagulation failure,.

**3. Non-Obstetric / Fortuitous Deaths** These include deaths from accidents, typhoid, and other infectious diseases.

**4. The "Three Delays" Model (Factors aggravating mortality)** The Government of India identifies three types of delays leading to maternal mortality:

- **Delay I:** Delay in recognizing the problem and deciding to seek care (lack of birth preparedness).
- **Delay II:** Delay in reaching the health facility (lack of transport or referral facility).
- **Delay III:** Delay in receiving treatment at the center (unequipped facility, lack of blood, medicines, or trained personnel).

**5. Other Associated Factors**

- Age >35 years (risk is 3-4 times higher).
- High parity (risk is 3 times greater in para 5 or above).
- Low socioeconomic status, malnutrition, and substandard clinical care,.

---

**METHODS TO REDUCE MATERNAL MORTALITY**

**1. Health Sector Actions & Clinical Interventions**

- **Basic and Continuous Care:** Quality antenatal, intranatal, and postnatal care with continuous risk assessment throughout pregnancy.
- **Skilled Birth Attendant (SBA):** Ensuring an SBA (doctor, midwife, or nurse) is present at every birth,. At least "three cleans" must be maintained during delivery to prevent sepsis.
- **Emergency Obstetric Care (EmOC):** Provision of EmOC at First Referral Units (FRUs) including anesthetic services, blood transfusion facilities, cesarean delivery, and manual removal of placenta,,.
- **Functional Referral System:** Ensuring seamless integration of domiciliary and institutional services with adequate transport facilities.
- **Family Planning and Safe Abortion:** Providing confidential, effective family planning services to prevent unwanted pregnancies and offering safe abortion care,.
- **Medical Audits & Training:** Conducting maternal mortality conferences and audits to evaluate avoidable factors, alongside periodic refresher courses for obstetricians and midwives,.
- **Specific Clinical Protocols,,,:**
    - _Hemorrhage:_ Active management of the third stage with oxytocics, rapid volume replacement, and timely blood transfusion.
    - _Hypertension:_ Early detection and administration of Magnesium Sulfate ($MgSO_4$) for anti-seizure prophylaxis.
    - _Obstructed Labor:_ Consistent use of the partograph to detect delays early and prompt referral for operative delivery.
    - _Anemia:_ Routine Iron-Folic Acid (IFA) supplementation, treating underlying infections, and admission for severe anemia (Hb < 7 g/dL).

**2. Government and National Health Mission (NHM) Initiatives**

- **Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram (JSSK):** Implementing interventions specifically focusing on Empowered Action Group (EAG) states with low institutional deliveries,.
- **Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHA):** Utilizing ASHAs to map pregnant women, mobilize them for antenatal checks, and promote institutional deliveries.
- **Empowering Allied Health Workers:** Training SBAs (Auxiliary Nurse Midwives, Lady Health Visitors, and Staff Nurses) to administer life-saving drugs under specified situations, including Misoprostol, Oxytocin, $MgSO_4$, and antibiotics (Ampicillin/Metronidazole),,.

**3. Community and Societal Actions**

- Addressing social inequalities and promoting women's empowerment,.
- Increasing literacy rates among women, raising the marriageable age, and educating communities to utilize available maternity services,.

6. What are prenatal diagnostic tests and its indications?
**PRENATAL DIAGNOSTIC TESTS**

**Definition** Prenatal diagnostic tests are procedures performed during pregnancy to evaluate a fetus for the risk of chromosomal abnormalities, genetic diseases, or structural anomalies. They are primarily offered to screen-positive women to obtain fetal tissue or cells for confirmatory cytogenetic, biochemical, or DNA analysis.

**Classification of Prenatal Diagnostic Tests** Fetal tissues, cells, or parameters are obtained through invasive and noninvasive methods.

- **Noninvasive Methods:**
    - High-resolution Ultrasonography (USG).
    - Fetal echocardiography.
    - Cell-free fetal DNA (cf-DNA) analysis from maternal plasma or blood.
    - Fetal cell isolation from maternal blood (e.g., from trophoblast cells or nucleated red blood cells).
- **Invasive Methods:**
    - Chorionic Villus Sampling (CVS).
    - Amniocentesis.
    - Cordocentesis or Percutaneous Umbilical Blood Sampling (PUBS).
    - Fetoscopy.
    - Pre-implantation genetic diagnosis (PGD) (e.g., polar body, blastomere, or trophectoderm biopsy).

---

**INDICATIONS FOR PRENATAL DIAGNOSTIC TESTS**

The indications are broadly categorized into maternal risk factors and prenatal/fetal risk factors.

**I. Maternal Risk Factors:**

- Advanced maternal age (greater than 35 years).
- Previous child born with a chromosomal anomaly.
- Previous baby born with a Neural Tube Defect (NTD).
- Family history of neural tube defects.
- One or both parents being known carriers of sex-linked or autosomal genetic traits.
- One parent is a known carrier of a balanced chromosomal translocation.
- History of recurrent miscarriages.

**II. Prenatal / Fetal Risk Factors:**

- Abnormal maternal serum screening results (e.g., elevated/decreased MSAFP, abnormal Quadruple or First-trimester combined tests).
- Abnormal ultrasound findings indicating structural anomalies.
- Presence of soft tissue markers of chromosomal anomaly on ultrasonography (e.g., increased nuchal translucency, absent nasal bone).
- Amniotic fluid disorders such as polyhydramnios or oligohydramnios.
- Severe symmetrical Fetal Growth Restriction (FGR).
- Uncontrolled maternal diabetes mellitus in the periconceptional period.
- Maternal contact with teratogenic infections (e.g., Rubella, Cytomegalovirus) or intake of teratogenic drugs.

---

**SPECIFIC INDICATIONS FOR INVASIVE PROCEDURES**

**1. Chorionic Villus Sampling (CVS):**

- _Cytogenetic diagnosis:_ To detect chromosomal anomalies (e.g., Trisomy 21, Turner's syndrome) by examining cultured fetal trophoblast cells.
- _DNA analysis:_ To diagnose single-gene disorders (e.g., cystic fibrosis, Tay-Sachs disease, beta-thalassemia) using specific DNA probes or Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR).

**2. Amniocentesis:**

- _Diagnostic (Early months / 15-20 weeks):_ Karyotyping for chromosomal/genetic disorders, sex-linked disorders, inborn errors of metabolism, and detection of neural tube defects (via elevated amniotic fluid Alpha-Fetoprotein).
- _Diagnostic (Later months):_ Assessment of fetal pulmonary maturity (L/S ratio, Phosphatidylglycerol), evaluating the severity of fetal hemolysis in Rh-sensitized mothers via spectrophotometric analysis (optical density at 450 nm), and detecting meconium-stained liquor indicating fetal distress.

**3. Cordocentesis (Percutaneous Umbilical Blood Sampling):**

- _Cytogenetic diagnosis:_ Rapid karyotyping using fetal white blood cells (cultured within 24-48 hours).
- _Hematological evaluation:_ Diagnosis of fetal anemia, Rh disease, hemoglobinopathies, and bleeding disorders like autoimmune thrombocytopenia.
- _Fetal infections:_ Detection of specific IgM antibodies or viral DNA (e.g., toxoplasmosis, viral infections).
- _Fetal blood gas and acid-base status:_ Assessment in cases of severe Fetal Growth Restriction (FGR).
- _Fetal therapy:_ For administering intrauterine blood transfusions or drug therapy.


7. Define preterm labour and management of preterm labour.
**DEFINITION OF PRETERM LABOR** Preterm labor is defined as labor that starts before the 37th completed week (<259 days) of gestation, calculated from the first day of the last normal menstrual period. The lower limit of viability varies geographically; it is considered 20 weeks in developed countries and 26–28 weeks in low and middle-income countries.

**MANAGEMENT OF PRETERM LABOR** The primary goals of management are to delay delivery by at least 48 hours to allow antenatal glucocorticoid therapy to act, to facilitate the _in utero_ transfer of the mother to a tertiary care center equipped with a Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU), and to ensure safe delivery with minimal birth trauma and asphyxia.

**1. General Measures**

- **Bed Rest:** The patient is advised bed rest, preferably in the left lateral position.
- **Hydration:** Adequate maternal hydration is maintained.
- **Hospitalization & Transfer:** Immediate antenatal transfer of the mother (with the fetus _in utero_) to a facility with an advanced NICU is essential.

**2. Medical Management to Arrest Labor (Tocolysis)** Tocolytic agents are used for a short-term duration (1–3 days) to inhibit uterine contractions, providing a window of time for corticosteroid administration and maternal transfer.

- **Commonly used Tocolytics:** Calcium channel blockers (e.g., Nifedipine), Magnesium sulfate, Cyclo-oxygenase inhibitors (e.g., Indomethacin), Betamimetics (e.g., Terbutaline, Ritodrine), and Oxytocin receptor antagonists (e.g., Atosiban).
- **Contraindications to Tocolysis:**
    - _Maternal:_ Severe pre-eclampsia/hypertension, active cardiac disease, uncontrolled diabetes, and antepartum hemorrhage (abruption or previa).
    - _Fetal:_ Non-reassuring fetal status (fetal distress), intrauterine fetal death, lethal congenital anomalies, and gestational age ≥34 weeks.
    - _Obstetric:_ Premature rupture of membranes (PROM), chorioamnionitis, and advanced cervical dilatation (>4 cm).

**3. Fetal Protective Therapies**

- **Antenatal Corticosteroids:** Administered if the gestation is <34 weeks to accelerate fetal lung maturation and significantly reduce the incidence of Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS), Intraventricular Hemorrhage (IVH), Necrotizing Enterocolitis (NEC), and Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA).
    - _Regimen:_ Betamethasone 12 mg IM, 24 hours apart for two doses (preferred) OR Dexamethasone 6 mg IM every 12 hours for four doses.
- **Neuroprotection:** Magnesium sulfate (MgSO4) is administered when pregnancy is <32 weeks to reduce the risk of neonatal cerebral palsy.
- **Antibiotic Prophylaxis:** Prophylactic broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g., IV Ampicillin, Cefazolin, or Clindamycin) are given to prevent neonatal Group B Streptococcus (GBS) infection.

**4. Management During Labor and Delivery**

- **Mode of Delivery:** Vaginal delivery is the preferred route. Routine cesarean delivery is not recommended and should be reserved strictly for obstetric indications (e.g., malpresentation, prior cesarean, or non-reassuring fetal status).
- **Intrapartum Monitoring:** Continuous Electronic Fetal Monitoring (EFM) is advised due to the high susceptibility of the preterm fetus to hypoxia.
- **Conduct of Delivery:**
    - A liberal episiotomy and the prophylactic use of forceps or ventouse may be employed to cut short the second stage of labor and prevent intracranial stress/trauma to the fragile fetal head.
    - A neonatologist/pediatrician must be present during the delivery.
    - **Delayed Cord Clamping (DCC):** The umbilical cord should be clamped after a delay of at least 60–90 seconds to provide the preterm neonate with a higher circulating blood volume and hematocrit.

8. Indications and prerequisites for forceps application
**INDICATIONS FOR FORCEPS APPLICATION**

The indications for forceps delivery are broadly categorized into maternal and fetal conditions. In modern obstetrics, it is primarily used to cut short the second stage of labor safely.

**1. Maternal Indications:**

- **Inadequate expulsive efforts** or situations where maternal pushing is absent.
- **Maternal exhaustion** (distress) during labor.
- **Prolonged second stage of labor:** Non-progress of labor beyond the cut-off value (Primigravida: 3 hours with regional anesthesia (RA) or 2 hours without RA; Multipara: 2 hours with RA or 1 hour without RA).
- **Medical conditions avoiding Valsalva maneuver:** Where bearing-down efforts are contraindicated, such as severe pre-eclampsia, eclampsia, cardiac disease, hypertensive crises, cerebrovascular diseases, and spinal cord injuries.
- **Previous Cesarean Section:** To cut short the second stage of labor and prevent strain on the uterine scar.
- **Patients under epidural analgesia**.
- **Prophylactic (Elective) Forceps:** To shorten the second stage when maternal and/or fetal complications are anticipated, sparing the mother from the strain of bearing down and preventing fetal cerebral injury.

**2. Fetal Indications:**

- **Fetal distress:** Indicated by a non-reassuring fetal heart rate (CTG) or meconium-stained liquor, requiring prompt extraction.
- **Prematurity (< 34 weeks):** Forceps is preferred over the ventouse as the forceps blades act as a protective cage for the fragile fetal cranium against the pressure of the birth canal.
- **Aftercoming head of breech delivery:** To allow controlled delivery of the head and lessen the dangers of sudden decompression.
- **Face presentation:** specifically in the mentoanterior position.
- **Fetal coagulopathy:** Suspected acute bleeding diathesis (e.g., hemophilia) or thrombocytopenia.
- **Other conditions:** Low birth weight baby, postmaturity, and cord prolapse (when the cervix is fully dilated and the head is low down).

---

**PREREQUISITES FOR FORCEPS APPLICATION**

Before undertaking an operative vaginal delivery with forceps, the following conditions must be strictly fulfilled to minimize maternal and fetal complications (SOGC, RANCZOG guidelines):

**Fetal and Maternal Criteria:**

- **Head must be engaged:** The fetal head must be engaged, meaning the head is $\le$ 1/5 palpable per abdominal examination.
- **Cervix must be fully dilated:** To prevent severe cervical lacerations and entrapment.
- **Membranes must be ruptured**.
- **Exact fetal head position must be known:** Clinical assessment is vital to ensure correct application of the blades.
- **Pelvis deemed adequate:** There must be no clinical evidence of cephalopelvic disproportion (CPD).
- **Caput and moulding:** Must not be more than +2.
- **Bladder must be emptied:** Usually with a simple rubber catheter to provide more pelvic space and prevent bladder injury during extraction.
- **Adequate maternal analgesia:** Pudendal block with perineal infiltration for low/outlet forceps, or regional block (spinal/epidural) for mid-cavity and rotational deliveries.
- **Informed consent:** Verbal or written consent with prior clear explanation of the risks, benefits, and alternatives must be obtained.

**Operational and Facility Criteria:**

- **Experienced operator:** The procedure must be performed by an obstetrician skilled in forceps application.
- **Aseptic techniques:** Strict surgical asepsis must be maintained.
- **Presence of a neonatologist:** Essential for immediate neonatal resuscitation.
- **Back-up plan:** Facilities for an immediate emergency Cesarean Section must be ready in case of a failed forceps trial.
- **Willingness to abandon:** The operator must be willing to abandon the procedure in favor of a Cesarean delivery if difficulties are faced, or if there is no descent after three pulls with correctly applied instruments.


9. What are the types of episiotomy and what is 3rd degree perineal tear?
**EPISIOTOMY**

**Definition:** Episiotomy (perineotomy) is a surgically planned incision on the perineum and the posterior vaginal wall during the second stage of labor. It is purposefully inflicted as a second-degree perineal injury to enlarge the vaginal introitus and facilitate the safe delivery of the fetus.

**Types of Episiotomy:** There are four types of episiotomy, though only the first two are commonly practiced:

1. **Mediolateral Episiotomy:**
    - The incision is made downwards and outwards from the midpoint of the fourchette at a 60° angle off the midline.
    - It is directed diagonally in a straight line towards the midpoint between the anus and the ischial tuberosity (keeping it about 2.5 cm away from the anus).
    - This is the most common and safest type to prevent sphincter injury.
2. **Median Episiotomy:**
    - The incision commences from the center of the fourchette and extends posteriorly along the midline for about 2.5 cm.
3. **Lateral Episiotomy:**
    - The incision starts about 1 cm away from the center of the fourchette and extends laterally.
    - It is completely condemned in modern obstetrics because of its associated drawbacks, especially the high chance of injury to the Bartholin's duct.
4. **'J' Shaped Episiotomy:**
    - The incision begins in the center of the fourchette, is directed posteriorly along the midline for about 1.5 cm, and then curves downwards and outwards along the 5 or 7 o'clock position to avoid the anal sphincter.
    - It is not widely done because the tissue apposition is not perfect and the repaired wound tends to be puckered.

---

**THIRD-DEGREE PERINEAL TEAR**

**Definition:** A third-degree perineal tear is an obstetric injury to the perineum that involves the perineal muscles and the anal sphincter complex (external and/or internal anal sphincter), but does not extend into the anal/rectal epithelium.

**Classification (RCOG Guidelines):** To standardize the extent of sphincter damage and guide repair, the third-degree tear is further sub-classified into three grades:

- **Grade 3a:** Tear involves less than 50% (<50%) of the External Anal Sphincter (EAS) thickness.
- **Grade 3b:** Tear involves more than 50% (>50%) of the External Anal Sphincter (EAS) thickness.
- **Grade 3c:** Tear involves both the External Anal Sphincter (EAS) and the Internal Anal Sphincter (IAS).

_(Note: If the injury further involves the anal epithelium/mucosa, it is classified as a fourth-degree tear)._


10. Ante partum fetal surveillance

**ANTEPARTUM FETAL SURVEILLANCE**

**OBJECTIVES** The primary goals of antepartum fetal surveillance are the assessment of fetal wellbeing, the prevention of fetal death, and the avoidance of unnecessary interventions.

**METHODS OF FETAL SURVEILLANCE** The assessment of fetal wellbeing is broadly divided into Clinical, Biophysical, and Biochemical methods.

**I. CLINICAL EVALUATION** Clinical parameters evaluated at every antenatal visit to screen for fetal growth and wellbeing include:

- **Maternal Weight Gain:** Less than normal or falling weight gain suggests intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR).
- **Blood Pressure:** Essential to detect pre-eclampsia, which can impair fetal growth.
- **Symphysis Fundal Height (SFH):** Measured in centimeters, it normally corresponds to the gestational age in weeks (± 2 cm) between 24 and 36 weeks. A measurement falling below the 10th centile indicates suspected fetal growth restriction (FGR).
- **Abdominal Girth:** Gradual diminution of girth beyond term suggests placental insufficiency and oligohydramnios.
- **Fetal Movement Count:** A simple, non-invasive, and ideal first-line screening test started at 28 weeks. Loss of fetal movements is commonly followed by disappearance of FHR within 24 hours.
    - _Cardiff ‘Count 10’ Formula:_ The mother counts movements starting at 9 AM. She must report to the physician if there are less than 10 movements in 12 hours on two successive days, or no movement in 12 hours.
    - _Daily Fetal Movement Count (DFMC):_ Three counts of 1 hour each (morning, noon, evening) are taken. The total is multiplied by 4 to give the 12-hour count. Less than 10 kicks in 12 hours indicates fetal compromise.

**II. BIOPHYSICAL EVALUATION** **1. Non-Stress Test (NST)**

- It is a continuous electronic monitoring of the fetal heart rate (FHR) along with recording of fetal movements (cardiotocography).
- **Reactive (Reassuring) NST:** Presence of two or more accelerations of FHR (more than 15 beats/min above baseline, lasting > 15 seconds) in a 20-minute observation period.
- **Non-reactive (Non-reassuring) NST:** Absence of fetal reactivity. It is associated with a higher perinatal death rate.
- _Vibroacoustic Stimulation (VAS):_ Can be used to change the fetal sleep state from quiet to active; a reactive NST following VAS indicates a healthy fetus.

**2. Contraction Stress Test (CST) / Oxytocin Challenge Test**

- Assesses the respiratory function of the fetoplacental unit during induced uterine contractions (via oxytocin).
- **Positive CST:** Persistent late decelerations with 50% or more of uterine contractions (indicates high risk of fetal hypoxia/IUD).
- **Negative CST:** No late or significant variable decelerations (associated with good fetal outcome).

**3. Ultrasonography (USG)**

- **Growth Profile:** Serial measurements of Biparietal Diameter (BPD), Head Circumference (HC), Abdominal Circumference (AC), and Femur Length (FL) at 2-3 week intervals. AC is the single best measurement reflecting fetal nutrition.
- **Amniotic Fluid Volume (AFV):** Diminishing AFV indicates hypoxia and placental insufficiency. An Amniotic Fluid Index (AFI) ≤ 5 cm or a Single Deepest Vertical Pocket (SDVP) < 2 cm indicates oligohydramnios and an increased risk of perinatal mortality.

**4. Fetal Biophysical Profile (BPP)** It utilizes real-time ultrasonography to evaluate 5 parameters. Each normal parameter scores 2 points, and abnormal scores 0 (Total maximum score = 10).

- **Components:**
    1. _NST:_ Reactive pattern.
    2. _Fetal Breathing Movements:_ ≥1 episode lasting >30 seconds in 30 minutes.
    3. _Gross Body Movements:_ ≥3 discrete body/limb movements in 30 minutes.
    4. _Fetal Muscle Tone:_ ≥1 episode of active extension with return to flexion.
    5. _Amniotic Fluid Volume:_ ≥1 pocket measuring 2x2 cm.
- **Interpretation:** A score of 8-10 is normal (low risk of asphyxia). A score of 6 is equivocal (suspect chronic asphyxia). A score of 4 or less is abnormal, highly correlated with fetal acidemia, and strongly indicates the need for immediate delivery.

**5. Modified Biophysical Profile**

- Consists of NST and sonographically determined Amniotic Fluid Index (AFI). It takes less time and has a high positive predictive value.

**6. Doppler Ultrasound Velocimetry** Evaluates downstream vascular resistance and multi-vessel fetal cardiovascular status.

- **Umbilical Artery (UA):** Normally, the Systolic/Diastolic (S/D) ratio, Pulsatility Index (PI), and Resistance Index (RI) decrease as pregnancy advances. Reduced, Absent (AEDV), or Reversed End-Diastolic Velocity (REDV) indicates extreme placental vascular resistance and is a severe sign of fetal hypoxia.
- **Middle Cerebral Artery (MCA):** Hypoxemia causes cerebral vasodilatation (brain-sparing effect), leading to increased diastolic velocity and decreased PI.
- **Cerebroplacental Ratio (CPR):** Calculated by dividing MCA PI by UA PI. A CPR <5th percentile indicates fetal compromise.
- **Ductus Venosus (DV):** Abnormal (absent or reversed) ‘a-wave’ indicates fetal acidemia and impending cardiac failure, strongly predicting stillbirth.

**III. BIOCHEMICAL EVALUATION** Mainly done in late pregnancy via amniocentesis to assess fetal pulmonary maturity to prevent Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS) if early delivery is required.

- **Lecithin/Sphingomyelin (L/S) ratio:** A ratio ≥ 2.0 indicates pulmonary maturity.
- **Phosphatidylglycerol (PG):** Presence in amniotic fluid reliably indicates lung maturation.
- **TDx-FLM (Fluorescence Polarization):** Ratio of surfactant to albumin > 55 mg/g indicates maturity.

11. What is engagement of head? Causes of non-engagement of head in primigravida at term

**DEFINITION OF ENGAGEMENT** Engagement is defined as the condition when the greatest horizontal plane of the fetal head, which is the biparietal diameter, has passed the plane of the pelvic brim.

_Significance:_ The engagement of the head is a highly reassuring clinical sign in obstetrics because it always excludes cephalopelvic disproportion at the pelvic brim, serving as the best clinical pelvimeter.

**CAUSES OF NON-ENGAGEMENT OF HEAD IN PRIMIGRAVIDA AT TERM** In a majority of primigravidae, the engagement of the fetal head occurs between 38 and 42 weeks, or even during the first stage of labor. However, if the head fails to engage in a primigravida even at the 38th week, the underlying causes must be thoroughly evaluated.

The causes of a non-engaged (floating) head in a primigravida at term are classified as follows:

**1. Fetal Causes**

- **Deflexed Head:** A deflexed attitude of the fetal head brings a larger anteroposterior diameter to engage, which makes it difficult to pass through the pelvic brim.
- **Big Baby / Macrosomia:** A large fetal head out of proportion to the maternal pelvis prevents engagement.
- **Hydrocephalus:** An abnormally enlarged fetal head prevents descent into the pelvic brim.

**2. Maternal Pelvic Causes**

- **Cephalopelvic Disproportion (CPD):** A contracted maternal pelvis in relation to the fetal head size.
- **High Pelvic Inclination:** A high angle of inclination prevents the uterine axis from coinciding with the axis of the pelvic inlet, thereby delaying or preventing engagement.

**3. Uterine and Amniotic Fluid Factors**

- **Polyhydramnios:** An excessive volume of liquor amnii increases fetal mobility and prevents the head from sinking into the pelvis.
- **Poor yielding of the lower uterine segment:** Poor formation or inadequate yielding of the lower uterine segment physically prevents the fetal head from sinking down into the pelvis.

**4. Space-Occupying Lesions in the Pelvis**

- **Placenta Previa:** Implantation of the placenta in the lower uterine segment mechanically obstructs the descent and engagement of the presenting part.
- **Pelvic Tumors:** Presence of tumors such as an ovarian tumor or a uterine fibroid occupying the pelvic cavity and preventing the head from entering the brim.

**5. Functional / Unexplained**

- **Functional:** In approximately 20% of cases, no obvious cause or pathology can be detected for the failure of the head to engage.


12. Management of eclampsia and its complications.

**MANAGEMENT OF ECLAMPSIA**

The principles of management of eclampsia involve general care, arresting convulsions, controlling hypertension, preventing complications, and expediting delivery. Eclampsia should be managed in a tertiary care hospital with multidisciplinary intensive care facilities.

**1. FIRST AID TREATMENT (Outside the Hospital / During Transport)**

- **Transfer:** The patient must be urgently shifted to a referral/tertiary care hospital equipped with obstetric and neonatal intensive care units.
- **Sedation & Anticonvulsant:** Administer Midazolam 1–2 mg IV (or Diazepam 10 mg IV) and a prophylactic loading dose of Magnesium sulfate (4 g IV + 10 g IM) before transfer.
- **Antihypertensive:** Administer Labetalol 20 mg IV to control severe hypertension.
- **During Transport:** The patient must be accompanied by a trained medical personnel, kept in the lateral decubitus position, and airways must be cleared.

**2. GENERAL & SUPPORTIVE MANAGEMENT IN HOSPITAL**

- **Positioning & Airway:** Place the patient in a left lateral recumbent position to prevent aspiration and aortocaval compression. Maintain oral airway; a mouth gag or tongue blade is inserted between the teeth during the premonitory stage to prevent tongue bite.
- **Oxygenation & Suction:** Frequent suctioning of vomitus and oral secretions is done. Administer oxygen via face mask at 8–10 L/minute until cyanosis disappears.
- **Catheterization & IV Lines:** Establish one or two wide-bore IV cannulas. Insert a self-retaining Foley catheter to continuously monitor hourly urine output and test for proteinuria.
- **Fluid Balance:** Start crystalloid infusion (Ringer's solution or normal saline). Total fluid should not exceed the previous 24 hours' urinary output plus 1000 mL (insensible loss), usually keeping total fluids ≤ 2 liters in 24 hours (or ≤ 125 mL/hour) to prevent pulmonary edema.
- **Monitoring:** Monitor pulse, BP, and respiratory rate half-hourly. Continuously monitor oxygen saturation (SpO2 >95%) using a pulse oximeter. Monitor fetal heart rate (FHR) continuously.

**3. SPECIFIC MANAGEMENT**

**A. Anticonvulsant Therapy** Magnesium sulfate ($MgSO_4$) is the drug of choice as it acts as a membrane stabilizer, neuroprotector, and effectively controls fits without depressing the mother or infant. It is superior to diazepam or phenytoin in reducing recurrent seizures and maternal death.

- **Pritchard Regimen (IM/IV):**
    - _Loading dose:_ 4 g (20% solution) IV slowly over 3–5 minutes, followed by 10 g (50% solution) deep IM (5 g in each buttock).
    - _Maintenance dose:_ 5 g (50%) IM 4 hourly in alternate buttocks.
- **Zuspan Regimen (IV):**
    - _Loading dose:_ 4–6 g IV slowly over 15–20 minutes.
    - _Maintenance dose:_ 1–2 g/hour IV infusion.
- **Monitoring $MgSO_4$ Toxicity:** Repeat doses are given _only_ if: (1) Patellar reflexes (knee jerks) are present, (2) Respiratory rate is >12/minute, and (3) Urine output is >30 mL/hour. Therapeutic serum level is 4–7 mEq/L.
- **Toxicity Management:** If toxicity occurs (respiratory depression, loss of reflexes, cardiac arrest), stop the drug and administer the antidote: **10% Calcium gluconate 1 g (10 mL) IV slowly**.
- **Recurrent Fits:** Give a further 2 g IV bolus of $MgSO_4$ over 5 minutes. If seizures persist, Midazolam (1-2 mg IV), Diazepam, or Thiopentone sodium with general anesthesia/intubation may be required.
- **Duration:** $MgSO_4$ is continued for 24 hours after the last seizure or delivery, whichever is later.

**B. Antihypertensive Therapy** If BP remains >160/110 mm Hg, antihypertensives are administered to maintain SBP at 140–160 mm Hg and DBP at 90–100 mm Hg.

- **Labetalol:** 10–20 mg IV every 10 minutes (maximum 300 mg).
- **Hydralazine:** 5 mg IV every 30 minutes (maximum 30 mg).
- **Nifedipine:** 10–20 mg orally.

**C. Management of Immediate Complications**

- **Pulmonary Edema:** Administer IV Furosemide (40 mg).
- **Prophylactic Antibiotics:** Ceftriaxone 1 g IV twice daily is given to prevent infection (e.g., aspiration pneumonia).

**4. OBSTETRIC MANAGEMENT** Delivery (termination of pregnancy) is the only definitive treatment and should be accomplished within 6–8 hours, even if fits are not controlled.

- **Fits Controlled & Patient in Labor:** If the cervix is favorable, augment with low rupture of membranes (ARM) and Oxytocin infusion. Epidural anesthesia can be used. The second stage is curtailed using prophylactic forceps or ventouse.
- **Fits Not Controlled / Unfavorable Cervix:** If fits are uncontrolled within 6–8 hours, or if there are obstetric indications (e.g., malpresentation, severe prematurity <30 weeks with unfavorable cervix, fetal distress), prompt **Cesarean Section** is indicated.
- **Postpartum Care:** Administer Oxytocin (10 IU IM or IV) strictly to prevent postpartum hemorrhage (avoid ergometrine as it causes severe hypertension).

---

**COMPLICATIONS OF ECLAMPSIA**

Eclampsia is a multisystem disorder associated with high maternal (2-20%) and perinatal (10-20%) mortality if unattended.

**1. Maternal Complications**

- **Neurological / Cerebral:** Cerebral edema, cerebral hemorrhage, occipital lobe ischemia, retinal detachment (causing dimness of vision or blindness), psychosis, and prolonged coma.
- **Respiratory / Pulmonary:** Pulmonary edema, aspiration pneumonia, Adult Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS), and pulmonary embolism.
- **Cardiovascular:** Acute left ventricular failure, postpartum cardiomyopathy, cardiopulmonary arrest, and shock.
- **Renal:** Acute kidney injury (AKI) / acute renal failure, oliguria, and anuria.
- **Hepatic:** Hepatic necrosis, subcapsular hematoma, and jaundice.
- **Hematological:** Thrombocytopenia, Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC), and HELLP syndrome.
- **Injuries:** Tongue bite (clonic stage), injuries from falling off the bed, and bed sores.
- **Obstetric / Puerperal:** Placental abruption, postpartum hemorrhage, and puerperal sepsis.

**2. Fetal and Neonatal Complications**

- **Prematurity:** Spontaneous or iatrogenic preterm birth is the major cause of mortality.
- **Intrauterine Asphyxia:** Caused by intense vasospasm, placental infarction, or retroplacental hemorrhage (abruption).
- **Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR):** Secondary to chronic placental insufficiency.
- **Drug effects:** Neonatal respiratory depression from anticonvulsants/sedatives.
- **Intrauterine Fetal Death (IUFD) / Stillbirth.**

13. Puerperium

**DEFINITION** Puerperium is defined as the period following childbirth during which the body tissues, especially the pelvic organs, revert back approximately to the prepregnant state both anatomically and physiologically,. The woman during this period is termed a puerpera.

**DURATION AND PHASES** It begins as soon as the placenta is expelled and lasts for approximately 6 weeks. It is arbitrarily divided into three phases:

- **Immediate:** Within the first 24 hours.
- **Early:** Up to 7 days.
- **Remote:** Up to 6 weeks.

**INVOLUTION OF THE UTERUS** Involution is the process whereby the genital organs revert back almost to the state as they were before pregnancy, predominantly observed in the body of the uterus,.

- **Anatomical Changes:** Immediately following delivery, the uterus measures about 20 x 12 x 7.5 cm and weighs about 1,000 g,. By the end of 6 weeks, its measurement is almost similar to the nonpregnant state, weighing about 60 g.
- **Physiological Changes:**
    - **Muscles:** There is no decrease in the number of muscle fibers, but there is a substantial reduction in the myometrial cell size,.
    - **Blood Vessels:** Changes are pronounced at the placental site. Arteries undergo constriction, thrombosis, hyalinization, and fibrinoid endarteritis,. Veins are obliterated by thrombosis and endophlebitis, followed by the growth of new blood vessels (angiogenesis),.
    - **Endometrium:** Regeneration of the endometrium starts by the 7th day and is restored by day 16, except at the placental site which takes about 6 weeks for complete regeneration,,.
- **Clinical Assessment of Involution:** The rate of involution is assessed clinically by noting the fundal height in relation to the symphysis pubis after emptying the bladder. Following delivery, the fundus lies about 13.5 cm above the symphysis pubis. It steadily decreases by 1.25 cm (0.5 inches) in 24 hours, and by the end of the 2nd week, the uterus becomes a pelvic organ.

**LOCHIA** Lochia is the vaginal discharge for the first fortnight during the puerperium, originating from the uterine body, cervix, and vagina,,.

- **Composition:** It consists of blood, shreds of fetal membranes, decidua, vernix caseosa, lanugo, and meconium.
- **Types based on color:**
    1. **Lochia rubra (Red):** Lasts for 1-4 days,.
    2. **Lochia serosa (Yellowish/Pink):** Lasts for 5-9 days,.
    3. **Lochia alba (Pale white):** Lasts for 10-15 days,.
- **Clinical Importance:**
    - _Odor:_ An offensive fishy smell is normal, but a malodorous discharge indicates infection or retained vaginal plugs,.
    - _Amount:_ Scanty or absent lochia signifies infection or lochiometra; excessive amount indicates infection.
    - _Color:_ Persistence of red color beyond the normal limit signifies subinvolution or retained bits of conceptus.

**GENERAL PHYSIOLOGICAL CHANGES**

- **Pulse and Temperature:** Pulse rate may be slightly raised for a few hours post-delivery but settles to normal by the second day. Temperature should not exceed 37.2°C (99°F) within the first 24 hours. A slight reactionary rise on the 3rd day may occur due to breast engorgement, lasting no more than 24 hours,.
- **Urinary Tract:** The bladder mucosa becomes edematous and hyperemic. Bladder capacity increases, often with decreased sensation, leading to a risk of overdistention and retention,. Pronounced diuresis occurs on the 2nd or 3rd day.
- **Gastrointestinal Tract and Weight Loss:** Increased thirst and constipation are common,. Aside from the weight loss of delivery (5-6 kg), a further loss of about 2 kg occurs during the puerperium chiefly caused by diuresis.
- **Blood and Circulation:** Leukocytosis up to 25,000/mm³ occurs following delivery, likely due to the stress of labor. A hypercoagulable state persists with increases in fibrinogen, factor VIII, and platelets, which increases the risk of thrombosis,.
- **Ovarian Function:** In non-lactating women, menstruation usually returns by the 12th week (mean time 7-9 weeks). In lactating mothers, the hypothalamo-pituitary-ovarian axis remains inactive for a longer period due to prolactin secretion, leading to lactational amenorrhea.

**MANAGEMENT OF NORMAL PUERPERIUM** The principles of management are: (1) To restore the health of the mother, (2) To prevent infection, (3) To promote exclusive breastfeeding and care of the breasts, and (4) To counsel for contraception,.

- **Immediate Attention:** Close observation for at least 1 hour after delivery (the fourth stage of labor) to monitor vitals, uterine retraction, and vaginal bleeding,. Emotional support is essential,.
- **Rest and Early Ambulation:** Early ambulation provides a sense of well-being, promotes normal voiding/bowel function, facilitates uterine drainage to hasten involution, and crucially lessens the risk of puerperal venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism,.
- **Diet:** A normal diet of her choice is given. Lactating mothers require high calories (an extra 750 kcal) and adequate fluid intake.
- **Asepsis:** Strict asepsis and proper perineal wound dressing are required to prevent nosocomial and puerperal infections.
- **Postpartum Exercises:** Recommended to improve the muscle tone of the abdominal, pelvic floor, and perineal muscles.
- **Contraception Counseling:** Nonlactating women should initiate contraceptive measures after 3 weeks, while lactating women should start 3 months after delivery.

**ABNORMALITIES OF PUERPERIUM (COMPLICATIONS)** Deviations from the normal course can lead to various complications:

1. **Puerperal Pyrexia and Sepsis:** Temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or more on two separate occasions 24 hours apart (excluding the first 24 hours). Sepsis involves genital tract infections like endometritis, endomyometritis, or pelvic cellulitis,,.
2. **Subinvolution:** Impaired retardation of uterine involution, commonly caused by retained bits of tissue, endometritis, or excessive enlargement (e.g., twins),.
3. **Urinary Complications:** Postpartum urinary retention and urinary tract infections (UTI),.
4. **Breast Complications:** Breast engorgement, cracked/retracted nipples, mastitis, and breast abscess,.
5. **Thromboembolic Disorders:** Puerperal venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism,.
6. **Psychiatric Disorders:** Postpartum blues, postpartum depression, and puerperal psychosis,.

7. Effacement of cervix

**DEFINITION** Effacement (also known as 'taking up' of the cervix) is the process by which the muscular fibers of the cervix are pulled upward and merge with the fibers of the lower uterine segment. This process leads to the progressive thinning and shortening of the cervix.

**CLINICAL FEATURES AND PHYSIOLOGY**

- **Timing:** It typically occurs during the first stage of labor, but can occur even before the onset of labor in primigravidae.
- **Mechanism:** The process of effacement causes the expulsion of the cervical mucus plug, which presents clinically as 'show'.
- **Progression:** The anterior lip of the cervix is generally the last part to be effaced.

**DIFFERENCES BASED ON PARITY** Understanding the difference in effacement between primigravidae and multiparae is crucial:

- **In Primigravidae:** Effacement usually _precedes_ dilatation of the cervix. The cervix may become completely effaced, feeling as thin as a piece of paper, even before it has dilated enough to admit a fingertip.
- **In Multiparae:** Effacement and dilatation occur _simultaneously_. This combined process is often more abrupt following the rupture of the membranes.

**CLINICAL ASSESSMENT AND EXPRESSION**

- Effacement is determined clinically during a vaginal examination by assessing the length of the cervical canal in the vagina.
- **Percentage:** It is expressed in terms of percentage (e.g., 25%, 50%, or 100%).
    - **100% Effaced:** The cervix is completely taken up and is less than 0.25 cm thick.
    - **Ripe Cervix:** A cervix is considered clinically "ripe" when it is 80% effaced, which corresponds to a length of less than 1.5 cm.
- **'Rim' of Cervix:** This clinical term is used when the depth of the cervical tissue surrounding the os measures about 0.5 to 1 cm.

15. Couvelaire uterus
**SYNONYM** Uteroplacental apoplexy.

**DEFINITION** Couvelaire uterus is a pathological entity first described by Couvelaire, met with in association with a severe form of concealed abruptio placentae. It is characterized by the massive intravasation (percolation) of blood into the uterine musculature extending all the way up to the serous coat.

**DIAGNOSIS** The condition cannot be diagnosed clinically; it can only be definitively diagnosed by direct visualization during a laparotomy or cesarean section.

**PATHOLOGICAL FEATURES** **Gross / Naked Eye Appearance:**

- **Color:** The uterus takes on a characteristic dark port-wine color, which may be patchy or diffuse.
- **Spread:** The discoloration tends to occur initially at the uterine cornu before spreading to other areas, especially over the placental site.
- **Hemorrhages:** Subperitoneal petechial hemorrhages are noted under the uterine peritoneum, which may also extend into the broad ligament.
- **Associated Findings:** There may be free blood present in the peritoneal cavity or the formation of a broad ligament hematoma.

**Microscopic Appearance:**

- **Muscle Necrosis:** The uterine muscle fibers over the affected area undergo necrosis.
- **Infiltration:** There is marked infiltration of blood and fluid between the muscle bundles. This muscular dissociation occurs predominantly in the middle and outer muscle layers of the uterus.
- **Vascular Changes:** The blood vessels exhibit acute degenerative changes accompanied by thrombosis.
- **Serosal Splitting:** On occasion, the serosa may split, which allows blood to enter the peritoneal cavity directly.

**CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE & MANAGEMENT**

- **Uterine Contractility:** Despite the extensive infiltration of blood and muscle necrosis, the myometrial hematoma rarely interferes with the uterus's ability to contract following delivery.
- **Role of Hysterectomy:** Because the uterus typically retains its ability to contract, the mere visual presence of a Couvelaire uterus during a cesarean section is **not** an indication _per se_ for performing a hysterectomy. The uterus should be conserved unless there is intractable postpartum hemorrhage that fails to respond to conservative measures.
- **Complication:** In rare instances, a Couvelaire uterus can lead to the spontaneous rupture of an otherwise uninjured uterus during pregnancy.


16. Neonatal jaundice.
**DEFINITION** Neonatal jaundice is defined as the yellow discoloration of the skin and mucous membranes caused by the accumulation of excess bilirubin in the tissues and plasma, clinically evident when the serum bilirubin level exceeds 7 mg/dL. A value greater than 15 mg/dL is considered severe. It develops in about 80% of term newborns and most preterm newborns.

**BILIRUBIN METABOLISM IN NEONATES**

- **Source:** Breakdown of red blood cells (RBCs) in the reticuloendothelial system yields biliverdin, iron (reutilized), and carbon dioxide. Biliverdin is reduced to bilirubin by biliverdin reductase (1 g of hemoglobin produces 35 mg of bilirubin).
- **Transport and Conjugation:** Unconjugated bilirubin bounds to serum albumin and is transported to liver cells, where it is taken up by cytoplasmic ligandin (Y protein). It is conjugated by the enzyme Uridine Diphosphoglucoronate Glucoronosyl Transferase (UGT1A1) into water-soluble Conjugated Bilirubin (CB).
- **Excretion:** Conjugated bilirubin is excreted via the gastrointestinal tract in the stool. However, neonatal intestinal $\beta$-glucuronidase can convert CB back to unconjugated bilirubin, which is reabsorbed into the liver (enterohepatic circulation).

**CLASSIFICATION AND TYPES** Neonatal jaundice is broadly classified into Physiological and Pathological (Non-physiological) jaundice.

**1. Physiological Jaundice**

- **Onset and Duration:** Usually appears on the 2nd or 3rd day of life and disappears by the 7th to 10th day (slightly later in premature neonates).
- **Bilirubin Levels:** In term infants, it reaches 3–5 mg/dL on the 3rd day, up to a normal limit of 12 mg/dL. In preterm infants, the peak level can safely reach 12–15 mg/dL in the first week.
- **Causes of Excess Bilirubin:**
    - Increased red cell volume and shorter RBC lifespan (90 days vs. 120 days in adults).
    - Transient decreased conjugation due to low UGT activity.
    - Decreased hepatic excretion and decreased liver cell uptake due to low ligandin levels.
    - Increased enterohepatic circulation due to decreased gut motility and high intestinal $\beta$-glucuronidase levels.
- **Aggravating Labor Factors:** Birth trauma, use of oxytocin, hypoxic-ischemic insults, and delayed cord clamping.
- **Treatment:** No specific treatment is required; frequent breastfeeding is advised. In premature babies, phototherapy or phenobarbitone may be used if levels rise near critical thresholds.

**2. Pathological Jaundice (Non-Physiological)**

- **Absolute Diagnostic Criteria:**
    - Jaundice appearing within 24 hours of birth.
    - Bilirubin level increasing at a rate of >5 mg/dL/day or >0.2 mg/dL/hour.
    - Conjugated bilirubin >2 mg/dL.
    - Clinical jaundice persisting >1 week in a term infant or >2 weeks in a preterm infant.
    - Jaundice requiring phototherapy or presenting in a sick baby.
- **Causes of Unconjugated Hyperbilirubinemia:**
    - Hemolytic disease (Rh incompatibility, ABO incompatibility).
    - RBC membrane defects (congenital spherocytosis) or enzyme defects (G6PD deficiency).
    - Extravasated blood (cephalhematoma, intraventricular hemorrhage).
    - Sepsis, iatrogenic causes (drugs), hypothyroidism, and infant of a diabetic mother.
    - _Breast Milk Jaundice:_ Caused by inhibition of glucuronyl transferase by $3\alpha, 20\beta$-pregnanediol and excess fatty acids in breast milk. Peaks at 20-30 mg/dL by the 14th day. Temporary withdrawal of breastfeeding resolves it.
- **Causes of Conjugated Hyperbilirubinemia:** Neonatal hepatitis, TORCH infections, biliary atresia, trisomy 21/18, cystic fibrosis, and metabolic disorders like galactosemia.

**CLINICAL EVALUATION AND DIAGNOSIS**

- **Clinical Assessment:** Visual inspection by blanching the skin; dermal icterus progresses in a cephalocaudal direction. According to Kramer's criteria, dermal zones correspond to serum bilirubin: Zone 1 (Face) = 5 mg/dL, Zone 2 = 10 mg/dL, Zone 3 = 12 mg/dL, Zone 4 = 15 mg/dL, Zone 5 (Palms/soles) = >15 mg/dL. Transcutaneous Bilirubin (TcB) devices are also used.
- **Laboratory Investigations (Required if bilirubin >12 mg/dL):**
    - Blood group (ABO, Rh) of mother and infant, and Direct Coombs' test.
    - Total, conjugated, and unconjugated bilirubin levels.
    - Complete hemogram including reticulocyte count and peripheral smear (to detect hemolysis, sepsis, or spherocytosis).
    - Serum albumin (to assess bilirubin binding capacity).
    - Other tests: G6PD screening, thyroid function tests, liver function tests, and urine for reducing substances (to rule out galactosemia).

**COMPLICATION: KERNICTERUS** Kernicterus (Bilirubin Encephalopathy) is a chronic, permanent, and often fatal sequela of bilirubin toxicity developing in the first year of life.

- **Pathology:** High levels of unbound, free unconjugated bilirubin cross the blood-brain barrier, causing permanent damage to the basal ganglia, cerebellum, cranial nerve nuclei, brainstem, and hippocampus.
- **Critical Level:** The critical level of bilirubin to cause kernicterus in a healthy term infant is >20 mg/dL (340 $\mu$mol/L). Hypoxia, acidosis, hypothermia, sepsis, and prematurity lower the threshold for toxicity.
- **Clinical Features:** Lethargy, hypotonia, poor feeding, loss of neonatal reflexes, oculogyric crisis, choreoathetoid cerebral palsy, sensorineural hearing loss, limitation of upward gaze, and dental enamel dysplasia.

**MANAGEMENT OF NEONATAL JAUNDICE** The primary objective is to prevent bilirubin encephalopathy (kernicterus) and rapidly reduce serum bilirubin levels.

**1. Phototherapy**

- **Mechanism:** Exposure to blue or blue-green light (460-490 nm wavelength) degrades bilirubin via photo-oxidation and structural isomerization into a water-soluble, less toxic polar isomer (lumirubin) that is readily excreted in bile and urine.
- **Indications:** Used in moderate cases (e.g., when bilirubin rises >12 mg/dL) guided by hour-specific nomograms taking gestational age and neurotoxicity risk factors into account.
- **Procedure & Precautions:** The maximum surface area of the infant is exposed, and eyes and genitals are shielded. Continuous hydration must be maintained (breastfeeding or IV fluids) to counter increased insensible water loss.
- **Side Effects:** Watery diarrhea, skin rashes, dehydration, "bronze baby syndrome" (dark brown skin discoloration), hypocalcemia, and retinal damage. Discontinued once levels drop safely below threshold.

**2. Exchange Transfusion**

- **Indications:** Rapid rise of bilirubin >1 mg/dL/hour despite phototherapy, total bilirubin $\ge$ 20 mg/dL, or cord blood bilirubin >4 mg/dL with Hb <11 g/dL in severe Hemolytic Disease of the Newborn.
- **Mechanism:** A double-volume exchange (approx. 160 mL/kg) replaces 85% of the circulating RBCs and reduces the bilirubin level by 50%, while also removing circulating maternal antibodies.
- **Blood selection:** Fresh (<7 days old) Rh-negative whole blood matching the baby’s ABO group (or 'O' group).
- **Complications:** Air embolism, thrombosis, hypervolemia, arrhythmias, hypocalcemia, hyperkalemia, and necrotizing enterocolitis.

**3. Adjuvant Therapies**

- **Intravenous Immune Globulin (IVIG):** Administered (0.5 to 1 g/kg over 2 hours) in isoimmune hemolytic disease (Rh/ABO incompatibility) to block Fc-mediated RBC destruction.
- **Prophylaxis:** Administration of Vitamin K and antibiotics (if sepsis is suspected). Encouraging early and frequent exclusive breastfeeding minimizes hypoglycemia and facilitates biliary excretion.

17. Discordant twins
**DEFINITION** Discordant twin growth refers to a significant disparity in the size and weight of the two fetuses in a twin pregnancy. While some degree of discordant growth is considered normal in dizygotic twins, true pathological discordance involves an Estimated Fetal Weight (EFW) difference of 25% or more between the fetuses. Selective Growth Restriction (sGR) is the specific clinical term used when the EFW difference is >20% between the fetuses.

**INCIDENCE**

- Discordant twin growth is observed in approximately 20% of twin pregnancies.
- It is seen in over 50% of monochorionic twins that are complicated by Twin-Twin Transfusion Syndrome (TTTS).

**ETIOLOGY AND CAUSES** Pathological discordance in twins can arise due to several maternal, fetal, or placental factors:

- **Twin-Twin Transfusion Syndrome (TTTS):** Commonly seen in monochorionic diamniotic (MCDA) twins, leading to a donor twin (IUGR, oligohydramnios) and a recipient twin (macrosomia, polyhydramnios).
- **Placental Insufficiency:** Unequal sharing of the placenta or unequal placental reserve.
- **Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR):** Affecting only one fetus.
- **Fetal Anomalies:** Structural or chromosomal malformations occurring exclusively in one fetus.
- **Iatrogenic:** Discordant fetal growth can occur as a complication following selective fetal termination procedures.
- **Twin Anemia-Polycythemia Sequence (TAPS):** A related condition in monochorionic twins causing chronic and severe hemoglobin discordance (usually an Hb concentration difference > 5 g%) without the typical amniotic fluid discordance of TTTS.

**CLINICAL STAGING (Based on Selective Growth Restriction - sGR)** In cases of sGR, staging is performed based on the severity of the affection, primarily determined by umbilical artery (UA) Doppler velocimetry:

- **Stage I:** Characterized by growth discordance, but with normal, positive diastolic velocities maintained in both fetal umbilical arteries.
- **Stage II:** Characterized by growth discordance accompanied by Absent or Reversed End-Diastolic Velocities (AREDV) in one or both fetuses.
- **Stage III:** Characterized by growth discordance with cyclical umbilical artery diastolic waveforms over several minutes (intermittent AREDV or iAREDV).

**MANAGEMENT AND TIMING OF DELIVERY** The management depends on the severity of the discordance, Doppler findings, and gestational age:

- **Stage I sGR:** Expectant management with close surveillance is done, and delivery is preferably planned between 34 and 36 weeks of gestation.
- **Stage II and Stage III sGR:** Due to the high risk of fetal compromise, delivery is planned earlier, typically by 32 weeks of gestation. Antenatal corticosteroids must be administered prior to delivery to accelerate fetal lung maturation.
- **Mode of Delivery:** Discordance influences the mode of delivery. Cesarean section is specifically indicated for the delivery of the second twin if Twin B is larger (EFW >20% than Twin A) and is in a noncephalic presentation.


18. Types of caesarean section, indications for upper segment caesarean section

**TYPES OF CESAREAN SECTION**

Cesarean section operations are broadly classified based on the anatomical site of the uterine incision:

**1. Lower Segment Cesarean Section (LSCS):** This is the standard and most commonly performed method where the extraction of the baby is done through an incision made in the lower uterine segment through a transperitoneal approach. Based on the exact uterine incision, it is further divided into:

- **Low Transverse Incision:** The most commonly used incision (90%) because it is easy to repair, has less blood loss, and carries a lower risk of scar rupture in subsequent pregnancies.
- **Lower Vertical Incision:** Made vertically in the lower segment, which can be extended upwards if needed.
- **'J' Incision:** An upward vertical extension of the initial transverse incision.
- **Inverted 'T' Incision:** An upward vertical extension originating from the midpoint of the transverse incision.

**2. Classical or Upper Segment Cesarean Section:** In this operation, the baby is extracted through a longitudinal incision made in the upper segment (body) of the uterus.

**3. Extraperitoneal Cesarean Section:** The lower segment is approached extraperitoneally by dissecting through the space of Retzius. It was practiced in the past for severely infected cases, but with potent antibiotics, it is rarely performed today and is largely obsolete.

---

**INDICATIONS FOR UPPER SEGMENT (CLASSICAL) CESAREAN SECTION**

In modern obstetrics, the indications for classical cesarean section are very limited. The operation is only performed under forced circumstances where a lower segment approach is either difficult, risky, or impossible.

**A. When the Lower Uterine Segment (LUS) approach is difficult:**

- **Dense Adhesions:** Presence of dense adhesions limiting access to the lower uterine segment due to previous abdominal operations.
- **Severe Pelvic Deformity:** Severe contracted pelvis (e.g., osteomalacic or rachitic pelvis) associated with a pendulous abdomen.
- **Prematurity:** Preterm delivery (e.g., 26-28 weeks) where the lower uterine segment is poorly formed and inadequately developed.

**B. When the Lower Uterine Segment (LUS) approach is risky:**

- **Pelvic Tumors:** Presence of a big fibroid (myoma) located anteriorly on the lower segment obstructing access. Attempting myomectomy here increases blood loss and risks ending in hysterectomy.
- **Carcinoma Cervix:** To prevent the dissemination of malignant growth and minimize postoperative sepsis.
- **Previous Fistula Repair:** Following a previous repair of a high vesicovaginal fistula (VVF).

**C. Placental and Fetal Factors:**

- **Placenta Previa:** Complete anterior placenta previa with highly engorged vessels in the lower segment, which poses a severe risk of hemorrhage.
- **Morbidly Adherent Placenta:** Placenta Accreta Spectrum (PAS), where cutting into the placental site must be avoided.
- **Transverse Lie:** Especially in a dorso-inferior position where extraction through the lower segment is mechanically extremely difficult.

**D. Other Special Circumstances:**

- **Planned Cesarean Hysterectomy:** When removal of the uterus is already decided.
- **Perimortem Cesarean Section:** An extreme emergency procedure performed on a dying woman or a woman who has suffered total cardiopulmonary arrest. It is done rapidly to achieve a live birth within 5 minutes of maternal death.

19. Types of pelvis
**CLASSIFICATION OF FEMALE PELVIS** On the basis of the shape of the pelvic inlet, the female pelvis is divided into four parent types. In clinical practice, intermediate forms with a combination of features (e.g., gyne-android) are commonly found, where the first part of the nomenclature relates to the posterior segment and the second part to the anterior segment.

The four parent types and their incidence are:

1. **Gynecoid (50%)**
2. **Anthropoid (25%)**
3. **Android (20%)**
4. **Platypelloid (5%)**

---

**1. GYNECOID PELVIS (50%)** This is the classic normal female pelvis.

- **Inlet:** Round in shape. Both anterior and posterior segments are spacious.
- **Sacrum:** Well curved from above downwards and side to side; Sacral angle > 90°.
- **Sacrosciatic notch:** Wide and shallow.
- **Sidewalls:** Straight or slightly divergent.
- **Ischial Spines:** Not prominent.
- **Pubic arch & Subpubic angle:** Curved arch with a wide subpubic angle of about 85°.
- **Obstetric Outcome:** Highly favorable. Head engages in transverse or oblique diameter. Internal rotation is easily anterior. No difficulty occurs during delivery.

**2. ANTHROPOID PELVIS (25%)**

- **Inlet:** Anteroposteriorly oval. The anteroposterior diameter is longer than the transverse diameter.
- **Sacrum:** Long, narrow, and straight. Inclined posteriorly (Sacral angle > 90°).
- **Sacrosciatic notch:** More wide and shallow.
- **Sidewalls:** Straight or divergent.
- **Ischial Spines:** Not prominent.
- **Pubic arch & Subpubic angle:** Long and curved arch; slightly narrow subpubic angle.
- **Obstetric Outcome:** Head typically engages in the anteroposterior diameter (Direct occipitoanterior or occipitoposterior position). Non-rotation is common, leading to a higher incidence of face-to-pubis delivery.

**3. ANDROID PELVIS (20%)** This resembles the male pelvis and is a common cause of obstructed labor.

- **Inlet:** Triangular or heart-shaped. Short posterior segment with a narrow anterior segment.
- **Sacrum:** Short, straight, and inclined forwards. Sacral angle is < 90°.
- **Sacrosciatic notch:** Narrow and deep.
- **Sidewalls:** Convergent (funnel-shaped cavity).
- **Ischial Spines:** Prominent.
- **Pubic arch & Subpubic angle:** Long and straight arch; narrow subpubic angle.
- **Obstetric Outcome:** Unfavorable. Engagement occurs in transverse or oblique diameter. Anterior rotation is difficult and early arrest above the ischial spines (e.g., deep transverse arrest) is common. Delivery is difficult with an increased chance of perineal injuries.

**4. PLATYPELLOID PELVIS (5%)** This is a flattened pelvis.

- **Inlet:** Transversely oval. Both anterior and posterior segments are short and flat.
- **Sacrum:** Normal curve but inclined posteriorly. Sacral angle > 90°.
- **Sacrosciatic notch:** Slightly narrow and small.
- **Sidewalls:** Divergent.
- **Ischial Spines:** Not prominent.
- **Pubic arch & Subpubic angle:** Short and curved arch; very wide subpubic angle (> 90°).
- **Obstetric Outcome:** Head engages in the transverse diameter. Engagement is delayed and difficult, occurring by exaggerated parietal presentation (asynclitism). Internal anterior rotation occurs very late in the perineum, but there is no difficulty in actual delivery once rotation happens.

---

**ABNORMAL / DEFORMED PELVIS (CONTRACTED PELVIS)** Due to nutritional, developmental, or disease factors, the pelvis can be grossly deformed, altering the size or shape enough to affect the normal mechanism of labor.

- **Rachitic Flat Pelvis:** Sacral promontory is pushed downwards and forwards producing a "reniform" (kidney) shaped inlet. The anteroposterior diameter is markedly shortened.
- **Osteomalacic Pelvis:** Due to softening of bones (calcium/vitamin D deficiency), the lateral walls are pushed inwards, making the inlet triradiate or beak-shaped.
- **Asymmetrical / Obliquely Contracted Pelvis:**
    - **Naegele's Pelvis:** Arrested development of one ala of the sacrum.
    - **Scoliotic Pelvis:** Scoliosis pushes the acetabulum inwards on the weight-bearing side, causing oblique asymmetry.
- **Robert's Pelvis:** Extremely rare transversely contracted pelvis where both sacral alae are absent and fused with the innominate bones.
- **Kyphotic Pelvis:** Sacrum is tilted backwards in the upper part and forwards in the lower part, causing extreme funneling with a diminished outlet.



20. Deep transverse arrest

**DEEP TRANSVERSE ARREST (DTA)**

**Definition** Deep Transverse Arrest (DTA) is defined as an arrest disorder in the second stage of labor where the fetal head is deep into the pelvic cavity, the sagittal suture is positioned in the transverse (bispinous) diameter, and there is no progress in the descent of the head even after ½ to 1 hour following full dilatation of the cervix.

**Mechanism and Pathogenesis**

- DTA is a specific type of arrested occiput posterior position.
- During the mechanism of labor in an occiput posterior position, the occiput may undergo an incomplete internal rotation of only 1/8th of a circle anteriorly.
- Because of this short rotation, the sagittal suture comes to lie in the transverse bispinous diameter. Further anterior rotation ceases, and the head is arrested in this deep transverse plane.
- Alternatively, it can also result from the non-rotation of a commonly primary occiput transverse position.

**Causes** The arrest in rotation and descent usually arises from a combination of maternal and fetal factors:

- **Faulty Pelvic Architecture:** Abnormal pelvic shapes, such as an android pelvis or mid-pelvic contraction, physically restrict the rotation of the fetal head.
- **Inadequate Uterine Contractions:** Weak uterine action or myometrial fatigue fails to provide the necessary driving force for descent and internal rotation.
- **Deflexion of the Fetal Head:** Moderate to extreme deflexion presents larger diameters of the fetal skull to the maternal pelvis, obstructing proper rotation.
- **Epidural Analgesia:** This can lead to the relaxation of the pelvic floor muscles, decreasing the resistance that is physiologically required to guide the internal rotation of the fetal head.

**Diagnosis** The diagnosis is made clinically during the second stage of labor when there is secondary arrest (failure of descent or rotation).

- **Abdominal Examination:** The head is engaged but descent is halted.
- **Vaginal Examination (P/V):**
    - The cervix is fully dilated.
    - The head is deep in the pelvic cavity, situated at or below the level of the ischial spines.
    - The sagittal suture is distinctly palpated in the transverse bispinous diameter.
    - Prolonged arrest often leads to the development of a large caput succedaneum and severe moulding, which can obscure landmarks.

**Management** If there is a failure to progress despite adequate uterine contractions for ½–1 hour in the second stage, operative intervention is mandatory. The choice of intervention depends heavily on clinical assessment, station of the head, and pelvic adequacy.

- **Ventouse (Vacuum Extraction):** This is a highly suitable method for arresting occiput transverse positions. It assists in descent and allows the fetal head to auto-rotate as it comes down the birth canal.
- **Rotational Forceps:** DTA with asynclitism of the fetal head is one of the classic indications for the use of rotational forceps (such as Keilland's forceps). The "wandering method" is a popular technique for application, where the anterior (superior) blade is inserted first along the side wall of the pelvis.
- **Cesarean Section:** This is the safest and most definitive line of management if there is clinical evidence of cephalopelvic disproportion (CPD), if the head remains high (above the ischial spines), or if there is non-reassuring fetal status/fetal distress. It is also indicated if a trial of ventouse or forceps fails.

21. Bandl’s ring
**SYNONYMS** Pathological Retraction Ring, Tonic Uterine Contraction and Retraction.

**DEFINITION** Bandl's ring is a pathological circular groove encircling the uterus, formed at the junction between the active, tonically contracted, and thickened upper uterine segment and the distended, thinned-out, passive lower uterine segment. It is the end result of tonic uterine contraction and retraction due to severe obstructed labor.

**PATHOGENESIS**

- **Response to Obstruction:** In obstructed labor, there is a gradual increase in the intensity, duration, and frequency of uterine contractions.
- **Tonic Contraction:** The relaxation phase becomes progressively shorter, ultimately leading to a state of continuous tonic contraction, though retraction continues.
- **Formation of the Ring:** As the upper segment retracts and becomes thicker, the lower segment elongates and becomes progressively thinner to accommodate the fetus being driven downwards. The distinct circular junction between these two segments is the Bandl's ring.
- **Progression:** As obstruction persists, the Bandl's ring progressively moves higher up towards the umbilicus.
- **Effect of Parity:**
    - _In Primigravidae:_ Further retraction usually ceases in response to obstruction, leading to a state of uterine exhaustion.
    - _In Multiparae:_ Retraction continues relentlessly with progressive circumferential dilatation and stretching of the lower segment until it ultimately gives way and ruptures.

**CLINICAL FEATURES** **Symptoms:**

- The patient is in extreme agony from continuous pain and discomfort, and becomes highly restless.
- Features of maternal exhaustion, dehydration, and ketoacidosis appear early.

**Signs (Abdominal Examination):**

- **Uterine feel:** The upper segment is hard, tense, and tender. The lower segment is distended and highly tender.
- **Palpability of the Ring:** The pathological retraction ring is distinctly felt per abdomen as a groove placed obliquely across the uterus. _(Note: This differentiates it from a constriction ring, which is not felt abdominally)._
- **Round Ligaments:** They are taut and tender.
- **Fetal Parts & FHS:** Fetal parts are difficult to palpate due to the tense uterus. Fetal Heart Sound (FHS) is usually absent due to severe fetal anoxia.

**Signs (Vaginal Examination):**

- The lower segment is intensely pressed by the forcibly driven presenting part.
- Classic features of obstructed labor (e.g., severe caput/moulding, edematous vagina) are present.

**COMPLICATIONS**

- **Maternal:** Severe exhaustion, dehydration, ketoacidosis, and sepsis. If neglected, it invariably leads to rupture of the uterus (especially in multiparae) and maternal death.
- **Fetal:** Severe fetal hypoxia, anoxia, and early intrauterine fetal death.

**MANAGEMENT**

- **Prevention:** Strict use of the partograph for labor monitoring, early diagnosis of malpresentations or cephalopelvic disproportion, and timely intervention with Cesarean section can completely prevent this condition.
- **Definitive Treatment:** Relief of the obstruction is an absolute emergency. Immediate **Cesarean delivery** is the treatment of choice, provided rupture of the uterus has been carefully excluded. Rapid correction of dehydration and ketoacidosis must be initiated simultaneously.


22. Non-stress test
**NON-STRESS TEST (NST)**

**Definition and Principle**

- The Non-Stress Test (NST) is a primary, non-invasive biophysical screening test used for the antenatal assessment of fetal well-being.
- It involves the continuous electronic monitoring of the Fetal Heart Rate (FHR) along with the simultaneous recording of fetal movements using cardiotocography.
- **Principle:** The test is based on the physiological association between FHR accelerations and fetal movements. These accelerations are presumably reflex-mediated, and their presence reliably indicates a healthy, well-oxygenated fetus.
- Importantly, the test is highly valuable for identifying fetal _wellness_ rather than fetal illness.

**Timing and Indications**

- Testing is generally initiated after 30 weeks of gestation.
- It is specifically indicated for high-risk pregnancies, such as those complicated by Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR) or diabetes mellitus, where it should be performed twice weekly.

**Procedure and Interpretation** The FHR is monitored for a standard observation period of 20 minutes.

- **Reactive (Reassuring) NST:**
    - Defined as the presence of two or more accelerations of the FHR within a 20-minute observation period.
    - The accelerations must peak at more than 15 beats per minute above the baseline and last for longer than 15 seconds.
    - A reactive pattern is highly reassuring and is associated with a low perinatal death rate of about 5 per 1,000.
- **Non-reactive (Non-reassuring) NST:**
    - Defined as the absence of any fetal reactivity or accelerations within the testing window.
    - This is non-reassuring and is associated with a significantly higher perinatal death rate of about 40 per 1,000, necessitating further fetal evaluation.

**Role of Vibroacoustic Stimulation (VAS)**

- A non-reactive NST may sometimes simply be due to the fetus being in a quiet (non-REM) sleep state.
- Vibroacoustic Stimulation (VAS) is a harmless adjunct used to change the fetal sleep state from quiet to active (REM) sleep.
- If the NST becomes reactive following VAS, it indicates a healthy fetus.

**Reliability and Clinical Value**

- **Low False-Negative Rate:** The test is highly reliable when reactive, possessing a false-negative rate of less than 1% (0.5%).
- **High False-Positive Rate:** The test has a high false-positive rate of 50% or more. Therefore, a non-reactive NST does not definitively diagnose fetal compromise but indicates the need for further testing.
- **Integral Component of BPP:** The NST serves as a core parameter in the Fetal Biophysical Profile (BPP) and the Modified Biophysical Profile (which combines the NST with the sonographic measurement of the Amniotic Fluid Index).
