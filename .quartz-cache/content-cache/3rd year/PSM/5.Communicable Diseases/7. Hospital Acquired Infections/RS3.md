1. HAIs
	**HOSPITAL-ACQUIRED INFECTIONS (NOSOCOMIAL INFECTIONS)**
	
	**1. DEFINITION**
	
	- Nosocomial or hospital-acquired infections are defined as infections acquired during hospital care which were not present or incubating at the time of admission.
	- Typically, infections occurring more than 48 hours after admission are considered nosocomial.
	- The term also encompasses infections acquired in the hospital but appearing after discharge, as well as occupational infections acquired by the facility's staff.
	
	**2. EPIDEMIOLOGICAL DETERMINANTS (THE CHAIN OF INFECTION)** Hospital-acquired infections can be systematically analysed from three angles: Sources, Routes of spread, and Recipients.
	
	**A. Sources of Infection**
	
	- **Patients:** Patients suffering from infectious diseases, discharging wounds, infected skin lesions (boils, bedsores), and urinary tract infections act as common sources.
	- **Hospital Staff:** Doctors, nurses, and ward boys who come in close contact with patients can be important sources or carriers (e.g., carrying _Staphylococcus aureus_ in the nose or skin, _Haemolytic streptococci_ in the throat, or _Salmonella_ in the gut).
	- **Environment:** The hospital environment, including dust, linen, bedclothes, furniture, sinks, door handles, and the air itself, is heavily laden with microorganisms.
	
	**B. Routes of Spread**
	
	- **Direct Contact:** Transfer of organisms directly from the hands of health care staff to a susceptible patient.
	- **Droplet Infection:** Release of infected droplets from the nose and throat during coughing or sneezing.
	- **Air-borne & Dust:** Release of hospital dust containing pathogens into the air.
	- **Hospital Procedures (Iatrogenic):** Transmission through invasive procedures like catheterization, intravenous lines, unsterile surgical instruments, dressings, bedpans, etc.
	
	**C. Susceptible Recipients**
	
	- All hospitalized patients are potential recipients, but susceptibility is greatly increased in severely ill patients, those on immunosuppressive or corticosteroid therapy, and those in intensive care units, urological wards, geriatric wards, and special baby care units.
	
	**3. COMMON TYPES OF NOSOCOMIAL INFECTIONS** The four most common types of hospital-acquired infections, primarily associated with invasive medical devices or procedures, include:
	
	1. **Urinary Tract Infections (UTI):** Defined as a positive urine culture with at least $10^5$ bacteria/ml, often secondary to prolonged catheterization.
	2. **Surgical Site Infections:** Any purulent discharge, abscess, or spreading cellulitis at a surgical site within a month post-operation.
	3. **Respiratory Infections (Pneumonia):** Marked by respiratory symptoms (cough, purulent sputum) and new infiltrates on a chest radiograph, frequently associated with ventilators.
	4. **Vascular Catheter Infections / Septicaemia:** Characterized by inflammation, lymphangitis, or purulent discharge at the catheter insertion site, or fever and rigors with a positive blood culture.
	
	**4. PREVENTION AND CONTROL MEASURES (HIGH-YIELD)** Effective prevention requires strict adherence to infection control practices and policies.
	
	**A. General Preventive Measures**
	
	- **Hand-washing:** The most common route of infection is via hands. Hand-washing with soap and water or a suitable alcohol-based disinfectant is paramount. The WHO guidelines "Clean Care is Safer Care" must be strictly followed.
	- **Isolation:** Infectious patients must be isolated and kept away from highly susceptible patients.
	- **Standard (Routine) Precautions:** Must be applied to all patients. This includes the "no touch technique", wearing gloves, gowns, masks, and eye protection when anticipating contact with blood, body fluids, or mucous membranes, and safe handling of sharps.
	- **Staff Hygiene:** Staff suffering from skin diseases, sore throats, or diarrhoeal diseases must be kept away from work until cured. Regular changing of aprons and outer clothing is essential.
	- **Environmental Control:**
	    - **Dust Control:** Suppression of dust using wet dusting and vacuum cleaning.
	    - **Disinfection:** Proper disinfection of patient articles (urine, faeces, sputum) and strict sterilization of all medical instruments.
	    - **Droplet Control:** Proper bed spacing, prevention of overcrowding, and adequate ventilation.
	
	**B. Specific Measures for Invasive Devices and Procedures**
	
	- **Urinary Catheters:** Aseptic technique during insertion, maintaining a closed drainage system, and limiting the duration of catheterization.
	- **Surgical Sites:** Ensuring a clean operating environment, limiting preoperative hospital stay, optimal antibiotic prophylaxis, and proper preoperative local skin preparation.
	- **Ventilator-associated Pneumonia:** Aseptic intubation and suctioning, limiting duration of ventilation, and using non-invasive ventilation where possible.
	- **Vascular Devices:** Closed systems, surgical asepsis for central line insertion, limiting the frequency of dressing changes, and prompt removal if infection is suspected.
	
	**C. Administrative and Occupational Health Measures**
	
	- **Control of Infection Committee:** Every hospital must have this committee to formulate and monitor policies regarding admissions, isolation, and disinfection procedures.
	- **Staff Immunization:** Hospital staff should be immunized against Hepatitis A and B, Influenza (yearly), MMR, Tetanus, and Diphtheria. Mantoux screening for TB should be a baseline requirement.
	- **Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP):** Clear policies must be strictly implemented for occupational exposure to HIV, Hepatitis B and C, Rabies, and other pathogens.
1. Nosocomial Infections
	**NOSOCOMIAL (HOSPITAL-ACQUIRED) INFECTIONS**
	
	**1. DEFINITION**
	
	- A nosocomial or hospital-acquired infection is defined as an infection originating in a patient while in a hospital or other health care facility, which was not present or incubating at the time of admission.
	- **Timeframe:** Infections occurring more than 48 hours after admission are usually considered nosocomial.
	- **Scope:** It includes infections that are acquired in the hospital but appear after discharge, as well as infections acquired by the staff of the health care facility.
	
	**2. EPIDEMIOLOGY OF NOSOCOMIAL INFECTIONS** Hospital-acquired infections are determined by three main factors:
	
	**A. Sources of Infection**
	
	- **Patients:** Patients suffering from infectious diseases (e.g., discharging wounds, infected skin lesions, respiratory infections like pulmonary tuberculosis, or urinary tract infections) are potential sources.
	- **Hospital Staff:** Doctors, nurses, and ward boys who come in close contact with patients can carry pathogens, such as _Staphylococcus aureus_ (in the nose or skin), Haemolytic streptococci (in the throat), and _Salmonella_ (in the gut).
	- **Environment:** The hospital environment, including dust, linen, bed clothes, sinks, door handles, and air, is laden with microorganisms.
	
	**B. Routes of Spread**
	
	- **Direct Contact:** Transferred directly from the hands of healthcare workers to susceptible patients.
	- **Droplet & Air-borne:** Through coughing, sneezing, or the release of hospital dust into the air.
	- **Hospital Procedures:** Via invasive procedures like catheterization, intravenous (IV) procedures, and the use of infected catgut, dressings, or bedpans.
	
	**C. Recipients (Susceptible Hosts)**
	
	- While all hospitalized patients are potential recipients, susceptibility is significantly higher in those who are severely ill, immunocompromised, or under corticosteroid therapy.
	- High-risk areas include intensive care units (ICUs), urological wards, geriatric wards, and special baby care units.
	
	**3. HIGH-YIELD EXAMPLES OF NOSOCOMIAL INFECTIONS** The four most common nosocomial infections are urinary tract infections, surgical wound infections, pneumonia, and primary bloodstream infections.
	
	Specific examples and their simplified criteria include:
	
	- **Urinary Tract Infection (UTI):** Diagnosed by a positive urine culture (1 or 2 species) with at least $10^5$ bacteria/ml, occurring with or without clinical symptoms.
	- **Surgical Site Infection:** Characterized by any purulent discharge, abscess, or spreading cellulitis at the surgical site appearing during the month after the operation.
	- **Respiratory Infection (Pneumonia):** Presents with respiratory symptoms and at least two signs such as purulent sputum or a new infiltrate on a chest radiograph consistent with infection.
	- **Vascular Catheter Infection:** Indicated by inflammation, lymphangitis, or purulent discharge at the insertion site of the catheter.
	- **Septicaemia (Primary Bloodstream Infection):** Clinically manifests as fever or rigors along with at least one positive blood culture.
	- **Other Examples:** Transmission of blood-borne viruses like Hepatitis B.
