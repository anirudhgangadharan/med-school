1. Epidemiology, clinical course, and prevention of hepatitis B infection
	**HEPATITIS B INFECTION**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION & PROBLEM STATEMENT**
	
	- Hepatitis B is an acute systemic infection with major pathology in the liver, caused by the Hepatitis B virus (HBV).
	- It is endemic globally, with 60% of the world's population living in high-infection areas.
	- WHO estimates that 254 million people are living with chronic Hepatitis B infection, and it caused an estimated 1.1 million deaths in 2022, primarily from cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma.
	
	**2. EPIDEMIOLOGICAL DETERMINANTS** **A. Agent Factors**
	
	- **Agent:** HBV is a complex, 42-nm, double-shelled DNA virus belonging to the Hepadnaviridae family.
	- **Morphology:** It occurs in three forms in patient serum: small spherical particles (22-nm, which are non-infectious but antigenic), tubular forms, and the "Dane particle," which is the actual infectious virus.
	- **Viral Antigens:** The virus possesses three distinct antigen-antibody systems useful for diagnosis: surface antigen (HBsAg), core antigen (HBcAg), and "e" antigen (HBeAg).
	- **Reservoir:** Man is the only reservoir of infection (both cases and carriers). A persistent carrier state is defined as the presence of HBsAg for more than 6 months.
	- **Infective Material:** Contaminated blood is the main source, but the virus is also present in saliva, vaginal secretions, and semen.
	- **Resistance:** The virus is highly stable and can survive for at least 7 days on environmental surfaces.
	
	**B. Host Factors**
	
	- **Age-Dependent Outcome:** The risk of developing chronic infection is inversely related to age. It occurs in 80-90% of perinatal infections, 30-50% in early childhood (<6 years), and less than 5% in late infections (>5 years).
	- **High-Risk Groups:** Surgeons, healthcare/laboratory personnel, recipients of blood transfusions, homosexuals, prostitutes, IV drug abusers, and infants of carrier mothers have a significantly higher risk of acquiring the infection.
	- **Co-infection:** HIV-infected individuals co-infected with HBV have a markedly increased risk of developing liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma.
	
	**3. INCUBATION PERIOD** Ranges from 30 to 180 days, with an average of 75 days.
	
	**4. MODES OF TRANSMISSION**
	
	- **Parenteral Route:** The most important route. Spread occurs via infected blood/blood products through needle pricks, surgical/dental procedures, immunization, tattooing, ear piercing, or sharing razors and toothbrushes.
	- **Perinatal (Vertical) Transmission:** Spread from carrier mothers to their babies, mostly occurring at birth due to maternal blood leaking into the baby's circulation. Without vaccination at birth, the majority of infants born to HBeAg-positive mothers become chronically infected.
	- **Sexual Transmission:** Transmitted via intimate contact through saliva, vaginal fluids, and semen. Male homosexuals and sexually promiscuous individuals are at very high risk.
	- **Horizontal Transmission:** Child-to-child spread occurs through close physical contact via skin cuts or grazes while playing or sharing a bed.
	
	**5. CLINICAL COURSE & COMPLICATIONS**
	
	- **Acute Hepatitis B:** The onset is often preceded by gastrointestinal symptoms (nausea, anorexia), fever, and later jaundice. It is usually a self-limiting disease characterized by acute inflammation and hepatocellular necrosis, with a case fatality rate of 0.5 to 1%. Mortality from fulminant hepatitis B (acute liver failure) can be as high as 70%.
	- **Chronic Hepatitis B (CHB):** Defined as persistent HBV infection. While inactive in some, it frequently causes progressive liver fibrosis, leading to cirrhosis, end-stage liver disease, and a high risk of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).
	- **Prognosis of CHB:** Untreated patients have an 8-20% cumulative risk of developing cirrhosis over 5 years. Those with cirrhosis face a 20% annual risk of hepatic decompensation and a 1-5% annual risk of developing liver cancer.
	
	**6. PREVENTION AND CONTAINMENT** **A. Active Immunization (Hepatitis B Vaccine)**
	
	- **Vaccine Type:** Recombinant DNA vaccine containing HBsAg. It is highly effective and safe.
	- **Schedule in Infants:** Under the National Immunization Schedule, the first dose must be given within 24 hours of birth to prevent perinatal transmission. This is followed by three additional doses at 6, 10, and 14 weeks of age (commonly administered as part of the Pentavalent vaccine).
	- **Adult Schedule:** For high-risk adults, two doses are given 4 weeks apart, with a third dose given 4 to 6 months after the second dose.
	- **Efficacy:** The complete series induces lifelong protective antibody levels in >95% of infants, children, and young adults.
	
	**B. Passive Immunization (Hepatitis B Immunoglobulin - HBIG)**
	
	- **Indications:** Used for immediate, short-term protection (lasts ~3 months) in persons accidentally exposed to HBsAg-positive blood (e.g., needle stick injury in HCWs), sexual contacts of acute cases, and newborns of carrier mothers.
	- **Dosage:** 0.05 to 0.07 ml/kg of body weight, administered as early as possible, ideally within 6 hours and no later than 48 hours post-exposure.
	
	**C. Passive-Active Immunization**
	
	- This is the gold standard for post-exposure prophylaxis and for newborns of carrier mothers. HBIG provides immediate passive immunity, while the Hepatitis B vaccine provides long-term active immunity.
	- Both are administered simultaneously but at different anatomical sites within 24 hours of exposure/birth, followed by the remaining vaccine doses at 1 and 6 months.
	
	**D. General Preventive Measures**
	
	- Screening of all blood and organ donors for HBsAg.
	- Promoting voluntary blood donation rather than relying on commercial donors.
	- Strict sterilization of medical and dental instruments, and prioritizing the use of disposable needles and syringes.
	- Health education for carriers to avoid sharing razors or toothbrushes, to use barrier contraception (condoms), and to refrain from donating blood.
	
	**7. TREATMENT**
	
	- **Acute Infection:** No specific antiviral therapy exists for acute Hepatitis B. Management is purely supportive, aimed at maintaining nutritional and fluid balance.
	- **Chronic Infection:** Can be managed with antiviral drugs to suppress viral replication. The WHO recommends oral treatments like tenofovir or entecavir. While they rarely cure the infection, they effectively slow the progression of cirrhosis, reduce the incidence of liver cancer, and improve long-term survival. Since they only suppress the virus, treatment is often lifelong.
	
1. Hepatitis B—modes of transmission/prevention (primary) and control
	
	**HEPATITIS B: MODES OF TRANSMISSION, PREVENTION, AND CONTROL**
	
	**1. MODES OF TRANSMISSION** Hepatitis B virus (HBV) is transmitted through a wide variety of epidemiological settings. The main routes of transmission include:
	
	- **Parenteral Route:** This is a major route of transmission, occurring through infected blood and blood products. It spreads via skin pricks, handling of infected blood, and accidental inoculations during surgical or dental procedures, immunization, tattooing, ear/nose piercing, circumcision, and acupuncture. The sharing of razors and toothbrushes is also implicated.
	- **Perinatal (Vertical) Transmission:** Infection from HBV carrier mothers to their babies is a major source of chronic infection, particularly in highly endemic areas. Most infections occur at birth due to the leak of maternal blood into the baby's circulation or accidental ingestion/inoculation of blood. Without a birth-dose vaccination, the majority of children born to HBeAg-positive mothers become chronically infected.
	- **Sexual Transmission:** The virus is present in body fluids including saliva, vaginal, menstrual, and seminal fluids. The disease is readily spread through intimate contact and sexual routes. Sexually promiscuous individuals and male homosexuals are at a very high risk.
	- **Horizontal Transmission (Child-to-Child):** Occurs through close physical contact between children with minor skin lesions like cuts or grazes. Often happens when children play together or share the same bed.
	
	---
	
	**2. PRIMARY PREVENTION** Since there is no specific treatment for acute Hepatitis B, prevention is the most effective approach.
	
	**A. Active Immunization (Hepatitis B Vaccine)**
	
	- **Vaccine Type:** Recombinant DNA vaccine containing HBsAg. It is highly safe and effectively induces protective antibody levels in >95% of infants and young adults.
	- **Dose & Route:** Administered via intramuscular (IM) injection. The deltoid muscle is preferred for adults and older children; the anterolateral thigh is used for infants and children under 2 years. The paediatric dose (<10 years) is 0.5 ml (10 mcg), and the adult dose is 1.0 ml (20 mcg).
	- **Infant Schedule (Universal Immunization):** A birth dose must be given as soon as possible, ideally within 24 hours of birth, to prevent perinatal transmission. This is followed by three additional doses given at 6, 10, and 14 weeks of age, usually as part of the Pentavalent vaccine (DPT + Hep B + Hib).
	- **Adult Schedule:** For high-risk adults, the routine schedule is two doses separated by at least 4 weeks, with a third dose 4 to 6 months after the second dose.
	
	**B. Passive Immunization (Hepatitis B Immunoglobulin - HBIG)**
	
	- **Indications:** Provides immediate, short-term protection (lasts ~3 months) for individuals accidentally exposed to HBsAg-positive blood (e.g., needle-stick injuries in surgeons/nurses), newborns of carrier mothers, and sexual contacts of acute cases.
	- **Dosage & Timing:** The recommended dose is 0.05 to 0.07 ml/kg body weight, administered as early as possible, ideally within 6 hours and no later than 48 hours post-exposure.
	
	**C. Passive-Active Immunization**
	
	- **Method:** This is the ideal prophylactic approach for newborns of carrier mothers and healthcare workers following accidental exposure.
	- **Schedule:** HBIG and the Hepatitis B vaccine are administered simultaneously at different anatomical sites within 24 hours of exposure/birth. The vaccine is then repeated at 1 and 6 months.
	
	**D. General Preventive Measures**
	
	- **Blood Safety:** Mandatory screening of all blood and organ donors for HBsAg and rejection of positive units. Promotion of voluntary blood donation over commercial/purchased blood.
	- **Infection Control:** Strict sterilization of all medical and dental instruments and adherence to safe injection practices.
	- **Health Education:** Carriers must be educated to avoid sharing razors or toothbrushes, to use barrier contraceptives (condoms), and to refrain from donating blood.
	
	---
	
	**3. CONTROL MEASURES**
	
	- **Management of Acute Hepatitis B:** There is no specific antiviral treatment for acute infection. Clinical management is purely supportive and aimed at maintaining adequate nutritional balance, comfort, and replacing fluids lost due to vomiting or diarrhoea.
	- **Management of Chronic Hepatitis B (CHB):**
	    - **Screening:** Early identification of CHB enables timely care and prevents transmission. High-risk groups (household/sexual contacts, people living with HIV, people who inject drugs, sex workers) should be routinely screened for HBsAg.
	    - **Antiviral Therapy:** WHO recommends oral antiviral treatments like **Tenofovir** or **Entecavir** for chronic cases. These drugs profoundly suppress viral replication, slow the progression of liver fibrosis and cirrhosis, and reduce the incidence of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Treatment is typically lifelong.
	- **Epidemiological Surveillance:** Surveillance of viral hepatitis is critical to map the disease burden. It involves monitoring acute infections to detect outbreaks, conducting biomarker surveys to estimate the prevalence of chronic infections, and tracking the incidence of long-term sequelae like cirrhosis and liver cancer (HCC) to evaluate the impact of control programmes.
1. Hepatitis B vaccine—schedule
	**HEPATITIS B VACCINE: DOSAGE AND SCHEDULE**
	
	**1. VACCINE TYPE & COMPOSITION**
	
	- It is a recombinant DNA vaccine containing Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg).
	- It is available as a monovalent formulation or as a fixed-dose combination (e.g., Pentavalent vaccine: DPT + Hib + Hep B).
	
	**2. DOSAGE, ROUTE, AND SITE OF ADMINISTRATION**
	
	- **Adults (≥20 years):** 1.0 ml (10–20 micrograms).
	- **Children (<10 years):** 0.5 ml (half the adult dose).
	- **Route:** Intramuscular (IM) injection. Intradermal administration is not recommended.
	- **Site:**
	    - **Infants and children <2 years:** Anterolateral aspect of the mid-thigh.
	    - **Adults and older children:** Deltoid muscle.
	    - _Note:_ Gluteal injection is strictly contraindicated as it deposits the vaccine in fat rather than muscle, resulting in a poor immune response (fewer seroconversions).
	
	**3. SCHEDULE FOR INFANTS (National Immunization Schedule - India)**
	
	- **Birth Dose:** A single dose of monovalent Hepatitis B vaccine must be given as soon as possible after birth, ideally **within 24 hours**. This is critical to prevent perinatal (mother-to-child) transmission.
	- **Primary Series:** Three additional doses are administered at **6 weeks, 10 weeks, and 14 weeks** of age. Under the Universal Immunization Programme (UIP) in India, these doses are given as part of the Pentavalent vaccine.
	
	**4. SCHEDULE FOR ADULTS & HIGH-RISK GROUPS** Routine pre-exposure vaccination is recommended for adults at high risk (e.g., healthcare workers, frequent recipients of blood products, injecting drug users, and sexual contacts of carriers).
	
	- **Standard Schedule:** Administered in 3 doses at **0, 1, and 6 months**.
	    - 1st dose: At elected date.
	    - 2nd dose: 1 month after the 1st dose.
	    - 3rd dose: 4 to 6 months after the 2nd dose.
	- **Accelerated Schedule:** If needed, the minimum interval between the 1st and 2nd dose is 4 weeks, and between the 2nd and 3rd dose is 8 weeks. The 1st and 3rd doses must be separated by no less than 16 weeks.
	
	**5. HIGH-YIELD EXCEPTIONS & PRECAUTIONS**
	
	- **Interrupted Schedule:** If the primary series is interrupted, **do not restart** the vaccine series or add extra doses.
	    - If interrupted after the 1st dose, give the 2nd dose as soon as possible, and ensure a minimum of 4 weeks before giving the 3rd dose.
	    - If only the 3rd dose is delayed, administer it as soon as possible.
	- **Premature Infants (Low Birth Weight < 2000g):** Infants born prematurely may not respond well to the vaccine at birth. The birth dose given to these infants does not count towards the primary series. They require 3 additional doses starting at 1 month of chronological age (according to the national schedule).
	- **Pregnancy and Lactation:** Neither pregnancy nor lactation is a contraindication for the use of the Hepatitis B vaccine.
	- **Co-administration:** The birth dose of Hepatitis B can be given safely alongside the BCG vaccine and Oral Polio Vaccine (OPV), provided they are injected at different anatomical sites.

1. Rotavirus
	**ROTAVIRUS**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION & PROBLEM STATEMENT**
	
	- Rotavirus, first discovered in 1973, is the leading cause of severe, dehydrating diarrhoea in children aged under 5 years globally.
	- It accounts for 15-25% of pathogens identified in children with acute diarrhoea in treatment centres in developing countries.
	- Globally, it is responsible for an estimated 25 million outpatient visits, more than 2 million hospitalizations, and between 420,000 to 494,000 deaths (as of 2008) each year, predominantly in low-income countries.
	
	**2. EPIDEMIOLOGICAL DETERMINANTS**
	
	- **Agent Factors:** The virus is shed in extremely high concentrations (>10¹⁰ particles/gram) and for many days in the stools and vomit of infected individuals.
	- **Host Factors:** In developing countries, three-quarters of children acquire their first episode before 12 months of age, whereas in developed countries, the first episode is frequently delayed until 2-5 years of age. Severe rotavirus gastroenteritis is largely limited to children between 6 and 24 months of age. Rotavirus reinfection is common, although the primary infection is usually the most clinically significant.
	- **Environmental Factors:** In temperate climates, the incidence of rotavirus gastroenteritis typically peaks during the winter season. In tropical settings, it occurs throughout the year but increases in frequency during the drier, cool months, though marked seasonality may be masked by high background levels.
	
	**3. MODE OF TRANSMISSION**
	
	- Transmission occurs primarily by the faecal-oral route.
	- It spreads directly from person to person or indirectly via contaminated fomites.
	- The universal occurrence of rotavirus infections indicates that clean water supplies and good hygiene practices alone are unlikely to have a substantial effect on halting virus transmission.
	
	**4. PREVENTION & VACCINATION** Vaccination is the primary strategy for the prevention of rotavirus diarrhoea. Two live, oral, attenuated rotavirus vaccines were licensed in 2006: the monovalent human rotavirus vaccine (Rotarix) and the pentavalent bovine-human reassortant vaccine (RotaTeq). Both vaccines have demonstrated very good safety and efficacy profiles.
	
	**A. Global Vaccine Schedules**
	
	- **Rotarix:** Administered orally in a 2-dose schedule. The first dose can be administered at 6 weeks (no later than 12 weeks of age), and the second dose requires an interval of at least 4 weeks. The 2-dose schedule should be completed by 16 weeks, and no later than 24 weeks of age.
	- **RotaTeq:** Recommended as 3 oral doses at 2, 4, and 6 months of age. The first dose should be administered between 6-12 weeks, with subsequent doses at intervals of 4-10 weeks, and all 3 doses must be administered before 32 weeks of age.
	
	**B. Rotavirus Vaccine in India (National Immunization Schedule)**
	
	- Rotavirus vaccine was introduced in India in 2016 in a phased manner (starting in Odisha, Himachal Pradesh, Haryana, and Andhra Pradesh) and now covers the whole country under the Universal Immunization Programme (UIP).
	- It is administered as a 3-dose series along with the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd doses of the Pentavalent vaccine at 6, 10, and 14 weeks of age.
	- The dosage is 5 drops for Rotavac (liquid vaccine) or 2.5 ml for Rotasiil (lyophilized vaccine) given orally, and it can be given up to one year of age.
	
	**C. Adverse Effects & Precautions**
	
	- Post-marketing surveillance has detected a small increased risk of intussusception (approximately 1-2 cases per 100,000 vaccinated infants) shortly after the administration of the first dose of the rotavirus vaccine.
	- There is a potentially higher risk of intussusception when the first dose is given to infants older than 12 weeks.
	- Consequently, current rotavirus vaccines should not be used in catch-up vaccination campaigns where the exact age of the vaccinees may be difficult to ascertain.
	
1. Briefly describe the Rotavirus vaccine
	**ROTAVIRUS VACCINE**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION**
	
	- **Purpose:** The rotavirus vaccine is administered to prevent severe, dehydrating gastroenteritis and diarrhoea caused by the rotavirus, which is a leading cause of severe diarrhoea in children under 5 years of age globally.
	- **Nature of Vaccine:** It is a live, oral, attenuated vaccine.
	
	**2. TYPES OF VACCINES AVAILABLE** Currently, two major types of licensed rotavirus vaccines are widely used:
	
	- **Monovalent Vaccine (Rotarix™):** A live attenuated human rotavirus vaccine.
	- **Pentavalent Vaccine (RotaTeq™):** A live attenuated bovine-human reassortant vaccine.
	- _In India (under UIP):_ Two formulations are used—**Rotavac** (liquid vaccine) and **Rotasiil** (lyophilized/freeze-dried vaccine).
	
	**3. ADMINISTRATION & SCHEDULE**
	
	**A. Under the National Immunization Schedule (NIS), India:**
	
	- **Schedule:** Administered in a 3-dose schedule at **6 weeks, 10 weeks, and 14 weeks** of age (given concurrently with the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd doses of the Pentavalent and OPV vaccines). It can be given until 1 year of age.
	- **Route:** Oral.
	- **Dose:**
	    - **5 drops** for Rotavac (liquid vaccine).
	    - **2.5 ml** for Rotasiil (lyophilized vaccine).
	
	**B. WHO Recommended Schedule:**
	
	- **Rotarix™ (2-Dose Schedule):** Given at 2 and 4 months of age. The first dose should be administered between 6 to 12 weeks of age, and the series must be completed by 16-24 weeks of age.
	- **RotaTeq™ (3-Dose Schedule):** Given at 2, 4, and 6 months of age. The first dose is given between 6-12 weeks, with subsequent doses at 4-10 week intervals. All 3 doses must be completed before 32 weeks of age.
	
	**4. ADVERSE EFFECTS & PRECAUTIONS**
	
	- **Common Side Effects:** The vaccines have demonstrated very good safety profiles.
	- **Rare Complication (High-Yield):** Post-marketing surveillance has detected a small, rare increased risk of **intussusception** (approximately 1-2 cases per 100,000 infants vaccinated).
	- **Important Precaution:** The risk of intussusception is potentially higher if the first dose is given to infants older than 12 weeks. Consequently, the vaccine is **strictly not recommended for catch-up vaccination campaigns** where the exact age of the child is difficult to ascertain.
	
	**5. STORAGE AND HANDLING (COLD CHAIN)**
	
	- **Storage Temperature:** It must be stored in the Ice Lined Refrigerator (ILR) at a temperature of **+2°C to +8°C**. It should be kept on the top shelf of a domestic refrigerator.
	- **Heat Sensitivity:** It is heat-sensitive (Group C and D).
	- **Reconstitution Rule:** If using the lyophilized (freeze-dried) formulation, it must be reconstituted with the provided diluent. Once reconstituted, it can be kept at +2°C to +8°C but **must be discarded after 4 hours**.
	- **Open Vial Policy:** The Open Vial Policy (which allows reuse of partially used multidose vials for up to 28 days) **does NOT apply** to the Rotavirus vaccine.
