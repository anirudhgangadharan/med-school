1. Write briefly about measures to be taken to control a cholera outbreak 
	**MEASURES TO CONTROL A CHOLERA OUTBREAK**
	
	The control of a cholera outbreak requires a comprehensive and integrated approach, as recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines for cholera control. The following high-yield measures must be implemented promptly:
	
	**1. Verification of the Diagnosis**
	
	- It is critical to confirm the outbreak as quickly as possible.
	- Diagnosis is confirmed by identifying _Vibrio cholerae_ O1 or O139 in the stools of the patient through laboratory culture or polymerase chain reaction (PCR).
	- Under field conditions, rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) or immunochromatographic dipstick tests can be used for presumptive diagnosis, though stool samples should still be sent for confirmation.
	- Once the presence of cholera is confirmed in a community, it is not necessary to culture the stools of every subsequent case or contact.
	
	**2. Notification**
	
	- Cholera is a notifiable disease locally and nationally.
	- Health workers at all levels (including community health workers and multipurpose workers) must be trained to identify and immediately notify cases to the local health authority.
	- Under the International Health Regulations (IHR), while mandatory international notification is no longer strictly required for every case, public health events involving cholera must be assessed against IHR criteria to determine the need for official notification to the WHO.
	- An area is declared free of cholera only when twice the incubation period (i.e., 10 days) has elapsed since the death, recovery, or isolation of the last case.
	
	**3. Early Case-Finding**
	
	- An aggressive, active search for cases (mild, moderate, and severe) must be launched in the community to initiate prompt treatment.
	- Early detection allows for the identification of infected household contacts and assists epidemiologists in determining the modes of transmission to apply specific interventions.
	
	**4. Establishment of Treatment Centres**
	
	- Time is of the essence; easily accessible treatment facilities must be established immediately within the community.
	- Mildly dehydrated patients (accounting for over 90% of cases) should be treated at home or locally with Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT).
	- Severely dehydrated patients requiring intravenous (IV) fluids should be transferred to the nearest hospital or treatment centre, receiving ORT en route if possible.
	- If hospitals are distant, local schools or public buildings should be converted into temporary treatment centres to avoid transporting cases over long distances, which can spread the disease.
	- Mobile teams should be established at the district level and deployed promptly to assist local workers in epidemic areas.
	
	**5. Rehydration Therapy**
	
	- This is the most effective life-saving measure; effective rehydration therapy has brought cholera mortality rates down to less than 1%.
	- Therapy may be oral (using WHO-recommended Oral Rehydration Salts) or intravenous (using Ringer's Lactate or Diarrhoea Treatment Solution for severe cases).
	
	**6. Adjuncts to Therapy (Antibiotics)**
	
	- Antibiotics should be administered as soon as vomiting stops, usually after 3 to 4 hours of oral rehydration.
	- Commonly used antibiotics include doxycycline, tetracycline, fluoroquinolones, macrolides (azithromycin, erythromycin), and TMP-SMX (cotrimoxazole).
	- It is vital to identify the antibiotic susceptibility of the circulating _V. cholerae_ O1 strain. If diarrhoea persists after 48 hours of treatment, antibiotic resistance should be suspected.
	- **Contraindications:** Anti-diarrhoeals, anti-emetics, antispasmodics, cardiotonics, and corticosteroids must **not** be used in the treatment of cholera.
	
	**7. Epidemiological Investigations**
	
	- General sanitation measures must be applied immediately, while concurrent epidemiological studies are undertaken to define the extent of the outbreak and identify the specific modes of transmission.
	- Institutions like the National Institute of Communicable Diseases (NICD) or the National Institute of Cholera and Enteric Diseases (NICED) can assist in investigating outbreaks and conducting phage typing.
	
	**8. Sanitation Measures**
	
	- **Water Control:** Water is the most important vehicle of transmission. Properly treated water with free residual chlorine (0.5 mg/litre) must be provided. In rural emergencies, boiling or chlorination of water is mandatory.
	- **Excreta Disposal:** Sanitary latrines must be constructed and their proper use promoted. Defecating in open areas or near water sources must be strictly avoided. Handwashing with soap after defecation is critical.
	- **Food Sanitation:** Improve food sanitation by ensuring foods are sold under hygienic conditions, promoting the consumption of hot, thoroughly cooked foods, and ensuring proper cleaning and drying of cooking utensils. Fly control acts as a general indicator of sanitation levels.
	- **Disinfection:** Concurrent and terminal disinfection is necessary. Patient stools, vomit, soiled clothes, and latrines must be disinfected using a coal tar disinfectant (like cresol with a Rideal-Walker coefficient >10) or good quality bleaching powder. Dead bodies must be handled with aseptic precautions.
	
	**9. Chemoprophylaxis**
	
	- **Mass chemoprophylaxis is NOT recommended** for the entire community because it requires treating roughly 10,000 people to prevent one severe case, its effects are short-lived, and it has historically failed to stop outbreaks.
	- Chemoprophylaxis is only advised for close household contacts or within closed communities where cholera has struck.
	- **Drug of choice:** Tetracycline (given over 3 days) or a single oral dose of doxycycline (if strains are not resistant).
	
	**10. Vaccination (Oral Cholera Vaccine - OCV)**
	
	- Vaccination is a supplementary measure and not a replacement for water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) interventions.
	- Currently, safe and effective Oral Cholera Vaccines (OCVs) are available, including Dukoral (WC-rBS), Sanchol, mORCVAX, and Euvichol.
	- These are usually administered in a 2-dose schedule and provide temporary immunity.
	
	**11. Health Education and Community Engagement**
	
	- Health education is the most effective prophylactic measure in the long term.
	- Education should focus on: (a) the simplicity and effectiveness of Oral Rehydration Therapy, (b) the necessity of early reporting for prompt treatment, (c) food hygiene practices, (d) hand washing with soap before eating and after defecation, and (e) the consumption of safe water and hot, cooked foods.
	- Local culture and beliefs must be respected when promoting hygiene practices and adapting funeral practices for individuals who die from cholera to prevent further infections among attendees.
