**ANTHROPOZOONOSES**
	- **Definition:** These are infections or infectious diseases transmitted to man from lower vertebrate animals.
	- **High-Yield Examples:**
	    1. Rabies
	    2. Plague
	    3. Hydatid disease

2. Amplify zoonoses with examples
	
	**1. DEFINITION** Zoonoses are defined by the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee as "those diseases and infections [the agents of] which are naturally transmitted between [other] vertebrate animals and man".
	
	**2. PUBLIC HEALTH IMPORTANCE**
	
	- **Magnitude:** More than 150 zoonoses have been recognized worldwide.
	- **Economic Impact:** Beyond causing significant morbidity and mortality in humans, zoonoses are responsible for massive economic losses in animals, meat, milk, and other animal-derived foods and products.
	- **Vulnerability:** Developing countries face more severe losses due to less developed veterinary and public health services and unfavourable environmental conditions. In India, the problem is highly significant because nearly 68.86% of the population is rural, living in close and constant contact with domestic and wild animals.
	
	**3. CLASSIFICATION BASED ON RESERVOIR AND DIRECTION OF TRANSMISSION** Zoonoses can be classified according to their maintenance hosts and the direction of disease transmission:
	
	- **Anthropozoonoses:** Infections transmitted to man from lower vertebrate animals.
	    - _Examples:_ Rabies, Plague, Hydatid disease, Anthrax, and Trichinosis.
	- **Zooanthroponoses:** Infections transmitted from man to lower vertebrate animals.
	    - _Example:_ Human tuberculosis in cattle.
	- **Amphixenoses:** Infections maintained in both man and lower vertebrate animals that may be transmitted in either direction.
	    - _Examples:_ _Trypanosoma cruzi_ (Chagas disease) and _Schistosoma japonicum_.
	
	**4. CLASSIFICATION BASED ON LIFE CYCLE OF THE INFECTING ORGANISM** This classification has considerable teaching and epidemiological value:
	
	- **Direct Zoonoses:** The agent is transmitted from an infected vertebrate host to a susceptible vertebrate host by direct contact, by contact with a fomite, or by a mechanical vector. The agent itself undergoes little or no propagative changes and no essential developmental change during transmission.
	    - _Examples:_ Rabies, Brucellosis, and Trichinosis.
	- **Cyclo-zoonoses:** The disease agent requires more than one vertebrate host species, but no invertebrate host, in order to complete its developmental cycle.
	    - _Examples:_ Human taeniasis (involving cattle/pigs and man), Echinococcosis (Hydatid disease), and pentastomida infections.
	- **Meta-zoonoses:** The agent is transmitted biologically by invertebrate vectors. In the invertebrate vector, the agent multiplies, develops, or both. There is always an extrinsic incubation (prepatent) period before the vector can transmit the infection to another vertebrate host.
	    - _Examples:_ Plague (transmitted by fleas), Arbovirus infections (like Dengue, Yellow fever, and Japanese encephalitis transmitted by mosquitoes), and Schistosomiasis.
	- **Sapro-zoonoses:** The disease agent requires both a vertebrate host and a non-animal developmental site or reservoir (such as organic matter, soil, and plants) for its cycle.
	    - _Examples:_ Larva migrans and some mycoses.
	
	**5. CLASSIFICATION BASED ON CAUSATIVE AGENTS WITH EXAMPLES** Zoonotic diseases are caused by a wide variety of biological agents. A high-yield list of important zoonoses includes:
	
	**A. Bacterial Zoonoses**
	
	- **Plague:** Wild rodents and commensal rats (Reservoirs).
	- **Brucellosis:** Cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, camels, and dogs.
	- **Anthrax:** Herbivores, pigs.
	- **Leptospirosis:** Rodents, mammals.
	- **Human Salmonellosis:** Cattle, swine, rodents, and poultry.
	
	**B. Viral Zoonoses**
	
	- **Rabies:** Dogs, foxes, bats, skunks, jackals, and mongooses.
	- **Yellow fever:** Monkeys.
	- **Japanese encephalitis:** Pigs (amplifiers) and wild birds (Ardeid birds).
	- **Kyasanur Forest Disease (KFD):** Monkeys and rodents.
	- **Monkeypox:** Monkeys, rope squirrels, tree squirrels, and rodents.
	- **Nipah virus infection:** Fruit bats (Pteropodidae) and pigs.
	- **Lassa fever:** Multi-mammate rat.
	
	**C. Rickettsial Zoonoses**
	
	- **Scrub typhus:** Rodents (rats, mice) via trombiculid mites.
	- **Murine (endemic) typhus:** Rodents (Rattus rattus, Rattus norvegicus) via fleas.
	- **Q fever:** Sheep, cattle, goats, and ticks.
	- **Indian tick typhus:** Rodents and dogs.
	
	**D. Protozoan Zoonoses**
	
	- **Leishmaniasis:** Dogs, wild carnivores, rodents, and other mammals.
	- **Toxoplasmosis:** Cats, mammals, and birds.
	- **Trypanosomiasis:** Game animals and cattle.
	
	**E. Helminthic Zoonoses**
	
	- **Echinococcosis (Hydatid disease):** Dogs, wild carnivores, and domestic/wild ungulates (sheep, cattle).
	- **Taeniasis:** Cattle (for _T. saginata_) and Swine (for _T. solium_).
	- **Trichinellosis:** Swine, rodents, wild carnivores, and marine mammals.
	
	**6. PREVENTION AND CONTROL OF ZOONOSES** The control of zoonoses is a major challenge requiring a coordinated veterinary public health approach. The principal components of control include:
	
	- **Control in Animals (The Reservoir):**
	    - Accurate diagnosis of the zoonotic condition in animals.
	    - Treatment of infected animals where feasible.
	    - Immunization (e.g., vaccinating dogs against rabies, or heifers with B. abortus strain 19 against brucellosis).
	    - Quarantine of suspected animals and destruction/slaughter of heavily infected animal reservoirs (e.g., test and slaughter approach in brucellosis).
	- **Control of Vehicles of Transmission:**
	    - Establishing strict food hygiene practices, including milk and meat inspection.
	    - Ensuring the safety of animal by-products like wool, hides, horn, bones, and fat.
	    - Proper disposal of animal carcasses, aborted foetuses, and biological wastes.
	    - Rigorous environmental disinfection procedures.
	- **Prevention and Treatment in Man (The Host):**
	    - Protection of high-risk occupational groups (e.g., farmers, abattoir workers, veterinarians) via immunization and chemoprophylaxis.
	    - Implementation of occupational health monitoring programs.
	    - Early diagnosis and prompt specific treatment of human cases.
	    - Health education regarding personal hygiene, safe food practices, and avoiding high-risk contacts.
	    - Prevention of environmental and food contamination.
