1. Kyasanur Forest disease—epidemiological factors, prevention and control measures.
	**KYASANUR FOREST DISEASE (KFD)**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION** Kyasanur Forest Disease (KFD), locally known as "monkey disease", is a febrile disease associated with haemorrhages. It was first recognized in 1957 in the Shimoga district of Karnataka State in India, following investigations of monkey deaths in the Kyasanur forest.
	
	**2. EPIDEMIOLOGICAL DETERMINANTS**
	
	**A. Agent Factors**
	
	- **Agent:** The causative agent is the KFD virus, which is an arbovirus belonging to the group B togaviruses (flaviviruses). It is antigenically related to other tick-borne flaviviruses like the Far Eastern tick-borne encephalitis and Omsk haemorrhagic fever.
	- **Viraemia:** Unlike many other arbovirus infections, KFD produces a prolonged viraemia in humans lasting for 10 days or more.
	
	**B. Natural Hosts and Reservoirs (High-Yield Concept)**
	
	- **Main Reservoirs (Maintenance Hosts):** Small mammals, particularly rats and squirrels, act as the true reservoirs of the virus. Birds and bats are considered less important hosts.
	- **Amplifying Hosts:** Monkeys act as amplifiers for the virus; however, they are not effective maintenance hosts because most of them die from the KFD infection. Deaths of monkeys are considered heralders of this disease in endemic areas.
	- **Tick-Multipliers:** Cattle provide _Haemaphysalis_ ticks with plentiful blood meals, leading to a population explosion among the ticks. Cattle play no part in virus maintenance.
	- **Incidental Host:** Man is merely an incidental or "dead-end" host and plays no role in the transmission cycle of the virus.
	
	**C. Vectors**
	
	- The virus has a complex life cycle involving a variety of tick species.
	- At least 15 species of hard ticks belonging to the genus _Haemaphysalis_, particularly _H. spinigera_ and _H. turtura_, are proven vectors. KFD has also been isolated from soft ticks.
	
	**D. Host Factors**
	
	- **Age and Sex:** The majority of affected cases are between 20 and 40 years of age, with a higher attack rate in males than in females.
	- **Occupation and Human Activity:** Cultivators and villagers who visit forests to cut wood or accompany their cattle are at the highest risk.
	
	**E. Environmental and Seasonal Factors**
	
	- **Seasonality:** The highest number of human and monkey infections occurs during the drier months, from January to June.
	- This epidemic period correlates perfectly with the peak nymphal activity of ticks and the period of greatest human activity in the forest (before the onset of the monsoon rains in June).
	
	**3. INCUBATION PERIOD & MODE OF TRANSMISSION**
	
	- **Transmission:** The disease is transmitted to humans exclusively by the bite of infective ticks, especially in their nymphal stages. There is no evidence of man-to-man transmission.
	- **Incubation Period:** Estimated to be between 3 and 8 days.
	
	**4. PREVENTION AND CONTROL MEASURES**
	
	The control of KFD requires a multi-pronged approach targeting the vector, protecting the host, and immunization:
	
	**A. Control of Ticks (Vector Control)**
	
	- **Insecticidal Spraying:** Application of insecticides such as carbaryl, fenthion, naled, or propoxur at the rate of 2.24 kg of active ingredient per hectare. This can be done by power equipment or aircraft-mounted equipment.
	- **Targeting "Hot Spots":** Spraying must be aggressively carried out in "hot spots"—areas where monkey deaths have been reported—specifically covering a radius of 50 metres around the spot of the monkey deaths.
	- **Cattle Restriction:** Since cattle roaming freely in the forest contribute to a heavy tick population, restricting cattle movement helps bring about a reduction in the vector population.
	
	**B. Vaccination**
	
	- The population at risk in endemic areas should be immunized with the killed KFD vaccine.
	
	**C. Personal Prophylaxis**
	
	- **Protective Clothing and Repellents:** Individuals exposed to the risk of infection should wear adequate protective clothing and use insect repellents like dimethylphthalate (DMP) or DEET.
	- **Tick Checks:** Workers should regularly examine their bodies for ticks at the end of each day and remove them promptly.
	- **Health Education:** Educating the community to avoid sitting or lying down on the ground in forest areas is a crucial preventive measure.
