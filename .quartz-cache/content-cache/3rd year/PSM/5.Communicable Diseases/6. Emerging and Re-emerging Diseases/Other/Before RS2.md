1. Write about postexposure prophylaxis for HIV
	**OCCUPATIONAL POST-EXPOSURE PROPHYLAXIS (PEP) FOR HIV**
	
	**1. DEFINITION AND INDICATIONS** Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) is highly recommended for healthcare personnel who experience occupational exposure to blood, tissues, or other body fluids that may contain the Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV).
	
	**2. TYPES OF HIGH-RISK EXPOSURES**
	
	- **Percutaneous injury:** Examples include a needlestick injury or a cut with a sharp object.
	- **Mucocutaneous contact:** Contact of mucous membranes or non-intact skin (e.g., exposed skin that is chapped, abraded, or irritated due to dermatitis) with potentially infectious body fluids.
	
	**3. CLASSIFICATION OF BODY FLUIDS (HIGH-YIELD)**
	
	- **Potentially Infectious Fluids:** Blood, semen, vaginal secretions, cerebrospinal fluid, synovial fluid, pleural fluid, peritoneal fluid, pericardial fluid, and amniotic fluid.
	- **Non-Infectious Fluids (unless visibly bloody):** Faeces, nasal secretions, saliva, sputum, sweat, tears, urine, and vomitus.
	
	**4. TIMING AND DURATION (CRITICAL GUIDELINES)**
	
	- **Immediate Initiation:** PEP must be initiated as soon as possible after the exposure, preferably within hours.
	- **Do not delay:** Do not delay the administration of PEP while waiting for the source patient's HIV test results.
	- **The 72-Hour Window:** Animal studies demonstrate that PEP is likely to be less effective if started more than 72 hours after exposure. Without PEP, HIV replication occurs within 48 to 72 hours in regional lymph nodes close to the exposure site, followed by viremia within 72 to 120 hours of inoculation. Initiating therapy after a longer interval (e.g., 1 week) might still be considered for exposures representing an extremely high risk for transmission.
	- **Duration of Therapy:** The full course of the PEP regimen must be completed for **4 weeks**.
	
	**5. RECOMMENDED DRUG REGIMENS (Duration: 4 Weeks)**
	
	**A. Preferred Regimens for Adults** Must include a 3-drug combination:
	
	- **Regimen 1:** Raltegravir (400 mg twice daily) PLUS Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/Emtricitabine (300 mg-200 mg once daily).
	- **Regimen 2:** Dolutegravir (50 mg once daily) PLUS Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/Emtricitabine (300 mg-200 mg once daily). _(High-Yield Note: Lamivudine may be safely substituted for Emtricitabine in either of the above regimens)_.
	
	**B. Paediatric Regimens (Children < 10 years)**
	
	- **Lopinavir/ritonavir (LPV/r)** is recommended as the preferred third drug for HIV post-exposure prophylaxis in children younger than 10 years.
	- Age-appropriate alternative regimens can be identified among ATV/r, RAL, DRV, EFV, and NVP.
	
	**6. SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS IN WOMEN**
	
	- **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:** Dolutegravir is considered an acceptable alternative to raltegravir for patients intending to become pregnant or presenting in the first trimester; however, they must be advised of the small risk for teratogenicity when administered in the 1st trimester. There is no known elevated risk beyond the first trimester.
	- **Contraceptive Counselling:** Non-pregnant individuals of childbearing potential must be strictly counselled about the need for birth control while completing a PEP regimen.
1. List out the various routes of transmission of HIV infection
	**ROUTES OF TRANSMISSION OF HIV INFECTION**
	
	The Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) is transmitted from person to person through three basic modes:
	
	**1. SEXUAL TRANSMISSION**
	
	- **Primary Route:** AIDS is first and foremost a sexually transmitted disease.
	- **Types of Contact:** Any unprotected vaginal, anal, or oral sex can spread the virus.
	- **Relative Risk:**
	    - Anal intercourse carries a higher risk of transmission than vaginal intercourse because it is more likely to injure the tissues of the receptive partner.
	    - The chances of transmission of HIV from male to female are twice as likely as from female to male. Women are biologically more vulnerable because a larger surface area is exposed and semen contains a higher concentration of the virus than vaginal/cervical fluids.
	- **Co-factors:** The presence of a co-existing Sexually Transmitted Disease (STD) such as syphilis, chancroid, or herpes facilitates transmission, making the risk 8 to 10 times higher.
	
	**2. BLOOD CONTACT (PARENTERAL TRANSMISSION)**
	
	- **Blood and Blood Products:** HIV is transmitted by the transfusion of contaminated whole blood cells, platelets, and factors VIII and IX derived from human plasma. The risk of contracting HIV from a unit of infected blood is estimated to be over 95 per cent.
	- **Injecting Drug Use:** Needle-sharing among drug users who inject heroin, cocaine, or other drugs is a major cause of AIDS transmission because exposure is repeated frequently.
	- **Skin-Piercing Instruments:** Any unsterilized skin-piercing instruments (including needles, syringes, ear-piercing, tattooing, acupuncture, or scarification tools) that have been previously used on an infected person can transmit the virus.
	
	**3. MATERNAL-FOETAL (VERTICAL) TRANSMISSION**
	
	- **Modes of Vertical Spread:** The virus can pass from an infected mother to her child through three specific routes:
	    - In utero (across the placenta).
	    - Intrapartum / Peripartum (during delivery).
	    - Postnatal (via breast-feeding).
	- **Transmission Rate:** In the absence of any preventive interventions, the rate of transmission from mother to child varies from 20 to 25 per cent.
	- **Risk Factors:** The risk of infecting the child is significantly higher if the mother is newly infected or if she has already developed advanced AIDS.
	
	**HIGH-YIELD NOTE ON NON-TRANSMISSION:**
	
	- There is absolutely no evidence that HIV is transmitted through mosquitoes or any other insects.
	- It is not transmitted by casual social contact (including within households), sharing food, or water.
	- There is no evidence of spread to health care workers from casual professional contact with AIDS patients.

2. Contact tracing in sexually transmitted infections.
	
	**CONTACT TRACING (PARTNER SERVICES) IN SEXUALLY TRANSMITTED INFECTIONS**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION AND OBJECTIVES**
	
	- Partner services and contact tracing make an important contribution to averting further transmission of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) and preventing reinfection.
	- Assisting in the detection of infection in sexual partners reduces the risks of transmission before they have further unprotected sexual exposure with other susceptible partners.
	- Pregnant women with curable sexually transmitted infections should be assisted to notify their sexual partners to receive treatment and prevent reinfection.
	
	**2. GENERAL PRINCIPLES OF PARTNER MANAGEMENT**
	
	- **Voluntary Engagement:** Partner service interventions should only be offered if the engagement in these services is completely voluntary.
	- **Confidentiality:** Strict confidentiality must be ensured for the index case as well as for that person's partners.
	- **Counselling and Testing:** Partners should be referred for voluntary counselling and testing for HIV, syphilis, and Hepatitis B.
	- **Abstinence & Protection:** Couples must be advised to maintain sexual abstinence during the entire course of treatment. Condoms should be provided, and education on their correct and consistent use must be imparted.
	- **Follow-up:** A return visit should be scheduled after 7 days to evaluate compliance and test results.
	
	**3. SYNDROME-SPECIFIC GUIDELINES FOR PARTNER MANAGEMENT** Under the syndromic management of STIs, contact tracing and treatment are tailored to the clinical presentation of the index case:
	
	**A. Urethral Discharge (in Males)**
	
	- Notification and treatment of the female partners of men with urethritis are of the highest priority.
	- This is one of the best ways of identifying women who are at high risk of having asymptomatic gonococcal and chlamydial infections.
	- All recent female partners must be treated for gonorrhoea and chlamydia after ruling out pregnancy and a history of allergies.
	
	**B. Vaginal Discharge**
	
	- The current partner is treated only if there is no improvement after the initial treatment of the index patient.
	- However, if the partner is symptomatic, both the client and the partner must be treated using the standard protocols.
	
	**C. Lower Abdominal Pain in Females (Pelvic Inflammatory Disease)**
	
	- All sexual partners from the past 2 months must be treated.
	- Male partners should be treated empirically for urethral discharge, which covers gonorrhoea and chlamydia.
	
	**D. Genital Ulcers**
	
	- All partners who have been in contact with the patient in the last 3 months must be traced and treated.
	- Partners should be treated specifically for syphilis and chancroid.
	
	**E. Inguinal Bubo**
	
	- All partners who were in contact with the patient in the last 3 months must be evaluated and treated.
	- These partners should be treated for chancroid and Lymphogranuloma venereum (LGV).
	
	**F. Scrotal Swelling**
	
	- The partner needs to be evaluated and treated depending on their specific clinical findings.

3. Write case detection in HIV in India
	
	**CASE DETECTION OF HIV IN INDIA (NATIONAL AIDS CONTROL PROGRAMME)**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION & GOALS**
	
	- Case detection is the fundamental first step in the continuum of HIV care and prevention.
	- India's National AIDS Control Programme (NACP) focuses on closing the testing gap to achieve the UNAIDS "95-95-95" target, where the first "95" stands for **95% of people living with HIV (PLHIV) knowing their HIV status**.
	- Case detection acts as a gateway to the "Treat All" policy, enabling rapid/same-day initiation of Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) for newly diagnosed individuals.
	
	**2. GUIDING PRINCIPLES FOR TESTING** All HIV testing services in India are strictly guided by the **WHO's "5 Cs" principle**:
	
	1. **C**onsent
	2. **C**onfidentiality
	3. **C**ounselling (Pre-test information and post-test counselling)
	4. **C**orrect test results
	5. **C**onnection to care and treatment.
	
	**3. SERVICE DELIVERY MODELS FOR CASE DETECTION (HIGH-YIELD)** India utilizes a multi-pronged, decentralized approach to maximize HIV case detection across different geographic and demographic settings:
	
	**A. Integrated Counselling and Testing Centres (ICTC)** There is a massive network of over 15,000 ICTCs providing diagnostic, prevention, and care services. They operate under three main models:
	
	- **Standalone ICTC (SA-ICTC):** Located at Medical Colleges, District Hospitals, Sub-district Hospitals, and CHCs. They handle a high client load and are staffed with a full-time counsellor and laboratory technician.
	- **Facility-Integrated ICTC (F-ICTC):** Set up below the block level at 24x7 PHCs, and in private facilities (under Public-Private Partnership models). Staff at these existing facilities are trained in HIV counselling and testing.
	- **Mobile ICTC:** A mobile van equipped with a room for general examination, counselling, and space for processing blood samples. Managed by a paramedical team, it reaches hard-to-reach areas, vulnerable populations, and operates with flexible working hours.
	
	**B. Community-Based Screening**
	
	- To offer HIV testing to every pregnant woman and eliminate vertical transmission, community-based HIV screening is conducted by frontline health workers (Auxiliary Nurse Midwives / ANMs) at the sub-centre level.
	
	**C. Prevention of Parent-To-Child Transmission (PPTCT)**
	
	- **Universal Screening:** Routine offer of HIV counselling and testing to all pregnant women enrolled in antenatal care, using an **"opt-out"** approach.
	- This detects HIV-positive pregnant women to initiate life-long ART and prevent mother-to-child transmission.
	
	**D. Early Infant Diagnosis (EID)**
	
	- Maternal HIV antibodies cross the placenta, making standard antibody tests unreliable in infants.
	- Case detection for HIV-exposed infants relies on **HIV-DNA PCR testing at 6 weeks of age** to establish a confirmatory diagnosis and link the infant to ART services.
	
	**E. Targeted Interventions (TI) for High-Risk Groups (HRGs)**
	
	- Special focus is placed on screening populations where the epidemic is concentrated. This includes Female Sex Workers (FSWs), Men who have Sex with Men (MSM), Injecting Drug Users (IDUs), and Transgenders.
	- **Bridge Populations:** Migrant workers and long-distance truckers are also actively targeted for testing as they form links between urban/rural areas and high/low-risk groups.
	- **Link Worker Scheme:** A community-based outreach strategy to detect cases and address HIV prevention needs of HRGs and vulnerable populations in highly vulnerable rural districts.
	
	**F. TB-HIV Co-ordination**
	
	- Since TB is the most common opportunistic infection, **Provider-Initiated HIV Testing and Counselling (PITC)** is implemented with a target of 100% coverage for all diagnosed TB patients and presumptive TB cases.
	
	**G. Blood Safety Programme**
	
	- Mandatory screening of all donated blood units for HIV (along with Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, Syphilis, and Malaria) acts as an essential surveillance and case detection tool.
	
	**4. LABORATORY DIAGNOSTIC TOOLS**
	
	- **Screening Tests:** Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) and Rapid Diagnostic Tests (RDTs). They are highly sensitive (>99.9%) and form the first line of case detection.
	- **Confirmatory Tests:** Western Blot is used to weed out false-positive results. It detects specific antibodies to viral core protein (p24) and envelope glycoprotein (gp41). When combined with ELISA, specificity is >99.99%.
	- **Self-Testing:** The current trend includes simple, cheap, reliable HIV self-testing kits whose results can be read on the spot without laboratory back-up.

	- Paucibacillary cases must receive combined multidrug therapy (MDT) because primary dapsone resistance is becoming widespread.

	- **Rifampicin:** 600 mg once a month, given under supervision.
	- **Clofazimine:** 300 mg once a month, given under supervision; **AND** 50 mg daily, self-administered.
	- **Dapsone:** 100 mg daily, self-administered.
	
	**3. STANDARD REGIMEN FOR CHILDREN (10–14 YEARS)**
	
	- **Rifampicin:** 450 mg once a month, given under supervision.
	- **Clofazimine:** 150 mg once a month, given under supervision; **AND** 50 mg every other day, self-administered.
	- **Dapsone:** 50 mg daily, self-administered.
	- _(High-Yield Note: Children under the age of 10 years should receive appropriately reduced doses of these three drugs)_.
	
	**4. DURATION OF TREATMENT**
	
	- The standard duration of treatment for Paucibacillary (PB) leprosy is **6 months**.
	- The patient is required to complete 6 monthly PB blister packs within a maximum period of **9 months**.

	**CASE FINDING METHODS IN LEPROSY**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION AND OBJECTIVES** The primary aim of case detection in leprosy is to identify and register all cases as early as possible after they become evident, thereby interrupting the transmission of infection and preventing the development of deformities. While ideally patients should seek medical care voluntarily, the insidious and symptomless nature of early leprosy, combined with social stigma, often leads to a lag period of 2 to 3 years before voluntary reporting. Therefore, active methods of case detection are strictly required.
	
	**2. EPIDEMIOLOGICAL CASE FINDING METHODS (BASED ON PREVALENCE)** The choice of the case-finding method is directly related to the prevalence rate of leprosy in the specific region:
	
	**A. Contact Surveys (For Low Endemicity Areas)**
	
	- **Indication:** Areas where the prevalence of leprosy is generally low (less than 1 case per 1000 population).
	- **Method:** The technique of choice is the thorough examination of all contacts, particularly household contacts, children, and persons reported to be suspected cases.
	- **Precaution:** Examinations must be arranged with discretion to avoid causing social alarm.
	
	**B. Group Surveys / "Skin Camps" (For Moderate Endemicity Areas)**
	
	- **Indication:** When the prevalence is about 1 per 1000 population or higher.
	- **Method:** Active screening of specifically selected groups such as preschool and school children, people living in slums, industrial labour, and military recruits for all types of skin diseases. Organizing "skin camps" brings out additional cases of leprosy while providing services for other skin conditions.
	- **Limitation (High-Yield):** The epidemiological value of school surveys is considerably diminished if the school enrolment in the area is less than 70 per cent of all children in the 6–14 years age group.
	
	**C. Mass Surveys (For Hyperendemic Areas)**
	
	- **Indication:** Recommended only in hyperendemic areas where the prevalence of leprosy is 10 or more per 1000 population.
	- **Method:** Total population surveys involving the examination of each and every individual, family by family, through house-to-house visits.
	- **Requirement:** A massive coverage of not less than 95 per cent of the population must be obtained for it to be effective. It requires high-quality, multipurpose teamwork backed fully by administration and local community participation.
	
	**3. NEWER INITIATIVES & NLEP STRATEGIES IN INDIA** To effectively reach the "hidden" or subclinical cases (the submerged portion of the disease iceberg), the National Leprosy Eradication Programme (NLEP) has implemented several innovative active case detection strategies:
	
	- **Involvement of ASHAs (ABSULS):** Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs) and multipurpose workers are actively involved in bringing out suspected leprosy cases from their villages before any sign of disability appears. This is operationalized under the "ASHA Based Surveillance for Leprosy Suspects" (ABSULS).
	- **Leprosy Case Detection Campaign (LCDC):** A specific campaign strategy aimed at early case detection and timely treatment in highly endemic districts.
	- **Focused Leprosy Campaign (FLC):** Involves house-to-house surveys in villages or urban areas, covering at least 300 households around a detected case (especially if a Grade 2 disability case is found).
	- **Special Plans for Hard-to-Reach Areas:** Designed to find cases in populations living in difficult terrains, naxalite-affected areas, and other geographically challenging locations where routine surveillance is poor.
	- **Neighbourhood Contact Tracing:** Undertaking immediate contact surveys to identify the source of infection in the neighbourhood of every newly detected child case or multibacillary case.

	**BACTERIOLOGICAL EXAMINATION IN LEPROSY: BACTERIAL AND MORPHOLOGICAL INDICES**
	
	Skin smears (slit and scrape method) are stained using the Ziehl-Neelsen method to detect _Mycobacterium leprae_. The microscopic findings are expressed using two high-yield indices: the Bacterial Index (BI) and the Morphological Index (MI).
	
	---
	
	### **1. BACTERIAL INDEX (BI)**
	
	- **Definition:** The Bacteriological (or Bacterial) Index indicates the density of leprosy bacilli in the smears.
	- **Components:** It includes both living (solid-staining) and dead (fragmented or granular) bacilli.
	- **Significance:** It is the only objective way of monitoring the benefit of treatment.
	- **Scale Used:** It is based on **Ridley's logarithmic scale**, which ranges from 0 to 6+. It is calculated by observing the number of bacilli in an average microscopic field using an oil-immersion objective.
	
	**Grading on Ridley's Scale:**
	
	- **0:** No bacilli in any of the 100 oil-immersion fields.
	- **1+:** 1–10 bacilli, on average, in 100 oil-immersion fields.
	- **2+:** 1–10 bacilli, on average, in 10 oil-immersion fields.
	- **3+:** 1–10 bacilli, on average, in each oil-immersion field.
	- **4+:** 10–100 bacilli, on average, in each oil-immersion field.
	- **5+:** 100–1000 bacilli, on average, in each oil-immersion field.
	- **6+:** More than 1000 bacilli, on average, in each oil-immersion field.
	
	**Calculation for the Patient:**
	
	- The overall BI of the patient is calculated by adding up the index from each site examined and dividing the total by the number of sites examined.
	- _Rule of Examination:_ When the BI is 1+ or 2+, at least 100 oil-immersion fields must be examined. When the BI is 3+, 4+, 5+, or 6+, at least 25 oil-immersion fields should be examined.
	
	---
	
	### **2. MORPHOLOGICAL INDEX (MI)**
	
	- **Definition:** It is the percentage of solid-staining bacilli (viable bacilli) out of the total number of free-standing bacilli examined in a stained smear.
	- **Procedure:** The MI is calculated after examining 200 pink-stained, free-standing (i.e., not in clumps) bacilli under the microscope.
	
	**Criteria for "Solid Rods" (Viable Bacilli):** To be classified as a solid rod, the bacillus must fulfill all of the following criteria:
	
	1. Uniform staining of the entire organism.
	2. Parallel sides.
	3. Rounded ends.
	4. Length is 5 times that of the width.
	
	**Calculation for the Patient:**
	
	- The total of the MIs for all sites divided by the number of sites gives the average MI for the body.
	
	**Clinical Significance (High-Yield):**
	
	- **Viability:** It is widely believed that _only_ solid-staining organisms are viable (living).
	- **Treatment Monitoring:** Accurate evaluation of the MI is a valuable indicator of the patient's response to treatment with anti-leprosy drugs, particularly during the first few months.
	- **Drug Resistance:** A static or rising MI helps to signal the emergence of drug resistance.
	
	_(Note: A related concept is the **Solid-fragmented-granular (SFG) percentage**, which records the percentages of solid, fragmented, and granular bacilli separately. It gives a better picture of bacterial morphology and is considered an even more sensitive indicator of the patient's response to treatment than the MI alone.)_


	1. What is morphological index?
	**MORPHOLOGICAL INDEX (MI) IN LEPROSY**
	
	**1. Definition**
	
	- The Morphological Index (MI) is defined as the percentage of solid-staining bacilli observed in a stained smear.
	- It is based on the widely accepted belief that only the solid-staining organisms represent viable (living) _M. leprae_ bacilli.
	
	**2. Procedure and Calculation**
	
	- During microscopic examination, the investigator distinguishes and counts the number of solid-staining organisms versus irregularly staining bacilli.
	- The MI is specifically calculated after examining **200 pink-stained, free-standing bacilli**, meaning bacilli that are separate and not in clumps.
	- **Average MI:** The average MI for the body is derived by adding the MI from each site examined and dividing that total by the number of sites.
	
	**3. Criteria for "Solid Rods" (High-Yield)** For a bacillus to be classified as a solid rod (viable organism), it must strictly fulfil the following criteria:
	
	- Uniform staining of the entire organism.
	- Parallel sides.
	- Rounded ends.
	- Length must be 5 times that of the width.
	
	**4. Clinical and Prognostic Significance**
	
	- **Evaluating Treatment Response:** The MI is a highly valuable clinical indicator of a patient's response to multi-drug therapy, especially during the crucial first few months of treatment.
	- **Signalling Drug Resistance:** A failure of the MI to fall, or a subsequent rise during treatment, serves as an early signal for the emergence of drug resistance.
	- _Note:_ Accurate determination of the MI is technically demanding and requires much skill and experience from the microscopist.
	
	**5. Recent Advances: SFG Percentage**
	
	- The **Solid-fragmented-granular (SFG) percentage** uses basically the same procedure as the MI, but it separately records the percentages of solid, fragmented, and granular bacilli.
	- Because it provides a more detailed breakdown, the SFG percentage gives a better overall picture of bacterial morphology and is considered a more sensitive indicator of the patient's response to treatment than the standard MI.

1. Define blanket treatment with suitable example
	**BLANKET TREATMENT (MASS TREATMENT)**
	
	**1. Definition**
	
	- Blanket treatment, also known as mass treatment, is a disease control strategy where therapeutic drugs or treatments are administered to the entire community or a specific population group, regardless of whether the individuals actually have the disease or not.
	
	**2. Rationale & Epidemiological Basis**
	
	- **High Latent Infection:** This approach is utilized when there are at least 4 to 5 cases of latent (incubating) infection for every single clinical case of active disease in the community.
	- **Interruption of Transmission:** Since individuals with latent infections can develop the disease at any time and act as a reservoir, treating everyone simultaneously becomes a critical factor in effectively interrupting disease transmission.
	
	**3. Suitable Examples (High-Yield)**
	
	- **Trachoma Control:** Blanket treatment is specifically indicated when the prevalence of severe and moderate trachoma is more than 3 per cent among children under 10 years of age. In this situation, the treatment consists of applying 1 per cent tetracycline ophthalmic ointment to _all_ children in the community, either twice daily for 5 days each month, or once daily for 10 days each month for 6 months.
	- **Yaws Eradication (Total Mass Treatment):** In hyperendemic areas where the prevalence of clinically active yaws is more than 10 per cent, the entire population is considered at risk. The entire community, including cases and healthy individuals, is given a mass dose of penicillin to interrupt transmission.
	- **Other Diseases:** The mass treatment approach is also highly relevant in the control of lymphatic filariasis, pinta, and bejel.

1. Universal precautions in HIV infection.
	**UNIVERSAL (STANDARD) PRECAUTIONS IN HIV INFECTION**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION**
	
	- Universal precautions, now broadly encompassed under "Standard Precautions", are mandatory infection control practices applied universally to the care of all patients, regardless of their presumed infection status.
	- The primary objective is to limit health care worker contact with all secretions, biological fluids, skin lesions, mucous membranes, and blood or body fluids.
	
	**2. PERSONAL PROTECTIVE EQUIPMENT (PPE) / BARRIER NURSING** Health care workers must use protective barriers to prevent exposure to potentially infective materials:
	
	- **Gloves:** Must be worn for each contact that may lead to contamination, including contact with blood, body fluids, secretions, excretions, mucous membranes, and contaminated items. Inexpensive plastic gloves are usually sufficient.
	- **Gowns/Aprons:** Gowns should be made of washable material, buttoned or tied at the back, and protected by a plastic apron if necessary. They must be worn where contamination of clothes is anticipated.
	- **Masks and Eye Protection:** Surgical masks made of cloth or paper, along with eye protection, should be used to protect the face and mucous membranes from splashes of blood or body fluids.
	
	**3. HAND HYGIENE & SAFE PRACTICES**
	
	- **Handwashing:** Hands must be washed promptly after contact with any infective material. Hands must also be washed immediately after removing gloves.
	- **No-Touch Technique:** Health care workers should employ a "no touch technique" wherever possible during patient care to minimize direct exposure.
	
	**4. MANAGEMENT OF SHARPS & INJECTIONS**
	
	- **Careful Handling:** All sharps (needles, scalpels, etc.) must be handled with extreme care to prevent percutaneous injuries.
	- **Disposable Items:** Pre-sterilized disposable syringes and needles should be used as far as possible.
	- **Rational Use:** Injections should be avoided unless they are absolutely necessary.
	
	**5. ENVIRONMENTAL CLEANING & EQUIPMENT CARE**
	
	- **Spill Management:** Any spills of infective material must be cleaned up promptly.
	- **Patient-Care Equipment:** Ensure that all equipment, supplies, and linen contaminated with infective material are safely discarded, disinfected, or sterilized between each patient use.
	- **Infected Linen:** If no washing machine is available for laundering linen soiled with infective material, the linen can be safely boiled.
	
	**6. WASTE DISPOSAL**
	
	- Appropriate and safe waste handling must be ensured for all infectious and biological wastes generated during patient care.
	
	**7. OCCUPATIONAL POST-EXPOSURE PROPHYLAXIS (PEP)**
	
	- If a breach in universal precautions occurs (e.g., a needle-stick injury or mucous membrane exposure to potentially infectious body fluids), Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP) is highly recommended for healthcare personnel to prevent HIV transmission.

	**COMPLICATIONS OF SEXUALLY TRANSMITTED DISEASES (STDs)**
	
	Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) can have serious consequences and sequelae beyond the immediate impact of the acute infection. While the overall incidence of STDs may be higher in men, the morbidity and long-term complications are generally much more severe in women.
	
	The complications can be systematically enumerated as follows:
	
	**1. GYNAECOLOGICAL COMPLICATIONS (IN WOMEN)**
	
	- **Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID):** Infections like Gonorrhoea and Chlamydia are major causes of PID.
	- **Infertility:** A long-term consequence of untreated PID and salpingitis.
	- **Ectopic Pregnancy:** Increased risk due to tubal damage from prior infections.
	- **Chronic Pelvic Pain:** A long-term sequela of PID.
	
	**2. COMPLICATIONS IN MEN**
	
	- **Epididymo-orchitis:** Inflammation of the epididymis and testes, commonly due to Gonococcal and Chlamydial infections.
	- **Sub-fertility:** Can occur as a long-term consequence of testicular tissue damage and scarring.
	- **Urethral Strictures:** A potential long-term consequence of gonococcal urethritis.

	1. Postexposure prophylaxis for HIV.
	**OCCUPATIONAL POST-EXPOSURE PROPHYLAXIS (PEP) FOR HIV**
	
	**1. INDICATIONS** Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) is highly recommended for healthcare personnel who experience occupational exposure to blood, tissues, or other body fluids that may contain the Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV).
	
	**2. TYPES OF HIGH-RISK EXPOSURES**
	
	- **Percutaneous injury:** e.g., a needlestick injury or a cut with a sharp object.
	- **Mucocutaneous contact:** Contact of mucous membranes or non-intact skin (e.g., chapped, abraded, or dermatitis-affected skin) with potentially infectious body fluids.
	
	**3. CLASSIFICATION OF BODY FLUIDS**
	
	- **Potentially Infectious Fluids:** Blood, semen, vaginal secretions, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), synovial fluid, pleural fluid, peritoneal fluid, pericardial fluid, and amniotic fluid.
	- **Non-Infectious Fluids (unless visibly bloody):** Faeces, nasal secretions, saliva, sputum, sweat, tears, urine, and vomitus.
	
	**4. TIMING AND DURATION (CRITICAL)**
	
	- **Initiation:** PEP must be initiated as soon as possible after the exposure, preferably within hours.
	- **Do not delay:** Do not delay the administration of PEP while waiting for the source patient's HIV test results.
	- **The 72-Hour Window:** Animal studies demonstrate that PEP is likely to be less effective if started more than 72 hours after exposure. This is because HIV replication occurs in regional lymph nodes within 48 to 72 hours, followed by viremia within 72 to 120 hours of inoculation.
	- **Late Presentation:** Initiating therapy after a longer interval (e.g., 1 week) might still be considered only for exposures that represent an extremely high risk for transmission.
	- **Duration of Therapy:** The full course of PEP must be completed for **4 weeks**.
	
	**5. RECOMMENDED DRUG REGIMENS (Duration: 4 Weeks)**
	
	**A. Preferred Regimens for Adults** Must include a 3-drug combination:
	
	- **Regimen 1:** Raltegravir (400 mg twice daily) + Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/Emtricitabine (300 mg/200 mg once daily).
	- **Regimen 2:** Dolutegravir (50 mg once daily) + Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/Emtricitabine (300 mg/200 mg once daily). _(High-Yield Note: Lamivudine may be substituted for Emtricitabine in either of the above regimens)_.
	
	**B. Paediatric Regimens (Children < 10 years)**
	
	- **Lopinavir/ritonavir (LPV/r)** is recommended as the preferred third drug for post-exposure prophylaxis in children younger than 10 years.
	- Alternative options include ATV/r, RAL, DRV, EFV, and NVP based on the child's age.
	
	**6. SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS IN WOMEN**
	
	- **Pregnancy & Teratogenicity:** Dolutegravir is considered an acceptable alternative to raltegravir for patients intending to become pregnant or presenting in the first trimester. However, they must be advised of the small risk of teratogenicity when given during the first trimester.
	- **Counselling:** Non-pregnant individuals of childbearing potential must be counselled strictly about the need for birth control while completing the PEP regimen.

	**COMMON OPPORTUNISTIC INFECTIONS IN AIDS**
	
	AIDS represents the end-stage of HIV infection, characterized by severe immunodeficiency due to the destruction of CD4+ T-helper cells. This leaves the patient vulnerable to a host of life-threatening opportunistic infections (OIs) and unusual malignancies. Death is often the result of uncontrolled or untreatable opportunistic infections.
	
	The common opportunistic infections, categorized by the type of causative pathogen, are as follows:
	
	**1. BACTERIAL INFECTIONS**
	
	- **Tuberculosis (Mycobacterium tuberculosis):** TB is the most common opportunistic disease associated with HIV in many developing regions and is typically seen relatively early in the disease course. HIV-positive individuals are 30 to 50 times more likely to develop active TB. It is the leading cause of HIV-associated mortality. It can present as pulmonary or extrapulmonary TB.
	- **Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC):** Disseminated MAC infection is a severe bacterial complication that occurs in at least one-third of AIDS patients.
	- **Severe/Recurrent Bacterial Infections:** Include recurrent severe bacterial pneumonia, septicaemia (including non-typhoidal Salmonella), meningitis, bone/joint infections, and acute necrotizing ulcerative stomatitis.
	
	**2. FUNGAL INFECTIONS**
	
	- **Oropharyngeal and Oesophageal Candidiasis:** Caused by a yeast fungus, it is highly common and often the first symptom early in the course of the disease. Oral thrush presents with soreness, redness, and white plaques on the tongue, mouth, and throat. Oesophageal infection presents with dysphagia (difficulty swallowing) and retrosternal pain.
	- **Cryptococcal Meningitis:** A serious fungal infection of the central nervous system that tends to occur when the T-helper cell count drops to around 100. It usually presents with fever, headache, vomiting, and neck stiffness.
	- **Other Fungal Infections:** Penicillosis, disseminated histoplasmosis, and coccidioidomycosis are also severe fungal infections noted in advanced AIDS.
	
	**3. PARASITIC / PROTOZOAL INFECTIONS**
	
	- **Pneumocystis carinii (jiroveci) Pneumonia (PCP):** A major parasitic lung infection that tends to occur when the T-helper cell count drops to around 100. Symptoms include a dry, non-productive cough, shortness of breath, occasional pain on breathing, fever, and weight loss.
	- **Toxoplasma Encephalitis:** A protozoal infection of the central nervous system that typically appears when the CD4 count is around 100. It presents with focal neurological signs (such as mild hemiplegia or stroke) resulting from localized brain damage, seizures, or altered mental status.
	- **Cryptosporidiosis and Isosporiasis:** _Cryptosporidium_ and _Isospora_ are important causes of persistent, chronic diarrhoea with severe wasting (often contributing to "slim disease") in immunodeficient patients.
	
	**4. VIRAL INFECTIONS**
	
	- **Cytomegalovirus (CMV) Retinitis:** A late-stage opportunistic infection that occurs when T-helper cell counts fall below 50. It causes inflammation of the retina which may lead to blindness. CMV can also infect other organs.
	- **Herpes Zoster (Shingles):** Viral inflammation of the central nervous system presenting with localized pain and burning sensations, followed by skin blistering (vesicle eruption) and ulceration.
	- **Herpes Simplex:** Chronic, progressive, or disseminated herpes simplex infection (lasting for more than 1 month or visceral at any site) is a classic AIDS-defining viral infection.
	- **Hairy Leukoplakia:** Associated with viral infection, presenting as white patches on the sides of the tongue in vertical folds resembling corrugations.
	- **Progressive Multifocal Leukoencephalopathy (PML):** A severe demyelinating viral disease of the central nervous system seen in Stage 4 HIV disease.
	
	**5. OPPORTUNISTIC MALIGNANCIES (VIRAL-ASSOCIATED)**
	
	- **Kaposi Sarcoma:** A malignancy typically seen relatively early in the disease. It presents as reddish-brown or purplish plaques or nodules on the skin and mucous membranes (e.g., mouth or gut) and can be rapidly progressive or invasive.
	- **Lymphomas and Cancers:** Non-Hodgkin's lymphoma (a cancer of the lymph nodes and spleen) is a late complication of AIDS. Invasive cervical carcinoma (linked to Human Papilloma Virus) is also strongly associated with advanced HIV.

	**MOTHER-TO-CHILD TRANSMISSION (MTCT) OF HIV**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION AND TRANSMISSION DYNAMICS**
	
	- **Modes of Transmission:** The Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) can pass from an infected mother to her foetus or infant through three primary routes: across the placenta (in utero), during delivery (intrapartum), or via breast-feeding (postnatal).
	- **Transmission Rate:** In the absence of any preventive interventions, the rate of mother-to-child transmission ranges from 20 to 25 per cent.
	- **Timing:** Transmission during the peripartum period (around the time of birth) accounts for one-third to two-thirds of the overall numbers infected, depending heavily on whether breast-feeding transmission occurs or not.
	- **Risk Factors:** The risk of infection to the child is significantly higher if the mother is newly infected or if she has already developed advanced AIDS. Infants and children who acquire HIV progress rapidly to AIDS.
	
	**2. GENERAL PRINCIPLES OF PREVENTION**
	
	- Transmission of HIV from mother to child can be prevented almost entirely by utilizing anti-retroviral (ARV) drug prophylaxis, performing an elective caesarean section before the onset of labour and rupture of membranes, and by completely refraining from breast-feeding.
	- In industrialized nations, using a substantial efficacy triple combination of drugs has reduced the transmission rate to below 2 per cent in the absence of breast-feeding.
	- However, in economically poor and developing countries, an elective caesarean section is often not a safe or feasible option, making pharmacological prophylaxis and safe feeding practices the cornerstone of prevention.
	
	**3. EVOLUTION OF PPTCT PROGRAMME IN INDIA** The Prevention of Parent-To-Child Transmission (PPTCT) programme was launched in India in 2002. The goal is to offer HIV testing to every pregnant woman (universal coverage) to detect all HIV-positive mothers and eliminate MTCT.
	
	- **2002 Strategy:** Initially started using single-dose Nevirapine (SD-NVP) prophylaxis for the HIV-positive pregnant woman during labour and for her newborn immediately after birth.
	- **2012 Strategy:** Transitioned to WHO's "Option B", utilizing multi-drug ARV prophylaxis.
	- **Current Strategy (Since 2013-14):** India adopted the policy to initiate **lifelong Antiretroviral Therapy (ART)** using a triple-drug regimen for all pregnant and breast-feeding women living with HIV, absolutely regardless of their CD4 count or WHO clinical stage. WHO has strongly recommended this lifelong ART approach for all pregnant and breast-feeding women since 2016.
	
	**4. ESSENTIAL PACKAGE OF PPTCT SERVICES (HIGH-YIELD)** The essential package of PPTCT services provided in India includes the following 11 core components:
	
	1. **Routine Screening:** Routine offer of HIV counselling and testing to all pregnant women enrolled into antenatal care, utilizing an "opt-out" option.
	2. **Family-Centric Approach:** Ensuring the active involvement of the spouse and other family members, moving away from a strictly "ANC-Centric" approach.
	3. **Maternal ART:** Provision of life-long ART (comprising Tenofovir + Lamivudine + Efavirenz or TDF+3TC+EFV) to all pregnant and breast-feeding HIV-infected women, regardless of CD4 count and clinical stage.
	4. **Safe Delivery:** Promotion of institutional deliveries for all HIV-infected pregnant women.
	5. **Associated Care:** Provision of care for associated conditions, including Sexually Transmitted Infections/Reproductive Tract Infections (STI/RTI), Tuberculosis (TB), and other opportunistic infections.
	6. **Nutritional & Psychosocial Support:** Provision of proper nutrition, counselling, and psychosocial support for HIV-infected pregnant women.
	7. **Infant Feeding Practices:** Provision of counselling and support for the initiation of exclusive breast-feeding within one hour of delivery as the preferred option, which should be continued for 6 months.
	8. **Infant ARV Prophylaxis:** Provision of ARV prophylaxis to the infants from birth up to a minimum of 6 months.
	9. **Routine Healthcare Integration:** Integrating the follow-up of HIV-exposed infants into routine healthcare services, including the national immunization schedule.
	10. **Early Infant Diagnosis (EID) & CPT:** Ensuring the prompt initiation of Co-trimoxazole Prophylactic Therapy (CPT) and Early Infant Diagnosis (EID) using HIV-DNA PCR at 6 weeks of age onwards.
	11. **Community Outreach:** Strengthening community follow-up and outreach through local community networks to comprehensively support HIV-positive pregnant women and their families.
	
	**5. SPECIFIC MANAGEMENT OF THE HIV-EXPOSED INFANT**
	
	- **Co-trimoxazole Prophylaxis:** Co-trimoxazole prophylaxis is recommended for HIV-exposed infants starting from 4-6 weeks of age. This must be continued until HIV infection has been definitively excluded by an age-appropriate HIV test to establish a final diagnosis, which is done after the complete cessation of breast-feeding.
	- **Immunization (BCG Rule):** Children who are known to be HIV-infected, even if asymptomatic, should not be immunized with the BCG vaccine due to the risk of disseminated BCG disease. However, because of the high coverage of PPTCT interventions, the majority of infants born to HIV-infected mothers remain uninfected and should receive the BCG vaccine to protect against severe TB.
	- **Virological Testing:** Accurate diagnosis in the first year of life cannot rely on standard antibody tests because maternal HIV antibodies cross the placenta. Therefore, direct demonstration of the virus using HIV-DNA PCR is required, typically performed at or around 6 weeks of age.

	Approximately 25 per cent of leprosy patients who are not treated at an early stage develop deformities of the hands, feet, and face. As a single disease entity, leprosy is one of the foremost causes of deformities and crippling worldwide.

	- **Ear:** Nodules on the ear and elongated ear lobules.

	- **Paralytic / Motor:** Claw hand and wrist-drop.

	- **Trophic / Secondary:** Ulcers, swollen hand, and absorption (shortening or loss) of digits.
	
	**C. Feet**
	
	- **Paralytic / Motor:** Foot-drop and clawing of the toes.
	- **Structural:** Inversion of the foot, collapsed foot, and swollen foot.
	- **Trophic / Secondary:** Plantar ulcers, callosities, and absorption of the toes.
	
	**D. Other Deformities**
	
	- Gynaecomastia (enlargement of male breasts) and perforation of the palate.

	**2. HIGH-YIELD NOTE ON CLAWING**
	
	**Definition and Presentation** Clawing is a classic and highly visible deformity in leprosy, presenting as a flexion contracture of the digits. It most commonly manifests as **Claw Hand** or **Clawing of the toes**.
	
	**Pathogenesis and Mechanism**
	
	- **Primary Cause:** Clawing is a direct result of motor paralysis caused by the infiltration and damage of peripheral nerve trunks by _M. leprae_.
	- **Sequence of Events:** The specific process leading to this deformity follows a predictable pathway: Nerve damage $\rightarrow$ Loss of motor function $\rightarrow$ Muscle weakness $\rightarrow$ Contracture (Clawing).
	
	**Nerves Involved**
	
	- **Claw Hand:** Results primarily from the involvement and destruction of the **Ulnar nerve** and/or the **Median nerve**, which supply the intrinsic muscles of the hand.
	- **Claw Toes:** Results from the involvement of the nerves supplying the foot, specifically the **Posterior tibial nerve**.
	
	**Consequences of Clawing** Because clawing is almost always accompanied by a loss of sensation (due to simultaneous sensory nerve damage), the abnormally postured fingers and toes are highly vulnerable to repeated, unnoticed injuries and pressure. Over time, these neglected injuries lead to secondary secondary disabilities such as chronic ulcers, secondary bacterial infections, and the ultimate absorption (auto-amputation) of the affected digits.
14. Describe the lepromin test
	**LEPROMIN TEST**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION**
	
	- The lepromin test is an immunological test used primarily to evaluate the cell-mediated immunity (CMI) of an individual against _Mycobacterium leprae_.
	- It is strictly **not a diagnostic test**.
	
	**2. PROCEDURE**
	
	- **Dosage & Route:** 0.1 ml of lepromin antigen is injected intradermally.
	- **Site:** The inner aspect of the forearm.
	- **Readings:** The reaction is routinely read twice: at 48 hours and at 21 days.
	
	**3. TYPES OF REACTIONS (HIGH-YIELD)** Two distinct types of positive reactions are observed based on the timing and immunological mechanism:
	
	**A. Early Reaction (Fernandez Reaction)**
	
	- **Timing:** Develops rapidly within 24 to 48 hours and typically disappears after 3 to 4 days.
	- **Clinical Feature:** Redness and induration at the injection site.
	- **Criteria for Positivity:** The test is read at 48 hours; it is positive if the diameter of the red area exceeds **10 mm**.
	- **Mechanism:** It is a delayed hypersensitivity reaction induced by the "soluble" constituents of the leprosy bacilli. It corresponds to the Mantoux reaction seen in tuberculosis.
	- **Significance:** Indicates whether a person has been previously sensitized by exposure to or infection by the leprosy bacilli.
	
	**B. Late Reaction (Mitsuda Reaction)**
	
	- **Timing:** Develops late, becomes apparent in 7 to 10 days, reaches its maximum size in 3 to 4 weeks, and is officially read at **21 days**.
	- **Clinical Feature:** Formation of a nodule at the inoculation site, which may occasionally ulcerate and heal with scarring (if a crude antigen is used).
	- **Criteria for Positivity:** A nodule of more than **5 mm** in diameter at the end of 21 days.
	- **Mechanism:** Induced by the bacillary (particulate) component of the antigen.
	- **Significance:** Indicates an active and competent cell-mediated immune (CMI) response.
	
	**4. EPIDEMIOLOGICAL & CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE (VALUE OF THE TEST)** Although not used for diagnosing leprosy, the lepromin test has immense clinical and prognostic value:
	
	- **Evaluation of Immune Status:** It is widely accepted as a highly useful tool to evaluate the cell-mediated immune status of a leprosy patient.
	- **Classification of Leprosy:** It aids in classifying the type of disease along the leprosy spectrum. The test is strongly positive in typical tuberculoid cases and becomes progressively weaker towards the lepromatous end, with typical lepromatous cases being entirely lepromin negative (indicating a failure of CMI).
	- **Estimating Prognosis:** A lepromin-negative individual is at a significantly higher risk of developing progressive, multibacillary (lepromatous) leprosy. Conversely, lepromin-positive individuals either escape clinical disease entirely or develop the milder, paucibacillary form.
	
	**5. LIMITATIONS (WHY IT IS NOT A DIAGNOSTIC TEST)**
	
	- **False Positives:** Positive results can occur in non-cases. For instance, BCG vaccination is capable of converting the lepromin reaction from negative to positive in a large proportion of healthy individuals. In endemic areas, up to 80% or more of healthy adults over 19 years of age may be lepromin positive.
	- **False Negatives:** It yields negative results in active lepromatous and near-lepromatous cases despite the presence of the disease, due to specific CMI anergy.

	**COUNSELLING FOR AN HIV POSITIVE INDIVIDUAL**
	
	Counselling is a fundamental component of HIV/AIDS management, serving as a gateway to prevention, treatment, care, and support services. It is guided globally by the **WHO's "5 Cs" principle** for all HIV testing services: **C**onsent, **C**onfidentiality, **C**ounselling, **C**orrect test results, and **C**onnection to care and treatment.
	
	For an HIV-positive individual, counselling is a continuous process spanning from the initial diagnosis to life-long antiretroviral therapy (ART) management.
	
	**1. POST-TEST COUNSELLING & DISCLOSURE**
	
	- **Result Disclosure:** The positive test result must be communicated clearly, maintaining strict confidentiality.
	- **Implications & Support:** The patient must be counselled regarding the implications of the disease for themselves and their sexual partners.
	- **Psychosocial Support:** Assess the patient's immediate emotional reaction. For People Living with HIV (PLHIV) who have psychosocial concerns or comorbid substance use, it is crucial to involve relevant persons like family members, relatives, peers, or dedicated counsellors for enhanced support.
	
	**2. ART PREPAREDNESS COUNSELLING** Before initiating Antiretroviral Therapy (ART), thorough preparedness counselling is mandatory.
	
	- **Treatment Adherence:** Patients must be educated that ART is a life-long commitment. Strict adherence is emphasized to achieve viral suppression and prevent the emergence of drug-resistant mutations.
	- **Adherence Monitoring:** Adherence must be assessed by the counsellor at every visit, providing necessary support.
	- **Follow-up:** The ART counsellor must proactively contact the patient to check on their general well-being within 2 weeks of ART initiation.
	
	**3. POSITIVE LIVING & LIFESTYLE MODIFICATION**
	
	- **Nutrition and Self-Care:** Patients are counselled on maintaining adequate nutrition, personal hygiene, and self-care practices to support their immune system.
	- **Positive Prevention:** Educate the patient on "positive prevention" strategies, which include preventing the onward transmission of the virus to others. This involves education on safe sexual behaviour and the correct, consistent use of condoms.
	
	**4. COUNSELLING FOR PREGNANT WOMEN (PPTCT)** Under the Prevention of Parent-To-Child Transmission (PPTCT) programme, HIV-positive pregnant women require specialized counselling.
	
	- **Family-Centric Approach:** Move from an "ANC-Centric" to a "Family-Centric" approach by ensuring the active involvement of the spouse and other family members.
	- **Infant Feeding Practices:** Provide counselling and support for the initiation of exclusive breast-feeding within one hour of delivery as the preferred option, continued for 6 months.
	- **Maternal Health:** Offer nutritional, psychological, and psychosocial support.
	
	**5. COUNSELLING FOR CO-MORBIDITIES & OPPORTUNISTIC INFECTIONS**
	
	- **Tuberculosis (TB):** Since TB is the most common opportunistic infection, PLHIV should be counselled about TB symptoms (cough, fever, weight loss, night sweats). They should be educated on the need for Isoniazid Preventive Therapy (IPT) and advised on cough hygiene measures.
	- **Family Planning:** Female patients, particularly those on treatments with potential teratogenic effects (like certain second-line TB drugs or specific ARVs), must receive special counselling on safe family planning and contraceptive methods.

1. Write epidemiology of HIV infection. What are the preventive and control measures taken in India?
	**EPIDEMIOLOGY OF HIV INFECTION**
	
	**1. Problem Statement**
	
	- **Global:** In 2023, there were an estimated 39.9 million people living with HIV globally, with 1.3 million new infections and 6,30,000 AIDS-related deaths.
	- **India:** India has a "concentrated epidemic" which is driven primarily by sexual transmission (accounting for 86% of new infections). The overall adult prevalence continues to be low at 0.20%, and the epidemic size has stabilized. In 2023, India had an estimated 25.44 lakh people living with HIV, 68.45 thousand new infections, and 35.87 thousand AIDS-related deaths.
	
	**2. Epidemiological Determinants** **A. Agent Factors**
	
	- **Agent:** The Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) is a retrovirus (RNA virus) measuring 1/10,000th of a millimetre in diameter. There are two types: HIV-1 and HIV-2.
	- **Pathogenesis:** The virus replicates in actively dividing T4 lymphocytes (CD4+ helper cells) and destroys them, breaking down the body's immune system.
	- **Resistance:** HIV is easily killed by heat and is readily inactivated by ether, acetone, 20% ethanol, and beta-propiolactone, but it is relatively resistant to ionizing radiation and ultraviolet light.
	- **Reservoir:** Cases and symptomless carriers. Once infected, a person remains infected for life.
	- **Source of Infection:** The virus is found in the greatest concentration in blood, semen, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Lower concentrations are detected in tears, saliva, breast milk, urine, and cervical/vaginal secretions.
	
	**B. Host Factors**
	
	- **Age and Sex:** Most cases occur among sexually active persons aged 20–49 years. Globally, 44% of all new infections occur among women and girls, who are biologically more susceptible to the virus.
	- **High-Risk Groups:** Key populations include female sex workers (FSWs), men who have sex with men (MSM), transgender people, injecting drug users (IDUs), and bridge populations like migrant workers and long-distance truck drivers.
	- **Immunity:** Infection causes a profound lymphopenia and a reversed ratio of T-helper to T-suppressor cells, leading to severe opportunistic infections and neoplasms.
	
	**C. Modes of Transmission**
	
	- **Sexual Transmission:** First and foremost route. Any unprotected vaginal, anal, or oral sex can transmit the virus. The presence of another sexually transmitted disease (STD) increases the risk of HIV transmission by 8–10 times.
	- **Blood Contact:** Transmitted by contaminated whole blood, platelets, and factors VIII and IX. Needle-sharing among injecting drug users is a major route of transmission in India's north-eastern states.
	- **Maternal-Foetal (Vertical) Transmission:** Can pass from an infected mother to the foetus through the placenta, during delivery, or via breast-feeding. Without intervention, the transmission rate varies from 20 to 25%.
	
	**D. Incubation Period**
	
	- Highly variable, ranging from a few months to 10 years or even more from initial infection to the development of clinical AIDS.

	**PREVENTIVE AND CONTROL MEASURES IN INDIA**
	
	The National AIDS Control Programme (NACP) operates with the vision of achieving an AIDS-free India under the "Three Zeros" goal: zero new infections, zero AIDS-related deaths, and zero discrimination. The programme also aims for the UNAIDS "95-95-95" fast-track targets (95% know their status, 95% of those diagnosed are on ART, 95% of those on ART are virally suppressed).
	
	**1. Primary Prevention Strategies**
	
	- **Information, Education and Communication (IEC):** Health education is a key tool to generate awareness and sustain safe sexual behaviours. Programmes include the Adolescence Education Programme in schools and Red Ribbon Clubs in colleges to encourage peer-to-peer messaging and voluntary blood donation.
	- **Targeted Interventions (TI):** Aimed at high-risk groups (FSWs, MSM, IDUs) and bridge populations (truckers, migrants). Interventions include condom promotion, treatment of STIs, and specific IDU interventions like needle/syringe exchange programmes and Opioid Substitution Therapy (OST).
	- **Link Worker Scheme:** A community-based outreach strategy to address HIV prevention and care needs in highly vulnerable rural areas.
	- **Blood Safety Programme:** Mandatory screening of all donated blood for HIV, Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, Malaria, and Syphilis. Professional blood donation is banned, and voluntary blood donation is heavily promoted.
	- **STI/RTI Control:** Integration of STI management at the primary care level using a "Syndromic Approach". NACO provides free pre-packed, colour-coded STI/RTI kits to designated "Suraksha Clinics".
	
	**2. Prevention of Parent-to-Child Transmission (PPTCT)**
	
	- **Universal Screening:** Routine offer of HIV counselling and testing to all pregnant women enrolled in antenatal care.
	- **Maternal ART:** Provision of lifelong ART (using a triple-drug regimen of TDF + 3TC + EFV) for all pregnant and breast-feeding women living with HIV, regardless of their CD4 count or clinical stage.
	- **Safe Delivery & Infant Care:** Promotion of institutional deliveries and provision of ARV prophylaxis to exposed infants from birth up to a minimum of 6 months.
	- **Early Infant Diagnosis (EID):** Confirmatory testing using HIV-DNA PCR is done for exposed infants at 6 weeks of age to ensure rapid linkage to treatment.
	
	**3. Care, Support, and Treatment (CST)**
	
	- **"Treat All" Policy:** Lifelong Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) is recommended for all people living with HIV, irrespective of clinical status or CD4 cell count, with an emphasis on rapid or same-day ART initiation.
	- **First-line ART Regimen:** The preferred fixed-dose combination (FDC) for adults and adolescents (>10 years, >30 kg) is the TLD regimen: Tenofovir (300 mg) + Lamivudine (300 mg) + Dolutegravir (50 mg) given as a single daily pill.
	- **Higher-level ART:** Second-line and third-line ART are provided for patients experiencing treatment failure through Centres of Excellence (CoE) and ART-Plus centres.
	- **Monitoring:** Regular clinical and laboratory follow-up, including CD4 count every 6 months and viral load testing at 6 months, 12 months, and annually thereafter.
	
	**4. Management of Opportunistic Infections (OIs)**
	
	- **Co-trimoxazole Prophylactic Therapy (CPT):** Administered to eligible PLHIV to prevent severe bacterial, fungal, and protozoan infections.
	- **TB-HIV Coordination:** TB is the most common opportunistic infection. All patients at ART centres are screened for TB using a 4-symptom complex. Patients without active TB are provided with Isoniazid Preventive Therapy (IPT) for 6 months. Patients with active TB are started on anti-TB treatment followed by ART.
	
	**5. Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP)**
	
	- **Occupational Safety:** Recommended for healthcare personnel exposed to potentially infectious body fluids via percutaneous injury or mucosal contact.
	- **Regimen & Timing:** PEP should be initiated as soon as possible, preferably within hours (and not delayed beyond 72 hours). The preferred regimen is a 4-week course of Raltegravir (or Dolutegravir) plus Tenofovir/Emtricitabine (or Lamivudine).

	**FACTORS RESPONSIBLE FOR THE SPREAD OF HIV INFECTION IN INDIA**
	
	The HIV epidemic in India is concentrated and driven by several behavioural, social, and biological factors.
	
	**1. Modes of Transmission**
	
	- **Sexual Transmission:** This is the primary driver of the epidemic in India, accounting for approximately 86 per cent of new HIV infections.
	- **Parent-to-Child (Vertical) Transmission:** Transmission from an infected mother to her foetus or infant during pregnancy, delivery, or breast-feeding.
	- **Injecting Drug Users (IDUs):** A major route of transmission, particularly in India's north-eastern states (like Manipur and Nagaland). Risk is amplified by early initiation of drug use, high frequency of injecting, and the sharing of needles and syringes.
	- **Contaminated Blood and Blood Products:** Transmission through transfusion of infected whole blood, platelets, and plasma factors.
	
	**2. High-Risk and Bridge Populations**
	
	- **High-Risk Groups (HRGs):** The epidemic is heavily concentrated among female sex workers (FSWs), men who have sex with men (MSM), and transgender people.
	- **Migrant Workers (Bridge Population):** Migrants form a crucial link between urban and rural areas, often transmitting the virus from high-risk groups to low-risk general populations (e.g., their wives in rural northern India).
	- **Truck Drivers (Bridge Population):** Truckers exhibit high vulnerability as they frequently engage in unprotected sex with female sex workers as well as their regular partners, facilitating the spread into the general population.
	- **Vulnerable Youth:** Impoverished, unemployed, mobile youth, and street children are at high risk due to a lack of information and limited access to preventive measures.
	
	**3. Biological and Co-infection Factors**
	
	- **Presence of STDs:** A co-existing sexually transmitted disease (like syphilis, chancroid, or herpes) in either partner facilitates HIV transmission, making the risk 8 to 10 times higher due to mucosal ulcerations and inflammation.
	- **Gender Vulnerability:** Women, especially younger adolescent girls, are biologically more susceptible to HIV infection because of a larger exposed mucosal surface and higher viral concentrations in semen.
	
	**4. Socio-Economic and Behavioural Factors**
	
	- **Poverty and Prostitution:** In developing nations, poverty and lack of employment often drive commercial sex work, which acts as a major reservoir for the virus.
	- **Urbanization and Migration:** Rapid urbanization, geographical mobility, and relative isolation from family promote casual sexual relationships.
	- **Gender Inequalities:** Differential access to services, lack of negotiation power for safe sex, and sexual violence heavily increase women's vulnerability to HIV.

	**PERSONAL PROTECTIVE MEASURES AGAINST HIV INFECTION**
	
	Prevention remains the mainstay of HIV control. The following personal protective and biomedical interventions are recommended to prevent HIV acquisition:
	
	**1. Sexual Preventive Measures**
	
	- **Safe Sexual Behaviour:** Avoiding indiscriminate sex, having mutually faithful monogamous relationships, and reducing the number of sexual partners.
	- **Condom Use:** Consistent and correct use of male and female condoms along with condom-compatible lubricants. Male condoms can reduce heterosexual transmission by at least 80 per cent.
	- **Voluntary Medical Male Circumcision (VMMC):** Promoted as it demonstrates an approximately 60 per cent reduction in the risk of female-to-male sexual transmission.
	
	**2. Prevention of Blood-Borne Transmission**
	
	- **Safe Injection Practices:** Strictly using pre-sterilized, disposable syringes and needles, and avoiding injections unless absolutely necessary.
	- **Avoiding Shared Items:** Abstaining from sharing personal hygiene items that may come into contact with blood, such as razors and toothbrushes.
	- **Needle/Syringe Programmes:** For injecting drug users, participating in needle and syringe exchange programmes to avoid sharing contaminated equipment.
	- **Opioid Substitution Therapy:** Engaging in treatments like methadone or buprenorphine therapy to reduce injecting behaviour.
	
	**3. Medical and Pharmacological Prophylaxis**
	
	- **Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP):** The use of antiretroviral (ARV) drugs by HIV-negative individuals at high risk to effectively prevent HIV acquisition.
	- **Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP):** Taking a combination of ARV drugs (e.g., Dolutegravir + Tenofovir + Emtricitabine) as soon as possible after a potential exposure (preferably within hours, and no later than 72 hours) for 4 weeks.
	- **Treatment of STDs:** Prompt diagnosis and treatment of co-existing sexually transmitted diseases to reduce the biological susceptibility to HIV.
	
	**4. Occupational Precautions (For Healthcare Workers)**
	
	- **Standard Precautions:** Washing hands promptly after contact with infective material, and using a "no touch" technique wherever possible.
	- **Personal Protective Equipment (PPE):** Wearing gloves, gowns, masks, and eye protection when in contact with blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, or non-intact skin.
	- **Safe Handling of Sharps:** Extreme care in handling and safely disposing of needles and sharp instruments to prevent percutaneous injuries.
