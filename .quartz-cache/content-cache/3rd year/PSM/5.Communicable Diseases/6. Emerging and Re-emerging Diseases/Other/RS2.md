1. Describe the natural history (epidemiology) of tetanus and outline the strategies adopted for its prevention and control
	1. **EPIDEMIOLOGY AND CONTROL OF TETANUS**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION** Tetanus is an acute disease induced by the exotoxin of _Clostridium tetani_, clinically characterized by persistent muscular rigidity punctuated by painful paroxysmal spasms of the voluntary muscles. Classical presentations include:
	
	- **Trismus:** "Lock-jaw" (spasm of masseters).
	- **Risus sardonicus:** Spasm of facial muscles.
	- **Opisthotonos:** Spasm of muscles of the neck and back. The case fatality rate is very high, varying from 40 to 80 per cent in untreated cases.
	
	**2. EPIDEMIOLOGICAL DETERMINANTS (NATURAL HISTORY)**
	
	**A. Agent Factors**
	
	- **Agent:** _Clostridium tetani_, a gram-positive, anaerobic, spore-bearing organism.
	- **Spores:** Terminal spores give the organism a characteristic "drum-stick" appearance. They are highly resistant to heat, phenol, and cresol. They are best destroyed by autoclaving for 15 minutes at 120°C or by gamma irradiation.
	- **Reservoir of Infection:** The natural habitat of the organism is soil and dust. Bacilli are also found in the faeces of herbivores (like cows and horses) and humans.
	- **Exotoxin:** The bacilli multiply under anaerobic conditions and produce a highly potent exotoxin called "tetanospasmin". Its principal action is to block the inhibition of spinal reflexes.
	- **Period of Communicability:** None. Tetanus is not transmitted from person to person.
	
	**B. Host Factors**
	
	- **Age:** Commonly affects the active age group (5 to 40 years) due to higher predisposition to trauma. Neonatal tetanus affects newborns.
	- **Sex:** Higher incidence in males (more exposed to trauma, and apparently more sensitive to the toxin). Females are at special risk during delivery or abortion (puerperal tetanus).
	- **Occupation:** Agricultural workers are at special risk due to frequent soil contact.
	- **Immunity:** There is **no natural immunity**; it must be acquired via active immunization. A clinical attack of tetanus does _not_ confer immunity, because the lethal dose of the toxin is too small to stimulate an immune response.
	- **Herd Immunity:** Tetanus is a unique disease in which herd immunity does _not_ protect the individual.
	
	**C. Environmental and Social Factors**
	
	- Tetanus is more common in rural areas than in urban areas, particularly where floors are earthen and animals live close to humans.
	- Social factors promoting transmission include unhygienic customs (e.g., application of dust or animal dung to wounds), unhygienic delivery practices (using unsterilized instruments to cut the umbilical cord), and lack of primary health care.
	
	**3. MODE OF TRANSMISSION & INCUBATION PERIOD**
	
	- **Transmission:** Infection is acquired by the contamination of wounds with tetanus spores. This includes trivial pin pricks, abrasions, puncture wounds, burns, unsterile surgery, unsterile division of the umbilical cord, ear infections (otitis media), and animal bites.
	- **Incubation Period:** Usually 6 to 10 days, but can range from 1 day to several months. Long incubation periods occur when spores lie dormant in wounds. A shorter incubation period generally indicates a worse prognosis.
	
	**4. CLINICAL TYPES OF TETANUS**
	
	1. **Traumatic:** Following injury (major or trivial).
	2. **Puerperal:** Follows abortion or normal labour; the post-abortal uterus is highly favourable for spore germination.
	3. **Neonatal Tetanus:** Typically caused by infection of the umbilical stump. The first symptoms appear around the 7th day, hence locally called the **"8th-day disease"**.
	4. **Otogenic:** Infection introduced into the ear via foreign bodies like matchsticks or pencils.
	5. **Idiopathic:** No definite history of injury.
	
	---
	
	**5. STRATEGIES FOR PREVENTION AND CONTROL**
	
	Prevention of tetanus relies on active immunization, passive immunization, appropriate wound care, and clean delivery practices.
	
	**A. Active Immunization** The goal is to ensure a protective level of antitoxin (0.01 IU/ml serum) throughout life.
	
	- **Combined Vaccines (for infants):** Pentavalent vaccine (containing DPT) is given at 6, 10, and 14 weeks of age, replacing the older DPT primary schedule. DPT is used for booster doses at 16-24 months and 5-6 years of age.
	- **Monovalent Vaccines (for adults/pregnant women):** Purified tetanus toxoid (TT) or Tetanus and adult Diphtheria (Td) is used. A primary course consists of 2 doses given 1-2 months apart, followed by a booster after 1 year.
	
	**B. Passive Immunization** Indicated for temporary, immediate protection of non-immune individuals who sustain tetanus-prone injuries.
	
	- **Human Tetanus Hyperimmunoglobulin (TIG):** The drug of choice. Dose is 250 IU. It provides longer protection (up to 30 days) and does not cause serum sickness/allergic reactions.
	- **Anti-Tetanus Serum (ATS - Equine):** Used if TIG is unavailable. Dose is 1500 IU subcutaneously. It requires prior sensitivity testing due to high risks of allergic reactions/anaphylaxis, and provides shorter protection (7-10 days).
	
	**C. Simultaneous Active and Passive Immunization** For non-immune persons with tetanus-prone wounds, 1500 units of ATS or 250 units of TIG are given in one arm, while 0.5 ml of tetanus toxoid is injected into the other arm. This provides immediate passive protection and initiates long-lasting active immunity (must be followed up with a complete toxoid course).
	
	**D. Antibiotic Prophylaxis** A single IM injection of long-acting benzathine penicillin (1.2 mega units) or a 7-day oral course of erythromycin can kill vegetative forms of _C. tetani_, but _not_ spores. Antibiotics must be given within 6 hours of injury and are an adjunct to, not a substitute for, immunization.
	
	**E. Prevention of Tetanus After Injury (Wound Care)**
	
	- All wounds must receive immediate and thorough surgical toilet (debridement of foreign bodies, soil, and necrotic tissue) to abolish the anaerobic environment that favours spore germination.
	- Toxoid and/or immunoglobulin are administered based on the patient's immunization history (e.g., completely immunized within 5 years require nothing more for clean wounds, whereas unimmunized individuals require full toxoid course + TIG/ATS for contaminated wounds).
	
	**F. Maternal and Neonatal Tetanus Elimination (MNTE)** Maternal and Neonatal Tetanus is a proxy for the quality of basic healthcare. India was officially certified as having eliminated MNT in 2015. Strategies include:
	
	1. **Immunization of Pregnant Women:** 2 doses of TT/Td given as early as possible during pregnancy (with an interval of at least 1 month), or 1 booster dose if the mother was previously fully immunized.
	2. **Clean Delivery Practices:** Promotion of the **"Five Cleans"** – clean hands, clean delivery surface, clean blade for cutting the cord, clean tie for the cord, and no unhygienic application (like cowdung/ash) on the umbilical stump.
	3. **Institutional Deliveries:** Promoted through schemes like Janani Suraksha Yojana (JSY) and training of traditional birth attendants (Dais).

	**TREATMENT STRATEGY OF LEPROSY ERADICATION (MULTIDRUG THERAPY)**
	
	The strategy for leprosy control and eradication is based primarily on effective chemotherapy, specifically Multidrug Therapy (MDT). Due to the widespread emergence of dapsone-resistant strains from previous monotherapy, the WHO recommended MDT to effectively cure the disease and prevent further drug resistance.
	
	**1. OBJECTIVES OF MULTIDRUG THERAPY (MDT)**
	The main objectives of MDT in leprosy are:
	*   To interrupt transmission of the infection in the community by sterilizing infectious patients as rapidly as possible using bactericidal drugs.
	*   To ensure early detection and treatment of cases to prevent deformities.
	*   To prevent the emergence of drug resistance.
	*   *Additional benefits:* Curtailed duration of treatment, improved patient compliance, cost-effectiveness, and decreased workload.
	
	**2. CLINICAL CLASSIFICATION FOR TREATMENT**
	Patients are grouped based on clinical characteristics to select the correct MDT regimen:
	*   **Paucibacillary (PB) Leprosy:** 1 to 5 skin lesions, no nerve or only one nerve involvement, and negative skin smears at all sites.
	*   **Multibacillary (MB) Leprosy:** 6 or more skin lesions, more than one nerve involved, or positive skin smear at any site.
	
	**3. DRUGS USED IN MDT**
	The MDT regimen utilizes three main bactericidal/bacteriostatic drugs:
	*   **Rifampicin (RMP):** Highly bactericidal. A single dose of 1500 mg or 3-4 daily doses of 600 mg kills 99.9% of viable organisms. It is always used in combination.
	*   **Dapsone (DDS):** Weakly bactericidal, cheap, and well-tolerated, given orally. 
	*   **Clofazimine (CLF):** Has both anti-leprosy and anti-inflammatory properties, which gives it an added advantage in suppressing and preventing lepra reactions. 
	
	**4. RECOMMENDED REVISED REGIMENS OF CHEMOTHERAPY (MDT)**
	*Note: India is implementing the WHO revised regimen from 1st April 2025, where PB cases also receive the 3-drug combination due to widespread primary dapsone resistance.*
	
	**A. Adult Regimen (MB and PB)**
	*   **Rifampicin:** 600 mg once a month, given under supervision.
	*   **Clofazimine:** 300 mg once a month, given under supervision; AND 50 mg daily, self-administered.
	*   **Dapsone:** 100 mg daily, self-administered.
	*(Note: If Clofazimine is totally unacceptable due to skin discoloration, it can be replaced by 250-375 mg daily of Ethionamide or Protionamide).*
	
	**B. Paediatric Regimen (Children aged 10–14 years)**
	*   **Rifampicin:** 450 mg once a month, given under supervision.
	*   **Clofazimine:** 150 mg once a month, given under supervision; AND 50 mg every other day, self-administered.
	*   **Dapsone:** 50 mg daily, self-administered.
	*(Note: Children under 10 years of age should receive appropriately reduced doses).*
	
	**5. DURATION OF TREATMENT**
	*   **Multibacillary (MB) Leprosy:** 12 monthly blister packs to be completed within 18 months.
	*   **Paucibacillary (PB) Leprosy:** 6 monthly blister packs to be completed within 9 months.
	
	**6. SPECIAL SITUATIONS IN MDT MANAGEMENT**
	*   **Pregnancy and Lactation:** MDT is safe and must be continued during pregnancy. Small quantities of drugs are excreted in breast milk without adverse reactions in infants, except for mild skin discoloration from clofazimine.
	*   **HIV Co-infection:** MDT is not contraindicated. Management and response to MDT in HIV-positive patients are exactly the same as in HIV-negative patients.
	*   **Defaulters:** A defaulter is a patient who has not collected treatment for 12 consecutive months. If they return showing signs of active disease (new skin lesions, new nerve involvement, lepromatous nodules, or lepra reaction), they are given a completely new course of MDT.
	*   **Lepra Reactions during MDT:** If a patient develops a lepra reaction (Type 1 or Type 2) while on treatment, MDT should **not** be stopped; the full course must be completed while simultaneously managing the reaction (e.g., with prednisolone).
	1. Prevention of neonatal tetanus.
	**PREVENTION OF NEONATAL TETANUS**
	
	Neonatal tetanus (often referred to as the "8th-day disease" in some regions) is a highly fatal disease with a case-fatality rate reaching 80–90% in the absence of high-quality medical care. It predominantly occurs when infants are delivered in non-aseptic conditions, particularly when the umbilical cord is cut with unclean instruments or the stump is dressed with contaminated materials like soil, ash, or cow dung.
	
	The prevention of neonatal tetanus is highly effective and relies on a multi-pronged public health approach.
	
	**1. ACTIVE IMMUNIZATION OF PREGNANT WOMEN** Maternal immunization with Tetanus Toxoid Containing Vaccines (TTCV) is the most critical intervention, as it provides protective antibodies that are transferred to the foetus.
	
	- **Unimmunized Pregnant Women:** Two doses of tetanus toxoid should be administered. The first dose is given as early as possible during pregnancy, and the second dose is given at least one month later and at least 3 weeks before the expected date of delivery. Under the National Immunization Schedule, these doses are typically administered between 16 and 36 weeks of gestation.
	- **Previously Immunized Women:** For women who have been immunized in a previous pregnancy, a single booster dose of tetanus toxoid is considered sufficient.
	- **Late Presentation:** If a pregnant woman seeks antenatal care very late in her pregnancy, she should not be denied vaccination; even a single dose of tetanus toxoid should be administered.
	- **High-Risk Areas:** In regions with a high incidence of neonatal tetanus or low antenatal care coverage, the primary 2-dose course can be extended to all women of child-bearing age through supplemental immunization campaigns.
	
	**2. CLEAN DELIVERY PRACTICES (THE "FIVE CLEANS")** Clean delivery practices alone have successfully controlled neonatal tetanus in many industrialized countries. In developing countries, health education and the training of Traditional Birth Attendants (TBAs) emphasize the strict observance of the **"Five Cleans"** during deliveries:
	
	1. **Clean hands:** Thorough handwashing with soap and water by the birth attendant.
	2. **Clean delivery surface:** Ensuring the surface where the delivery takes place is sanitary.
	3. **Clean blade:** Using a sterile or properly cleaned instrument to cut the umbilical cord.
	4. **Clean cord tie:** Using a clean, sterile thread or tie for the umbilical cord.
	5. **Clean cord care:** Leaving the umbilical stump dry and strictly avoiding the application of harmful substances like ash, cow dung, or soil.
	
	_To facilitate this, disposable home delivery kits are distributed to skilled birth attendants for each pregnancy_.
	
	**3. PASSIVE IMMUNIZATION FOR "AT-RISK" INFANTS**
	
	- **Indication:** Infants born to mothers who are unimmunized (i.e., have not received the required two doses of tetanus toxoid) are at an exceptionally high risk of developing neonatal tetanus.
	- **Prophylaxis:** These high-risk newborns can be temporarily protected by administering an injection of tetanus antitoxin (heterologous serum, 750 IU).
	- **Timing:** For this passive immunization to be effective, it must be administered within 6 hours of birth.
	
	**4. NATIONAL STRATEGIES & MNTE PROGRAMME** The overarching goal of the Maternal and Neonatal Tetanus Elimination (MNTE) initiative is to reduce the incidence of neonatal tetanus to less than 1 case per 1,000 live births in every district. Key public health strategies in India include:
	
	- **Promotion of Institutional Deliveries:** Schemes like _Janani Suraksha Yojana (JSY)_ provide conditional cash transfers to encourage women to deliver in health facilities, ensuring a hygienic environment.
	- **Community Mobilization:** Engagement of Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs) to generate demand for antenatal care, educate families on safe cord care practices, and facilitate institutional deliveries.
	- **Strengthening Surveillance:** Active surveillance to identify women at risk and detect any potential clustering of neonatal tetanus cases.
	1. Fernandez reaction.
	**FERNANDEZ REACTION (EARLY LEPROMIN REACTION)**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION**
	
	- The Fernandez reaction is the "early reaction" observed during the Lepromin test, which is performed by injecting 0.1 ml of lepromin intradermally into the inner aspect of the forearm.
	- It is primarily used as a tool to evaluate the cell-mediated immunity (immune status) of an individual.
	
	**2. TIMING AND DURATION**
	
	- An inflammatory response develops rapidly within 24 to 48 hours following the injection.
	- The reaction is transient and tends to disappear after 3 to 4 days.
	
	**3. IMMUNOLOGICAL BASIS**
	
	- The Fernandez reaction is a delayed hypersensitivity reaction.
	- It is specifically induced by the "soluble" constituents (antigens) of the leprosy bacilli, unlike the late reaction which is induced by the bacillary component.
	
	**4. CLINICAL FEATURES AND CRITERIA FOR POSITIVITY**
	
	- The reaction is physically evidenced by redness and induration at the site of inoculation.
	- **Positive Result:** The test is read at 48 hours, and it is considered positive if the diameter of the red area is more than 10 mm.
	
	**5. EPIDEMIOLOGICAL AND CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE**
	
	- An early positive reaction indicates whether or not a person has been previously sensitized by exposure to, and infection by, the leprosy bacilli.
	- In the context of detecting prior sensitization, the early (Fernandez) reaction is considered much superior to the late (Mitsuda) reaction.
	
	**6. ANALOGY**
	
	- The Fernandez reaction corresponds directly to the Mantoux reaction seen in tuberculosis, which is similarly caused by delayed hypersensitivity to the soluble antigens (PPD) of the tubercle bacilli.

	The cornerstone of leprosy control and treatment is Multidrug Therapy (MDT). Monotherapy is strictly contraindicated due to the high risk of drug resistance.

	- **Clofazimine (CLF):** Has both anti-leprosy and anti-inflammatory properties, making it highly valuable in suppressing and preventing lepra reactions.
	- _Alternative/Reserve Drugs:_ Ethionamide, protionamide, ofloxacin, minocycline, and clarithromycin.

	- **Paucibacillary (PB):** 6 monthly blister packs to be completed within 9 months.

	- **Defaulters:** A defaulter is a patient who misses treatment for 12 consecutive months. If they return showing signs of active disease (new skin lesions, new nerve involvement, or lepra reaction), they are given a completely new course of MDT.
	
	**7. MANAGEMENT OF LEPRA REACTIONS DURING TREATMENT**
	
	- If a patient develops a lepra reaction (Type 1/Reversal or Type 2/ENL) during treatment, **MDT should NOT be stopped**; the full course must be completed.

	**DEFORMITIES IN LEPROSY**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION** Approximately 25 per cent of leprosy patients who are not treated at an early stage develop anaesthesia and/or deformities of the hands and feet. As a single disease entity, leprosy is considered one of the foremost causes of deformities and crippling worldwide.
	
	**2. MECHANISM AND PATHOGENESIS OF DEFORMITIES** Deformities in leprosy develop due to three primary mechanisms:
	
	- **Direct Disease Process:** Direct tissue infiltration by the bacilli causes local damage (e.g., loss of eyebrows, and other facial deformities).
	- **Nerve Damage (Motor Paralysis):** Damage to the peripheral nerve trunks leads to paralysis of specific muscles (e.g., claw-hand, foot-drop, and lagophthalmos).
	- **Injury and Infection in Anaesthetic Parts:** Repeated, unnoticed injuries or secondary infections in denervated, anaesthetic areas (hands, feet, and eyes) result in complications like scar contractures of fingers, loss/absorption of hands and feet, and corneal ulceration.
	
	**3. TYPES OF DISABILITIES** Disabilities in leprosy are broadly classified into two categories:
	
	- **Primary Disabilities:** These occur directly as a result of nerve damage, manifesting as loss of sensation, paralysis, and dryness of the skin.
	- **Secondary Disabilities:** These occur as a result of neglected primary disabilities, leading to complications like ulcers and contractures.
	
	**4. REGIONAL CLASSIFICATION OF DEFORMITIES (HIGH-YIELD)** The specific deformities occurring in leprosy are systematically classified according to the affected body region:
	
	- **Face and Head:**
	    - Facies leonina, mask face, and sagging face.
	    - Madarosis: Loss of eyebrows (superciliary madarosis) and loss of eyelashes (ciliary madarosis).
	    - Eye Deformities: Lagophthalmos, corneal ulcers, and corneal opacities.
	    - Nose Deformities: Depressed nose and perforated nose.
	    - Ear Deformities: Nodules on the ear and elongated ear lobules.
	- **Hands:**
	    - Claw hand, wrist-drop, and swollen hand.
	    - Thumb-web contracture and hollowing of the interosseous spaces.
	    - Ulcers and absorption (shortening) of digits.
	- **Feet:**
	    - Foot-drop, inversion of the foot, collapsed foot, and swollen foot.
	    - Plantar ulcers and callosities.
	    - Clawing of the toes and absorption of the toes.
	- **Other Deformities:**
	    - Gynaecomastia and perforation of the palate.
	
	**5. STAGES OF NERVE INVOLVEMENT** The progression of nerve damage leading to disability occurs in three distinct stages:
	
	- **Stage I (Stage of nerve involvement):** Nerves become swollen (thickened) and tender due to the inflammatory response, but there is no loss of function. This condition is reversible if early action is taken.
	- **Stage II (Stage of nerve damage):** Thickened and painful peripheral nerves are associated with the loss of autonomic, sensory, and motor functions. This condition is reversible if suitable action is taken early, preferably within 6 months.
	- **Stage III (Stage of nerve destruction):** In long-standing cases (usually more than one year), the involved nerve becomes fibrosed, thin, and atrophic. The nerve is completely destroyed, and its function cannot be recovered.
	
	**6. HIGH-RISK GROUPS FOR DEVELOPING DISABILITY** Certain patients are at a higher risk of developing lepra reactions and severe neuritis, which precipitate these deformities. These include:
	
	- Patients with Multi-bacillary (MB) leprosy.
	- Patients with a past or present history of thickened, painful, or tender nerve trunks.
	- Patients presenting with skin lesions on the face.
	- Individuals in extremes of age (adolescents, old age) or in specific physiological states like pregnancy.
	- Patients with any inter-current infections.
	
	**7. WHO DISABILITY GRADING IN LEPROSY** Disabilities are assessed separately for the eyes, hands, and feet, and are classified into three grades (0 to 2):
	
	- **Grade 0:** Sensation and muscle power are normal, with normal vision and no lid gap.
	- **Grade 1:** Sensation is absent, but there is no visible deformity or severe visual impairment.
	- **Grade 2:** Characterized by severe impairments such as weak or paralyzed muscles, visible deformities, inability to count fingers at 6 metres, or the presence of a lid gap, red eye, or corneal ulcer/opacity. The highest grade given in any part is used as the overall Disability Grade for the patient.
5. **WINDOW PERIOD**
	
	**1. Definition**
	
	- The "window period" is the crucial time interval between the initial infection with a pathogen and the point at which antibodies (or viral nucleic acids) become detectable in the patient's bloodstream.
	
	**2. In the Context of HIV/AIDS (Most High-Yield)**
	
	- **Duration:** Following initial infection with HIV, it usually takes between **2 to 12 weeks** for HIV antibodies to appear in the bloodstream, though it is known to sometimes take even longer.
	- **Diagnostic Challenge:** During this period, the person will **test negative** on standard antibody blood tests because the immune system has not yet produced sufficient antibodies to be detected.
	- **High Infectivity:** Despite a negative test result, the person is **particularly infectious** to others during the window period. This is because the levels and concentration of the virus in the blood are extremely high during these early stages before antibody production begins.
	
	**3. In the Context of COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2)**
	
	- **Duration & Diagnosis:** For COVID-19, there may be a window period of **up to 5 days** after exposure.
	- **Clinical Significance:** During this 5-day window period, viral nucleic acids might not be detected, meaning false-negative results can occur even with highly sensitive Nucleic Acid Amplification Tests (NAAT) like RT-PCR.
	1. Case definition and diagnosis of AIDS
	**CASE DEFINITIONS FOR DIAGNOSIS OF AIDS**
	
	The diagnosis of AIDS for surveillance purposes is based on the clinical case definitions and the expanded case definitions provided by the World Health Organization (WHO).
	
	**I. WHO CLINICAL CASE DEFINITION FOR AIDS (Adults & Adolescents > 12 years)** An adult or adolescent is considered to have AIDS if at least **2 major signs** and at least **1 minor sign** are present in combination, provided these signs are not known to be due to a condition unrelated to HIV infection.
	
	- **Major Signs:**
	    - Weight loss of ≥ 10 per cent of body weight.
	    - Chronic diarrhoea for more than 1 month.
	    - Prolonged fever for more than 1 month (intermittent or constant).
	- **Minor Signs:**
	    - Persistent cough for more than 1 month (except for patients with Tuberculosis, where this is not considered a minor sign).
	    - Generalized pruritic dermatitis.
	    - History of herpes zoster.
	    - Oropharyngeal candidiasis.
	    - Chronic progressive or disseminated herpes simplex infection.
	    - Generalized lymphadenopathy.
	- _Note (High-Yield):_ The presence of either generalized **Kaposi sarcoma** or **cryptococcal meningitis** alone is sufficient for the diagnosis of AIDS for surveillance purposes.
	
	**II. WHO CLINICAL CASE DEFINITION FOR CHILDREN** A child is considered to have AIDS if at least **2 major signs** and **2 minor signs** are present, assuming there is no other known cause of immunosuppression.
	
	- **Major Signs:**
	    - Weight loss or abnormally slow growth.
	    - Chronic diarrhoea for more than 1 month.
	    - Prolonged fever for more than 1 month.
	- **Minor Signs:**
	    - Generalized lymph node enlargement.
	    - Oropharyngeal candidiasis.
	    - Persistent cough.
	    - Recurrent common infections (e.g., ear infection, pharyngitis).
	    - Generalized rash.
	- _Note:_ Confirmed HIV infection in the mother counts as an additional minor criterion.
	
	**III. EXPANDED WHO CASE DEFINITION FOR AIDS SURVEILLANCE** This expanded definition is simpler, has higher specificity, and relies on laboratory confirmation. An adult or adolescent (>12 years of age) is considered to have AIDS if a **test for HIV antibody gives a positive result**, AND one or more of the following indicator conditions are present:
	
	- > 10% body weight loss or cachexia, along with diarrhoea or fever (intermittent or constant) for at least 1 month, not known to be due to a condition unrelated to HIV infection.
	    
	- Cryptococcal meningitis.
	- Pulmonary or extra-pulmonary tuberculosis.
	- Kaposi sarcoma.
	- Neurological impairment that is sufficient to prevent independent daily activities (not due to trauma or cerebrovascular accident).
	- Invasive cervical cancer.
	- Candidiasis of the oesophagus (which may be presumptively diagnosed based on oral candidiasis accompanied by dysphagia).
	- Clinically diagnosed life-threatening or recurrent episodes of pneumonia (with or without aetiological confirmation).

	**MEASURES FOR CONTROL OF SEXUALLY TRANSMITTED DISEASES (STDs)**
	
	The control of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) requires a comprehensive, multi-disciplinary approach focusing on specific populations and integrating STI services into primary health care. The key measures for prevention and control are classified as follows:
	
	**1. PRIMARY PREVENTION & AWARENESS**
	
	- **Health Education & Behaviour Change:** Comprehensive education focusing on sexual and reproductive health to reduce risky sexual behaviour and improve the ability to recognize STI symptoms.
	- **Condom Promotion:** Ensuring access to and promoting the correct, consistent use of male and female condoms, along with condom-compatible lubricants.
	- **Targeted Interventions for High-Risk Groups (HRGs):** Implementing specific interventions for key populations (e.g., female sex workers, men who have sex with men, transgender people, and injecting drug users) which includes condom distribution, needle and syringe exchange programmes, and opioid substitution therapy.
	- **Voluntary Medical Male Circumcision (VMMC):** Promoted in high-burden settings as an important strategy for the prevention of heterosexually acquired HIV and other STIs in men.
	- **Prophylaxis:** Providing post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) when needed and suppressive therapy for individuals with herpes simplex virus (HSV).
	
	**2. CASE MANAGEMENT & TREATMENT (SYNDROMIC APPROACH)**
	
	- **Syndromic Management:** Due to the lack of affordable or available lab tests in many settings, WHO recommends the syndromic management of STDs. It uses flowcharts to diagnose and treat patients based on consistently recognized signs and symptoms (e.g., urethral discharge, vaginal discharge, genital ulcers, lower abdominal pain).
	- **Continuum of Care:** Services should be organized so that curable STIs are diagnosed and treated on the same day ("diagnose-and-treat" strategy).
	- **Colour-Coded Kits:** Under the National Programme, pre-packed colour-coded STI/RTI kits (Kit 1 to Kit 7) are provided free of supply to all designated clinics to ensure standardized syndromic treatment.
	- **Treatment of Complications:** Early diagnosis and treatment are crucial to prevent long-term sequelae such as pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), ectopic pregnancy, infertility, and neurosyphilis.
	
	**3. PREVENTION OF VERTICAL (MOTHER-TO-CHILD) TRANSMISSION**
	
	- **Antenatal Screening:** All pregnant women must be screened for HIV and syphilis at least once, ideally during the first antenatal care visit, to prevent adverse birth outcomes like stillbirth and congenital syphilis.
	- **Neonatal Care:** Ensuring all newborns receive the Hepatitis B birth dose.
	- **Ophthalmia Neonatorum Prophylaxis:** Routine prophylaxis should be administered during delivery in settings where mothers are not routinely screened for gonorrhoea and chlamydial infection.
	- **HSV Management:** Suppressive HSV therapy should be considered during the last trimester for pregnant women with suspected primary genital herpes to prevent neonatal herpes.
	
	**4. SCREENING AND PARTNER MANAGEMENT**
	
	- **Partner Services:** Averting further transmission and reinfection relies heavily on notifying and treating sexual partners of the index case. Engagement in these services must be voluntary and confidentiality must be strictly maintained.
	- **Screening Priority Populations:** Regular screening with quality-assured diagnostic tests should be targeted at priority populations, including individuals taking HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), sex workers, and men who have sex with men.
	
	**5. VACCINATION**
	
	- **Hepatitis B Vaccine:** Widely administered as part of the universal immunization schedule to prevent acute and chronic Hepatitis B infection and subsequent liver cancer.
	- **Human Papillomavirus (HPV) Vaccine:** Recommended primarily for girls aged 9 to 14 years (prior to sexual activity exposure) to prevent cervical, penile, and anal cancers.
	
	**6. PROGRAMMATIC INTEGRATION (NATIONAL STD CONTROL PROGRAMME)**
	
	- **Suraksha Clinics:** Integration of reproductive tract infections (RTIs) and STDs services at all levels of health care. STDs clinics functioning at district hospitals, medical colleges, and first referral units have been branded as "Suraksha Clinics".
	- **Linkage with HIV Services:** Prevention and case management for STIs are closely linked with HIV prevention services, as the presence of an STD facilitates the transmission of HIV.
