1. Classify diabetes mellitus. Describe the epidemiological factors causing the increased prevalence of the disease in India. 
	**CLASSIFICATION OF DIABETES MELLITUS**
	
	The World Health Organization (WHO) revised the classification of diabetes in 2019 to prioritize clinical care and help health professionals choose appropriate treatment. The current classification is as follows:
	
	**1. Type 1 Diabetes**
	*   Characterized by $\beta$-cell destruction (mostly immune-mediated) leading to absolute insulin deficiency.
	*   Onset is most common in childhood and early adulthood.
	
	**2. Type 2 Diabetes**
	*   The most common type of diabetes.
	*   Characterized by various degrees of $\beta$-cell dysfunction and insulin resistance.
	*   Commonly associated with overweight and obesity.
	
	**3. Hybrid Forms of Diabetes**
	*   **Slowly evolving immune-mediated diabetes of adults:** Similar to type 1 but often has features of the metabolic syndrome and retains greater $\beta$-cell function.
	*   **Ketosis-prone type 2 diabetes:** Presents with ketosis and insulin deficiency initially, but later does not require insulin (not immune-mediated).
	
	**4. Other Specific Types**
	*   **Monogenic diabetes:** Caused by specific gene mutations (defects of $\beta$-cell function or insulin action).
	*   **Diseases of the exocrine pancreas:** Conditions like trauma, tumors, or inflammation affecting the pancreas.
	*   **Endocrine disorders:** Diseases with excess secretion of hormones that act as insulin antagonists.
	*   **Drug- or chemical-induced:** Medicines and chemicals that impair insulin secretion or destroy $\beta$-cells.
	*   **Infection-related diabetes:** Certain viruses associated with direct $\beta$-cell destruction.
	*   **Uncommon specific forms of immune-mediated diabetes**.
	*   **Other genetic syndromes sometimes associated with diabetes**.
	
	**5. Unclassified Diabetes**
	*   Used temporarily when a clear diagnostic category cannot be established close to the time of diagnosis.
	
	**6. Hyperglycaemia First Detected During Pregnancy**
	*   **Diabetes mellitus in pregnancy:** Type 1 or type 2 diabetes first diagnosed during pregnancy.
	*   **Gestational diabetes mellitus:** Hyperglycaemia with blood glucose values above normal but below the diagnostic thresholds for diabetes, occurring during pregnancy.

	**EPIDEMIOLOGICAL FACTORS CAUSING INCREASED PREVALENCE IN INDIA**
	
	India is currently experiencing a rapid epidemiological transition, with an estimated 77 million diabetics, making it the second highest in the world. The surge in prevalence is driven by a complex interplay of genetic vulnerability and environmental changes.
	
	**1. AGENT FACTORS**
	*   The underlying physiological agent is insulin deficiency (absolute in type 1, partial/resistant in type 2).
	*   This results from $\beta$-cell destruction, decreased insulin sensitivity (reduced target cell receptors), abnormal insulin molecules, or genetic mutations.
	
	**2. HOST FACTORS (Intrinsic)**
	*   **Genetic Susceptibility (High-Yield):** The Indian population has a pronounced ethnic and genetic vulnerability to diabetes. Twin studies show approximately 90% concordance for Type 2 diabetes in identical twins, demonstrating a massive genetic component. 
	*   **Age:** Prevalence rises steeply with advancing age, typically coming to light in the middle years of life.
	*   **Obesity and Central Adiposity:** Obesity is a major risk factor, particularly "central" or abdominal adiposity (measured by waist circumference or waist-to-hip ratio). Central obesity is a powerful determinant of insulin resistance, which is the underlying abnormality in most Type 2 diabetes cases.
	*   **Maternal Diabetes & Low Birth Weight:** Offspring of diabetic pregnancies are at a high risk of developing type 2 diabetes at an early age. Furthermore, maternal malnutrition leading to intrauterine growth retardation (low birth weight babies), when followed by rapid catch-up growth later in childhood, significantly increases the risk of subsequent diabetes.
	
	**3. ENVIRONMENTAL & LIFESTYLE FACTORS (Extrinsic)**
	These are the primary drivers "unmasking" the genetic susceptibility in the Indian population.
	*   **Urbanization:** Rapid urbanization has brought about unfavourable modifications in lifestyle and dietary habits. The prevalence of diabetes in India is significantly higher in urban areas compared to rural populations.
	*   **Sedentary Lifestyle:** Physical inactivity and lack of exercise are major risk factors. A sedentary lifestyle alters the interaction between insulin and its receptors, directly leading to insulin resistance.
	*   **Dietary Changes:** 
	    *   There is an increased intake of energy-dense diets rich in saturated fats and refined carbohydrates.
	    *   A corresponding decrease in the intake of dietary fibre (non-starch polysaccharides) from whole grains, fruits, and vegetables contributes heavily to the impaired glucose tolerance.
	*   **Alcohol Consumption:** Excessive alcohol intake increases diabetes risk by promoting obesity and directly damaging the pancreas and liver.
	*   **Early Malnutrition:** Protein-energy malnutrition (PEM) during early infancy and childhood can cause permanent damage to the pancreatic $\beta$-cells, resulting in partial failure of $\beta$-cell function.
	*   **Social Class Changes:** While historically a disease of the affluent, rapid lifestyle changes, stress, and shifting nutritional habits have caused a rising epidemic of diabetes in the lower socio-economic classes and urban slums as well.
	1. Discuss the clinical classification of diabetes mellitus. Describe the epidemiological determinants and levels of prevention for diabetes mellitus.  
	**CLINICAL CLASSIFICATION OF DIABETES MELLITUS (WHO 2019)**
	
	The WHO classification of diabetes prioritizes clinical care to help health professionals choose appropriate treatment, particularly whether to start treatment with insulin. The clinical classification is as follows:
	
	- **Type 1 diabetes:** Characterized by B-cell destruction (mostly immune-mediated) and absolute insulin deficiency. Its onset is typically abrupt and most common in childhood and early adulthood. In its untreated state, it is usually associated with ketosis due to the virtual absence of circulating insulin.
	- **Type 2 diabetes:** The most common type, characterized by various degrees of B-cell dysfunction and insulin resistance. It is commonly associated with overweight and central obesity, is typically gradual in onset, and occurs mainly in the middle-aged and elderly.
	- **Hybrid forms of diabetes:** Includes slowly evolving immune-mediated diabetes of adults (which retains greater B-cell function) and ketosis-prone type 2 diabetes (which presents with ketosis but later does not require insulin).
	- **Other specific types:** Includes monogenic diabetes (defects of B-cell function or insulin action), diseases of the exocrine pancreas (e.g., trauma, tumours, inflammation), endocrine disorders, drug- or chemical-induced diabetes, infection-related diabetes, uncommon specific forms of immune-mediated diabetes, and other genetic syndromes.
	- **Unclassified diabetes:** A temporary category used when diabetes does not clearly fit into other categories, especially close to the time of diagnosis.
	- **Hyperglycaemia first detected during pregnancy:** This includes diabetes mellitus in pregnancy (Type 1 or Type 2 first diagnosed during pregnancy) and gestational diabetes mellitus (hyperglycaemia below diagnostic thresholds for true diabetes).

	**EPIDEMIOLOGICAL DETERMINANTS**
	
	**1. Agent Factors** The underlying cause is insulin deficiency, which is absolute in Type 1 diabetes and partial in Type 2 diabetes. The mechanisms include:
	
	- Pancreatic disorders (inflammatory, neoplastic, or cystic fibrosis).
	- Defects in the formation of insulin (synthesis of abnormal, less active insulin).
	- Destruction of beta cells by viral infections or chemical agents.
	- Decreased insulin sensitivity due to reduced adipocyte and monocyte insulin receptors.
	- Genetic defects (e.g., mutation of the insulin gene) and autoimmunity.
	
	**2. Host Factors**
	
	- **Age:** Prevalence rises steeply with age, with Type 2 usually coming to light in the middle years of life. Type 1 incidence is highest among the 10-14 year age group.
	- **Sex:** Overall male-female ratio is equal in some countries (like the UK), but an excess of male diabetics has been observed in South-East Asia.
	- **Genetic factors:** There is a strong genetic component in Type 2 diabetes, with twin studies showing approximately 90 per cent concordance in identical twins. In Type 1, concordance is only about 50 per cent.
	- **Genetic markers:** Type 1 diabetes is strongly associated with HLA-B8, B15, DR3, and DR4. Type 2 diabetes is not HLA-associated.
	- **Immune mechanisms:** Defective immunological mechanisms can cause cell-mediated and humoral activity against the body's own insulin-producing islet cells.
	- **Obesity:** Central adiposity (indicated by an increased waist-to-hip ratio) is a major determinant of insulin resistance and a powerful risk factor for Type 2 diabetes.
	- **Maternal diabetes:** Offspring of diabetic pregnancies are often large at birth, tend to develop obesity in childhood, and are at high risk of developing Type 2 diabetes at an early age.
	
	**3. Environmental Risk Factors**
	
	- **Sedentary lifestyle:** Lack of physical exercise is an important risk factor that may alter the interaction between insulin and its receptors, subsequently leading to Type 2 diabetes.
	- **Diet:** A high saturated fat intake is associated with higher fasting insulin, lower insulin sensitivity, and a higher risk of Type 2 diabetes. Conversely, high intakes of dietary fibre have a potential protective effect.
	- **Malnutrition:** Protein-energy malnutrition (PEM) in early infancy and childhood may result in partial failure of B-cell function.
	- **Alcohol:** Excessive alcohol intake damages the pancreas and liver, promotes obesity, and increases the risk of diabetes.
	- **Viral infections:** Viruses such as rubella, mumps, and human coxsackie virus B4 may trigger B-cell destruction in immunogenetically susceptible people.
	- **Chemical agents:** Agents like alloxan, streptozotocin, the rodenticide VALCOR, and high intake of cyanide-producing foods (e.g., cassava) are toxic to beta cells.
	- **Social factors:** Diabetes is strongly linked to urbanization, changes in lifestyle, and is increasingly prevalent in lower social classes.

	**LEVELS OF PREVENTION OF DIABETES MELLITUS**
	
	**1. Primary Prevention** Primary prevention is aimed at preventing the disease from occurring and involves two strategies:
	
	- **Population Strategy:** This focuses on primordial prevention, preventing the emergence of environmental risk factors in populations where they have not yet appeared. Measures include the maintenance of normal body weight through physical exercise and healthy nutritional habits, such as an adequate protein intake, high dietary fibre intake, and the avoidance of sweet foods. These measures should be fully integrated into community-based programs for non-communicable diseases.
	- **High-Risk Strategy:** Directed at target population groups, such as the obese and those with a sedentary lifestyle. The focus is on the correction of over-nutrition, promoting physical activity, and avoiding diabetogenic drugs like oral contraceptives and excessive alcohol. It also includes reducing factors that promote atherosclerosis, such as smoking, high blood pressure, and elevated cholesterol. _Note: Currently, there is no practical high-risk strategy or genetic counselling available for the primary prevention of Type 1 diabetes_.
	
	**2. Secondary Prevention** The goal of secondary prevention is early detection and adequate treatment to halt disease progression and prevent complications.
	
	- **Treatment Goals:** The aims are to maintain blood glucose levels as close to normal limits as practicable and to maintain ideal body weight. Treatment relies on diet alone, diet with oral antidiabetic drugs, or diet and insulin.
	- **Routine Monitoring:** Proper management requires periodic checking of blood sugar, blood pressure, weight, visual acuity, and urine for proteins and ketones. The feet must be meticulously examined for defective blood circulation and loss of sensation.
	- **Glycosylated Haemoglobin (HbA1c):** Half-yearly estimations of HbA1c should be performed, as it provides a long-term index of mean blood glucose control over the previous 2-3 months.
	- **Self-Care:** A crucial element of secondary prevention is patient education for self-care. This includes adherence to diet and drugs, home blood glucose monitoring, self-administration of insulin, and recognizing symptoms of hypoglycaemia and glycosuria.
	
	**3. Tertiary Prevention** Tertiary prevention aims to limit severe disabilities caused by the long-term complications of diabetes.
	
	- **Disability Limitation:** Interventions are implemented to prevent and manage late-stage complications such as blindness (diabetic retinopathy), kidney failure (nephropathy), coronary thrombosis, and gangrene of the lower extremities necessitating amputation.
	- **Specialized Diabetic Clinics:** Establishing specialized clinics and units in large towns and cities that are capable of providing diagnostic and management skills of a high order is a primary objective at this level.
	- **Registries:** Establishing local and national registries for diabetics is recommended to continuously evaluate the burden and facilitate clinical and epidemiological research.
	1. Epidemiological determinants of diabetes mellitus (type 2). 
	**EPIDEMIOLOGICAL DETERMINANTS OF DIABETES MELLITUS (TYPE 2)**
	
	Diabetes mellitus is a heterogeneous metabolic disorder characterized by hyperglycaemia. The epidemiological determinants of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus are multifactorial and are broadly classified into Agent, Host, and Environmental factors.
	
	**1. AGENT FACTORS** The underlying cause of diabetes is insulin deficiency, which is partial in Type 2 diabetes. This may be due to a variety of mechanisms:
	
	- **Decreased Insulin Sensitivity:** Due to a decreased number of adipocyte and monocyte insulin receptors.
	- **Pancreatic Disorders:** Inflammatory or neoplastic conditions affecting the pancreas.
	- **Defects in Insulin Formation:** Synthesis of an abnormal, biologically less active insulin molecule.
	- **Genetic Defects:** Such as mutations of the insulin gene.
	- The overall effect is reduced utilization of glucose leading to hyperglycaemia and glycosuria.
	
	**2. HOST FACTORS (NON-MODIFIABLE & MODIFIABLE)**
	
	- **Age:** Type 2 diabetes typically comes to light in the middle years of life and its frequency rises steeply with advancing age.
	- **Sex:** In some countries (e.g., UK), the male-female ratio is equal, while in South-East Asia, an excess of male diabetics has been observed.
	- **Genetic Factors (High-Yield):** The genetic nature of Type 2 diabetes is undisputed. Twin studies show approximately 90 per cent concordance in identical twins, demonstrating a very strong genetic component. Unlike Type 1 diabetes, Type 2 diabetes is **not** HLA-associated.
	- **Obesity (Central Adiposity):** Obesity is a major determinant of insulin resistance, the underlying abnormality in most Type 2 diabetes cases. The risk is strongly related to both the degree and duration of obesity. "Central obesity" (measured by waist circumference or waist-to-hip ratio) is a more powerful determinant of subsequent risk than Body Mass Index (BMI).
	- **Maternal Diabetes & Foetal Growth:** Offspring of diabetic pregnancies (including gestational diabetes) are often large at birth, tend to develop childhood obesity, and have a three-fold higher risk of developing Type 2 diabetes early in life. Additionally, maternal diabetes associated with intrauterine growth retardation (low birth weight) followed by rapid catch-up growth also increases the risk.
	
	**3. ENVIRONMENTAL RISK FACTORS** Susceptibility to diabetes is unmasked by environmental factors acting on genetically susceptible individuals:
	
	- **Sedentary Lifestyle:** Lack of physical exercise is a major risk factor as it alters the interaction between insulin and its receptors, leading to Type 2 diabetes.
	- **Dietary Factors:**
	    - _Fats:_ High intake of saturated fats is associated with a higher risk of impaired glucose tolerance and higher fasting insulin levels. Replacing saturated fats with unsaturated fatty acids (from vegetable sources) improves glucose tolerance and increases insulin sensitivity.
	    - _Dietary Fibre:_ High intake of dietary fibre (non-starch polysaccharides found in whole-grain cereals, vegetables, and fruits) is highly protective and reduces blood glucose and insulin levels.
	- **Malnutrition:** Protein-energy malnutrition (PEM) in early infancy and childhood may cause partial failure of beta-cell function, which can explain impaired carbohydrate tolerance seen in conditions like kwashiorkor.
	- **Alcohol:** Excessive alcohol consumption increases the risk of diabetes by promoting obesity and causing direct damage to the pancreas and liver.
	- **Chemical Agents:** Certain chemicals and toxins are known to destroy beta cells, such as alloxan, streptozotocin, the rodenticide VALCOR, and high intake of cyanide-producing foods like cassava.
	- **Stress:** Severe stress from surgery, trauma, or emotional situations can "bring out" the disease.
	- **Social Factors:** Rapid urbanization, changes in lifestyle, occupation, and economic status are strongly linked to the disease. A significant epidemiological feature is that diabetes is now increasingly being seen in the lower social classes due to rapid shifts in lifestyle, whereas historically it was a disease of the affluent.
1. Prevention of diabetes mellitus.
	
	**PREVENTION AND CARE OF DIABETES MELLITUS**
	
	The prevention and care of diabetes mellitus are based on three levels of prevention: Primary, Secondary, and Tertiary.
	
	**I. PRIMARY PREVENTION** Primary prevention strategies are divided into population and high-risk strategies.
	
	**A. Population Strategy**
	
	- **Type 1 Diabetes:** The scope for primary prevention is currently limited and probably not appropriate on the basis of current knowledge.
	- **Type 2 Diabetes:** Prevention is based on the elimination of environmental risk factors (primordial prevention), particularly in countries where they have not yet appeared.
	- **Key Interventions:** Maintenance of normal body weight through healthy nutritional habits and physical exercise. Nutritional measures include ensuring an adequate protein intake, high intake of dietary fibre, and avoidance of sweet foods.
	- **Integration:** These measures should be fully integrated into community-based NCD (non-communicable disease) prevention programmes.
	
	**B. High-Risk Strategy**
	
	- **Type 1 Diabetes:** There is no special high-risk strategy, and genetic counselling is not practically justified as a method of prevention at present.
	- **Type 2 Diabetes:** High-risk strategy targets individuals with a sedentary lifestyle, over-nutrition, and obesity to reduce the risk of diabetes and its complications.
	- **Avoidance of Risk Factors:** Subjects at risk should avoid diabetogenic drugs such as oral contraceptives and limit alcohol, which indirectly increases diabetes risk. Factors promoting atherosclerosis (smoking, high blood pressure, elevated cholesterol, and high triglycerides) must be aggressively reduced.
	
	**II. SECONDARY PREVENTION** Secondary prevention focuses on early detection, adequate treatment, and preventing complications.
	
	**A. Early Detection & Screening**
	
	- Mass screening of the entire population is not rewarding. Screening must be directed at "high-risk" groups: age 40 or over, family history of diabetes, obese individuals, women who have had a baby weighing over 4.5 kg, women with excess weight gain during pregnancy, and patients with premature atherosclerosis.
	- The "standard oral glucose tolerance test" using the 2-hour value after a 75 g oral glucose load is the cornerstone of diagnosis, as urine testing (for glycosuria) lacks sensitivity and produces too many false negatives.
	
	**B. Aims of Treatment**
	
	- To maintain blood glucose levels as close to normal limits as practicable and to maintain an ideal body weight.
	- Treatment modalities include diet alone (small balanced meals frequently), diet combined with oral antidiabetic drugs, or diet with insulin.
	
	**C. Proper Management & Routine Checking**
	
	- Good control of blood glucose actively protects against complications.
	- Periodic routine checks must evaluate blood sugar, urine (for proteins and ketones), blood pressure, visual acuity, and body weight.
	- Feet must be examined for defective blood circulation (using Doppler ultrasound probes), loss of sensation, and skin health.
	
	**D. Glycosylated Haemoglobin (HbA1c)**
	
	- HbA1c should be estimated at half-yearly intervals.
	- It provides a long-term index of glucose control, reflecting the mean blood glucose levels during the red cell life-time (the previous 2-3 months).
	
	**E. Self-Care**
	
	- Self-care is a crucial element in secondary prevention. The patient must take major responsibility for their care under medical guidance.
	- This includes adherence to diet and drugs, home blood glucose monitoring (using meters or test strips), self-administration of insulin, abstinence from alcohol, and recognizing symptoms of hypoglycaemia or glycosuria.
	- The patient must carry an identification card detailing their disease, treatment, and contact information.
	
	**III. TERTIARY PREVENTION** Tertiary prevention aims to limit disabilities caused by complications such as blindness, kidney failure, coronary thrombosis, and gangrene of the lower extremities.
	
	- **Diabetic Clinics:** The main objective is to organize specialized clinics and units in large towns and cities capable of providing high-order diagnostic and management skills.
	- **Research and Registries:** Tertiary centres should be involved in basic, clinical, and epidemiological research, and local/national registries for diabetics should be established.
	
	**IV. EVIDENCE-BASED INTERVENTIONS (HIGH-YIELD EXAM DATA)** The following individual interventions have proven efficacy in diabetes management:
	
	- **Lifestyle interventions in high-risk people:** Reduces incidence of type 2 diabetes by 35–58%.
	- **Metformin use in high-risk people:** Reduces incidence by 25–31%.
	- **Glycaemic control (for HbA1c >9%):** Achieves a 30% reduction in microvascular disease per 1% drop in HbA1c.
	- **Blood pressure control (for BP >130/80 mmHg):** Yields a 35% reduction in macro/microvascular disease per 10 mmHg drop.
	- **Annual eye examinations:** Results in a 60–70% reduction in serious vision loss.
	- **Foot care in high-risk patients:** Reduces serious foot disease by 50–60%.
	- **ACE inhibitor use:** Yields a 42% reduction in nephropathy and a 22% drop in cardiovascular disease.
	1. Pre-diabetes mellitus. 
	**PRE-DIABETES MELLITUS (IMPAIRED GLUCOSE TOLERANCE)**
	
	**1. CONCEPT AND DEFINITION**
	
	- **Definition:** Pre-diabetes describes a state intermediate between normal glucose metabolism and frank diabetes mellitus.
	- **Terminology:** In epidemiological and clinical terms, this "at-risk" group encompasses the categories of **Impaired Glucose Tolerance (IGT)** and **Impaired Fasting** glucose.
	
	**2. DIAGNOSIS**
	
	- Impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) can only be definitively identified and defined by utilizing the standard **oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)**.
	
	**3. PUBLIC HEALTH SIGNIFICANCE (EXAM ESSENTIALS)**
	
	- **Hidden Burden:** The true magnitude of diabetes and glucose abnormalities in a community is considerably higher when the categories of 'impaired fasting' and 'impaired glucose tolerance' are included in epidemiological estimates.
	- **Risk of Progression:** Individuals with impaired glucose tolerance are at a high risk of progressing to develop Type 2 diabetes mellitus. It serves as a crucial window for primary prevention.
	
	**4. PREVENTIVE MEASURES & INTERVENTIONS (HIGH-YIELD)** The progression from pre-diabetes (IGT) to Type 2 diabetes can be halted or delayed through specific lifestyle and dietary modifications:
	
	- **Weight Reduction:** Voluntary weight loss is a highly effective intervention. It improves insulin sensitivity and has been shown in several randomized controlled trials to significantly reduce the risk of progression from impaired glucose tolerance to Type 2 diabetes.
	- **Dietary Fibre:** High intakes of dietary fibre have been proven in controlled experimental studies to result in reduced blood glucose and insulin levels in individuals with impaired glucose tolerance.
	- **Fat Quality Modification:** In human intervention studies, replacing saturated fatty acids with unsaturated fatty acids (from vegetable sources) leads to improved glucose tolerance and enhanced insulin sensitivity.
	1. Clinical classification of diabetes mellitus. 
	**CLINICAL CLASSIFICATION OF DIABETES MELLITUS**
	
	The WHO (2019) classification system for diabetes prioritizes clinical care to help health professionals choose appropriate treatments, particularly regarding the need for insulin therapy at the time of diagnosis.
	
	**1. TYPE 1 DIABETES (INSULIN-DEPENDENT)**
	
	- **Pathology:** Characterized by B-cell destruction leading to absolute insulin deficiency. It is immune-mediated in over 90% of cases and idiopathic in less than 10%.
	- **Onset:** Typically abrupt; most commonly occurs in childhood and early adulthood (highest incidence among 10-14 year olds).
	- **Clinical Features:** The most severe form of the disease; usually associated with ketosis in its untreated state and is lethal unless promptly diagnosed and treated.
	- **Management:** Exogenous insulin is absolutely required to reverse the catabolic state, prevent ketosis, and reduce blood glucose.
	
	**2. TYPE 2 DIABETES (NON-INSULIN DEPENDENT)**
	
	- **Pathology:** The most common type, characterized by various degrees of B-cell dysfunction and insulin resistance.
	- **Onset:** Gradual in onset, occurring mainly in the middle-aged and elderly populations.
	- **Clinical Features:** Often discovered by chance. It is frequently mild, slow to ketosis, and compatible with long survival if given adequate treatment. It is commonly associated with overweight, central obesity, and a sedentary lifestyle.
	
	**3. HYBRID FORMS OF DIABETES**
	
	- **Slowly evolving, immune-mediated diabetes of adults:** Similar to type 1 but more often has features of the metabolic syndrome and retains greater B-cell function.
	- **Ketosis-prone type 2 diabetes:** Presents initially with ketosis and insulin deficiency but later does not require insulin; it is not immune-mediated.
	
	**4. OTHER SPECIFIC TYPES**
	
	- **Monogenic diabetes:** Caused by specific gene mutations resulting in defects of B-cell function or monogenic defects in insulin action.
	- **Diseases of the exocrine pancreas:** Conditions like trauma, tumors, or inflammation affecting the pancreas resulting in hyperglycaemia.
	- **Endocrine disorders:** Diseases associated with excess secretion of hormones that act as insulin antagonists.
	- **Drug- or chemical-induced:** Caused by medicines or chemicals that impair insulin secretion/action or destroy B-cells.
	- **Infection-related diabetes:** Associated with certain viruses known to cause direct B-cell destruction.
	- **Uncommon specific forms of immune-mediated diabetes**.
	- **Other genetic syndromes:** Chromosomal abnormalities and genetic disorders that increase the risk of diabetes.
	
	**5. UNCLASSIFIED DIABETES**
	
	- A temporary category used to describe diabetes that does not clearly fit into other categories, especially close to the time of initial diagnosis.
	
	**6. HYPERGLYCAEMIA FIRST DETECTED DURING PREGNANCY**
	
	- **Diabetes mellitus in pregnancy:** Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes that is first diagnosed during pregnancy.
	- **Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM):** Hyperglycaemia with blood glucose values above normal but below the diagnostic thresholds for clinical diabetes, occurring during pregnancy. Women with GDM, as well as their children, face an increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes in the future.
	
	_(Note: Impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) describes an intermediate "at-risk" state between normal glucose tolerance and diabetes mellitus, which can only be defined by the standard oral glucose tolerance test)_.
	1. Foot care in a diabetic patient.  
	**FOOT CARE IN A DIABETIC PATIENT**
	
	**1. RATIONALE AND PUBLIC HEALTH IMPORTANCE (HIGH-YIELD)**
	
	- **Complication Risk:** Diabetes is a major cause of disability through severe complications, including gangrene of the lower extremities.
	- **Amputation Rates:** In developed countries, more than half of all non-traumatic lower limb amputations are due to diabetes. Furthermore, these amputations are 10 times more common in diabetic individuals compared to non-diabetics.
	
	**2. CLINICAL EXAMINATION AND MONITORING** Routine checking of the feet is a critical component of secondary prevention and proper management in diabetic patients. The clinical assessment of the feet must include:
	
	- **Blood Circulation:** The feet should be systematically examined for any defective blood circulation. The use of Doppler ultrasound probes is specifically advised for this purpose.
	- **Neurological Assessment:** The patient must be evaluated for any loss of sensation (neuropathy).
	- **Dermatological Assessment:** The overall health and integrity of the skin on the feet must be periodically checked to prevent ulceration.
	
	**3. EFFICACY OF THE INTERVENTION**
	
	- Targeted foot care is an evidence-based individual intervention for diabetics.
	- Providing proper foot care to people with a high risk of developing ulcers yields a massive **50 to 60 per cent reduction in serious foot disease**.
