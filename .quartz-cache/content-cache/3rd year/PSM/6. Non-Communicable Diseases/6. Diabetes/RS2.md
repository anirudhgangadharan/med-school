	**RISK FACTORS OF DIABETES MELLITUS (MULTICAUSATION)**
	
	The aetiology of diabetes mellitus is complex and multifactorial, resulting from an interplay of agent, host, and environmental factors.
	
	**1. Agent Factors** The underlying cause is insulin deficiency, which is absolute in Type 1 and partial in Type 2. The mechanisms include:
	
	- **Pancreatic disorders:** Inflammatory, neoplastic conditions, or other disorders like cystic fibrosis.
	- **Defects in insulin formation:** Synthesis of an abnormal, biologically less active insulin molecule.
	- **Destruction of beta cells:** Due to viral infections or chemical agents.
	- **Decreased insulin sensitivity:** Resulting from a decreased number of adipocyte and monocyte insulin receptors.
	- **Genetic defects:** Such as mutations of the insulin gene.
	- **Autoimmunity:** Defective immunological mechanisms leading to cell-mediated or humoral attacks on the body's islet cells.
	
	**2. Host Factors**
	
	- **Age:** Prevalence rises steeply with age. Type 2 usually presents in middle age, while Type 1 incidence is highest among the 10-14 year age group.
	- **Sex:** In some regions, an excess of male diabetics is observed, though in countries like the UK, the ratio is equal.
	- **Genetic Factors:** Twin studies show approximately 90% concordance for Type 2 diabetes in identical twins, proving a strong genetic component. Concordance in Type 1 is only about 50%.
	- **Genetic Markers:** Type 1 is strongly associated with HLA-B8, B15, DR3, and DR4. Type 2 is not HLA-associated.
	- **Obesity:** Central (abdominal or visceral) adiposity, measured by an increased waist-to-hip ratio or waist circumference, is a powerful determinant of insulin resistance and a major risk factor for Type 2 diabetes.
	- **Maternal Diabetes:** Offspring of diabetic pregnancies are often large at birth, prone to childhood obesity, and have a high risk of developing Type 2 diabetes early in life. Intrauterine growth retardation and low birth weight, followed by rapid catch-up growth, also increase the risk.
	
	**3. Environmental Factors** Susceptibility is frequently unmasked by environmental factors acting on genetically prone individuals:
	
	- **Sedentary Lifestyle:** Lack of physical exercise alters the interaction between insulin and its receptors, leading to Type 2 diabetes.
	- **Diet:** High intake of saturated fats and low intake of dietary fibre (non-starch polysaccharides) increase the risk of impaired glucose tolerance and Type 2 diabetes.
	- **Malnutrition:** Protein-energy malnutrition (PEM) in early infancy and childhood may cause partial failure of beta-cell function.
	- **Alcohol:** Excessive consumption increases risk by promoting obesity and directly damaging the pancreas and liver.
	- **Viral Infections:** Rubella, mumps, and human coxsackie virus B4 can trigger beta-cell destruction in susceptible people.
	- **Chemical Agents:** Toxins like alloxan, streptozotocin, the rodenticide VALCOR, and high intake of cyanide-producing foods (like cassava) destroy beta-cells.
	- **Stress:** Surgery, trauma, and emotional stress can precipitate the disease.
	- **Social Factors:** Urbanization, changes in lifestyle, and lower socio-economic status are increasingly linked to the diabetes epidemic.
	
	---
	
	**COMPLICATIONS OF DIABETES MELLITUS**
	
	If undiagnosed or inadequately treated, diabetes leads to severe, irreversible chronic complications and premature death.
	
	- **Microvascular Complications:** Diabetic retinopathy (leading to blindness/visual impairment), diabetic nephropathy (leading to kidney failure), and diabetic neuropathy.
	- **Macrovascular Complications:** Greatly increased risk of coronary heart disease (ischaemic heart disease), peripheral arterial disease, and cerebrovascular disease (stroke).
	- **Acute/Other Complications:** Ketoacidosis (especially in untreated Type 1 diabetes), gangrene of the lower extremities frequently necessitating amputation (10 times more common in diabetics), cataracts, erectile dysfunction, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease.
	- **Infections:** Increased susceptibility to infectious diseases, particularly tuberculosis.
	- **Pregnancy Complications:** Gestational diabetes poses a high health risk to both the mother and the developing foetus (macrosomia, birth defects).
	
	---
	
	**CONTROL AND PREVENTION OF DIABETES MELLITUS (IN DISTRICT)**
	
	Prevention and control require an integrated approach organized across three levels:
	
	**1. Primary Prevention**
	
	- **Population Strategy (Primordial Prevention):** Aimed at preventing the emergence of risk factors. Key interventions include promoting the maintenance of normal body weight, encouraging regular physical exercise, and adopting healthy nutritional habits (adequate protein, high dietary fibre, and avoidance of sweet/refined foods). These must be integrated into community-based non-communicable disease programs.
	- **High-Risk Strategy:** Targeted at vulnerable individuals (the obese, sedentary individuals, those with a strong family history). Interventions involve correcting over-nutrition, avoiding diabetogenic drugs (like oral contraceptives), limiting alcohol, and reducing atherosclerotic factors like smoking and high blood pressure.
	
	**2. Secondary Prevention**
	
	- **Early Detection (Screening):** Mass screening is unrewarding; screening must target "high-risk" groups (age >40, family history, obesity, women with babies >4.5 kg or excess weight gain in pregnancy, and patients with premature atherosclerosis). The standard oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) or fasting/post-load blood glucose estimations are the cornerstones of diagnosis.
	- **Treatment & Management:** The aim is to maintain blood glucose levels as close to normal as possible and maintain ideal body weight. This is achieved through diet alone, diet with oral antidiabetic drugs, or diet and insulin.
	- **Routine Monitoring:** Periodic checks of blood sugar, urine (for proteins and ketones), blood pressure, visual acuity, body weight, and foot examinations for defective circulation (using Doppler) and loss of sensation. Glycosylated haemoglobin (HbA1c) should be estimated half-yearly to assess long-term glucose control.
	
	**3. Tertiary Prevention**
	
	- Aimed at limiting disability from advanced complications (blindness, kidney failure, amputations).
	- Establishment of specialized diabetic clinics in large towns and cities capable of high-level diagnostic and management skills.
	- Setting up local and national registries for diabetics to facilitate monitoring and epidemiological research.
	
	---
	
	**NOTE ON SELF-CARE IN DIABETES**
	
	Self-care is a crucial, high-yield element of secondary prevention in diabetes. The patient must take major responsibility for their own day-to-day care under medical guidance to optimize the effectiveness of primary health care.
	
	Key components of self-care include:
	
	- **Adherence to Treatment:** Strict compliance with prescribed diet and drug regimens.
	- **Monitoring:** Regular examination of one's own urine and home blood glucose monitoring (using immediate capillary blood glucose meters or test strips).
	- **Medication Administration:** Proper self-administration of insulin where applicable.
	- **Lifestyle Modifications:** Abstinence from alcohol, maintenance of optimum body weight, and engaging in physical activity.
	- **Symptom Recognition:** Being able to recognize the early clinical symptoms associated with glycosuria and life-threatening hypoglycaemia.
	- **Patient Identification:** Carrying an identification card that details the patient's name, address, telephone number, and specific disease/treatment details to assist in medical emergencies.
	- **Education:** Patients and their families must receive continuous health education to acquire a thorough working knowledge of the disease, ensuring long-term compliance and prevention of complications.

	**PLANNING FOR THE PREVENTION OF DIABETES IN A PRIMARY HEALTH CENTRE (PHC) AREA**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION & RATIONAL APPROACH** Planning for diabetes prevention in a PHC area is primarily guided by the **National Programme for Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases (NPCDCS)** and the **Ayushman Bharat Comprehensive Primary Health Care (CPHC)** guidelines. The prevention strategy is multifaceted and is implemented across three levels: Primary, Secondary, and Tertiary prevention.
	
	**2. PRIMARY PREVENTION (PREVENTING DISEASE ONSET)** The primary prevention of Type 2 diabetes aims to eliminate environmental risk factors at the community level. It involves two main strategies:
	
	**A. Population Strategy (Primordial Prevention)** Focuses on the entire community to prevent the emergence of risk factors.
	
	- **Dietary Modifications:** Educating the community to consume an adequate protein diet, high dietary fibre (non-starch polysaccharides like whole grains, vegetables), and avoid sweet and energy-dense foods.
	- **Physical Activity:** Promoting regular physical exercise and discouraging a sedentary lifestyle.
	- **Weight Management:** Encouraging the maintenance of normal body weight.
	- **Implementation:** These health promotion messages are delivered through mass media, community education, interpersonal communication, and during Village Health and Nutrition Days (VHNDs) by the PHC health team (MO, ANM, ASHA).
	
	**B. High-Risk Strategy** Directed at individuals who are highly vulnerable but have not yet developed diabetes.
	
	- **Target Group:** Individuals with a sedentary lifestyle, over-nutrition, and obesity (especially central adiposity).
	- **Interventions:** Correction of over-nutrition, promotion of physical activity, and avoidance of diabetogenic drugs (e.g., oral contraceptives).
	- **Atherosclerosis Prevention:** Aggressive reduction of associated risk factors like smoking, high blood pressure, and elevated cholesterol.
	
	**3. SECONDARY PREVENTION (EARLY DIAGNOSIS & TREATMENT)** The goal at the PHC level is to detect diabetes early, initiate treatment, and halt disease progression.
	
	**A. Screening Strategies (Under NPCDCS & HWCs)**
	
	- **Opportunistic & Population Screening:** Screening of all individuals **above the age of 30 years** and all pregnant women.
	- **Targeted High-Risk Screening:** Routine screening is highly emphasized for individuals over 40 years, those with a family history of diabetes, obese individuals, women who had a baby weighing >4.5 kg, women with excess weight gain in pregnancy, and patients with premature atherosclerosis.
	- **Testing Method at PHC/Sub-centre:** Blood glucose screening is routinely carried out using glucometers (strip method).
	
	**B. Diagnosis & Treatment**
	
	- **Diagnosis:** If random blood sugar is 140 mg/dl and above, the patient is referred to a medical officer for confirmation via fasting/post-prandial venous plasma glucose or the standard oral glucose tolerance test.
	- **Treatment:** The MO at the PHC initiates treatment (diet, oral antidiabetic drugs, or insulin) to maintain blood glucose within normal limits.
	- **Drug Dispensing:** Once diagnosed, the patient must receive at least a one-month supply of drugs from the PHC. Alternatively, a three-month drug supply can be stocked with the ANM at the sub-centre for monthly distribution.
	
	**C. Routine Monitoring & Follow-up**
	
	- **Periodic Check-ups:** Regular checking of blood sugar, blood pressure, weight, visual acuity, and urine for proteins and ketones.
	- **Foot Care:** Meticulous examination of feet for defective blood circulation, loss of sensation, and skin health to prevent ulcers and gangrene.
	- **HbA1c Monitoring:** Half-yearly estimations of glycosylated haemoglobin (HbA1c) to assess long-term glucose control (over the past 2-3 months).
	- **Patient Education (Self-Care):** Educating patients on home blood glucose monitoring, adherence to diet and drugs, recognizing symptoms of hypoglycaemia/glycosuria, and carrying a diabetic identification card.
	
	**4. TERTIARY PREVENTION (DISABILITY LIMITATION)**
	
	- **Referral System:** Complicated cases of diabetes (e.g., those developing retinopathy, nephropathy, coronary thrombosis, or diabetic foot) are referred from the PHC to the Community Health Centre (CHC) or District Hospital for specialized management.
	
	**5. ROLE OF THE PHC HEALTH TEAM (EXAM ESSENTIALS)** For the successful implementation of the diabetes prevention plan, the responsibilities are distributed among the health team:
	
	- **Medical Officer (MO):** Captain of the team; diagnoses cases, initiates treatment, manages complications, and refers difficult cases to higher centres.
	- **Health Worker Female (ANM):** Conducts blood sugar testing (using disposable kits) at the sub-centre/HWC level, distributes medications, and monitors patient compliance.
	- **ASHA / Community Health Volunteer:** Conducts community mobilization, prioritizes households with treatment defaulters, encourages patients to visit the sub-centre for regular check-ups, and facilitates behaviour and lifestyle modifications at the village level.

	**METHODS OF SCREENING FOR DIABETES MELLITUS**
	
	**1. URINE EXAMINATION**
	
	- **The Test:** Testing urine for the presence of glucose, typically 2 hours after a meal, has been a traditional method in medical practice.
	- **Major Limitations (High-Yield):**
	    - **Low Sensitivity:** The sensitivity of this test ranges only between 10 to 50 per cent. While glucose is found in the urine in severe cases, it is often absent in milder forms, resulting in a high rate of "false-negatives".
	    - **False-Positives:** Glycosuria can occasionally be found in perfectly normal people.
	- **Public Health Application:** Because of these significant inadequacies, urine testing is **not** considered an appropriate tool for case-finding or epidemiological population surveys.
	
	**2. BLOOD SUGAR TESTING** Due to the unreliability of urine tests, blood glucose measurement is the preferred method for screening and diagnosis. Mass screening programmes use three types of blood samples:
	
	- **Random Blood Sample:** This is considered unsatisfactory for epidemiological use, as it can only provide a crude estimate of the disease's frequency in the population.
	- **Fasting Blood Sample:** Using the fasting value alone is less reliable because it is difficult to assure a true fasting state (which requires an overnight fast of 8 to 14 hours) in the general population, potentially leading to spurious diagnoses.
	- **Standard Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT):** This remains the **cornerstone of diagnosis**. For epidemiological purposes, the 2-hour value after a 75 g oral glucose load is recommended, either used alone or in combination with the fasting value.
	- **Point-of-Care Devices:** When standard laboratory measurements are not available, point-of-care ("finger stick") devices can be used to measure capillary plasma glucose.
	
	**3. WHO DIAGNOSTIC CRITERIA (2019) FOR SCREENING & DIAGNOSIS** The World Health Organization recommends the following cut-off values for the diagnosis of diabetes:
	
	- **Fasting venous or capillary plasma glucose:** $\ge$ 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL).
	- **2-hour post-load venous/capillary plasma glucose:** $\ge$ 11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL).
	- **Random plasma glucose:** $\ge$ 11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL).
	- **HbA1c (Glycosylated Haemoglobin):** $\ge$ 6.5% (48 mmol/mol).
	
	**4. STRATEGY: WHOM TO SCREEN? (EXAM ESSENTIALS)**
	
	- **Avoid Mass Screening:** Screening the entire population for diabetes is generally not a rewarding exercise.
	- **Selective/High-Risk Screening:** Screening efforts must be strictly directed at "high-risk" groups to maximize the yield. These groups include:
	    - Individuals aged 40 years or older.
	    - Persons with a positive family history of diabetes.
	    - Obese individuals.
	    - Women who have previously delivered a large baby weighing over 4.5 kg (or 3.5 kg in constitutionally small populations).
	    - Women who exhibit excessive weight gain during pregnancy.
	    - Patients presenting with premature atherosclerosis.

	**RISK FACTORS / EPIDEMIOLOGICAL DETERMINANTS OF DIABETES MELLITUS**
	
	The aetiology of diabetes mellitus is multifactorial and is broadly classified into Agent, Host, and Environmental risk factors.
	
	**1. AGENT FACTORS** The underlying cause of diabetes is insulin deficiency, which is absolute in Type 1 diabetes and partial in Type 2 diabetes. This may be due to a variety of mechanisms:
	
	- **Pancreatic disorders:** Inflammatory, neoplastic, or other conditions like cystic fibrosis.
	- **Defects in insulin formation:** Synthesis of an abnormal, biologically less active insulin molecule.
	- **Beta-cell destruction:** Caused by viral infections or chemical agents.
	- **Decreased insulin sensitivity:** Due to decreased numbers of adipocyte and monocyte insulin receptors.
	- **Genetic defects and autoimmunity:** Such as mutations of the insulin gene and defective immunological mechanisms.
	
	**2. HOST FACTORS**
	
	- **Age:** Prevalence rises steeply with advancing age. Type 2 diabetes usually comes to light in the middle years of life. Malnutrition-related diabetes affects a large number of young people.
	- **Sex:** In some countries (e.g., UK), the male-female ratio is equal. In South-East Asia, an excess of male diabetics has been observed.
	- **Genetic factors (High-Yield):** The genetic nature of diabetes is undisputed. Twin studies show approximately 90 per cent concordance for Type 2 diabetes in identical twins, demonstrating a massive genetic component. In Type 1 diabetes, concordance is only about 50 per cent.
	- **Genetic markers:** Type 1 diabetes is strongly associated with HLA-B8, B15, DR3, and DR4. The highest risk is carried by individuals with both DR3 and DR4. Type 2 diabetes is **not** HLA-associated.
	- **Immune mechanisms:** Defective immunological mechanisms can cause cell-mediated and humoral activity against the body's own insulin-producing beta cells.
	- **Obesity (Central Adiposity):** Central adiposity (indicated by increased waist circumference or waist-to-hip ratio) is a major determinant of insulin resistance and a powerful risk factor for Type 2 diabetes. The risk is related to both the degree and duration of obesity.
	- **Maternal diabetes & Foetal growth:** Offspring of diabetic pregnancies are often large at birth, tend to develop childhood obesity, and have a high risk of developing Type 2 diabetes early in life. Additionally, intrauterine growth retardation (low birth weight) followed by rapid catch-up growth increases the risk of subsequent diabetes.
	
	**3. ENVIRONMENTAL & LIFESTYLE RISK FACTORS** Susceptibility to diabetes is often unmasked by environmental factors acting on genetically susceptible individuals.
	
	- **Sedentary lifestyle:** Lack of physical exercise alters the interaction between insulin and its receptors, leading to Type 2 diabetes.
	- **Dietary factors:** A high intake of saturated fats is associated with lower insulin sensitivity and a higher risk of Type 2 diabetes. Conversely, a high intake of dietary fibre (non-starch polysaccharides) is highly protective and reduces blood glucose and insulin levels.
	- **Malnutrition:** Protein-energy malnutrition (PEM) in early infancy and childhood may cause partial failure of beta-cell function, explaining the impaired carbohydrate tolerance seen in kwashiorkor.
	- **Alcohol:** Excessive alcohol consumption increases the risk of diabetes by promoting obesity and causing direct damage to the pancreas and liver.
	- **Viral infections:** Viruses such as rubella, mumps, and human coxsackie virus B4 can trigger B-cell destruction in immunogenetically susceptible people.
	- **Chemical agents:** Toxins like alloxan, streptozotocin, the rodenticide VALCOR, and high intakes of cyanide-producing foods (e.g., cassava) are toxic to pancreatic beta cells.
	- **Stress:** Severe stress from surgery, trauma, or emotional situations can precipitate or "bring out" the disease.
	- **Social factors:** Diabetes is strongly linked to urbanization and changes in lifestyle. A major epidemiological shift is that diabetes is now increasingly common in lower social classes, whereas historically it was a disease of the affluent.
	
	---
	
	**SUMMARY OF EVIDENCE ON LIFESTYLE FACTORS (EXAM ESSENTIAL)** Based on epidemiological data, the strength of evidence for lifestyle factors increasing the risk of Type 2 diabetes is categorized as follows:
	
	- **Convincing increased risk:** Overweight and obesity, abdominal obesity, physical inactivity, and maternal diabetes.
	- **Probable increased risk:** Saturated fats, and intrauterine growth retardation.
	- **Possible increased risk:** Total fat intake, trans-fatty acids, and excess alcohol.

	**WHO RECOMMENDED DIAGNOSTIC CRITERIA FOR DIABETES (2019)**
	
	The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends the following diagnostic cut-off values for identifying diabetes mellitus:
	
	**1. FASTING PLASMA GLUCOSE (FPG)**
	
	- **Venous or capillary plasma glucose:** $\ge$ 7.0 mmol/L ($\ge$ 126 mg/dL).
	- _Requirement:_ Must be measured after an overnight fast of 8 to 14 hours.
	
	**2. 2-HOUR POST-LOAD PLASMA GLUCOSE (OGTT)**
	
	- **Venous plasma glucose:** $\ge$ 11.1 mmol/L ($\ge$ 200 mg/dL).
	- **Capillary plasma glucose:** $\ge$ 12.2 mmol/L ($\ge$ 220 mg/dL).
	
	**3. RANDOM PLASMA GLUCOSE**
	
	- **Level:** $\ge$ 11.1 mmol/L ($\ge$ 200 mg/dL).
	- _Note:_ Commonly evaluated in the presence of symptoms, though the 2-hour value after a 75 g oral glucose load remains the cornerstone of diagnosis, especially for mass screening or epidemiological surveys.
	
	**4. GLYCOSYLATED HAEMOGLOBIN (HbA1c)**
	
	- **Diagnostic cut-off value:** $\ge$ 6.5% (48 mmol/mol).
	
	**EXAM ESSENTIAL NOTES:**
	
	- **Methodology Choice:** If standard laboratory measurement is unavailable, point-of-care ("finger stick") devices can be utilized, as they report glucose values in capillary plasma.
	- **Type 1 Suspects:** Plasma glucose testing is the preferred diagnostic method in people presenting with symptoms who are suspected of having Type 1 diabetes.

	**SELF-CARE IN DIABETES MELLITUS**
	
	**1. DEFINITION AND SIGNIFICANCE**
	
	- Self-care is a crucial element in the secondary prevention of diabetes mellitus.
	- It implies that the diabetic patient must take a major responsibility for their own care and management under medical guidance.
	- By teaching patients self-care practices, the massive burden on the official health care services is considerably reduced.
	
	**2. ESSENTIAL COMPONENTS OF DIABETIC SELF-CARE (HIGH-YIELD)** The core activities a diabetic patient must undertake include:
	
	- **Diet & Medication:** Strict adherence to prescribed dietary modifications and drug regimens.
	- **Blood Glucose Monitoring:** Routine examination of one's own urine and home blood glucose monitoring. Assessment of glycaemic control is greatly aided by immediate capillary blood glucose measurements using available meters or direct reading Haemoglucotest strips.
	- **Insulin Administration:** Learning the proper technique for the self-administration of insulin.
	- **Lifestyle Modifications:** Active maintenance of optimum body weight and strict abstinence from alcohol.
	- **Symptom Recognition:** The patient must be adequately educated to recognize the early warning symptoms associated with hypoglycaemia and glycosuria.
	- **Medical Follow-up:** Consistently attending periodic health check-ups for routine monitoring.
	
	**3. PATIENT IDENTIFICATION AND EDUCATION**
	
	- **Identification Card:** Every diabetic patient should carry a specific identification card showing their name, address, telephone number, and the exact details of the treatment they are receiving.
	- **Health Education:** The patient must possess a working knowledge of diabetes. Extensive education of both the patients and their family members is essential to optimize the overall effectiveness of primary health care services.
