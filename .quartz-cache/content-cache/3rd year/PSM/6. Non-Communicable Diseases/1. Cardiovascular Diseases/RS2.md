1. Role of 'diet' in cardiovascular diseases
	1. **ROLE OF DIET IN CARDIOVASCULAR DISEASES (CVD)**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION & GENERAL DIETARY PRACTICES**
	
	- Diet plays a paramount role in governing situations that favour the onset of cardiovascular diseases, particularly coronary heart disease (CHD) and hypertension.
	- Unhealthy dietary practices that cluster together and increase the risk of CVD include a high consumption of saturated fats, salt, and refined carbohydrates, coupled with a low consumption of fresh vegetables and fruits.
	- Adequate consumption of fruits and vegetables significantly reduces the risk of cardiovascular diseases.
	
	**2. ROLE OF DIETARY FATS AND CHOLESTEROL**
	
	- **Total Fat:** A diet rich in fat poses a major threat to human health, and dietary fat representing 40 per cent or more of the energy supply is identified as a major risk factor for CHD.
	- **Saturated Fatty Acids (SFAs):** High consumption of saturated fats is directly linked to heart disease. SFAs (especially C12, C14, and C16 acids) increase plasma cholesterol levels. Replacing SFAs with polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) decreases LDL cholesterol, lowers the total/HDL cholesterol ratio, and significantly decreases the risk of CHD events.
	- **Trans-Fatty Acids (TFAs):** TFAs derived from commercially partially hydrogenated vegetable oils (vanaspati) increase CHD risk factors and events to a severe extent.
	- **Polyunsaturated Fatty Acids (PUFAs):** Dietary unsaturated fatty acids with two or more double bonds lower plasma cholesterol by reducing the synthesis of VLDL. Essential fatty acids, specifically linoleic and arachidonic acids, inhibit platelet aggregation and prevent intravascular thrombus formation. Arachidonic acid metabolizes into prostacyclin, which relaxes coronary blood vessels and acts as the body's best bulwark against CHD.
	- **Cholesterol and Lipoproteins:** A persistently high serum cholesterol level can almost certainly precipitate a cardiac event such as CHD.
	    - **LDL (Low-Density Lipoprotein):** Delivers cholesterol to body cells; excessive levels lead to the accumulation of cholesterol in the smooth muscle cells of the vascular system, favouring atherosclerosis.
	    - **HDL (High-Density Lipoprotein):** Functions in the removal of cholesterol from cells and exerts a highly protective effect against the development of atherosclerosis.
	    - **Triglycerides:** High concentrations of serum triglycerides are also claimed to be an independent risk factor for CHD.
	
	**3. ROLE OF CARBOHYDRATES AND DIETARY FIBRE**
	
	- **Complex Carbohydrates:** Coronary heart disease rates are noted to be lowest in populations consuming high-carbohydrate diets. A historical decrease in the consumption of dietary carbohydrates has been associated with a steady increase in CHD mortality rates.
	- **Refined Carbohydrates:** Excessive consumption of refined carbohydrates and free sugars is causally linked to obesity and diabetes, which are major independent risk factors for CVD.
	- **Dietary Fibre:** Several epidemiological studies indicate an inverse association between fibre intake and the risk of CHD. Dietary fibre (particularly gums and pectins) binds to bile salts, preventing their reabsorption in the circulation and thus attenuating blood cholesterol levels.
	
	**4. ROLE OF SODIUM (SALT) AND POTASSIUM**
	
	- **Salt Intake:** High salt consumption is an extremely important determinant of high blood pressure and cardiovascular risk. Approximately 1.8 million deaths from CVD causes have been attributed globally to excess salt/sodium intake.
	- **Hypertension Link:** There are consistent correlations between dietary sodium intake and hypertension; populations ingesting more than 400 mmol/day of sodium have the highest incidence of hypertension, while primitive societies ingesting less than 60 mmol/day have virtually no hypertension.
	- **Potassium:** Low dietary potassium has been implicated in the aetiology of hypertension. Potassium supplements lower blood pressure, and the ideal sodium-to-potassium ratio in the diet is 1:1.
	
	**5. ROLE OF ALCOHOL**
	
	- High alcohol intake (defined as 75g or more per day) is a major independent risk factor for CHD, hypertension, and all cardiovascular diseases.
	- Harmful use of alcohol globally causes around 1.2 million deaths specifically from cardiovascular and digestive diseases.
	
	**6. ROLE OF DIETARY ANTIOXIDANTS**
	
	- Oxidative stress and reactive oxygen species (ROS) play a fundamental role in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular diseases.
	- Dietary antioxidants (Vitamins E, C, beta-carotene, flavonoids) mitigate this oxidant damage. Vegetables, fruits, legumes, and whole grains are excellent sources of these protective antioxidants.
	
	**7. ROLE OF WATER HARDNESS**
	
	- Epidemiological reports show an inverse statistical association between the hardness of drinking water and the death rate from cardiovascular diseases.
	- Areas supplied with soft drinking water show a significantly higher prevalence of arteriosclerotic heart disease, hypertension, and sudden cardiovascular deaths. Hardness of water appears to have a beneficial effect against CVD.
	
	**8. HIGH-YIELD: DIETARY GOALS ("PRUDENT DIET") FOR CVD PREVENTION** To prevent cardiovascular diseases, WHO Expert Committees recommend the following dietary goals:
	
	- Limit dietary fat to approximately 15-30% of total daily energy intake.
	- Saturated fats should contribute no more than 10% of total energy intake.
	- Unsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs/MUFAs) should be substituted for the remaining fat requirement.
	- Reduce dietary cholesterol to below 100 mg per 1000 kcal per day.
	- Avoid excessive consumption of refined carbohydrates and increase complex carbohydrates (vegetables, fruits, whole grains).
	- Reduce salt intake to 5 g daily or less.
	- Avoid or strictly restrict alcohol consumption.
	
- 2. State the modified Jone's criteria in acute rheumatic fever
	**MODIFIED JONES CRITERIA (2015 REVISION) FOR ACUTE RHEUMATIC FEVER**
	
	The 2015 WHO criteria for the diagnosis of Rheumatic Fever (RF) and Rheumatic Heart Disease (RHD) are based on the revised Jones criteria. A major update in the 2015 revision is the stratification of diagnostic criteria based on the population's epidemiological risk level (Low Risk versus Moderate/High Risk).
	
	**1. MAJOR CRITERIA**
	
	**In Low-Risk Populations:**
	
	- Carditis (Clinical or Subclinical).
	- Arthritis – strictly Polyarthritis.
	- Chorea.
	- Erythema marginatum.
	- Subcutaneous nodules.
	
	**In Moderate/High-Risk Populations:**
	
	- Carditis (Clinical or Subclinical).
	- Arthritis – Monoarthritis or Polyarthritis, and possibly Polyarthralgia.
	- Chorea.
	- Erythema marginatum.
	- Subcutaneous nodules.
	
	**2. MINOR CRITERIA**
	
	**In Low-Risk Populations:**
	
	- **Clinical:** Polyarthralgia.
	- **Clinical:** Hyperpyrexia ($\ge$ 38.5°C).
	- **Laboratory:** ESR $\ge$ 60 mm/h and/or CRP $\ge$ 3.0 mg/dl.
	- **ECG:** Prolonged PR interval (after taking into account age-related differences, provided carditis is not already counted as a major criterion).
	
	**In Moderate/High-Risk Populations:**
	
	- **Clinical:** Monoarthralgia.
	- **Clinical:** Hyperpyrexia ($\ge$ 38.5°C).
	- **Laboratory:** ESR $\ge$ 30 mm/h and/or CRP $\ge$ 3.0 mg/dl.
	- **ECG:** Prolonged PR interval (after taking into account age-related differences, provided carditis is not already counted as a major criterion).
	
	**3. ESSENTIAL PREREQUISITE**
	
	- For any diagnosis, there **must** be evidence of an antecedent group A $\beta$-hemolytic streptococcal infection.
	
	**4. RULES FOR DIAGNOSIS (HIGH-YIELD)**
	
	- **For the First Episode of RF:** Requires a confirmation of **2 Major criteria** OR **1 Major + 2 Minor criteria**.
	- **For Subsequent Episodes (Recurrence):** Requires a confirmation of **2 Major criteria** OR **1 Major + 2 Minor criteria** OR **3 Minor criteria**.
	
	_(Exam Note: Always highlight that the 2015 modifications officially included "subclinical carditis" (detected via echocardiography/Doppler) as a major criterion, and lowered the threshold for arthritis and inflammatory markers in moderate/high-risk populations to increase diagnostic sensitivity)_.

3. Describe the primary prevention of rheumatic heart disease.
	
	**PRIMARY PREVENTION OF RHEUMATIC HEART DISEASE (RHD)**
	
	**1. AIM AND RATIONALE**
	
	- **Objective:** The primary aim is to prevent the very first attack of Rheumatic Fever (RF).
	- **Mechanism:** This is achieved by identifying all patients with Group A streptococcal throat infection (pharyngitis) and treating them promptly and adequately with penicillin.
	
	**2. TARGET POPULATION & SURVEILLANCE**
	
	- **High-Risk Groups:** A viable and practical approach is to concentrate primary prevention efforts on "high-risk" groups, predominantly **school-age children**.
	- **Surveillance:** These children should be kept under regular surveillance for streptococcal pharyngitis. Maternal and Child Health (MCH) and school health services should be actively utilized for this purpose.
	
	**3. DIAGNOSIS AND MANAGEMENT STRATEGY**
	
	- **Ideal Approach:** Ideally, every sore throat should be swabbed and cultured. If streptococci are present, the child must be put on penicillin.
	- **Practical (Empirical) Approach:** Since facilities for throat swab cultures are not easily or widely available, it is medically justified to treat a sore throat with penicillin empirically, even without culture confirmation.
	
	**4. RECOMMENDED DRUG REGIMENS (HIGH-YIELD)** **A. Drug of Choice (Penicillin)**
	
	- **Intramuscular (IM) Regimen:** A single intramuscular injection of Benzathine Benzyl Penicillin.
	    - **Adults:** 1.2 million units.
	    - **Children:** 600,000 units.
	- **Oral Regimen:** Oral penicillin (Penicillin V or Penicillin G) given for a full course of **10 days**. This is considered the least expensive method for eradicating streptococci from the throat.
	
	**B. Alternative Drugs (For Penicillin-Allergic Patients)**
	
	- **1st Generation Cephalosporins:** Cefadroxil or Cefalexin.
	- **Macrolides:** Erythromycin, Clarithromycin, or Azithromycin.
	
	**5. PROGRAMMATIC CHALLENGES & LIMITATIONS**
	
	- **Logistical Hurdles:** While theoretically simple, primary prevention is practically difficult and often unfeasible in many developing countries.
	- **Cost-Effectiveness Issue:** To prevent just a single case of RHD, several thousand cases of streptococcal throat infections must be accurately identified and treated.
	- **Shift to Secondary Prevention:** Because of the impossible logistics and enormous financial constraints associated with primary prevention, public health efforts in developing countries are largely forced to concentrate on secondary prevention (preventing recurrences of RF).
