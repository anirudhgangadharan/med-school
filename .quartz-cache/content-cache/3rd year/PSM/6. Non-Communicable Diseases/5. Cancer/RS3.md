1. What is cancer registry? Write its public health importance.  
	**CANCER REGISTRY AND ITS PUBLIC HEALTH IMPORTANCE**
	
	**1. DEFINITION AND CONCEPT**
	
	- **Definition:** A cancer registry is a system that implies something more than just "notification" of the disease. It requires that a permanent record be established, that the cases be followed up, and that basic statistical tabulations be prepared on both the frequency of the disease and patient survival.
	- **Significance:** It is considered a _sine qua non_ (an absolutely essential condition) for any effective cancer control programme.
	
	**2. TYPES OF CANCER REGISTRIES** Cancer registries are basically of two types:
	
	- **Hospital-Based Registries:** These include data of patients treated by a particular institution, whether as in-patients or out-patients. They collect a uniform minimum set of data as recommended by the WHO. However, because a hospital population is a "selected" population, their use for broader epidemiological purposes is limited.
	- **Population-Based Registries:** These aim to cover the complete cancer situation in a defined geographic area. The optimum size of a base population for this type of registry is between 2 to 7 million. Setting up a population-based registry is usually an extension of a hospital-based registry.
	
	_Exam Note: In India, the National Cancer Registry Programme of the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) provides data on cancer incidence, mortality, and distribution through 28 population-based registries and 5 hospital-based registries._
	
	---
	
	**3. PUBLIC HEALTH IMPORTANCE (USES) OF CANCER REGISTRIES**
	
	**A. Assessment of Disease Burden**
	
	- **Magnitude:** Registries provide a strong base for assessing the exact magnitude of the cancer problem (disease load) in the community.
	- **True Incidence Rates:** Data from population-based registries alone can provide the true incidence rate of cancer in a given geographic area.
	
	**B. Epidemiological Research and Surveillance**
	
	- **Aetiological Clues:** They serve as an essential tool for initiating epidemiological enquiries into the causes and risk factors of cancer.
	- **Trend Analysis:** Registries are vital for the continuous surveillance of time trends of the disease over years.
	- **Special Studies:** Patients on a register frequently serve as subjects for special epidemiological and clinical studies.
	
	**C. Planning and Administration**
	
	- **Resource Allocation:** The data generated is critical for planning necessary cancer health care services, manpower, and infrastructure.
	
	**D. Evaluation of Control Measures**
	
	- **Evaluating Operational Activities:** Registry data helps in the evaluation of operational activities across all main areas of a cancer control programme.
	- **Evaluating Treatment:** Hospital-based registries with long-term follow-up of patients are of considerable value in evaluating the effectiveness of various diagnostic and treatment programmes.
	
	**E. Natural History and Prognosis**
	
	- **Survival Data:** Morbidity registers are a highly valuable source of information for understanding the natural course of the disease, calculating case fatality rates, monitoring the duration of illness, and assessing patient survival.
	1. Cancer screening in India. 
	**CANCER SCREENING IN INDIA**
	
	**1. CURRENT STATUS AND MAGNITUDE**
	*   **Late Diagnosis:** Facilities for screening and proper management of cancer patients are grossly limited in India. More than two-thirds of cancer patients are already in an advanced and incurable stage at the time of diagnosis.
	*   **Most Common Sites:** The two organ sites most commonly involved in India are the uterine cervix in women and the oropharynx in both sexes, representing approximately 50 per cent of all cancer cases. 
	*   **Accessibility:** Fortunately, both these cancers are easily accessible for physical examination and are highly amenable to early diagnosis.
	
	**2. BROAD METHODS OF CANCER SCREENING**
	Cancer screening approaches are classified into three types:
	*   **Mass screening by comprehensive cancer detection examination:** Rapid clinical examination of one or more body sites by a physician.
	*   **Mass screening at single sites:** Examination of specific sites, such as the uterine cervix, breast, or lung.
	*   **Selective screening:** Examination of individuals at special risk (e.g., parous women of lower socio-economic strata upwards of 30 years of age for cervical cancer, or chronic smokers for lung cancer).
	
	**3. NATIONAL STRATEGY & PROGRAMMES**
	*   **NPCDCS:** Under the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Cancer, Diabetes, Cardiovascular Diseases and Stroke (NPCDCS), secondary prevention focuses on the early detection and diagnosis of common cancers (cervix, mouth, breast, and tobacco-related cancers) through screening and self-examination methods.
	*   **Ayushman Bharat (Health & Wellness Centres):** At the primary care level, Health and Wellness Centres (HWCs) provide support for universal screening for oral, breast, and cervical cancers for the population aged 30 years and above.
	
	**4. SCREENING FOR SPECIFIC CANCERS IN INDIA (HIGH-YIELD)**
	
	**A. Cervical Cancer**
	*   **Pap Smear (Cervical Cytology):** The standard test to detect the prolonged early phase of cancer in situ. Policy suggests all women should have a Pap test at the age of 30 years, and every 3 years thereafter.
	*   **Visual Inspection Tests (Cost-Effective Alternatives):**
	    *   **VIA (Visual Inspection with Acetic acid):** Involves screening women using visual inspection after the application of a freshly prepared 5 per cent acetic acid solution. It detects well-defined opaque acetowhite lesions. Its sensitivity tends to be similar to cytology-based screening.
	    *   **VIAM:** VIA with magnification.
	    *   **VILI:** Visual inspection post-application of Lugol's iodine.
	*   **Single Visit Approach:** In India, VIA is followed by a "single visit approach" for further investigation and management at the district hospital, where treatment based on colposcopy is offered in the same visit.
	*   **HPV Testing:** WHO now encourages HPV-DNA and HPV mRNA tests as objective, simpler, and more cost-effective alternatives to cytology or visual inspection. 
	
	**B. Breast Cancer**
	*   **Breast Self-Examination (BSE):** All women should be encouraged to perform BSE. It is often the only feasible approach for wide population coverage in developing countries, as women frequently find lumps before a physician does.
	*   **Clinical Palpation:** Conducted by a physician, though it may be unreliable for large fatty breasts.
	*   **Mammography:** The most sensitive and specific method for detecting small tumours. However, routine mammography is not recommended for women under 35 years of age unless they are symptomatic or have a strong family history of early-onset breast cancer. 
	
	**C. Oral Cancer**
	*   **Direct Inspection:** Oral cancers are easily accessible for direct physical inspection.
	*   **Role of Health Workers:** Primary health care workers (such as village health guides and multi-purpose workers) are in a strategic position to detect oral cancers at an early (precancerous) stage during routine home visits.
	
	**D. Lung Cancer**
	*   **Methods:** Chest radiograph and sputum cytology.
	*   **Public Health Policy:** Mass screening for lung cancer is not recommended as a routine public health policy because it is expensive and has less potential for reducing mortality compared to primary prevention (like controlling the smoking epidemic).
	1. Viral causes of cancer 
	**VIRAL CAUSES OF CANCER**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION**
	Environmental factors and various infectious agents play a major role in carcinogenesis. It is estimated that biological agents (viruses and parasites) are responsible for 1 to 5 per cent of all human cancers. Globally, at least 2 million cancer cases per year (about 18 per cent of the global cancer burden) are attributable to a few specific chronic infections, with the principal infectious agents being viruses.
	
	**2. SPECIFIC ONCOGENIC VIRUSES AND ASSOCIATED MALIGNANCIES (HIGH-YIELD)**
	
	**A. Hepatitis B Virus (HBV) and Hepatitis C Virus (HCV)**
	*   **Associated Malignancy:** Hepatocellular carcinoma (Liver cancer).
	*   **Pathogenesis:** Chronic infection with HBV and HCV leads to progressive liver fibrosis and cirrhosis, eventually carrying a markedly increased risk of hepatocellular carcinoma.
	*   **Aggravating Factors:** Co-infections with HIV, HCV, and Hepatitis D Virus (HDV) along with alcohol use accelerate disease progression to liver cancer.
	
	**B. Human Papilloma Virus (HPV)**
	*   **Associated Malignancies:** Cancer of the uterine cervix, penile cancer, anal cancer, and oro-pharyngeal cancers. 
	*   **Key Features:** HPV is sexually transmitted and is considered a necessary cause for the development of cervical cancer. The virus is found in more than 95 per cent of cervical cancer cases.
	*   **Global Burden:** HPV infection is responsible for approximately 528,000 cases of cervical cancer and 311,000 cervical cancer deaths globally each year. 
	
	**C. Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV)**
	*   **Associated Malignancies:** Kaposi's sarcoma and Non-Hodgkin's lymphoma.
	*   **Key Features:** HIV causes severe immunosuppression, leaving the body vulnerable to unusual malignancies. The relative risk of Kaposi's sarcoma in HIV patients is extremely high, making it one of the first recognized manifestations of the AIDS epidemic. Non-Hodgkin's lymphoma (cancer of the lymph nodes and spleen) occurs as a late complication of AIDS. 
	
	**D. Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV)**
	*   **Associated Malignancies:** Burkitt's lymphoma and Nasopharyngeal carcinoma.
	
	**E. Human T-cell Leukaemia Virus (HTLV-1)**
	*   **Associated Malignancy:** Adult T-cell leukaemia/lymphoma.
	*   **Geographic Distribution:** This association has been particularly noted in the United States and southern parts of Japan. 
	
	**F. Cytomegalovirus (CMV)**
	*   **Associated Malignancy:** CMV is a suspected oncogenic agent. Classical Kaposi's sarcoma has been found to be associated with a higher prevalence of antibodies to CMV.
	
	**G. Other Suspected Viral Associations**
	*   **Hodgkin's Disease:** This lymphoma is also believed by some researchers to have a viral origin.
	
	**3. PUBLIC HEALTH IMPORTANCE & PREVENTION**
	The identification of viral causes of cancer has opened up exciting prospects for the primary prevention of specific malignancies through immunization. 
	*   **Hepatitis B Vaccine:** Universal infant immunization against HBV is a highly effective population-wide intervention for the primary prevention of primary liver cancer (hepatocellular carcinoma).
	*   **HPV Vaccine:** Vaccination of young girls against HPV is strongly recommended as the primary measure to prevent cervical cancer. Wider provision of HPV vaccines will also drastically reduce the incidence of penile and anal cancers.
	1. Prevention of cancers. 
	**PREVENTION AND CONTROL OF CANCERS**
	
	Cancer control consists of a series of measures based on present medical knowledge aimed at reducing the number of new cases, increasing the number of cures, and reducing invalidism. It is estimated that at least one-third of all cancers are preventable. The basic approach relies heavily on Primary and Secondary prevention.
	
	**I. PRIMARY PREVENTION** Primary prevention aims to eliminate or modify the environmental, economic, social, and behavioural factors responsible for cancer causation.
	
	- **Control of Tobacco and Alcohol:** This offers the greatest hope for reducing tobacco-induced and alcohol-related cancer deaths. The control of tobacco smoking alone could reduce the total burden of cancer by over a third. Measures include banning smoking in public places, enforcing bans on tobacco advertising, and raising taxes.
	- **Personal Hygiene:** Improvements in personal and genital hygiene can lead to a significant decline in the incidence of certain types of cancer, specifically cancer of the uterine cervix.
	- **Protection from Radiation:** Special efforts must be made to reduce the amount of radiation exposure (including unnecessary medical radiation like X-rays) received by each individual to a minimum.
	- **Occupational Exposures:** Measures to protect workers from occupational exposure to industrial carcinogens (e.g., asbestos, benzene, aniline dyes) must be strictly enforced in industries.
	- **Immunization (High-Yield):**
	    - Vaccination against the **Hepatitis B virus** is a major preventive measure against primary liver cancer (hepatocellular carcinoma).
	    - Vaccination against the **Human Papillomavirus (HPV)** is a highly effective intervention for the prevention of cervical cancer.
	- **Control of Foods, Drugs, and Cosmetics:** These items must be continually tested and monitored for the presence of possible carcinogens.
	- **Treatment of Precancerous Lesions:** Early detection and prompt treatment of precancerous conditions (such as cervical tears, intestinal polyposis, warts, chronic gastritis, chronic cervicitis, and adenomata) is one of the cornerstones of cancer prevention.
	- **Dietary Modifications:** Promoting public awareness about healthy diets, reducing the intake of foods high in fat, and avoiding preserved, cured, and salted foods.
	- **Cancer Education:** Health education must be aggressively directed at "high-risk" groups. The public must be taught to recognize the 8 early warning signs or "danger signals" of cancer to motivate them to seek early diagnosis and treatment.
	
	**II. SECONDARY PREVENTION** Secondary prevention aims at the early detection of the disease, preferably at a pre-invasive (_in situ_) or pre-malignant stage, followed by prompt treatment.
	
	- **Cancer Registration:** Cancer registration is a _sine qua non_ (an absolutely essential condition) for any cancer control programme. Hospital-based and population-based registries provide a strong base for assessing the magnitude of the problem, surveillance of time trends, and evaluation of treatment programmes.
	- **Early Detection and Screening:** Rapidly applied screening tests are the main weapon for early detection because most cancers begin as localized lesions, and early diagnosis offers a very high rate of cure.
	    - _Cervical Cancer:_ Can be detected using the Pap smear (cervical cytology), Visual Inspection with 5% Acetic Acid (VIA), or objective HPV-DNA testing.
	    - _Breast Cancer:_ Early detection techniques include Breast Self-Examination (BSE), palpation by a physician, and Mammography.
	    - _Oral Cancer:_ Readily accessible for direct physical inspection by primary health care workers, allowing detection at the precancerous stage (e.g., leukoplakia).
	- **Treatment:** Treatment facilities must be made available to all cancer patients, utilizing surgical removal, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or a multi-modality approach.
	- **Palliative Care:** For patients whose cancer is beyond the curable stage, the goal must shift to providing comprehensive pain relief. Freedom from cancer pain is considered a fundamental right of cancer patients.
1. Enumerate danger (warning) signals of cancer 
	
	**WARNING (DANGER) SIGNALS OF CANCER**
	
	Cancer organizations in many countries remind the public of the following eight early warning signs or "danger signals" of cancer. This is a crucial component of cancer education aimed at motivating the public to seek early diagnosis and treatment.
	
	The 8 danger signals of cancer are:
	
	1. **A lump or hard area in the breast**.
	2. **A change in a wart or mole**.
	3. **A persistent change in digestive and bowel habits**.
	4. **A persistent cough or hoarseness**.
	5. **Excessive loss of blood at the monthly period or loss of blood outside the usual dates**.
	6. **Blood loss from any natural orifice**.
	7. **A swelling or sore that does not get better**.
	8. **Unexplained loss of weight**.
	
	**Public Health Importance:** These danger signals form the core of mass cancer education campaigns. Such education must be aggressively directed at "high-risk" groups to ensure early case detection, which is one of the cornerstones of cancer prevention.
	1. Screening of cancer cervix. 
	**SCREENING FOR CANCER CERVIX**
	
	**1. RATIONALE & APPROACH**
	
	- Cervical cancer follows a progressive course and has a prolonged early phase of cancer in situ, making it highly amenable to early detection through screening.
	- Organized population-based screening programmes have markedly reduced the incidence and mortality from cervical cancer in developed nations.
	- Selective screening focuses on high-risk groups, such as parous women of lower socio-economic strata who are 30 years of age and older.
	
	**2. TARGET AGE & FREQUENCY (WHO GUIDELINES)**
	
	- **General Population:** Screening should start from 30 years of age with a validated HPV test, repeated every 5 to 10 years.
	- **Women living with HIV:** Because they are at a much higher risk, screening must begin earlier at 25 years of age and must be performed more frequently, every 3 to 5 years.
	
	**3. METHODS OF SCREENING**
	
	**A. Cytological Screening (Pap Smear)**
	
	- The Papanicolaou (Pap) smear or cervical cytology is the traditional standard test used to detect the prolonged early phase of cancer in situ.
	- Current policy suggests all women should have a Pap test at the age of 30 years, and then every 3 years thereafter, alongside periodic pelvic examinations.
	- **Limitation:** It requires excessive resources in terms of laboratories, equipment, and trained personnel, making it less viable for widespread use in developing countries.
	
	**B. Visual Inspection-Based Tests** These serve as cost-effective alternative screening methods with sensitivities comparable to cytology-based screening, and they are easy to carry out by trained health workers.
	
	- **Types:**
	    - **VIA:** Visual inspection with 5 per cent acetic acid.
	    - **VIAM:** VIA with magnification.
	    - **VILI:** Visual inspection post application of Lugol's iodine.
	- **Procedure (VIA):** The cervix is visually inspected after the application of a freshly prepared 5 per cent acetic acid solution (5 ml of glacial acetic acid mixed with 95 ml distilled water).
	- **Positive Result:** A test is considered positive if there is detection of well-defined opaque acetowhite lesions close to the squamo-columnar junction, a well-defined circum-orificial acetowhite lesion, or dense acetowhitening of an ulceroproliferative growth on the cervix.
	
	**C. HPV (Human Papillomavirus) Testing**
	
	- The WHO currently strongly encourages countries to transition to HPV tests, including HPV-DNA and HPV mRNA tests.
	- **Mechanism:** HPV-DNA testing detects high-risk strains of HPV (which cause almost all cervical cancers), while HPV mRNA detects HPV infections leading to cellular transformation.
	- **Advantages:** Unlike visual inspection, it is an objective test; it is simpler, prevents more pre-cancers and cancer, and is far more cost-effective than visual inspection techniques or Pap smears.
	
	**4. MANAGEMENT & FOLLOW-UP (THE "SINGLE VISIT" APPROACH)**
	
	- To prevent loss to follow-up, screening tests like VIA are often followed by a "single visit approach" for further investigation and management at a district hospital.
	- In this approach, treatment based on colposcopy is offered to the patient during the exact same visit.
	- Pap smears and biopsies are taken simultaneously to ensure that there is a cytological and histopathological back-up for the surgical interventions.
	- In resource-limited settings, women testing positive for HPV may be treated immediately without waiting for diagnostic verification.

	1. Vaccines for cancer. 
	**VACCINES FOR CANCER (PRIMARY PREVENTION)**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION**
	
	- Vaccination is a key population-wide intervention that forms a critical part of the primary prevention of certain cancers.
	- While these vaccines do not treat existing malignancies, effective vaccines exist to prevent specific chronic viral infections that are known to be oncogenic (cancer-causing).
	
	**2. HUMAN PAPILLOMAVIRUS (HPV) VACCINE**
	
	- **Cancers Prevented:** It primarily prevents cervical cancer (for which HPV is the main cause), as well as penile and anal cancers.
	- **Target Population:** The WHO recommends vaccinating girls aged 9 to 14 years, as the vaccine works best if administered prior to exposure to the virus (before the onset of sexual activity).
	- **Male Vaccination:** Some countries have also started vaccinating boys, as the vaccine effectively prevents HPV-related cancers in males as well.
	- **Public Health Impact:** Wider provision of the HPV vaccine is expected to drastically reduce new cases of these cancers globally.
	- **Exam Essential Note:** HPV vaccination does _not_ replace the need for routine cervical cancer screening; population-based screening programmes are still essential to identify and treat pre-cancers and cancers.
	
	**3. HEPATITIS B VACCINE**
	
	- **Cancers Prevented:** Primary liver cancer (hepatocellular carcinoma).
	- **Mechanism:** It prevents infection by the Hepatitis B virus (HBV), which is a major viral and environmental cause of liver cancer.
	- **Vaccine Properties:** The currently used recombinant Hepatitis B vaccine is made by inserting a segment of the HBV gene into a yeast cell, which produces large amounts of the Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg). It is identical to the natural antigen but does not contain viral DNA and cannot produce infection.
	- **Public Health Impact:** Immunization against the Hepatitis B virus presents an exciting prospect for the prevention of primary liver cancer and drastically reduces new cases.
	1. Screening for breast cancer 
	**SCREENING FOR BREAST CANCER**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION AND RATIONALE**
	
	- There is clear evidence that screening for breast cancer has a positive and favourable effect on reducing mortality from the disease.
	- The basic techniques employed for the early detection of breast cancer range from simple self-examinations to advanced imaging modalities.
	
	**2. METHODS OF SCREENING (HIGH-YIELD)** The primary methods used for breast cancer screening are classified as follows:
	
	**A. Breast Self-Examination (BSE)**
	
	- **Concept:** All women should be actively encouraged to perform BSE.
	- **Utility:** Breast cancers are more frequently discovered by women themselves rather than by a physician during a routine clinical examination.
	- **Public Health Importance:** Although its overall effectiveness has not been adequately quantified, it remains a highly useful adjuvant to early case detection. In many developing countries, BSE is often the only feasible approach for achieving wide population coverage for a long time to come.
	
	**B. Palpation by a Physician (Clinical Breast Examination)**
	
	- A routine clinical examination performed by a physician.
	- **Limitation:** It may be unreliable and miss small tumours, particularly in large, fatty breasts.
	
	**C. Thermography**
	
	- **Advantage:** The major benefit is that the patient is not exposed to any harmful radiation.
	- **Disadvantage:** Unfortunately, it is not a highly sensitive diagnostic tool.
	
	**D. Mammography**
	
	- **Concept:** It is the most sensitive and specific method available for detecting small tumours that are sometimes missed on physical palpation.
	- **Potential Drawbacks & Limitations:**
	    1. **Radiation Exposure:** A screening mammogram may deliver a dose of up to 500 milliroentgens (compared to just 30-40 milliroentgens for a standard chest X-ray). There is medical concern regarding the accumulated radiation exposure from repeated mammographies and the paradoxical risk of radiation-induced breast cancer.
	    2. **Resource Intensive:** It requires high-standard technical equipment and highly experienced radiologists, which severely limits its widespread use for mass screening purposes.
	    3. **False-Positives:** Biopsies taken from suspicious lesions detected on mammography may end up yielding false-positive results in as many as 5 to 10 cases for every single actual case of cancer detected.
	- **Age Guidelines (Exam Essential):** Medical opinion is strongly against routine mammography in the very young. Routine X-ray mammography is **not recommended** for women under 35 years of age, unless they are symptomatic or possess a strong family history of early-onset breast cancer.
