1. Describe epidemiological triad with reference to cancer. [D09]
	**EPIDEMIOLOGICAL TRIAD IN CANCER**
	
	**1. CONCEPT OF THE TRIAD**
	
	- The traditional epidemiological triad consists of three interacting factors: the Agent, the Host, and the Environment.
	- The mere presence of these factors in the prepathogenesis period is not sufficient; an interaction among the three is required to initiate the disease process in the human host.
	- Since cancer is a chronic disease with a multifactorial aetiology, the concept of a single "agent" is often replaced by "causative factors" or "risk factors" in the advanced model of the triangle of epidemiology.
	
	**2. AGENT (CAUSATIVE) FACTORS** Environmental factors and various agents are generally held responsible for 80 to 90 per cent of all human cancers.
	
	- **Biological Agents (Viruses and Parasites):**
	    - _Hepatitis B and C viruses:_ Causally related to hepatocellular carcinoma (liver cancer).
	    - _Human Papilloma Virus (HPV):_ A chief suspect and necessary cause of cancer of the cervix.
	    - _Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV):_ Associated with Burkitt's lymphoma and nasopharyngeal carcinoma.
	    - _Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV):_ Associated with Kaposi's sarcoma and Non-Hodgkin's lymphoma.
	    - _Parasites:_ Parasitic infections like schistosomiasis in the Middle East produce carcinoma of the bladder.
	- **Chemical Agents & Occupational Exposures:**
	    - _Tobacco:_ The major environmental cause of cancers of the lung, larynx, mouth, pharynx, oesophagus, bladder, and pancreas.
	    - _Alcohol:_ Excessive intake of alcoholic beverages is associated with oesophageal and liver cancer.
	    - _Industrial Carcinogens:_ Exposure to benzene, arsenic, cadmium, chromium, vinyl chloride, asbestos, and polycyclic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are proven causative factors for various occupational cancers.
	- **Dietary Agents:**
	    - A high-fat diet is linked to breast cancer, beef consumption to bowel cancer, and smoked fish to stomach cancer.
	
	**3. HOST (INTRINSIC) FACTORS** In epidemiological terminology, the human host is referred to as the "soil" where the disease develops.
	
	- **Age:** Many chronic and degenerative diseases, including cancer, show a progressive increase in prevalence with advancing age. However, certain cancers like Hodgkin's disease, leukaemia, and female breast cancer exhibit a "bimodality," showing two separate peaks in their age incidence curves.
	- **Sex:** Variations in disease frequency between sexes are ascribed to biological, cultural, and behavioural differences. Prostate, colorectum, stomach, and liver cancers are much more common in males, while breast and cervical cancers are specific to or more common in females.
	- **Genetic Factors & Heredity:** A genetic predisposition may be involved in 10-25 per cent of cases of cancer of the breast or colon. Some cancers are clearly inherited, such as familial polyposis coli, familial non-polyposis colon cancer, and retinoblastoma. Additionally, children with Down's syndrome (mongols) are more likely to develop leukaemia.
	- **Customs, Habits, and Lifestyle:** Indigenous forms of tobacco use, such as chewing betel quid (pan) or "khaini," are strongly associated with oral cancer. The custom of "reverse smoking" of cigars (chutta) is associated with epidermoid carcinoma of the hard palate.
	
	**4. ENVIRONMENTAL (EXTRINSIC) FACTORS**
	
	- **Physical Environment:** There are numerous physical environmental factors related to cancer, including sunlight, ionizing radiation (such as X-rays and radioactive substances), and air and water pollution. Exposure to radiation can give rise to malignancies like leukaemia and skin cancer.
	- **Socio-Cultural Environment:** Cancer cervix is more common in lower socio-economic groups, largely reflecting poor genital hygiene, early marriage, early coitus, and multiple sexual partners. Conversely, breast cancer is more prevalent in higher socio-economic groups, which is explained by risk factors such as late marriage, higher age at first childbirth, and shorter periods of breast-feeding.
	- **Occupational Environment:** The workplace environment can expose workers to specific carcinogens. For example, skin cancers are a hazard among coal tar and dye-stuff workers, while bladder cancer is associated with the aniline dye, rubber, and gas industries.
	1. Primordial prevention of cancer. 
	**PRIMORDIAL PREVENTION OF CANCER**
	
	**1. CONCEPT AND DEFINITION**
	
	- Primordial prevention is considered primary prevention in its "purest sense".
	- It is defined as the prevention of the emergence or development of risk factors in countries or population groups in which they have not yet appeared.
	- **Rationale:** Many adult health problems, including chronic non-communicable diseases like cancer, have their early origins in childhood. This is because childhood is the critical time when lifestyles, such as smoking, eating patterns, and physical exercise habits, are formed.
	
	**2. AIMS AND OBJECTIVES**
	
	- The primary aim is to establish and maintain conditions that minimize hazards to health.
	- It seeks to inhibit the emergence of underlying environmental, economic, social, and behavioural conditions leading to disease causation.
	- Specific efforts are directed towards discouraging children from adopting harmful lifestyles before the risk factors can even develop.
	
	**3. TARGET POPULATION**
	
	- The interventions target the total population as well as selected groups.
	- It is achieved largely through broad public health policy and health promotion activities.
	
	**4. KEY INTERVENTIONS (HIGH-YIELD)** Because the aetiology of chronic diseases like cancer is multifactorial, prevention demands a complex mix of interventions.
	
	- **Health Education:** The main intervention in primordial prevention is through individual and mass education. This includes promoting public awareness about healthy diets and regular physical activity, effectively utilizing the mass media.
	- **Discouraging Harmful Habits:** It focuses heavily on preventing the initial uptake of tobacco and alcohol,. Tobacco use in various forms is the major environmental cause of cancers of the lung, larynx, mouth, pharynx, oesophagus, bladder, and pancreas. Excessive alcohol intake is associated with oesophageal and liver cancer.
	- **School-Based Interventions:** Establishing healthy nutrition environments in schools and implementing national physical activity guidelines, such as school-based physical activity programmes for children.
	
	**5. LEGISLATIVE AND POLICY SUPPORT** Primordial prevention requires strong governmental and legislative backing to prevent the emergence of carcinogenic risk factors at the population level. Key policy options include:
	
	- **Tobacco Control:** Protecting the public from tobacco smoke by banning smoking in public places, enforcing stringent bans on tobacco advertising, promotion, and sponsorships, and raising taxes on tobacco products.
	- **Alcohol Control:** Restricting access to retailed alcohol, enforcing bans on alcohol advertising, and raising taxes on alcohol.
	- **Dietary Regulations:** Placing restrictions on the marketing of foods and beverages that are high in salt, fats, and sugar, alongside implementing food taxes and subsidies to promote healthier diets.
	1. Warning (danger) signals of cancer with examples.  
	**WARNING (DANGER) SIGNALS OF CANCER**
	
	**1. INTRODUCTION & RATIONALE**
	Early detection and prompt treatment of early cancer and precancerous conditions provide the best possible protection against cancer. An important area of primary prevention is cancer education, which aims to motivate the public to seek early diagnosis and treatment by recognizing early warning signs.
	
	**2. THE 8 DANGER SIGNALS WITH CLINICAL EXAMPLES (HIGH-YIELD)**
	Cancer organizations remind the public of the following 8 early warning signs or "danger signals" of cancer:
	
	*   **A lump or hard area in the breast**
	    *   *Example:* Suggestive of **Breast Cancer**. The disease most commonly presents as a painless lump or thickening in the breast, or an alteration in the size, shape, or appearance of the breast. 
	*   **A change in a wart or mole**
	    *   *Example:* Suggestive of **Skin Cancer**, which is the most common occupational cancer (accounting for nearly 75% of occupational cancers). 
	*   **A persistent change in digestive and bowel habits**
	    *   *Example:* Suggestive of **Stomach or Colorectal Cancer**. Patients with stomach cancer often present with non-specific symptoms such as gastric discomfort, which explains why most cases are diagnosed at an advanced stage.
	*   **A persistent cough or hoarseness**
	    *   *Example:* Suggestive of **Lung or Laryngeal Cancer**. A chronic cough is a major warning sign, especially in individuals with a history of tobacco smoking, which is responsible for 80 to 90 per cent of all lung cancer cases.
	*   **Excessive loss of blood at the monthly period or loss of blood outside the usual dates**
	    *   *Example:* Suggestive of **Cervical Cancer**. Early-stage cervical cancer presents as irregular blood spotting or light bleeding between periods, postmenopausal spotting, or bleeding after sexual intercourse. 
	*   **Blood loss from any natural orifice**
	    *   *Example:* Can be indicative of cancers in the gastrointestinal or urinary tracts. For instance, **Bladder Cancer** is strongly associated with occupational exposure to aromatic amines in the dye and rubber industries and can present with blood in the urine.
	*   **A swelling or sore that does not get better**
	    *   *Example:* Suggestive of **Oral Cancer** or advanced **Breast Cancer**. For example, precancerous lesions in the mouth (like leukoplakia) can progress to invasive carcinoma if the sore does not heal. Similarly, breast wounds or ulcerations that erode through the skin and do not heal require a biopsy.
	*   **Unexplained loss of weight**
	    *   *Example:* A general systemic sign often seen in advanced stages of malignancies. For instance, patients with **Stomach Cancer** frequently complain of unexplained weight loss and fatigue.
	
	**3. PUBLIC HEALTH SIGNIFICANCE (EXAM ESSENTIALS)**
	*   **Target Population:** Cancer education regarding these danger signals must be aggressively directed at "high-risk" groups (e.g., school children, occupational groups, and youth clubs).
	*   **Impact of Screening:** As much as 75 per cent of all cancers occur in body sites that are easily accessible. Because most cancers begin as localized lesions, recognizing these danger signals when the disease is in the early stages ensures a much higher rate of cure. For instance, survival rates decrease by 2.7 times for breast cancer if detected at Stage IV compared to Stage I.
	1. Describe the method of screening for cancer cervix 
	**METHODS OF SCREENING FOR CANCER CERVIX**
	
	Cancer of the cervix follows a progressive course from epithelial dysplasia to carcinoma in situ to invasive carcinoma. This prolonged early phase (carcinoma in situ persists for >8 years on average) makes it highly amenable to early detection through screening.
	
	The primary methods of screening for cervical cancer are:
	
	**1. CYTOLOGICAL SCREENING (PAP SMEAR)**
	
	- **The Test:** The Papanicolaou (Pap) smear or cervical cytology has historically been the standard screening tool to detect pre-invasive (in situ) or pre-malignant stages.
	- **Schedule:** Current policy suggests that all women should have a Pap test at the age of 30 years, followed by screening every 3 years. A periodic pelvic examination is also recommended.
	- **Drawback:** It requires significant resources, including laboratories, equipment, and highly trained personnel, which can be expensive for developing countries.
	
	**2. VISUAL INSPECTION-BASED TESTS** These are cost-effective alternative screening methods, especially useful in resource-limited settings. Their sensitivity is comparable to cytology-based screening.
	
	- **Types:**
	    - **VIA:** Visual inspection with 5% acetic acid.
	    - **VIAM:** VIA with magnification.
	    - **VILI:** Visual inspection post-application of Lugol's iodine.
	- **VIA Procedure & Interpretation:** The cervix is visually inspected after applying a freshly prepared 5% acetic acid solution (5 ml glacial acetic acid + 95 ml distilled water).
	- **Positive VIA:** A test is considered positive if there is detection of well-defined opaque acetowhite lesions close to the squamo-columnar junction, a circum-orificial acetowhite lesion, or dense acetowhitening of an ulceroproliferative growth.
	
	**3. HPV (HUMAN PAPILLOMAVIRUS) TESTING** WHO currently strongly encourages countries to use HPV tests, including HPV-DNA and HPV mRNA tests, over visual inspection or cytology.
	
	- **Mechanism:** HPV-DNA testing detects high-risk strains of HPV (which cause almost all cervical cancers), and HPV mRNA detects infections leading to cellular transformation.
	- **Advantages:** It is an objective test, simpler, prevents more pre-cancers, and is more cost-effective than Pap smears or VIA.
	
	---
	
	**WHO SCREENING GUIDELINES & PROTOCOLS (HIGH-YIELD)**
	
	**Target Age & Frequency:**
	
	- **General Population:** Screening should start at **30 years of age** with a validated HPV test, repeated every **5 to 10 years**.
	- **Women living with HIV:** Screening must start earlier, at **25 years of age**, and must be done more frequently, every **3 to 5 years**.
	
	**The "Single Visit Approach" (Screen-and-Treat):**
	
	- To minimize loss to follow-up, screening tests (like VIA) are often followed by a "single visit approach".
	- Patients with positive results are further evaluated and managed at a district hospital on the same day.
	- Treatment is based on colposcopy, with Pap smear and biopsy acting as cytological and histopathological back-ups for the intervention. In resource-limited settings, HPV-positive women may be treated immediately without diagnostic verification.
	1. Describe the epidemiological features of oral cancers in India 
	**EPIDEMIOLOGICAL FEATURES OF ORAL CANCER IN INDIA**
	
	**1. MAGNITUDE OF THE PROBLEM**
	
	- **Global & National Burden:** Oral cancer is one of the ten most common cancers worldwide and constitutes a major public health problem in India.
	- **Leading Cancer:** In India, cancer of the lip and oral cavity is among the top three most frequent cancers in both men and women.
	
	**2. EPIDEMIOLOGICAL DETERMINANTS (RISK FACTORS)**
	
	**A. Tobacco (The Principal Risk Factor)**
	
	- Approximately 90 per cent of oral cancers in South East Asia are directly linked to tobacco chewing and smoking.
	- Epidemiological surveys in India (e.g., Ernakulam, Srikakulam, Bhavnagar) established that oral cancer and precancerous lesions occur almost exclusively among those who smoke or chew tobacco.
	- **Site of Lesion:** Oral cancer almost always occurs on the side of the mouth where the tobacco quid is typically kept.
	- **Highest Risk:** The risk of developing oral cancer is 36 times higher for individuals who sleep with the tobacco quid in their mouth compared to non-chewers.
	
	**B. Alcohol**
	
	- High concentrations of alcohol can cause oral cancer, and alcohol exhibits a strong synergistic effect when combined with tobacco use.
	
	**C. Pre-Cancerous Stage**
	
	- The natural history of the disease frequently includes a recognizable pre-cancerous stage.
	- Pre-cancerous lesions such as **leukoplakia** and **erythroplakia** can be clinically detected for up to 15 years prior to their progression into invasive carcinoma.
	
	**D. High-Risk Groups**
	
	- These include tobacco chewers, bidi smokers, users of betel quid, and individuals who have a habit of keeping a tobacco quid in the mouth while sleeping.
	
	**E. Cultural Patterns & Habits (Exam Essential)** Indigenous forms of smoking and chewing characteristic to various regions play a crucial role:
	
	- **Betel Quid (Pan):** The most common form of tobacco chewing in India, consisting of betel leaf, arecanut, lime, and tobacco.
	- **Khaini:** A mixture of sun-dried tobacco flakes and slaked lime, commonly kept in the mouth and slowly sucked.
	- **"Reverse Smoking":** The unique cultural habit of smoking a cigar (_chutta_) with the burning end inside the mouth. This is heavily prevalent in the eastern coastal regions of Andhra Pradesh and is strongly associated with **epidermoid carcinoma of the hard palate**.
	
	**3. PREVENTION AND CONTROL STRATEGIES**
	
	**A. Primary Prevention**
	
	- Oral cancer is highly amenable to primary prevention. Elimination of tobacco habits from the community can bring about a massive reduction in disease incidence.
	- Interventions include intensive public education, motivating lifestyle changes, and strong legislative measures to ban or restrict the sale of tobacco.
	
	**B. Secondary Prevention (Early Detection)**
	
	- Since the oral cavity is easily accessible for direct physical inspection, early detection at the precancerous stage is highly feasible.
	- Leukoplakia can often be completely cured simply by the cessation of tobacco use.
	- In developing countries, over 50 per cent of oral cancers are unfortunately detected only at an advanced stage.
	- Primary health care workers (such as multipurpose workers) play a strategic role in conducting early screening during routine home visits.
1. Methods of cancer screening.  
	
	**METHODS OF CANCER SCREENING**
	
	**1. DEFINITION AND RATIONALE**
	
	- **Definition:** Cancer screening is the search for unrecognized malignancy by means of rapidly applied tests.
	- **Rationale for Screening:** It is possible and highly effective because:
	    - Malignant disease is often preceded for months or years by a premalignant lesion, the removal of which prevents cancer.
	    - Most cancers begin as localized lesions, and early detection offers a very high rate of cure.
	    - Up to 75 per cent of all cancers occur in body sites that are easily accessible for examination.
	
	**2. BROAD CATEGORIES OF SCREENING METHODS** Cancer screening approaches are broadly classified into three types:
	
	- **Mass screening by comprehensive cancer detection examination:** Involves a rapid clinical examination of one or more body sites by a physician.
	- **Mass screening at single sites:** Comprises the targeted examination of single sites, such as the uterine cervix, breast, or lung.
	- **Selective screening:** Refers to the examination of individuals thought to be at special risk, such as parous women of lower socio-economic strata (upwards of 30 years of age) for cervical cancer, or chronic smokers for lung cancer.
	
	**3. SPECIFIC SCREENING METHODS BY ORGAN SITE (HIGH-YIELD)**
	
	**A. Screening for Cervical Cancer** Organized population-based screening has markedly reduced cervical cancer incidence and mortality.
	
	- **Pap Smear (Cervical Cytology):** The standard test to detect the prolonged early phase of cancer in situ. Current policy suggests all women should have a Pap test at the age of 30 years, and then every 3 years thereafter, alongside periodic pelvic examinations.
	- **Visual Inspection Tests:** Used as cost-effective alternatives to cytology in developing countries. They include:
	    - **VIA (Visual Inspection with Acetic acid):** Inspection after application of freshly prepared 5 per cent acetic acid to detect opaque acetowhite lesions.
	    - **VIAM:** VIA with magnification.
	    - **VILI (Visual Inspection post application of Lugol's iodine):** Another alternative visual test.
	- **HPV Testing:** WHO now encourages HPV-DNA tests (detects high-risk strains causing almost all cervical cancers) and HPV mRNA tests (detects infections leading to cellular transformation). It is an objective test, simpler, and more cost-effective than visual inspection or Pap smears.
	    - _Schedule:_ Start from 30 years of age in the general population (every 5 to 10 years) and from 25 years for women living with HIV (every 3 to 5 years).
	
	**B. Screening for Breast Cancer** The basic techniques for early detection include:
	
	- **Breast Self-Examination (BSE):** All women should be encouraged to perform BSE. It is often the only feasible approach for wide population coverage in many countries, and women frequently find lumps before a physician does.
	- **Palpation by a Physician:** A clinical examination, though it may be unreliable for large fatty breasts.
	- **Thermography:** Has the advantage of no radiation exposure, but it is not a highly sensitive tool.
	- **Mammography:** The most sensitive and specific method for detecting small tumors missed on palpation.
	    - _Drawbacks:_ Potential risk from radiation exposure, requires high-standard equipment and experienced radiologists, and biopsies of suspicious lesions may yield false-positives.
	    - _Note:_ Routine mammography (X-rays) is not recommended for women under 35 years unless they are symptomatic or have a strong family history of early-onset breast cancer.
	
	**C. Screening for Lung Cancer**
	
	- **Methods:** At present, there are only two procedures capable of detecting early-stage lung cancer: chest radiograph and sputum cytology.
	- **Public Health Policy:** Mass screening for lung cancer is not recommended as a routine public health policy. It is expensive and appears to have less potential for reducing mortality compared to primary prevention (like smoking cessation).
