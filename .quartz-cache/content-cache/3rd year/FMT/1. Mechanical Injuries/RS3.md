### PEDESTRIAN INJURIES IN MOTOR VEHICLE ACCIDENTS

#### I. Classification and Mechanism

Pedestrian injuries result from mechanical forces, often involving significant deceleration. Injuries are generally categorized based on the sequence of impact:

- **Primary Impact Injuries:** Caused by the vehicle initially striking the victim (e.g., bumper).
- **Secondary Impact Injuries:** Injuries caused when the victim subsequently strikes the vehicle (e.g., hood/windshield).
- **Secondary Injuries (Tertiary Injuries):** Injuries sustained when the victim strikes fixed objects, typically the ground.

#### II. Types of Injuries Sustained

|Feature|Injury Pattern/Location|Clinical Relevance|
|:--|:--|:--|
|**Primary Impact**|**Bumper Injuries:** Typically involve the legs/thighs.|Injuries may be severe (fractures of tibia/fibula) or externally minimal. Fracture is often **spiral or wedge-shaped**. In **children**, the impact is often higher, causing **fracture of the femur**.|
|**Secondary Impact**|Victim strikes the vehicle surface (e.g., hood, windshield, pillars).|May cause superficial abrasions or **severe injuries** from striking the bonnet/windscreen. Injuries may include fractures of ribs, arms, and pelvic fractures.|
|**Secondary Injuries**|Occur when the victim hits the road surface or nearby objects.|Includes **abrasions, contusions, and fractures** of various bones. Fractures of the **skull and base** are common.|
|**Specific Patterns**|**Patterned Abrasions:** The **pattern of the tyre tread**, radiator grille, or headlamp rim may be imprinted on the skin (impact abrasions).|Abrasions and bruising may show the pattern of the object causing the injury.|
|**Rear Impact**|If struck from behind, superficial **linear tears** on the abdomen or inguinal region may result from skin overstretching.|May be associated with **fracture-dislocation of the lumbar or thoracic spine**.|
|**Run Over Injuries**|Produced by shearing and crushing force of a vehicle wheel passing over the victim.|Cause crush injuries and **internal avulsion** of tissue down to the bone. Injuries run circumferentially across the body.|
|**Internal Trauma**|Severe internal injury can occur without major external marks due to the compressive/absorbable nature of the abdominal wall.|**Rupture of the aorta** (commonly below the arch) and tearing of the pulmonary trunk, arteries, and veins can occur. Liver and spleen lacerations are also possible.|

### III. Note on Whiplash Injury

**Definition:** Whiplash injury is sustained from a **violent acceleration or deceleration force** applied to the passenger or occupant (e.g., in a car collision).

**Mechanism:**

1. **Sudden Deceleration:** When a vehicle stops suddenly, the head continues to move forward (acute hyperflexion).
2. **Reactionary Hyperextension:** The head is then violently thrown backward, creating a rapid, forceful extension-flexion movement, the "whiplash".
3. **Pathophysiology:** This movement causes **contusion or laceration of the spinal cord**. It may occur **without a fracture of the vertebral column**.
4. **Injuries/Consequences:**
    - **Atlanto-occipital dislocation** is common.
    - **Fracture-dislocation** in the lower cervical spine (e.g., C5 and C6) may occur.
    - Fatal contusion or laceration of the spinal cord may occur, sometimes associated with fractures of the cervical vertebrae.
    - The **brainstem** and upper spinal cord can be injured by this motion, leading to instantaneous death in severe cases.
    - In infants, violent shaking can cause subdural haemorrhage and intraocular bleeding ("infantile whiplash syndrome").

5. COUP AND CONTRECOUP INJURY

#### I. Core Concepts & Definitions

- **Coup Injury (Coup Lesion):** Injury located directly beneath the area of impact, resulting directly from the impacting force.
    - Produced by the initial impact causing compression or local deformation of the skull.
    - Usually seen when the **head is struck** (e.g., by a blow).
- **Contrecoup Injury (Contrecoup Lesion):** Injury present in an area opposite the side of the impact.
    - Lesions result primarily from the rapid **deceleration** of the moving head (e.g., hitting a firm surface during a fall).
    - Usually seen when the **head is in motion** (e.g., a fall).

#### II. Mechanism and Pathophysiology

|Feature|Mechanism|
|:--|:--|
|**Coup**|Produced by localized compression forces or distortion of the skull bone at the point of impact.|
|**Contrecoup**|Caused by rotational forces (shear strains) as the brain glides or slides against the arresting skull. The momentum of the brain causes it to strike the inner skull wall opposite the impact site.|
|**Shearing**|Rotational or gliding movements of the cerebral hemispheres relative to the fixed brainstem cause shear strains, leading to vessel damage and contusions.|
|**Vacuum Effect**|Formation of a cavity (vacuum) opposite the point of impact as the brain lags behind the moving skull, damaging tissues at the contrecoup pole.|

#### III. Location and Types

- **Brain Contusions:** Commonly occur in the **frontal and temporal lobes**.
- **Blow to the Head (Deceleration/Acceleration Forces):**
    - **Blow to the Occipital Region:** Severe and extensive **contrecoup lesions** usually found in the frontal region.
    - **Blow to the Side (Temporal/Parietal):** Produces a lateral coup lesion and a more prominent **contrecoup contusion** on the opposite hemisphere.
    - **Blow to the Top (Vertex):** Produces minimal coup contusion but generally results in prominent contrecoup **subtemporal or uncal contusion**.
- **Coup vs. Contrecoup Severity:** Contrecoup lesions are often **more extensive** than coup lesions.

#### IV. Clinical and Forensic Relevance

- **Forensic Differentiation:** A blow usually produces **coup** contusions, while a fall usually produces **contrecoup** contusions (coup lesions being small or absent in falls).
- **Associated Pathology:** Intracranial haemorrhages, particularly **subdural haemorrhage (SDH)**, are frequently seen in association with contusions.
- **Age Considerations:** Contrecoup injuries are **rare before the age of three years**.
- **Other Organ Sites:** Contrecoup injury can also occur in the **heart and lungs**.

3. Intracranial hemorrhage - detailed
### INTRACRANIAL HAEMORRHAGE (ICH) CLASSIFICATION AND FEATURES

#### I. Extradural Haemorrhage (EDH) / Epidural Haemorrhage

- **Location:** Between the inner surface of the skull and the **dura mater**.
- **Aetiology:** Almost exclusively due to **trauma**.
- **Vessel Source:** Primarily rupture of the **Middle Meningeal Artery** (often the posterior branch). Bleeding is arterial.
- **Associated Injury:** Strongly associated (approx. 90% of cases) with an overlying **fissured fracture** of the skull, typically involving the squamous temporal bone.
- **Haematoma:** Clot is rubbery, reddish-purple, typically **ovoid or circular**. It is sharply defined and limited by the dural adherence at the skull sutures.
- **Clinical Course (Classic):** Initial loss of consciousness (concussion), followed by a **lucid interval** (occurs in 30–40% of cases), and subsequent rapid neurological deterioration (coma).
- **Fatal Mechanism:** Rapidly expanding clot increases intracranial pressure, leading to **compression of the brainstem** and respiratory failure. Secondary pontine (Duret) haemorrhages may occur.

#### II. Subdural Haemorrhage (SDH)

- **Location:** In the **subdural space** between the dura mater and the arachnoid.
- **Aetiology:** Nearly always **traumatic**, resulting from rotational acceleration/deceleration forces (shear stress). Often follows relatively slight trauma, often without skull fracture.
- **Vessel Source:** Primarily rupture of **bridging/communicating veins** near the sagittal sinus. Bleeding is generally venous/capillary.
- **Occurrence:** Common in infants, children, and **older persons** (due to brain atrophy/shrinkage). High incidence in chronic alcoholics.
- **Haematoma:** Typically spreads as a sheet over the upper lateral surface of the cerebral hemispheres. It may cover the entire hemisphere or be bilateral.
- **Classification by Onset:**
    - **Acute:** Symptoms develop rapidly/immediately.
    - **Subacute:** Symptoms develop days to 2–3 weeks post-injury.
    - **Chronic:** Symptoms develop weeks or months later; often encapsulated by fibrous membranes.

#### III. Subarachnoid Haemorrhage (SAH)

- **Location:** In the **subarachnoid space**, mixing diffusely with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).
- **Traumatic Cause:** Most **common form of traumatic ICH**. Caused by lacerations/contusions of the brain or rupture of bridging veins due to head injury. Common in acute alcoholism.
- **Spontaneous Cause:** Most often (95% of cases) due to rupture of a **Berry Aneurysm** (saccular aneurysm), commonly found at the base of the brain.
- **Clinical Presentation:** Severe headache (**“thunderclap headache”**), neck stiffness, and vomiting.

#### IV. Intracerebral (ICH) and Intraventricular Haemorrhage (IVH)

- **Location (ICH):** In the substance/parenchyma of the brain.
- **Traumatic ICH:** Usually occurs near the surface, typically in the frontal or temporal regions, resulting from the **coup-contrecoup** mechanism.
- **Non-Traumatic ICH (Stroke):** Spontaneous rupture of vessels, commonly in hypertensives; typical sites include the basal ganglia, pons, or cerebellum.
- **Intraventricular Haemorrhage (IVH):** Bleeding into the ventricular system; often results from the extension of a deep ICH. Traumatic IVH as a sole finding is rare.


4. Whiplash injury and its mechanism


Whiplash injury is a form of trauma sustained from a **violent acceleration or deceleration force** applied to a vehicle occupant or passenger. This injury affects the **spinal cord**.

#### Mechanism of Injury

1. **Causation:** Whiplash injury is typically caused by a violent acceleration or deceleration force applied to the individual, such as in a **car collision**.
2. **Hyperflexion/Hyperextension:** When a vehicle stops suddenly (deceleration), the head continues to move forward, leading to acute **hyperflexion**. This is followed by a reactionary phase where the head is thrown violently backward, causing acute **hyperextension**. This rapid, forceful extension-flexion movement constitutes the "whiplash".
3. **Spinal Cord Trauma:** This extreme movement causes **contusion or laceration of the spinal cord**.
4. **Associated Findings:**
    - The injury may occur **without a fracture of the vertebral column**.
    - **Atlanto-occipital dislocation** is a common consequence.
    - In the lower cervical spine (e.g., C5 and C6), **fracture-dislocation** may occur.
    - Severe cases can involve fatal contusion or laceration of the spinal cord, sometimes associated with **cervical vertebral fractures**.
    - In motor vehicle accidents, spinal injuries may occur due to **sudden hyperextension of the neck** following initial impact.
    - In the thoracic spine, fracture-dislocation may occur due to the **violent acceleration or deceleration force**.
    - The **brainstem** and upper spinal cord can be injured by this motion, potentially leading to instantaneous death in severe cases.

#### Clinical Relevance

- In infants, violent shaking can cause subdural haemorrhage and intraocular bleeding, a condition referred to as **infantile whiplash syndrome**.
- When whiplash causes sudden, violent extension-flexion of the head, laceration of tendons and joint capsules, intra-articular haemorrhages, and separation of the cartilaginous lining of articular surfaces may occur.
- Immediate incapacitation or death can result from injury to the medulla or upper spinal cord, often associated with fractures and/or dislocations in the atlanto-occipital and atlanto-axial regions.
- Whiplash injury of the spinal cord is typically seen in the occupants of the front seat.
- Head restraints (head rests) help prevent hyperextension of the neck to some degree.

5. Punch drunk syndrome
### PUNCH DRUNK SYNDROME (Dementia Pugilistica)

**HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY**

- **Definition/Synonyms:** Chronic traumatic encephalopathy or dementia pugilistica.
- **Context:** Seen as a long-term sequel of **boxing injuries** and repetitive blunt force head trauma.

---

#### PATHOPHYSIOLOGY & MECHANISM

- Caused by **prolonged repetitive punching** resulting in accumulated damage.
- Mechanism involves **small haemorrhages** and **degenerative changes** in the brain tissue over time.
- Immediate fatal cases (from single severe blows) are often due to **subdural haemorrhage** (SDH), typically in the middle cranial fossae, caused by tearing of dural emissary veins.

---

#### CLINICAL FEATURES (Punchdrunk Syndrome)

|Feature|Description|
|:--|:--|
|**Onset**|May arise **years after the last injury**.|
|**Early Signs**|Deterioration in **speed and coordination**.|
|**Classic Triad / Later State**|Characterised by:1. **Speech:** Slurred speech.2. **Motor:** Stiff limbs, **ataxia**, **broad-based gait**.3. **Cognitive/Behavioral:** Defective memory, slow thought process, **outbursts of violence**, **dementia**, and a **Parkinsonian-like facial appearance**.|

---

#### PATHOLOGY / AUTOPSY FINDINGS (Chronic Cases)

- **Haemorrhages:** Old or recent **subdural, subarachnoid and intracerebral haemorrhage**.
- **Axonal Injury:** Diffuse axonal injury (DAI) and **focal ischaemic lesions**.
- **Structural Changes:** Cortical atrophy. **Thinning or tears of corpus callosum**. Slight **hydrocephalus**.
- **Microscopic/Degenerative:** Scars or patches of **gliosis**. Loss of neurones, especially in the **cerebellum and substantia nigra**. Degenerative changes in **basal ganglia, thalamus, and septal regions**.
- **Similarity:** Changes are broadly similar to those seen in **Alzheimer’s disease**.
