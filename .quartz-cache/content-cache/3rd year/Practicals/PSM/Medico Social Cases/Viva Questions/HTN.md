**HYPERTENSION (HTN) VIVA: HIGH-YIELD EXAM NOTES**

**I. DEFINITION AND CLASSIFICATION**

- **Concept:** Blood pressure in a population is distributed continuously as a bell-shaped curve with no real separation between normotension and hypertension. It is defined in an operational way.
- **Classification (Adults $\ge$ 18 years, not on drugs):**
    - **Optimal:** Systolic < 120 and Diastolic < 80 mm Hg.
    - **Normal:** Systolic 120-130 and/or Diastolic 80-84 mm Hg.
    - **High Normal:** Systolic 130-139 and/or Diastolic 85-89 mm Hg.
    - **Grade 1 Hypertension:** Systolic 140-159 and/or Diastolic 90-99 mm Hg.
    - **Grade 2 Hypertension:** Systolic 160-179 and/or Diastolic 100-109 mm Hg.
    - **Grade 3 Hypertension:** Systolic $\ge$ 180 and/or Diastolic $\ge$ 110 mm Hg.
    - **Isolated Systolic Hypertension:** Systolic $\ge$ 140 and Diastolic < 90 mm Hg.
    - _Note:_ When systolic and diastolic BP fall into different categories, the higher category should be selected to classify the individual's blood pressure.

**II. IMPORTANT PUBLIC HEALTH CONCEPTS IN HTN**

- **"Rule of Halves":** Hypertension is an "iceberg" disease. In the general population, only about half of the hypertensive subjects are aware of their condition; only about half of those aware are being treated; and only about half of those treated are considered adequately treated.
- **"Tracking" of Blood Pressure:** This is the phenomenon of the persistence of the rank order of blood pressure. Low blood pressure levels tend to remain low, and high levels tend to become higher as individuals grow older. This knowledge is applied in identifying children and adolescents "at risk" of developing hypertension in the future.

**III. EPIDEMIOLOGICAL DETERMINANTS (RISK FACTORS)** **1. Non-Modifiable Risk Factors:**

- **Age:** Blood pressure rises with age in both sexes, and the rise is greater in those with higher initial blood pressure.
- **Sex:** At adolescence, men display a higher average level, but late in life the pattern may reverse, possibly due to post-menopausal changes in women.
- **Genetic Factors:** Inheritance is polygenic; children of two hypertensive parents have a 45 per cent possibility of developing hypertension, compared to 3 per cent if both parents are normotensive.
- **Ethnicity:** Higher blood pressure levels are consistently noted in black communities compared to other ethnic groups.

**2. Modifiable Risk Factors:**

- **Obesity:** Central obesity (indicated by increased waist-to-hip ratio) is positively correlated with high blood pressure.
- **Salt Intake:** A high salt intake (7-8 g per day) increases blood pressure proportionately, while a low sodium intake lowers it.
- **Diet:** High intake of saturated fats raises blood pressure, while consumption of dietary fibre is inversely related to hypertension risk.
- **Alcohol:** High alcohol intake raises systolic pressure more than diastolic.
- **Physical Inactivity:** Sedentary lifestyle contributes to obesity, which indirectly affects blood pressure.
- **Environmental Stress:** Over-activity of the sympathetic nervous system and psychosocial factors contribute to the pathogenesis.
- **Oral Contraceptives:** The oestrogen component in combined preparations is a common cause of secondary hypertension.

**IV. PREVENTION STRATEGIES** **1. Primary Prevention:**

- **Population Strategy:** Directed at the whole population to shift the blood pressure distribution towards "biological normality". Interventions include:
    - _Nutrition:_ Reduction of salt intake (to $\le$ 5 g/day or 100 mEq/day of sodium), moderate fat intake, and adopting the DASH (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) eating plan.
    - _Weight Reduction:_ Maintaining normal body weight (BMI 18.5–24.9).
    - _Exercise Promotion:_ Regular aerobic physical activity (e.g., brisk walking for $\ge$ 30 minutes/day).
    - _Behavioural Changes:_ Stress reduction, yoga, moderation of alcohol, and smoking cessation.
- **High-Risk Strategy:** Aimed at detecting high-risk subjects (e.g., positive family history, obesity) and preventing the attainment of blood pressure levels requiring treatment.

**2. Secondary Prevention:**

- **Early Case Detection:** Population screening, though it must be linked to follow-up and sustained care.
- **Treatment:** The aim is to obtain a blood pressure below 140/90 mm Hg, and ideally 120/80 mm Hg, to reduce the incidence of stroke and renal failure.
- **Patient Compliance:** Crucial, as treatment is life-long.

**V. GUIDELINES FOR MEASUREMENT OF BLOOD PRESSURE**

- **Sources of Error:** Accurate measurements are essential due to observer errors (hearing acuity), instrumental errors (cuff too small/leaking valve), and subject errors (fear, anxiety, posture).
- **Methodology:** The sitting position is recommended over the supine position. Phase I Korotkoff sound indicates systolic, and Phase V (disappearance) indicates diastolic pressure. It should be measured at least three times over 3 minutes, recording the lowest reading.

**VI. HYPERTENSION UNDER NATIONAL PROGRAMMES (NPCDCS)**

- Under the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Cancer, Diabetes, Cardiovascular Diseases and Stroke (NPCDCS), opportunistic screening using BP measurement is carried out for populations above 30 years at the Sub-Centre level.
- Suspected cases (systolic BP > 140 or diastolic BP > 90 mm Hg) are referred to Community Health Centres (CHC) or Medical Officers for confirmation and initiation of treatment.
- Once diagnosed, patients must receive at least a month's supply of drugs from the PHC, and ASHA/ANM workers conduct regular community follow-ups to ensure treatment compliance and lifestyle modification.
